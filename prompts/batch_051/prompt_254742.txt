I used to treat the central question about iterated amplification and distillation as a purely technical one, but a recent project handoff changed my mind: every component had been built and tested to spec, the compression that made the system fast passed all its checks, and the integrated thing nonetheless did something none of us would have endorsed — and none of us could point to the single step where that was lost. I now want to open a two-hour session of my monthly AI alignment reading group (about sixteen people, mostly engineers with a few philosophy students) with a hands-on activity that makes them feel that same unease before I have named IDA or its worries. The worry, as I would put it, is this: a complex task is broken into pieces small enough for a human to evaluate reliably; the pieces are recombined (amplification) and the resulting behavior is compressed into a fast, cheap policy meant to reproduce it (distillation). It is tempting to think that if each piece was handled competently and the compression passes every piece-level check, the whole task has been preserved. What I want the participants to discover for themselves is where that presumption breaks down — how a value that lives in the whole task can quietly drop out when you only ever check the parts, and how a "competent" distillation can pass all the local tests while missing exactly what the original task was for. The idea should show up in what happens during the activity, not in what I say afterward.

Please design a runnable activity for this group: roles, materials, timing within the session, rules, a concrete complex task with a value that is plausibly lost through decomposition, a step in which they compress what they have built, and a final test that reveals the gap. Include a payoff scheme if one helps and can be run without real money, and write the debrief so that it maps specific moments in the activity onto amplification, distillation, competence, and alignment. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be explicit about which feature of real IDA the classroom version cannot reproduce and what I should supply in discussion afterwards.
