In my former life as a research scientist I spent thirty years watching disputes in my own field either resolve around a decisive experiment or quietly die when someone showed that the two sides were measuring different quantities. Since retiring I have been reading moral philosophy and moral psychology, and the long-running argument about whether purity and sanctity are genuine moral values strikes me as possibly a case of the latter kind, though I cannot yet rule out that it is a straightforward factual or normative disagreement.

The debate, as I understand it. One side treats purity as a distinct domain of moral judgement: it points to the cross-cultural ubiquity of disgust-backed prohibitions concerning food, sex, and the treatment of corpses, to the felt authority of these prohibitions, and to the fact that people commonly treat such violations as objectively wrong rather than merely distasteful. On this view, to deny that purity is a moral value is to gerrymander morality so that it fits a modern, liberal template built around harm and autonomy. The other side treats purity and disgust as a pathogen-avoidance system that social groups have co-opted for policing boundaries, and therefore as something closer to a cognitive pollutant than a source of moral knowledge: on this view, purity judgements track contamination, not wrongness, and wherever they conflict with harm and autonomy they should be overruled.

What unsettles me as a scientist is that both camps appear to accept much the same empirical record, namely the ubiquity of the norms, the recruitment of disgust, the cultural variability, and the fact that people often confabulate harm-based justifications after the fact, and yet neither side moves. That is the signature, in my experience, of a dispute that survives because the parties are not, in fact, denying the same proposition. My working hypothesis is this: the defenders are chiefly making a claim about the architecture of moral cognition, that any descriptively adequate account of moral judgement must include a distinct purity domain; the critics are chiefly making a claim about moral epistemology, that the outputs of that domain do not by default carry justificatory force and often fail when measured against harm and autonomy. If that is right, the two positions are compatible, and the apparent contradiction rests on the single word "moral" meaning, at one moment, whatever a person's moral psychology treats as wrong and, at another, whatever survives rational scrutiny. But the participants write as though they are refuting one another.

I would like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is, not an adjudication of it. Is my compatibility reading correct? Are there residual disagreements that are genuinely empirical, about the function or dysfunction of purity norms or their developmental trajectory, and residual disagreements that are genuinely normative, about the authority of unreconstructed intuition? And, what I would most value, what would actually count as progress here: a distinction drawn, a prediction tested, a normative argument run, or a recognition that the question was ill-posed? I can follow an abstract argument; please do not trouble with citations. Please write in British English, and please write as continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write an email or a letter.
