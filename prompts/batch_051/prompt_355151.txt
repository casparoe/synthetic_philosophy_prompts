I've been turning over an analogy and I want a committed verdict on whether it's deep or superficial. Take epiphenomenalism about consciousness: the view that the brain causes conscious experience—qualia—but qualia cause nothing at all. Not the wince, not the reaching for the aspirin, not the report "I'm in pain." Those are caused directly by the brain, with the quale as a byproduct, like the heat from a locomotive not driving the wheels.

The famous problem with this view is the paradox of phenomenal judgment. If the pain never causes my judgment that I'm in pain, then how is that judgment about pain, and why should the epiphenomenalist trust his own arguments about qualia? It seems like the view undercuts the very reports and beliefs it's built on.

Here's the analogy I keep coming back to. In code, comments are causally inert: the compiler ignores them, and they never affect what the program does at runtime. Yet comments can be true or false descriptions of the code, and in a well-maintained codebase they track the code reliabily. That looks like exactly what the epiphenomenalist needs: a brain state could accurately represent a quale without being caused by it, the way a comment accurately represents a function without the comment affecting execution.

But I suspect the analogy is smuggling in a designer. Comments track the code only because a programmer reads the code and writes them, with access to both. For the analogy to actually help epiphenomenalism, you'd need something like a codebase that maintains its own comments in complete causal isolation from them—and maybe the point of the paradox is precisely that you can't have that without the comments being caused in the right content-respecting way by the thing they describe. Does the code-comment analogy show that the paradox of phenomenal judgment is overstated, or does it just make vivid why the paradox bites? Take a side and defend it—don't give me the "well it depends" tour.
