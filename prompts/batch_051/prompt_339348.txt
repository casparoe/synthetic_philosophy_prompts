I've been collecting my own gut reactions about when a reason for feeling something makes the feeling fitting, versus just being a reason to get yourself into the feeling, and I cant find the principle. A demon threatens to hurt my family unless I admire him: I have a reason to bring myself to admire him, but nothing about him is admirable, and the threat doesn't change that. A stranger offers me ten grand if I can genuinely fear his harmless tarantula for a minute: again, I have a reason to make the fear happen, but the spider still isn't scary. A herpetologist I trust tells me the snake on the path is a king cobra: now I have a reason to fear it, and the snake just is fearsome. Someone pulls a kid out of a burning car: their courage is why I admire them, and they're genuinely admirable for it. So my going theory is that the right kind of reason shows the object really has the property the feeling treats it as having — danger, admirability — while the wrong kind is about the payoffs of being in the state. But then a friend is dying, and it genuinely helps her when the people close to her can honestly admire the poems she wrote when she was young, which are honestly not good. I have a strong reason to try to admire those poems, and the reason is about her welfare, not the poems. Is that one the wrong kind? And why do I still want to tell the nurse 'try to see what's good in them,' as if the poems themselves called for admiration? I'd like a principle that gets all my verdicts, or close — and if one of them has to go, tell me which and why. Also tell me what the principle says about a case I keep going back and forth on: a boss who will fire me unless I feel genuinely grateful for an afterthought favor.
