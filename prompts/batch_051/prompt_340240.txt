I'm drafting a short paper for a philosophy-of-cognitive-science seminar and I keep going back and forth on the framing, so I'd like you to sanity-check my angle. The old cognitive penetration debate ran on a fairly sharp division of labour: perception is a modular, stimulus-driven process, and the interesting question is whether beliefs, desires or expectations can leak in and change the experience itself, rather than merely changing attention or the post-perceptual judgement. The standard no-penetration reply was that the alleged top-down effects are always confounded—by attention, memory or response bias—so a clean demonstration never actually materialises. Then the landscape shifted. The dominant computational framework for perception is now predictive processing, in which perception just is the brain's top-down generative model trying to cancel its own bottom-up prediction error: priors aren't an optional intruder, they're the mechanism. And a lot of current work studies perception through end-to-end trained vision models rather than modular feed-forward ones, so even the engineering metaphors have moved on.

My instinct is that the old no-penetration position doesn't so much get refuted as loose the distinction it needed—that the debate has to be restated, not settled. But I can see a rival reading: maybe the question was always about whether specifically doxastic states (my beliefs about the world, not just the model's priors) get in, and that question survives predictive coding untouched. I want your assessment as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, with no introduction or conclusion: the premise of the no-penetration argument that predictive processing bears on most directly; whether predictive processing actually demonstrates penetration or just renames it; the strongest repair of the no-penetration line; the strongest reason to think that line is dead; and what the surviving question should be called, if anything.
