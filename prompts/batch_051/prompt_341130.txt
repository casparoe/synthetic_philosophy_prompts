I spent my working life as a natural scientist, trained to be suspicious of any quantity that lacked an operational definition—a recipe for measuring it. Perhaps for that reason, the revealed-preference approach in economics struck me, once I began reading philosophy of economics in retirement, as refreshingly hard-headed. The practice, as I understand it, is this. Economists observe only an agent's choices among affordable options and infer from those choices a preference ordering: a bundle chosen when another was available reveals preference, and utility is then introduced as a number that merely represents that ordering. Textbook theory insists the number is an index, not a measurement of some inner magnitude of satisfaction. So far, so clean.

But the discipline does not stay that clean. In welfare economics and cost–benefit analysis, the very same inferred utilities are treated as magnitudes of well-being: they get added across persons, gains are compared with losses, and a policy is declared an improvement when the winners could, in principle, compensate the losers. Meanwhile, when agents' choices violate the standard axioms, behavioral economists call the agents irrational—as though the choice axioms were norms of sound reasoning rather than just summary descriptions of behavior. The practice seems to slide between a behaviorist notion of utility and a substantive one, often within a single argument.

I would like a philosopher's reconstruction of what that practice presupposes. Specifically: What view of utility, and of the relation between choice and welfare, is the practice of revealed-preference inference committed to? Does it strictly presuppose that utility is nothing over and above a numerical representation of choice, or do its welfare applications quietly presuppose something richer—a real, at least interpersonally comparable, magnitude of well-being? Which parts of the practice are merely compatible with the austere operationalist reading, and which parts rule it out? Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories of utility could license it? And if we instead took utility to be a genuine, measurable quantity of experienced satisfaction, what exactly in economists' daily work—the axioms, the welfare claims, the policy verdicts—would have to change?

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Please write in American English. At the end, say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure of.
