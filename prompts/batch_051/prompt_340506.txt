I am a novelist, not a philosopher by training, and I have been reading about induction while sketching a book about a character who becomes obsessed with prophecy and the logic of expectation. Out of that reading I have developed a view that I have not seen stated in quite this way, and I want an honest assessment before I let it organize the novel.

I call it the principle of narrative projection. The difference between 'green' and Goodman's 'grue' — where grue is the predicate according to which emeralds observed before a chosen future time count as grue because they are green, while emeralds not observed before that time count as grue because they are blue — is not, as I understand Goodman to hold, a matter of which predicate is better entrenched in our history of actual projections. It is a matter of which generalization tells the better story of the past. We project 'all emeralds are green' because that generalization casts the observations so far as a story with stable characters and no unmotivated plot turn. We refuse to project 'all emeralds are grue' because renarrating the same observations as a tale with a scheduled, unexplained color shift violates a basic requirement of narrative: a twist must be earned, not imported by a change of vocabulary. The principle, then, is that a predicate is projectible to unobserved cases just in case the generalization it would support provides the best available narration of the observed cases so far — the fewest gratuitous reversals, the most stable objects, the tightest causal through-line.

I can already feel the circularity: 'best narration' seems to smuggle in the very expectations the principle was meant to explain. But I do not want to abandon it too quickly. Does a version of this already exist under another name — a simplicity criterion, inference to the best explanation, a coherence account of confirmation, something in the literature on projectible predicates? What does it get right, and where does it break: which cases does it wrongly count as projectible or non-projectible? What are the three strongest objections, and is any of them fatal rather than merely uncomfortable? And if it fails as an account of induction, might it still be true of something smaller: as a description of how ordinary readers, or novelists, decide which continuation of a story is the intended one?
