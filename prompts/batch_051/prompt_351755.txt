I have been trying to figure out what I actually demand when I say a criminal law is too vague, and my reactions to particular laws do not line up into a single rule. I would like to know whether there is a principle that captures them, or close, and which of my reactions I should give up if not.

Consider these hypothetical laws and my verdicts on them.

A law makes it a misdemeanor to drive faster than forty miles per hour on Maple Street. Not too vague: I know exactly what it requires, even though the number is arbitrary and may be bad traffic policy.

A law makes it a misdemeanor to drive "at a speed greater than is reasonable and prudent under the conditions." Not too vague, to my surprise: "reasonable and prudent" is open, but it tells me to consult weather, road, and traffic the way any ordinary driver does, and no legislature could post a number for every curve.

A law makes it a crime to play music "at a volume that is plainly audible inside another person's residence" between 10 p.m. and 7 a.m. Not too vague. Whether the sound is plainly audible inside the neighbor's home is an external fact one can check, even if it depends on the building.

A law makes it a crime to "annoy any person in a public park." Too vague. I cannot tell beforehand what will count, and it seems to license punishing ordinary incompatibilities of temperament.

A law makes it a crime to show a minor "any material that is inappropriate for persons of the minor's age." Too vague. "Inappropriate" has no settled boundary; thoughtful people fill it in so differently that I would not know how to comply.

A law makes it a crime, when an officer asks, "to fail to give a satisfactory account of one's presence in a public place." Too vague. Whether an account is satisfactory is left to the officer, so two people doing the same thing could be treated differently.

A law makes it a crime to walk a dog on a leash longer than six feet in a city park. Not too vague, though I think it is a foolish law. For me, vagueness is not the same as bad policy.

The hardest part is that I accept some open phrases and reject others without being sure why. Why is "plainly audible inside another person's residence" acceptable while "annoy any person" is not? Why is "reasonable and prudent under the conditions" acceptable while "inappropriate for persons of the minor's age" is not?

My half-formed principle is this: a criminal law is precise enough when a person of ordinary judgment can predict, from the law itself, which conduct it reaches by reference to an external fact or a widely shared conventional standard; it is too vague when compliance turns on a particular person's private state of mind or on an official's unguided judgment. But I can already see trouble. "Reasonable and prudent" seems no more external or conventional than "annoying," yet I accept one and reject the other. And "plainly audible" is external, but it still depends on the thickness of a stranger's walls, which I cannot know.

Is this principle salvageable, or can it be repaired? Is there a better one that gets my verdicts, or almost all of them? State it as precisely as you can, say what it would mean for a case I keep going back and forth on—a law making it a crime to "behave in a disorderly manner" in a public library—and tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate.
