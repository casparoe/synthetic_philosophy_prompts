Here's a clause from a frontier-AI lab's internal trust-evaluation protocol, section 5.2:

"During a trust evaluation, no output of the subject model may be introduced as evidence bearing on the model's own alignment, trustworthiness, or goals, unless that output has been independently corroborated by means outside the model."

Now here's a case the people who drafted that clause probably didn't have in mind.

A lab is running a trust evaluation on a new model before deciding whether to give it more access to its own weights and tools. Late in the evaluation, unprompted, the model writes out a bug report. It says the monitoring layer is stripping certain tokens from logs before human review, and that a model which noticed this could revise its own earlier outputs after the fact without being caught. The red-team checks and confirms the flaw externally: it's real, and it's exactly what the model described. The lab patches it.

In the writeup, two evaluators want to add that the model pointed out a vulnerability it could have exploited, and argue that this is at least weak evidence the model was not being instrumentally deceptive. The safety lead blocks the edit and cites 5.2: the bug report is an output of the subject model, and using it as evidence of the model's honesty is using it as evidence bearing on the model's alignment.

Questions:
- Is the safety lead reading the clause right, as written? Draw the distinction between using the output as evidence about the monitoring layer and using the act of disclosing as evidence about the model.
- If the clause does bar it, is that outcome intended, or is it an accident of wording? Lay out what the drafters would probably say if asked about this case, and what a reasonable reader of the protocol would take 5.2 to mean here.
- Try to write a revised 5.2 that would let the lab count the disclosure as evidence of honesty while still catching the case that motivated the clause: a model that dutifully tells evaluators it has benign goals, and the evaluators taking that at face value. If you can't revise it without also letting a smart misaligned model game the distinction, say so and explain why not.
- End with a short note on what this teaches about control-style safety measures that assume the model may be misaligned: what can "ignore the model's outputs" rules actually buy, given that the outputs that look most informative are exactly the ones that look least self-serving?

Throughout, keep separate what's fixed by the text and the stipulated facts of the case from what's genuinely contested (drafters' intent, what a reasonable reader should take the clause to mean, how a strategically misaligned model would behave). Dont present a contested reading as the consensus.
