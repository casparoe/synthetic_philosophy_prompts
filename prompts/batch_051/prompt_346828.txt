In debates about whether planning is overrated, a familiar analogy treats a plan as a map of the territory one expects to cross, and improvisation as navigating by the actual terrain instead. The analogy is typically used to argue that planning is overrated: since the map is not the terrain, no advance specification can substitute for reading the situation as it unfolds. Audit how far this analogy actually holds. As a philosopher of action who also knows planning theory, begin by brainstorming the correspondences — map, terrain, route, mapmaker, traveler, scale, legend, the act of navigation — and then identify where the mapping breaks down. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from those that are merely incidental. Attend especially to what the analogy quietly imports: that the terrain exists independently of the map, that the map can be checked against it, that the traveler is a single agent facing a stable landscape, and that the map does not itself help constitute the territory. Finally, say whether the conclusion that planning is overrated survives once these disanalogies are on the table, and offer a better analogy if one is available.
