I'm a journalist who covers behavioral economics and organizational decision making, and there is a recurring pattern in my reporting that I've never had a precise term for. In one case, two logistics firms were bidding for a fixed-value municipal contract. With each round of concessions and added sweeteners the contract became less valuable to the eventual winner, yet neither felt able to walk away: at any given moment, stopping meant writing off everything already spent on proposals, lobbying, and preliminary hiring, while one more round might make the rival blink. In another case, two ride-sharing platforms were subsidizing drivers and passengers in the same city; executives on both sides could show on paper that even the winner might end up losing money, but each additional month of subsidies was justified as the move that would finally exhaust the other side's backers. In neither case was there an auctioneer collecting the wasted money; the resources were simply burned.

I keep reaching for the sunk-cost fallacy, but that label describes a single actor who throws good money after bad because of what they have already spent. The dynamic I'm describing is interactive: each side's additional cost changes the other side's arithmetic, so that at every step continuing can look rational even when the contest as a whole is likely to end with the winner worse off than if they had never entered. Does this configuration have an established name in decision theory or behavioral economics? How does it relate to the sunk-cost fallacy on one side and to escalation of commitment on the other? And would standard models of rational choice say that a perfectly clear-eyed agent could get trapped in it, or is the trap supposed to dissolve under full rationality?

Please analyze this as a decision theorist who also knows the behavioral-science literature. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the distinct candidate mechanisms or labels before narrowing to the one that fits best; I'm less interested in a bare name than in the anatomy of the trap — the mechanism by which locally sensible moves accumulate into a globally self-defeating contest, and the boundary between this phenomenon and mere stubbornness or poor cost accounting.
