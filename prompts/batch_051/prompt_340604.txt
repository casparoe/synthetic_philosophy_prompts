Analyze California's Proposition 13 (1978) as a case study for the principal–agent theory of direct democracy, on which ballot initiatives are justified as devices by which voters, the principal, correct a legislature, the agent, that is captured, unresponsive, or biased toward higher spending. I know the basic facts—it amended the state constitution to cap property tax rates, constrain future assessments, and impose a two-thirds legislative supermajority for subsequent state tax increases—so concentrate on the mapping rather than the narrative. Begin by specifying who the principal and agent are here, what the alleged agency failure of the 1970s was, and which features of Proposition 13 actually look like principal–agent control. Then let the case push back: an episodic majority permanently changed the decision rule under which future fiscal policy would be made, which looks less like monitoring an agent than like binding future principals. Is that kind of entrenchment an anomaly for the principal–agent view, or can the view absorb it? Conclude with what the episode shows about the theory—what a defensible principal–agent account of direct democracy would need to add, qualify, or abandon in order to handle entrenchment, temporary majorities, and the quality of voter information.
