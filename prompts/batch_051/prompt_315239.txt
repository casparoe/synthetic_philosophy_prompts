I'm in a philosophy of mathematics class, and we've been going over what it would even mean if a basic formal system turned out inconsistent. I have a bunch of loose verdicts about particular cases, but I cant find the principle that ties them together. Act as a philosopher of mathematics and logic: think through the cases carefully, maybe brainstorm a couple candidate principles first, then commit to one and defend it.

Here are my verdicts.

1. Naive set theory turned out inconsistent, via Russell's paradox, and I don't think that shows mathematics is inconsistent. It shows the theory was a bad formalization of the sets we intended.
2. If someone found a contradiction in ZFC tomorrow, I wouldn't conclude math is false or that sets don't exist; I'd expect mathematicians to patch the axioms the way they patched naive set theory, and most ordinary mathematics to survive in some weaker system.
3. But if someone found a short, checkable proof of 0=1 in Peano arithmetic, I'd be much more shaken. Not about whether 2+2=4, exactly—but about whether the natural numbers were ever pinned down by our axioms. It feels like a different kind of disaster than the Russell case.
4. If the "proof" of contradiction only counted as a proof in some nonstandard model, so no ordinary finite derivation existed, I want to say it doesn't count and poses no real threat.
5. If the inconsistency showed up only in some toy system nobody intended to use, I basically don't care.
6. But I also don't think consistency is just a safety check. Finding an inconsistency teaches us that the concepts behind the axioms were less coherent than we thought, not merely that we wrote down a bad string of symbols.

My tentative principle is roughly: an inconsistency shows a formal theory fails to pick out its intended subject matter, and how much it should shake us depends on how canonical that theory is for pinning down the intended structure. The problem is that verdict 3 treats PA as far more special than ZFC, even though both are canonical, and verdict 4 seems to need some clean distinction between standard and nonstandard proof that I can't state. And verdict 6 points away from treating it as just a modeling failure.

So: give me a precise principle (or a small set of candidates) that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, test it against my tentative principle, say which of my verdicts survive and which one(s) it forces me to give up, and apply it to a case I haven't considered. Also make explicit the distinction you're relying on between "formal system inconsistent" and "mathematical structure impossible." Be committed: if saving the principle means overturning one of my gut verdicts, say so and explain why that's the one to drop.
