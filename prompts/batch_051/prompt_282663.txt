Construct a thought experiment for a philosophy-of-punishment seminar. Its purpose is to challenge the common assumption that retributivism is the more severity-prone theory. The scenario must involve a single offender about whom a positive retributivist (treating deserved punishment as both required and bounded by proportionality) and a pure deterrence-based act-consequentialist (justifying punishment solely by expected reductions in future harm) agree that punishment is warranted, yet the consequentialist is committed to a substantially longer sentence than the retributivist. Both theories must accept the same facts about the offense, the offender's culpability, and the offender's prior record; the divergence must be generated by their different principles, not by factual disagreement or by stipulating a deterrent effect. The consequentialist's expectation of harm reduction must be made credible by features internal to the case, such as the offender's organizational role or recidivism risk. Constraints: an ordinary criminal-justice scenario, nothing science-fictional, no perfect foresight, no cited empirical studies, and keep the case description to one paragraph. Then do the check: state the sentence each theory assigns and the principle that yields it, explain why each theory cannot adopt the other's sentence, identify the single feature of the scenario doing the work, state how each verdict changes if that feature is removed, and close with the strongest reply a defender of the harsher-punishing theory could give against the case, indicating whether the reply succeeds.
