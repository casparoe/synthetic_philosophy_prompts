In my philosophy of religion reading group, two members got into a long argument about petitionary prayer to an omniscient and immutable God. I reconstructed their exchange from my notes as carefully as I could, and I would like you to adjudicate it move by move, saying who is right about what rather than just who wins overall.

Elena opened with a dilemma. If God is omniscient, God already knows what we need before we ask. If God is immutable, God cannot change what God was going to do in response to the asking. So petitionary prayer seems either redundant — asking for what God was already going to bring about — or theologically suspect — trying to move God to do something God would otherwise not have done. On classical theism, she claimed, both horns are bad.

Daniel said this is a false dilemma. A God who is outside time can eternally will the whole created order at once, including both the outcome and the prayer as one of its conditions. God does not change; God timelessly decrees a conditional structure, something like: if the prayer is offered, the outcome follows, and if not, not. Prayer can therefore be genuinely efficacious without God undergoing any change, because the request was always included in the single eternal decree.

Elena answered that this turns prayer into a mechanical trigger, not a request to which God responds. It also makes God look arbitrary, she said. A perfectly good God should want the good outcome whether or not the creature happens to ask. Unless the asking itself has some value, conditioning the good on the asking is worse than the dilemma it was meant to escape.

Daniel replied that the asking does have value. Petitionary prayer exercises dependence, trust, and attention to God, and it is part of how creatures come to align their wills with God's. God wills not only that goods exist but that they be brought about through created secondary causes, and petition is one of those secondary causes. So the arbitrariness objection, he said, assumes a world in which outcomes are the only thing that matters, and ignores the value of a world in which creatures genuinely participate in God's action.

Elena said this concedes her real point. If the value of petitionary prayer lies in trust, formation, and alignment, then the specifically petitionary content — asking for some concrete outcome — is dispensable. Thanksgiving, praise, and contemplation would do the same formative work without representing God as someone who needs to be asked. Worse, on Daniel's own account the asking does not provide God with any new information or reason, so the language of request is doing no work at all.

Daniel responded that the petitionary form is itself formative. A creature learning dependence does not merely contemplate dependence in the abstract; she makes concrete requests, and refraining from asking would be a failure of trust. He added an analogy: a good parent wants a child to ask for things even when the parent already knows the need and has already decided how to respond. The asking is not about informing or moving the parent; it is constitutive of the relationship. In the same way, the fact that God eternally wills the asking as a condition does not make the asking fake, any more than God's foreknowledge of a promise makes the promise empty.

Elena said the parent analogy fails at the exact point it is meant to prove. A human parent can be moved by the asking: the request can supply a new reason, and the parent can choose to grant or withhold partly because of how and whether the child asks. An immutable God cannot acquire reasons or be moved by a request. If the asking was always folded into God's single decree, the prayer is not a reason God acts; it is just another event in a timeline God eternally willed. That is a compatibilist rescue of prayer, she said, but it drains out the very petitionary character it was supposed to save, and makes the one who prays an instrument rather than a partner.

Daniel closed by asking why prayer should be singled out for this objection. On any doctrine of divine omniscience, human actions, requests, promises, and deliberations are all compatible with God's eternal foreknowledge, yet nobody concludes that they are all fake or pointless. If compatibilism is acceptable for those, he said, it should be acceptable for prayer as well.

Please adjudicate their exchange. For each of the moves above, say whether it is clearly mistaken, defensible but overstated, or clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall. I am especially interested in identifying where each of them overclaims, where one makes a concession that the other should have exploited, and what important considerations the two of them are both missing.
