On one common reading of ubuntu, being a person is not the same thing as being a member of Homo sapiens. A person is, as the proverb has it, a person through other people: personhood is something you acquire gradually by (a) being recognised and addressed by others, (b) taking part in shared practices of care and obligation, and (c) developing the capacities that make someone a moral agent — empathy, responsiveness to rebuke, a sense of shame or responsibility. On this view a newborn is at the becoming end of the scale, while an elder who has lived generously through others is at the other end; one can be more or less of a person.

Now take this case. A woman in her thirties was born with a condition that has left her, so far as anyone can tell, with no awareness of other people. She has never spoken, never made eye contact, never initiated an interaction, and never shown any sign of recognising a face or a voice. Her family nevertheless names her, feeds and washes her, talks to her at meals, celebrates her birthday, and treats an insult to her as an insult to the whole family. Suppose all of that care goes one way: nothing comes back.

Is she a person on the relational view? I mean person here as a term of art, not as a roundabout way of asking whether she matters or may be harmed.

Go through the criteria one by one. The bit I'm most interested in is whether the family's one-way behaviour toward her can do any of the work that other people are supposed to do in making someone a person. If you find yourself counting her as a person, say whether that is a genuine consequence of the view or a sign that you have slipped a non-relational idea of personhood back in. If you find the case shows the criteria need changing, say how they should be changed, and what the amended view then says about newborns, whose relational capacities are also mostly potential rather than actual.

If I've set the view up unfairly — for example, by treating a contested slogan as a worked-out theory, or by asking a who-counts question that the tradition would not pose in this form — say so plainly rather than answering the question as posed. Please write the answer in British English.
