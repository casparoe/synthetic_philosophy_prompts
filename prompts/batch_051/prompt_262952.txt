Since January I have been sitting on a hospital serious-incident review panel, and the disagreements that recur are nearly always about causation by omission: was a bad outcome caused by what someone did not do? We possess the facts in most cases; what we lack is a criterion. The panel agrees that an on-call paediatrician who ignored three escalating alerts whilst a child's oxygen levels dropped and never attended caused the subsequent brain injury by omission, and that the child's death was not caused by the off-duty porter in a different building who was never expected at the bedside. But the cases in between end in stalemate, and I have realised that the dispute is conceptual rather than factual. I would like a working definition of causation by omission — of when a person's not acting counts as a cause of an outcome — that classifies the following cases correctly and explains the difficult ones, without simply importing English tort law, which the panel is not tasked to apply.

The cases, as I would provisionally sort them, are these. Clear instances: (1) an on-call paediatrician ignores three pages about a deteriorating child and never attends; (2) a lifeguard on duty watches a swimmer drown without entering the water. Clear non-instances: (3) an off-duty porter in another building, who had no role in the child's care and was not at the bedside; (4) the residents of a city, none of whom donated to a fund that would have placed additional defibrillators in public buildings, after a man dies of cardiac arrest across the city. Borderline: (5) an off-duty anaesthetist visiting the ward notices the emergency and could act, but declines, saying the child is not her patient; (6) a hospital trust cancels its sepsis-recognition course to save money, and a nurse later misses an early sign in a patient who dies; (7) a patient refuses life-saving surgery, the team respects the refusal and does not operate, and the patient dies.

Please respond as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. The five points should be: the proposed definition; how it handles the two clear instances; how it handles the two clear non-instances; how it handles the three borderline cases, including any revisions they force; and which of your classifications is least secure, with a reason.
