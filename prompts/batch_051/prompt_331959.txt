I'm a third-year student taking a course on games and aesthetics, and we've been going in circles about a claim that came up in the readings: that when you appreciate a game as art, what your actually appreciating is the structure of choices and constraints the designer made — the options, trade-offs, pacing, pressure — and not mainly the graphics, story, music, or world, which is more like set dressing. I keep going back and forth on it because it makes the way people talk about walking simulators kind of mysterious: games where you mostly wander through a space and click on objects get called artistic all the time, but the actual decision-making seems really thin. I have to write a short paper defending the strongest version of the agency view, and I don't want to be stuck with the straw-man version.

Could you act as an expert philosopher of art who works on games, think carefully, and argue for the position that the aesthetic value of a game lies mainly in the quality of its designed agency rather than its presentational or narrative features? Call the relevant thing 'decision texture': the way choices are shaped, spaced, nested, and made to matter. Start with some brainstorming about the different senses of 'agency' a person could mean in a game — the mere availability of actions, the felt significance of actions, the ways a player can express themselves through their choices, and so on — then pick the sense that makes the view most plausible, state the view precisely, and defend it.

Please also handle at least these three objections, not just by brushing them off: (1) people often praise a game's art direction or writing exactly when the mechanics are minimal, so a view centered on decision texture seems to misdescribe real appreciation; (2) a game could have beautifully designed choices but treat trivial or awful subject matter, and the view would still call it aesthetically good, which seems wrong; (3) agency is something the player does, not something the designer made, so it is wierd to treat it as the artwork. Be honest about how much weight non-agential features (visuals, writing, sound) should still carry on the view you defend — I want the qualified version, not the absolutist one.
