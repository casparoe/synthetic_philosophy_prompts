I'm putting together a mini-unit for a philosophy of mind class and I want to screen Christopher Nolan's The Prestige alongside Dennett's brain-transplant story, 'Where Am I?'. But everytime I try to line them up I realize the film is doing something stranger than just "the duplicate is a different person," which is the version students always walk in with.

So here's the reading I want, in the weeds. In Dennett's piece the brain stays behind in a vat and the body goes off on a mission, radio-linked, and the puzzle is where Dennett actually is — in the vat, in the body, or just wherever his point of view seems to be. The Prestige stages the same puzzle but makes it brutal and, I think, stages two rival solutions against each other. Borden solves the transported-man problem with a double: two flesh-and-blood people with different pasts who agree to live one shared life. Angier solves it with Tesla's machine, which seems to make a copy of him each time, so that one Angier drowns in the locked tank while another appears on the balcony. From the inside, the man in the tank and the man on the balcony are both Angier, with the same memories up to the instant the machine fires, and the film never settles which is the real one — Angier says something like he never knows whether he'll be the prestige or the man in the box (not quoting exactly). That looks a lot like Dennett's answer, roughly that the question "which one is me?" has no answer beyond the point of view that's asking it. But then what do we do with the fact that one of them drowns every night? Dennett's story, if I remember it right, lets the duplicate problem sort of dissolve; the film turns the split into a murder and a life sentence.

I want the reading to do three things. First, actually put pressure on the point-of-view criterion: does the film vindicate the idea that you are wherever you seem to see from, or does it show that criterion gives out exactly when it's needed, because after the split there are two equally legitimate points of view and neither can be ruled out? Second, read the Borden/Fallon arrangement and the Angier machine through the same lens — a shared life built out of two different pasts versus one past branching into many lives — and say what that contrast shows about whether being the same person is more about a shared history or a shared ongoing consciousness. Third, be honest about where the film resists the Dennett reading: which scenes or details don't fit, and where would the reading have to overread something to make them fit?

And one meta thing: tell me which part of your reading is the most fragile — for instance, does it secretly depend on reading Tesla's machine as a duplicator rather than a transporter, and how would the reading change if the film had settled that? What would have to be different about the film, or about what you take Dennett's argument to be, to change your verdict?
