I'm giving a workshop presentation to an audience of environmental and resource economists — several of whom run benefit-cost analyses for regulatory agencies — plus two climate scientists who work on irreversibility and tipping points. They treat quantitative cost-benefit analysis as the default standard for policy evaluation and are suspicious of principles that sound like activism; the climate scientists care a great deal about irreversibility and about not letting caution arguments stall urgent action. My thesis is that the precautionary principle is defensible only in a narrow, procedural form: it shifts the default in favor of the status quo when (i) the proposed action may cause a severe harm that is effectively irreversible on the relevant timescale, (ii) the uncertainty is "deep" in the sense that there is no agreed probability model over the possible harms, and (iii) the proponent cannot show that the status quo itself carries a comparable risk of irreversible harm. When all three conditions hold, the proponent must meet a higher burden — not to prove the action safe, but to make a credible case that the status quo is no safer — before proceeding. I hold that this avoids the familiar paralysis objection because the principle fires only on the conjunction of the three conditions, and that it is a correction to cost-benefit analysis rather than a rejection of it.
