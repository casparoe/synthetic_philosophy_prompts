You are a professional philosopher of science who has been appointed as the neutral scribe for an adversarial collaboration on the metaphysics of causation, and the two camps have just finished a year of good-faith exchanges. Your task is to draft the joint statement they could all sign: not an adjudication of who is right, and not a dramatized dialogue, but a constructive document that makes the residual disagreement exact.

The two parties are:

Causal primitivists hold that causation is a fundamental and ineliminable feature of reality. On their view, at least some causal relations or causal powers cannot be fully reduced to non-causal facts — such as regularities, laws, probabilities, counterfactual dependence, or facts about interventions — and causal notions belong in the most basic description of the world.

Causal eliminativists hold that causation is not part of the fundamental furniture of the world. They take their starting point from the Russellian challenge: causal talk, however useful in everyday life and in the special sciences, is something a mature science progressively dispenses with, and it should not be treated as a basic feature of reality.

A third position, causal reductionism — the view that causal facts are real but entirely reducible to non-causal facts — should be treated as the important intermediate option rather than as a party to the collaboration. The document should say where each camp stands relative to reductionism, and whether a decisive argument for reductionism would move either camp, and how far.

Produce the document with these sections:

1. A numbered list of substantive claims both camps can sign. These should not be diplomatic evasions. They should cover at least: the role and usefulness of ordinary causal explanation; what the success of the causal-modeling formalism (interventions, directed acyclic graphs, structural equations) does and does not establish; what both camps accept about the status of causal vocabulary in fundamental physics as currently formulated; and what it would take for a special science to dispense with causal language.

2. A precise statement of the residual disagreement, formulated so that each side's own proponents would accept the wording of their position, with the two denied claims made explicit.

3. A crux table. Each row should state a sub-question such that settling it would genuinely move at least one camp; a concrete case, argument, or empirical finding both sides agree would bear on the sub-question; and each camp's prediction about it, recorded in advance. Consider cruxes such as these, but replace them with sharper formulations if you can: whether interventions can recover a unique causal order without causal assumptions already built in; whether the temporal asymmetry of cause and effect needs a causal ground or can be derived from non-causal features of statistical mechanics; whether the predictive and explanatory success of the special sciences would survive a full non-causal reformulation; whether a non-causal reformulation of physics could in principle recover the content of a singular causal claim such as the heating of a wire causing it to melt; and whether the absence of causal notions from current fundamental physics is evidence for eliminativism or merely an artifact of how physics is formulated.

4. A short note distinguishing the genuine disagreements from any part of the debate that is, on reflection, merely verbal.

Before drafting, spend time reconstructing the strongest version of each side, test candidate agreed claims against how a careful member of each camp would object, and revise until neither side would walk away from the document.

Write in American English, in a formal register suitable for a document both camps will put their names to. Do not include a bibliography or citations, and do not rely on outside sources; the document should stand on the arguments themselves.
