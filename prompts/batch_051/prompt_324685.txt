I run an undergraduate philosophy-and-economics reading group: roughly twenty students, two-hour sessions, no required preparation. We are about to read Keynes's essay Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren, and I want the session before the reading to be an activity that makes the group run into the underlying problem themselves instead of hearing it summarized.

The problem, as I understand it, is this. Once material scarcity is solved and work is no longer the organizing demand of a life, the difficult question—how to live well—does not disappear; it becomes the whole question. Scarcity had been doing hidden work: it gave shape to time, supplied a measure of success, and permitted most people to postpone asking what is worth doing for its own sake. Remove scarcity, and the inherited habits and metrics do not simply fade. A group of people handed complete freedom may, without any instruction, rebuild a subtle necessity out of position, measurable output, or busyness for its own sake. Keynes thought the transition would be jarring for exactly this reason, and that taking present enjoyment directly, rather than as a means to some further end, is a capacity that has to be cultivated.

Design a runnable game or structured simulation for my session, with concrete rules, materials, roles if needed, timing, and a debrief. The group size is about twenty, the room is a plain seminar room, and the total time is two hours including debrief. The activity must include at least two phases: a first phase in which participants' time is legitimately constrained by needs or tasks, followed by a second phase in which the constraint is lifted and everyone's basic requirements are announced to be satisfied for the rest of the game. The rules should be arranged so that during the second phase a work-like structure tends to re-emerge from the participants themselves—for instance, a scoreboard, a contribution ledger, a tally of produced items, or a positional ranking—and its appearance should feel chosen, not imposed by the facilitator. There should be some real but small stake, such as approval, a mild prize, or a visible record, so the pull is felt rather than merely imagined.

I want participants to come away seeing three things: (1) needs tied to subsistence are satiable, while needs tied to comparison are not, so the end of the first can leave the second quietly running the show; (2) habits and virtues formed under scarcity outlast scarcity itself, and can turn a condition of freedom back into a disguised form of coercion; (3) the deepest difficulty is not material but educational or cultural—learning to enjoy an activity for its own sake rather than as a means.

The debrief should map particular moments from the game onto these three points. It should identify the specific moment at which a participant could have resisted the re-emergence of work-like striving, and say honestly what resisting would have cost them at the time. It should also be frank about what a two-hour simulation cannot reproduce—among other things, the security of a whole life, habits built over years, and the fact that participants know they are being observed, so some apparent leisure may be performed for the facilitator. Finally, list the ways the activity is likely to go wrong when actually run—participants gaming the game, participants going passive, the group moralizing about which uses of free time are better—and explain how to handle each without lecturing. Until the debrief begins, the facilitator should not mention Keynes, the essay, or the thesis; the ideas should be produced by the game.
