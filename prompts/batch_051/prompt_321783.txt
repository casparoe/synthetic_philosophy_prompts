I have come to accept that "democratic backsliding" names a real and useful category: a distinctive pattern, mostly visible since the end of the Cold War, in which elected incumbents erode democracy gradually and through legal or semi-legal means — court-packing, changes to electoral rules that tilt the playing field, media capture, the delegitimization of rivals — without any single coup or formal suspension of the constitution. Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and, more disputedly, the United States are treated as its defining cases. Tell me what would count against this position. Which developments in those cases would have to be different for the concept to lose its grip: a full reversal of one country's erosion, a persuasive redescription of a parade case as ordinary partisan competition under high polarization, an incumbent coded as a backslider while the indicators of contestation and participation improve? Which methodological objections would have to succeed: that the concept has no non-arbitrary baseline, that it conflates democracy getting worse with democracy moving in a direction the labeler dislikes, that the verdicts are carried by a small set of coding decisions about what counts and who decides? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, a clear statement of which of them a defender of the concept should concede as genuinely counting against it, and an honest verdict on whether the concept has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if it has, whether that is a defect or the normal condition of this kind of political category.
