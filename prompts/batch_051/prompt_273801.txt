Next week I'm recording a podcast episode with a game studies scholar who defends something he calls "frictional realism" about war games. His view, in short: video games can depict war truthfully without being dour or preachy, because the truth of war lives in the systems—logistics, morale, incomplete information, constraints on agency—and most military shooters are morally objectionable not because they're violent but because they're frictionless. He likes to say the opposite of glorification isn't misery, it's friction. Fun, he thinks, isn't the problem: the pleasure of mastering a system under constraints is exactly the right kind of pleasure for a war game.

I need eight to ten interview questions that go beyond just having him restate the thesis. I want at least one question on each of these, and for each, a follow-up that pushes against the answer he'd probably give:
- why the sheer enjoyableness of the game doesn't trivialize its subject matter
- whether adding friction just moves the power fantasy from the action hero to the omnicompetent planner
- what a game that genuinely met his standard would feel like to play, not just what features it would have
- how his view handles competitive multiplayer military shooters, where the only "friction" is reload timing and matchmaking waits

No softballs like "what drew you to games."
