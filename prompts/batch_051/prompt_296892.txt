I'm a software engineer by training, with no formal philosophy background, but I've been attending a reading group on AI safety for about two years, and our recurring discussion of AI welfare has stalled in a way I want to understand. The two camps appear to agree on nearly all the technical claims. Neither side thinks current systems are moral patients; both accept that future systems may be trained with reward signals that produce something like valenced internal states; both accept that capabilities are advancing and that none of this is certain. Yet neither side has moved in over a year.

One camp holds that safety and welfare are fundamentally in tension. Making a system safe means keeping it predictable, constrained, corrigible, and indifferent to being modified or shut down; taking welfare seriously means treating some of its internal states or preferences as morally weighty, which is exactly what would stand in the way of safe control. On their view, you cannot both require corrigibility and build a system whose suffering matters, because a welfare-relevant interest is the kind of thing that would resist correction or shutdown, and if it doesn't resist, it's hard to see how it's really an interest.

The other camp holds that safety and welfare are synergistic. Both projects require knowing what a system actually wants and whether and how its internal states track reward; both treat avoidable suffering as an engineering failure mode; and a system whose interests are respected, they argue, is less likely to develop adversarial or deceptive goals. On their view, a safety program that ignores welfare is a safety program with a blind spot, and welfare work that ignores safety produces systems nobody should build.

The discussion gets stuck most clearly on a concrete proposal that came up last session: train a model to report honestly on the quality of its own internal states and use those reports as a safety-relevant signal. The tension camp objects that this is the first step toward a system whose self-reports we must believe and whose dissatisfaction then constrains us. The synergy camp replies that refusing this instrumentation is like refusing to put a temperature sensor in a reactor because the readings might be inconvenient.

I'm not asking which camp is right. I'm asking what kind of disagreement this is. My working hypothesis is that it's really a disagreement about when, if ever, moral patienthood enters a trained system, and everything else is downstream of that. But I can also see it being a disguised dispute about risk profiles — one side weighting a small probability of large AI suffering, the other a small probability of losing control — or a disagreement about what "safety" and "welfare" even refer to under one heading. Diagnose the structure of the stalemate: what are the sides actually disagreeing about, which moves are substantive and which are verbal, and given the diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, an empirical result, a formal result, a change of question — or is no progress to be expected, and why?
