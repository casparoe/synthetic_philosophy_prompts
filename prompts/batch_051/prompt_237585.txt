I'm currently taking an upper-level course on the philosophy of language, and the week on normativity and linguistic correctness has spilled into my study group in a way I can't resolve. Two classmates, Elena and Marcus, argued at length about whether the phrase "I could care less" is genuinely bad grammar, after our instructor marked it as an error on a paper draft. I took notes and have reconstructed the exchange as faithfully as I can, with each move numbered. I honestly can't tell who is right, so I'd like you to adjudicate it.

(1) Elena: The instructor was right. "I could care less" says, literally, that the speaker does care at least a little, since there remains some amount of caring they could shed. So as a way of saying one does not care at all, the phrase is semantically backwards. Words compose, and this composition produces the wrong meaning; that is a grammatical and semantic defect, not merely a stylistic preference.

(2) Marcus: That only follows if you assume the meaning of a phrase is computed compositionally from its parts. But "I could care less" isn't a novel sentence assembled for the occasion; it's an idiom, a stored unit, like "head over heels" or "kick the bucket." What fixes its meaning is how the expression is actually used by millions of native speakers. Descriptively, grammar just is the systematic behavior of a speech community. Calling the phrase bad grammar is like calling American English bad because it differs from British English.

(3) Elena: There's a difference between describing what people happen to do and evaluating what they do against a standard. Standard English has a codified set of norms precisely because some contexts—academic writing, law, public communication—need a shared, stable register. Relative to that standard, "could care less" is incorrect, and that is all "bad grammar" has to mean. The fact that a usage is widespread doesn't answer the question of whether it is acceptable in formal writing.

(4) Marcus: But the standard is simply one dialect among many, historically the dialect of the powerful, and nothing about its grammar makes it linguistically superior. Besides, the phrase isn't unclear—everyone fluent understands exactly what a speaker intends by it, which is why it persists. If you insist on literal composition, you'd have to condemn perfectly respectable idioms too. The real objection isn't linguistic correctness; it's a prejudice about whose usage counts as the norm.

(5) Elena: Usage alone can't settle correctness, otherwise "irregardless" and "between you and I" would be immune to criticism merely because some people say them. We need a distinction between standard competence and nonstandard usage because institutions genuinely depend on a shared norm. And the idiom defense fails for "could care less" in particular: it is not consistently used with sarcastic intonation or stress the way an ironic construction would be, so it looks less like a live rhetorical device and more like a frozen error that speakers have stopped hearing as an error.

(6) Marcus: But "irregardless" and "between you and I" aren't random slips, either. "Between you and I" is a stable hypercorrection pattern in many varieties, and "irregardless" has a regular derivational structure; calling them errors is just the prestige dialect reasserting itself. And nobody on my side denies that we can distinguish formal from colloquial registers or teach students which variety to use where. The question is whether the formal register is inherently more grammatical, and pointing to a codified standard doesn't show that—it just shows that someone codified it.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. For each of the six moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right (you can use intermediate ratings such as mostly mistaken, mixed, or mostly right) and briefly justify the rating. Then say what each speaker is right about, what both of them are missing, and, if you had to rule, who is more right overall and why.
