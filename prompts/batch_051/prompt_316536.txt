You are a philosopher of biology who works on the problem of biological individuality. Think carefully before answering; it may help to distinguish developmental, physiological, and evolutionary criteria for organismality, and to ask what theoretical work the word “organism” is being made to do at each step in the exchange.

I heard the following exchange at a workshop on the organism concept. Two biologists were arguing about the Portuguese man o’ war (Physalia physalis). I have set it out in numbered moves.

(1) Rana: “A Portuguese man o’ war is a colony, not a single organism. Each zooid—the float, the stinging tentacles, the feeding polyps, the reproductive parts—is a modified polyp or medusa, homologous to a whole free-living animal in other hydrozoans. A siphonophore develops from one fertilized egg, but that egg buds those zooids asexually; so it is a clone colony, like a colony of genetically identical aphids, not one organism.”

(2) Samir: “That homology argument proves too much. A worker ant is also a modified whole wasp, but nobody therefore calls an ant colony one organism. The real issue is current organization, not ancestry. The man o’ war’s zooids are as specialized as organs—some only sting, some only digest, some only reproduce—and none can survive alone. They share a continuous gastrovascular system and a single body. That is physiological integration of the sort we use when we call a body an organism.”

(3) Rana: “Then a termite mound passes your test too: morphologically specialized castes, reproductive division of labor, food exchange, and sterile workers that cannot survive alone. That is why biologists call such colonies superorganisms. But a superorganism is still a society of organisms, not an organism. You cannot use ‘integrated function’ as the criterion while refusing to count the cases the criterion actually picks out.”

(4) Samir: “Not every functional integration is organismal; the man o’ war is continuous. A termite colony is a society of physically separate bodies; the man o’ war is one body, with one outer boundary and one shared internal milieu. If a termite mound were fused into a single body with a shared bloodstream, I would count it as an organism too. My criterion is not mere division of labor; it is one body envelope containing the division of labor.”

(5) Rana: “But one body envelope is too easy to come by. Conjoined twins can share a body wall, a liver, and a bloodstream and still be two organisms with two nervous systems and two first-person perspectives. Colonial sea squirts share a tunic and blood vessels and are standardly classified as colonies. The man o’ war has no centralized brain at all, so your ‘one body’ line tracks the bag, not the individual. Maybe the honest conclusion is that it is a genetic individual, a functional individual, and a developmental colony all at once, and the word ‘organism’ merely picks one of those levels depending on your project.”

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each numbered move, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then state who is more right overall and what, if anything, the two of them are both missing. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning. Do not restate the question or include any preamble.
