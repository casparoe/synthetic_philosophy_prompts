I'm a philosophy professor, but I've sat through enough ML-adjacent seminars that I can hold my own when the grad students start talking about priors and out-of-distribution generalization. My class this term is mostly ML master's students, and I want to give them a handout that takes Keynes's views on probability and uncertainty and restates them in the vocabulary they already live in, partly so they stop thinking he was just some macro guy babbling about animal spirits.

Here are the Keynesian pieces I want you to translate. I'm giving you the content, so don't worry about digging anything up; just do the translation.

1. Probability as a logical relation between propositions — not a frequency, not a subjective degree of belief. Keynes thought a statement that the evidence supports the conclusion to degree p could be true or false, in something like the way a deductive entailment is.
2. Probabilities are often non-numerical. Some hypotheses can be ordered by evidential support; some can't be compared at all. What you get is a partial order, not a full real-valued ranking.
3. The weight of argument, from the Treatise on Probability: how much evidence you have, as distinct from what probability that evidence gives. A probability of 0.75 on almost no evidence isn't the same thing as 0.75 on a mountain of evidence.
4. The risk/uncertainty distinction from the General Theory (ch. 12). Under risk you can assign probabilities. Under uncertainty — his term — you can't, at least not with any real basis. His stock examples: the price of copper in twenty years, the obsolescence of a new invention, the future rate of interest.
5. What agents do under that kind of uncertainty: fall back on convention (assuming the present will continue, copying the crowd's average opinion, etc.) and on animal spirits, his term for the spontaneous urge to action rather than inaction.

Translate all five into ML vocabulary, the way my students actually talk: aleatoric versus epistemic uncertainty, posterior over hypotheses, model class, inductive bias, out-of-distribution generalization, calibration, effective sample size, misspecification, that sort of thing. You can assume I don't need any of it defined; if you have to stretch a term passed its normal use, just say so.

Then give me the residue report, because that's the part I actually care about. Go component by component: which ones translate cleanly, which only survive with some fudging, which have no expression in the ML vocabulary at all. Then step back and tell me whether the residue is the ML vocabulary's blind spot, or a sign that Keynes's claim was looser then it looks, or some of both. I'd rather you admit a translation is strained than pretend it's clean.

For the translation, give me a table with these columns: "Keynesian piece", "Your translation", "Fidelity" (clean / forced / no expression), and "What gets lost, if anything".

After the residue report, give me a short structured summary as a JSON object. Fields: an array "components" whose entries have "piece" (one of the five), "fidelity" (same three-value scale), and "one_sentence" (the single most important thing that survives or fails to survive); then "overall_verdict" (either "vocabulary_blindspot", "keynes_unclear", or "both") and "overall_one_sentence".
