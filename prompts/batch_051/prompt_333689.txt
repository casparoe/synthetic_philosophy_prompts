I have been reading the pragmatists on truth, and I keep running into a puzzle about what exactly the disagreement between Peirce and James amounts to. Peirce's account seems to identify truth with the belief on which inquiry would converge if it were carried far enough, so that truth is in some sense independent of what any finite inquirer currently finds satisfying. James, by contrast, characterizes truth in terms of the practical difference a true idea makes for us—its ability to be integrated with the rest of experience, confirmed by later experience, and relied upon in action. The textbook contrast treats Peirce as the objective "convergence" theorist and James as the subjective "satisfaction" theorist, but I suspect that contrast papers over a deeper conceptual difference: they may be answering different questions about truth—roughly, what truth consists in versus how we recognize and adopt truths.

I would like a careful explanation of the underlying conceptual issue, not a history lesson. Please set out the strongest version of each reading, explain what each philosopher would have to resist in the other's account, and assess whether the standard contrast rests on a conflation. In doing so, address the standard objection that James's view, read as a definition of truth, seems vulnerable to the reply that a false but comforting belief can "work" for someone, and the corresponding objection that Peirce's view looks like a definition in terms of an ideal limit that no actual inquiry ever reaches. Which reading of the Peirce–James relation does the least violence to both positions? Please write your answer in American English.
