I work as a policy analyst in the office that oversees our state's Department of Corrections, and for the past year I have been compiling every decision made by the Corrections Reclassification Panel, which rules on petitions from incarcerated people seeking transfer from standard secure facilities to Halvorsen House, a small rehabilitative unit modeled loosely on Norway's Halden Prison. A friend of mine, a public defender, and I recently had a long argument about whether the Panel is actually following any principle. His view is that the Panel is, at bottom, retributive: it will never send a person convicted of a crime against a person to Halvorsen House, no matter how much they have changed. My view was that the record shows the Panel cares about demonstrated rehabilitation — program completion, a clean disciplinary record, and low assessed risk. After several hours, we had to admit that the decisions we were fighting about are consistent with both stories, because the two factors almost always line up in the record.

Here are the Panel's recent decisions:

1. Mendez: possession of a controlled substance; nonviolent; completed the substance-use program; no disciplinary infractions in 30 months; low risk; no identified victim. Transfer granted.
2. Okafor: embezzlement; nonviolent; completed all recommended programming; one minor infraction 26 months ago, nothing since; low risk; the former employer did not object. Transfer granted.
3. Whitfield: burglary of an unoccupied residence; nonviolent; completed all recommended programming; no infractions in 24 months; low risk; the homeowner declined to submit a statement. Transfer granted.
4. Carver: armed robbery of a convenience store; violent; refused the recommended violence-reduction program; three infractions in the past 12 months; moderate-to-high risk; the harmed clerk opposed. Transfer denied.
5. Vance: vehicular homicide while intoxicated; violent; completed only the first module of substance-use treatment; one infraction 6 months ago; moderate risk; the victim's daughter opposed. Transfer denied.
6. Armand: aggravated assault of an intimate partner; violent; completed anger-management but not the full violence-reduction sequence; no infractions in 18 months; high risk; the survivor opposed. Transfer denied.
7. Grimaldi: second-degree robbery; violent; completed all recommended programming; no infractions in 24 months; high risk; the victim's family opposed. Transfer denied.

Next week the Panel must decide the petition of Delgado, convicted of voluntary manslaughter seven years ago, who has served about 60 percent of the sentence. Delgado has completed every recommended program, including the full violence-reduction sequence, has had no disciplinary infractions in 36 months, and currently scores low on the risk instrument. The victim's brother has submitted a letter saying that he does not oppose the transfer and believes Delgado should be given the chance. If the Panel wants to be consistent with its own precedents, what should it decide? I am less interested in a general essay on punishment than in a decision: lay out the candidate rules that fit the record equally well, say what each predicts in Delgado, identify the closest analog among the seven cases, state what principle the Panel would be committing itself to under each possible ruling, give your best guess about the rule the Panel is actually running and your reasoning, and name a further case that would settle which rule it is. End with a single line of the form \answer{grant the transfer} or \answer{deny the transfer}.
