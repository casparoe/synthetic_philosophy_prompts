I have been designing a typeface for a school project, and I keep running into a puzzle that starts out looking like an art problem and ends up looking like a problem about categories — or maybe about what a letter even is. Consider the letter 'a'. In a serif face it has a bowl, a stem, and a small tail; in a geometric sans it is barely more than a circle with a vertical bar; in careful print it is the double-story shape you see in this sentence; in most handwriting it is a single-story loop with a leg. And then there is the capital 'A', which shares almost no parts with any of the lowercase forms, yet we call it the same letter without a second thought. At the same time, the Cyrillic letter 'а' can be exactly the same shape as a Latin lowercase 'a', but when it appears in a Russian word, I take it to be a different letter entirely. So being the letter 'a' cannot be a simple matter of matching a shape, and it cannot be a simple matter of which alphabet surrounds it — or at least I do not see how it can be.

I would like a working definition of letter identity — of what makes a written mark a token of a particular letter — using 'a' as the test case. Here are the cases I want the definition to get right. (1) A double-story serif 'a', a single-story handwritten 'a', and a capital 'A' in the same document: same letter, three quite different shapes. (2) A Cyrillic 'а' in a font where it is shape-for-shape identical to the Latin 'a' used two words earlier: I would say this is a different letter, but I want the definition to explain what makes it different. (3) A lowercase Greek alpha, which resembles a Latin 'a' in some fonts: not an 'a'. (4) A five-year-old writes "cat" but draws the 'a' mirrored, like a backward loop: I would still count it as an 'a', just badly formed. (5) A logo in which one mark is meant to work both as a stylized 'a' and as an abstract triangle, depending on how the viewer looks at it. (6) A crack in a rock that happens to have exactly the outline of a printed 'a': surely no letter at all, but I want to know what is missing. (7) An invented script in a fantasy novel in which a glyph is copied from the Latin 'a' but given a different sound and a different name: is it still the letter 'a'?

Propose the definition, run it through all seven cases, and say where it gives a surprising or wrong answer — then either revise it or defend the surprising verdict. I suspect the real account will have to mix perception, intention or convention, and the role a shape plays inside a writing system; my worry is that these tests pull in different directions, and I would like to know whether a single definition can do all of that work or whether the concept of being the letter 'a' simply comes apart at the edges.
