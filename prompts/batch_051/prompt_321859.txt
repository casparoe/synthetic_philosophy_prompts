I just spent an hour arguing with a friend about whether a person knows the bank is open on Saturday. In the low-stakes version, the person looks up the hours, sees that the bank is open, and tells a companion; getting it wrong would mean a trivial errand. In the high-stakes version, the person is in the same evidential position, but a mistake would mean a large check bounces and fees pile up. We couldn't settle whether the person knows in both cases, only in the low-stakes case, or whether the word "knows" itself shifts with the conversation. Worse, we couldn't state clearly which positions we were actually occupying. I'm leading a philosophy reading group next week, and I'd like a sorting instrument so people can locate themselves before the discussion.

Please write a decision tree, titled "Where do you stand on stakes and knowledge?", that takes a respondent from a pair of short cases through diagnostic questions to one of the standard positions on pragmatic encroachment and epistemic contextualism. Each branch node should be a concrete case judgment or a claim about what would have to be true for a pair of answers to both be correct; it should be answerable by someone who has never seen the positions' names. Each leaf should carry the name of the position (or "unnamed" if no one defends it) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Make the tree between seven and nine branch questions. The leaves should include at least: strict or purist invariantism, attributor contextualism, assessor relativism, contrastivism, moderate pragmatic encroachment, subject-sensitive invariantism, and a stronger encroachment view on which knowledge is constitutively tied to what one may properly treat as a reason for action. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names, and flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate rather than forcing a false distinction. Include at least one question aimed at separating attributor contextualism from assessor relativism, and at least one aimed at separating moderate pragmatic encroachment from subject-sensitive invariantism; if the latter pair cannot be cleanly separated, the tree should say so in the final section.

The response should have these sections, in this order and at roughly these lengths:

1. "How to use this" (about 100 words): instructions for a respondent who has never read the literature.
2. "The cases" (about 150 words): two or three short scenarios that vary only the practical stakes, written so respondents can record their judgments before seeing any theory.
3. "The tree" (about 550 words): the branch questions, each phrased as a question about the cases or about what the respondent is willing to say about them, with options labeled A and B so the scoring key can refer to them.
4. "The leaves" (about 350 words): each terminal position named and given one sentence on its most awkward commitment, plus the path that leads to it.
5. "Scoring key" (about 200 words): how to convert a path into a leaf, and how to handle mixed or inconsistent paths.
6. "Where the tree is blunt" (about 150 words): which positions it cannot separate, and which unoccupied combinations of answers look coherent but unattractive or merely unexplored.

Write it in American English, for a mixed reading group: the tree should be usable by someone encountering these positions for the first time, but the leaf descriptions should be precise enough that someone who knows the literature recognizes the views.
