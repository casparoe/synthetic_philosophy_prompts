Im taking a philosophy of psychiatry class and we've spent the last two weeks on delusions, mostly the two-factor theory and the question of whether delusions are actually beliefs or something more like imaginings. I've been trying to turn one of our discussions into an argument that the two-factor theory pushes us toward saying delusions aren't really beliefs, and I cant tell whether it goes through or whether one of the steps is doing something sneaky. Here's the argument:

(1) On the two-factor theory, a delusion arises from two things: a first factor (something like an anomalous experience) and a second factor (a deficit in the belief-evaluation system that would normally reject the content).
(2) If the belief-evaluation system is impaired, then the resulting thought is not accepted on the basis of the person's own evidence—it isn't the person's evidence-tracking machinery doing its normal job.
(3) For a state to count as a belief, it has to be responsive to evidence in the right way: not that the person always updates perfectly, but the state has to be the sort of thing that can be revised when the evidence counts against it.
(4) So a delusion, as the two-factor theory explains it, lacks a core feature of belief.
(5) Therefore, if the two-factor theory is right, delusions aren't really beliefs.

I keep going back and forth about premise (3), because it seems to overshoot: lots of ordinary irrational beliefs (prejudices, self-serving beliefs, a gambler's belief that his system works) survive evidence against them and we still call them beliefs. But maybe that just shows those aren't beliefs either and I have to bite that bullet. Or maybe the real problem is in (2), since the theory only says the evaluation deficit is local to the delusional content, not that the person's evidential assessment is switched off globally. So: is the argument valid? If it is not, tell me exactly which step fails and on what reading. If there's an equivocation or a scope problem, name the term and the two readings.
