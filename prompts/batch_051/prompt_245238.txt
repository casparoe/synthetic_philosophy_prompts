I have been reporting a feature on why the mind–body problem increasingly looks like a problem about words rather than about substances, and I keep running into an argument I found genuinely hard to shake; among philosophers of mind it is known as Hempel's dilemma. It purports to show that physicalism cannot be stated as a substantive claim at all. I want to know whether it actually works.

The argument runs as follows. (1) Physicalism is a substantive claim only if the word "physical" has a determinate meaning that does not constantly shift as physics develops. (2) There appear to be exactly two ways of fixing such a meaning: tie it to present-day physics, or tie it to a completed and ideal future physics. (3) Tie it to present-day physics, and physicalism is almost certainly false, since current physics is known to be incomplete and its posited furniture has been revised many times; a metaphysical doctrine whose content changes with every revolution in the field is not a deep truth about everything. (4) Tie it to an ideal future physics, and physicalism becomes empty or trivially true, because no one can say in advance what an ideal physics will include; if some future physics were to posit something we now call mental, that thing would count as physical merely because the ideal theory mentioned it, and the claim that everything is physical would rule nothing out. (5) There is no third alternative: any proposed specification of the physical either rests on some actual physical theory, with all its historical limitations, or defers to the unknown content of a future one. (6) Therefore physicalism has no stable, substantive content, because it is either substantive and very likely false, or true and empty; there is no reading on which it is both true and informative.

Is this argument valid? If not, tell me exactly which step fails and why. If it is valid, tell me which premise you would reject and what you would put in its place; I am particularly interested in whether the fifth step is genuinely exhaustive, or whether there is a characterization of the physical that is neither tied to current physics nor deferred to an unknowable future. And if the dilemma cannot be blocked, I would like a verdict on what is left of physicalism: a research program, a stance on explanation, a family of claims that can be restated more carefully, or something else altogether.

Please answer in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write an email or a letter.
