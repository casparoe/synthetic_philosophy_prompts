I keep noticing a parallel between two things that don't usually get compared: what deepfakes are doing to the epistemic backstop that audio and video recordings used to give us, and what a compromised certificate authority does to the root of trust in HTTPS. In both cases, verification normally bottoms out somewhere you stop checking—a recording with a plausible chain of custody, or a root cert your browser ships with—and once that bottom level is no longer trustworthy, a whole pile of downstream items that are probably fine suddenly have to be re-examined. Is this a real structural analogy, or is the shared word trust doing all the work? Give the explicit mapping (is a recording like a leaf certificate? chain of custody like a certificate chain? the deepfake maker like someone with a stolen CA signing key?), say what would have to be true on both sides for results to transfer over, and then point to the place where the mapping breaks hardest. Test it by asking whether anything like certificate transparency or revocation has a workable counterpart for ordinary people dealing with possibly-faked recordings; if it doesn't, that's a prediction the analogy makes that fails.
