I teach a second-year undergraduate course called "Democracy, Choice, and Algorithms" (about 26 students, mostly econ and PPE, one 80-minute session), and I want to stage Arrow's impossibility theorem as a classroom game rather than just proving it on the board. The students have already seen preference rankings and Condorcet cycles, but I doubt they've internalized the impossibility part. The idea is that they should run into the theorem themselves: they'll rank some real, low-stakes alternatives individually (say, which of three topics gets a bonus review session, or which of three final-exam questions gets dropped), then in small groups they have to come up with a procedure for turning their individual rankings into one group ranking. I'll impose a few eminently reasonable-looking constraints as rules of the game — the procedure has to work for any pattern of rankings people hand in, it has to respect a unanimous preference, it can't just copy one person's ranking, and it should decide between two options using only information about how people rank those two — though you should phrase these as rules of play, not as theorem axioms, because I don't want the name "Arrow" or the word "impossibility" appearing until the very end. Different groups will likely invent different procedures, which is the point: I want the class to compare their procedures afterwards and discover, concretely, that each one either blows up somewhere (produces a cycle or a tie it can't break coherently) or secretly violates one of the rules they all accepted.

Please design this as a runnable 80-minute activity: rules, materials, a payoff scheme that doesn't use real money (e.g., snack tokens, bonus points, or a "voting rights" currency usable in a later in-class vote), a timeline, and a debrief that maps each moment of play onto the conditions — unrestricted domain, Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives, non-dictatorship — only after the failure has shown up in what they did. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'm thinking some groups will just average ranks, or pick a boring tie-break rule and then complain the constraints weren't the ones they'd have choosen — and how to handle that. And be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce: I'd guess a single class period can't produce the feeling of the theorem's full "for every possible rule" generality, since we'll only see a handful of rules, so tell me what I'll need to supply myself to bridge that. You're a professional social choice theorist and experienced games-for-teaching designer, so start by brainstorming two or three candidate mechanics, then settle on one and walk me through it in detail.
