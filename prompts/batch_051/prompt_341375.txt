I keep coming back to a parallel between two things that do not usually share a conversation.

On the perceptual side: opponent-process accounts of colour vision say hue is carried by antagonistic channels, one roughly red-versus-green and one roughly blue-versus-yellow. A single channel can signal one pole or the other relative to its baseline: excited for reddish, inhibited for greenish. What it cannot do, in the normal course of seeing, is signal both poles at once. That is why reddish green and bluish yellow are called forbidden colours: not because no surface could reflect a suitable spectrum, but because the encoding itself has no state for them. They are, as I understand it, unrepresentable in the human colour architecture. And yet a brain interface that drove colour circuits directly, bypassing the retina and the ordinary cone-opponent computation, might put the system into exactly the state the opponent-process machinery rules out, potentially producing a genuinely novel quale.

On the software side, this looks structurally identical to something I deal with all the time. A type system may declare that a value must be one of a finite set of constructors, say Colour = Red | Green | Blue, together with an invariant that the program never constructs a value that is both Red and Green. The underlying memory representation, however, can hold plenty of bit patterns that correspond to no constructor or that violate the invariant. In a memory-safe language, no safe data path constructs such values, but an unsafe coercion, a cast, or direct memory access can plant one anyway. After that, the program holds a value that the semantics refuses to describe: reading it may be undefined behaviour, may yield a garbage value, or may crash.

So here is the parallel as I see it: forbidden colours are to the visual system what out-of-invariant or constructorless values are to a typed program. In both cases, a generative constraint is enforced on normal inputs, a larger set of states is physically available, and there is a bypass (unsafe code; a brain interface) that can write the forbidden state into the machinery anyway.
