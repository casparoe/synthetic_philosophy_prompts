You are a grader for an undergraduate philosophy of language course. Below are the exam question and one student's answer.

Exam question:

Consider the generic statements:

(1) Ducks lay eggs.
(2) Mosquitoes carry malaria.

Both are standardly regarded as true, even though in each case the property is instantiated by only a restricted subset of the kind — and, in the case of the mosquitoes, a very small subset. Explain what a generic statement is and how it differs from a straightforward quantified claim such as "Most ducks lay eggs." Then evaluate at least two candidate semantic analyses of generics against these two examples, state which analysis you think best explains both, and defend it against the obvious objections.

Student answer:

Generics are generalizations over kinds, not claims about how many individual members of the kind satisfy a predicate. So "Ducks lay eggs" does not mean the same as "Most ducks lay eggs." A simple majority analysis is clearly false because fewer than half of all ducks — only the mature females — actually lay eggs, and yet the generic is true. A "most" analysis would likewise wrongly predict that "Ducks lay eggs" is false.

The correct analysis is a statistical view applied to the right comparison class. "Ducks lay eggs" is true because, among the ducks capable of laying eggs — mature females — nearly all do lay eggs at some point. The apparent counterexample arises only because we were counting ducks that lack the relevant capacity. Once we restrict the quantifier to the class determined by the predicate, the majority condition is satisfied.

The same analysis handles "Mosquitoes carry malaria." The relevant comparison class is not all mosquitoes but the mosquitoes capable of carrying malaria, principally Anopheles species. Within that class, infection rates can be high, and in endemic areas most such mosquitoes do carry the parasite. So the generic is true because most members of the properly restricted class have the property.

I therefore conclude that a generic "Ks are F" is true if and only if most of the Ks that are capable of being F are in fact F. Because generics tolerate exceptions, no counterexample really threatens this view: whenever a majority fails, we simply narrow the comparison class to the Ks that are suitable for the predicate in the same way. The statistical view, suitably restricted, is therefore fully adequate on its own, with no need for vague notions like a property's being characteristic or distinctive of the kind.
