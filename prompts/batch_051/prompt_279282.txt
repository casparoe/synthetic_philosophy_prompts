I'm an econ PhD student working on market microstructure and belief formation, and I've spent the last couple weeks trying to convince a political science friend that Aumann's agreement theorem and the Milgrom–Stokey no-trade theorem are deep results about what markets can and can't do rather than just formal curiosities. In the process I compressed the two results into one neat little argument, and now I'm worried it proves to much: taken at face value it seems to say that essentially nobody should ever trade based on private information, which would be a crazy thing to conclude from an a priori consistency result. Here's the argument as I'd present it:

(1) Suppose two traders, A and B, are expected-utility maximizers with a common prior, and each has a private signal about the value of some asset.
(2) Aumann's agreement theorem: if two Bayesians share a prior, and their posterior probabilities for the same event are common knowledge between them, then those posteriors must be equal. They can't agree to disagree.
(3) Now suppose A owns the asset and considers selling to B at price p. A will sell only if, conditional on everything she knows—including that B is actually willing to buy at p—she puts the asset's expected value at or below p. B will buy only if, conditional on everything he knows—including that A is willing to sell at p—he puts the expected value at or above p. Purely informational trade means both sides strictly gain in expectation, so A's conditional expectation is strictly below p and B's is strictly above p.
(4) But the event 'A is willing to sell at p and B is willing to buy at p' is, at the point of trade, common knowledge between them. And by the reasoning behind (2), two common-prior Bayesians whose valuations of the same asset are common knowledge must have the same conditional expectation of its value given the common-knowledge event. So step (3)'s two strict inequalities can't both hold.
(5) Therefore no purely informational trade can occur between rational common-prior Bayesians at all. That's the informal punchline of the no-trade theorem.
(6) Yet actual markets are chock-full of people trading on precisely this kind of private information—equities, FX, prediction markets. So the theorem would force us to say the people trading are irational, lack common priors, or aren't trading for information at all, each of which feels like a huge concession to get out of a formalism.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I'd like a diagnosis with numbered steps: which premise or step do you reject, and what do you have to give up when you reject it? If the argument is valid under its assumptions, then tell me which premise is least costly to abandon and why—I'm most attached to (2), the agreement result, since giving that up seems to gut the whole idea that rational people can't just talk past each other forever.
