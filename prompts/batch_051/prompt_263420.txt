I’ve been helping my aunt sort through her papers and got stuck on an advance directive she signed eight years ago, when she was in the early stages of Alzheimer’s but still legally competent. The document contains a clause I hadn’t seen before:

“In the event that I am diagnosed with any progressive, irreversible cognitive impairment, and if two physicians determine that I lack capacity to understand information relevant to a particular medical decision, then any and all preferences I may have expressed prior to that determination—whether orally or in writing—shall have no authority; my health care proxy shall decide that decision solely in accordance with my present best interests, even if this conflicts with my earlier expressed wishes.”

Now my aunt is in mid-stage Alzheimer’s. Most days she cannot reliably understand treatment options, but two or three times a week she has a clear hour or two in which she recognizes us, follows a conversation, and expresses stable preferences. Last week, during one of those lucid intervals, she said she now wants antibiotics for a recurring urinary infection, and that she does not want to be hospitalized again. Her proxy, my cousin, believes hospitalization is in her current best interests and says the directive requires him to disregard her statement because the diagnosis is irreversible and she had earlier signed the clause. I find this troubling: the clause seems designed to prevent the earlier self from tyrannizing a later self, yet here the later self is speaking during a lucid interval. The drafters almost certainly imagined a continuous, permanent loss of capacity, not fluctuation.

I want the response to work through what the clause, read literally, would require; what it would require read in light of its apparent purpose; and what the people who drafted or signed it would likely say if asked about fluctuating capacity. Then say which approach should govern and why, and whether the distinction between her current lucid preferences and her earlier written preferences can be cashed out without undermining the clause entirely. Please keep the answer under 650 words, and tell me which argument or further fact—for example about the medical course, the drafting history, or the theory of personal identity—would most likely change your verdict, and where your reasoning is most fragile.
