In discussions of why some nations are rich, I keep running into a particular way of defending the institutions-first view. The move treats certain political borders as though they were random assignment in a controlled trial: the two Nogaleses on either side of the U.S.–Mexico border, the two Koreas, the lands on opposite sides of the old Habsburg and Ottoman frontiers. The thought is that geography, disease burden, and something vaguely called "deep culture" are held roughly constant on both sides of the line, while the bundle of political and economic institutions differs, so the subsequent gap in long-run prosperity can be read as an estimate of the effect of inclusive versus extractive institutions — like a difference between treatment and control arms in a drug trial. This analogy is being made to do real work in my seminar: it licenses the conclusion that institutions, not geography or culture, are the dominant cause of national wealth, and it is then used to argue that institutional reform, rather than geographic or cultural interventions, should be the priority for development policy.

I want a full audit of the analogy. First lay out what corresponds to what: the treatment, the assignment mechanism, the control condition, the trial population, and the outcome measurement. Then show where the mapping breaks. I am especially interested in the respects in which a border fails to give what random assignment gives: borders are not placed at random and are often drawn where prior economic or political differences already existed; the "treatment" is not a single variable but an inseparable bundle of legal codes, bureaucracies, property rights, and state capacities; people, goods, capital, and threats flow across the line, so the two sides are not independent units; and institutions do not operate on a fixed population the way a drug does, because they shape who stays, who leaves, and what the people on each side become over decades. Then separate the disanalogies that genuinely threaten the intended conclusion from the ones that are only cosmetic, and say how much of the institutions-first conclusion survives once it is clear the experimental analogy was carrying part of the argument. If some other analogy would carry the evidence better, say what it is and what it would license instead.
