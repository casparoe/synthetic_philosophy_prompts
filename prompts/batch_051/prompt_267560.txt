Last night a friend and I argued for hours about whether religious belief is a kind of factual belief, and the argument left me wanting an outside audit of the position I kept finding myself defending. I have written the position down as five commitments:

1. Religious beliefs are not hypotheses about the world. Someone who believes in a Last Judgment is not making a prediction about a future event; they are living by a picture that gives their choices and failures a particular kind of seriousness.
2. Ordinary empirical evidence neither confirms nor disconfirms religious belief in the way it confirms or disconfirms empirical hypotheses. Treating religious belief as though it should answer to that sort of evidence is itself a confusion.
3. Nevertheless, some religious outlooks are more truthful, coherent, and adequate than others, and a person can be genuinely mistaken in religious matters—not merely inauthentic, shallow, or psychologically unwell.
4. Religious language is not merely expressive or emotive, and it is not just moral language in disguise; it has its own kind of content.
5. Religious belief is not a private inner phenomenon. Its content is held and shown in shared practices, rituals, and forms of life, and cannot be read off from an individual's mental states alone.

I am confident this set has internal pressure, but I can no longer tell which of the apparent conflicts are real and which are just the residue of thinking of religious language on the model of science. Please audit the set. Give me exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: the five points of tension or apparent tension among these commitments, ranked from most to least severe. For each, say what would have to give, or why the appearance of tension is misleading. American English, please.
