I'm a backend engineer who's been going down a causal inference rabbit hole on the side, mostly because of some experimentation and platform work at my job. There's something I keep noticing and I can't tell weather I'm pattern-matching or whether it's actually a real structural thing.

In causal discovery from observational data, the standard algorithms seem to lean hard on two assumptions. Faithfulness says, roughly, that the conditional independencies in your data mirror the graph structure: if two variables look independent, there's no edge between them, and you won't get some exact cancellation hiding a dependency that's really there. Minimality says that among all the graphs that could fit the data, you should prefer the sparsest one — the fewest edges. These two do a huge amount of work, because otherwise the algorithm would hand you a whole menu of graphs instead of one causal story.

Now here's the parallel. My team does blameless postmortems after outages, and I swear the reasoning is the same two moves. We have a service dependency graph, and we basically only take a service to be causally involved if its metrics correlate with the incident window; if service A and service B show no correlation we assume no edge between them. That feels exactly like faithfulness. Then we search for the smallest set of changes — deploys, config flips, feature flags, migrations — that explains the symptoms. Smallest diff that fits the data. That feels exactly like minimality.

Please act as a philosopher of science who also understands distributed systems. Think it through carefully; maybe start by laying out the mapping in explicit terms before you evaluate it. Is this a genuine structural parallel or just a loose analogy? What would have to be true in a postmortem for results to actually transfer? And where does it break: the causal discovery folks know faithfulness and minimality get into trouble with latent confounders, cycles, selection bias, and unfaithful distributions — do the software versions fail the same way or differently? Also, we can run interventions in software (rollbacks, canaries, feature flags, load tests), while causal discovery is usually stuck with observational data — so are the assumptions even doing the same job in both cases? I'd genuinely rather hear where it falls apart then a list of the ways it lines up.
