I keep tripping over an asymmetry in ordinary judgements about population. A policy that causes ten thousand extra deaths in a decade — say, by chronically polluting a river — is a humanitarian scandal. A policy that means ten thousand fewer children are born over the same decade — say, by making housing unaffordable for young families — is a demographic footnote at most, and plenty of people would call it an improvement. Both leave the future population ten thousand smaller than it would otherwise have been. Is there a principle that genuinely distinguishes the two, or just a habit of treating beings who already exist as the only ones that "count"? The hard version: if death is bad because it deprives someone of future life, then the never-conceived child is deprived of just as much future life. So why should one loss count and the other not? Give me the strongest principle you can find that produces the asymmetry, and then tell me whether you would keep it.
