People who defend nudging keep leaning on the cafeteria line: the manager has to put something at eye level, so she may as well put the fruit there — and by the same logic a government or employer can set defaults (pension enrollment, organ donation) because there's no neutral arrangement anyway and no one's freedom is being taken away. I want to know how far that analogy actually carries. Give me the mapping: what's the fruit, the eye-level shelf, the lunchroom, the kid, the manager in the policy case? Then the places it breaks — differences in who is choosing and who is doing the arranging, how easy it is to walk away, whether the alternatives are visible, whether the costs compound over a lifetime, whether the chooser knows they're being steered. Then tell me whether the argument that defaults are legitimate just because choice architecture is unavoidable survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or wether the analogy was doing all the work. If a better analogy would do the job more honestly, give me that too.
