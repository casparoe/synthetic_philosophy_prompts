I co-chair my university's academic integrity committee, and we've been stuck for three meetings on a revision to the honor code after an incident last fall. A student turned in a research essay whose citations looked real but werent: three of the six sources were plausible-sounding journal articles that don't exist, generated when the student asked an LLM to find him sources and it invented them on the spot. The student's defense was that he didn't make up the citations himself, he copied them from the tool in good faith, and he had no intention to deceive anyone about whether those articles existed. The committee is split. Some want to fold this under our existing fabrication rule, which defines fabrication as knowingly presenting invented information or sources as genuine. Others say it obviously isnt fabrication on that definition, because there was no knowledge. Others want a new offense for unverified reliance on AI output. And one member keeps insisting, using Frankfurt's old distinction between lying and bullshit, that the student didn't lie—he bullshitted: he didn't care whether the citations picked out real articles, and our policy has no category for indifference to truth as distinct from deliberate deception. I need to send the committee a memo before Thursday. Should we stretch fabrication to cover this, create a separate offense, or leave the conduct outside the honor code and treat it as a citation-skills failure? Whatever you recommend, give us a bottom line we can vote on, the reasoning behind it, and an honest treatment of the strongest objection to your recommendation. Write it as continuous prose—no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables—like a long email rather than a formatted memo.
