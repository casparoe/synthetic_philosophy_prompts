I would be grateful for comments on the following draft essay, and especially on the three objections listed at the end.

**Grammatical Categories as Attractor Kinds**

It is tempting to ask whether grammatical categories—parts of speech such as noun and verb, cases such as nominative and accusative, and classes such as prepositions—carve language at its joints. The available answers often fall into a dichotomy: either these categories are real in the way a natural-kind realist would require, or they are convenient labels that grammarians impose on a continuous and unruly reality. This essay sketches an intermediate position, which I call the attractor view.

Let the space of possible grammatical systems be an abstract space whose points are complete grammars, with similarity measured by the categories those grammars employ. A category is an attractor just in case grammatical systems tend to evolve toward, and then settle into, configurations containing it. An attractor is a basin in that space, not a point every possible grammar must reach.

On this view, noun and verb are strong attractors because several pressures converge on them. A distinction between referring expressions and predicating expressions is broadly useful across a wide range of communicative purposes; the linearity of speech and the limits of short-term memory favor reusing a small inventory of distributional classes; and ambiguity avoidance favors signaling whether an expression is being used to pick out an object or to attribute a property. Core cases, such as nominative and accusative, can be treated similarly, since they encode something languages are under pressure to track: which participant is acting and which is acted upon. Prepositions look like a weaker and more language-specific attractor. Languages can express relational material through case, verb marking, or word order, so the pressure to crystallize a distinct class of prepositions is less constant.

Attractor status alone, however, licenses only a weak conclusion: it tells us where grammars tend to go, not whether the resulting categories carve anything at the joints. For the stronger claim, I propose projectibility as the measure of naturalness. A grammatical category is natural-kind-like to the degree that knowing the behavior of a few members warrants confident inference about the behavior of other members. If membership in a category is predictive across several grammatical processes—inflection, selection, linear position—then the category behaves like a kind. If membership is predictive only under a list of exceptions, it behaves like a notation of convenience.

This yields a graded picture. The core of the noun/verb contrast is strongly projectible: once an item is nominal, much follows about how it inflects, what it combines with, and what positions it occupies. Cases have a projectible core around the encoding of clausal participants, while their outer extensions—quirky case, lexicalized case, semantically opaque assignment—are less so. Prepositions are the most mixed: the class as a whole is partly negative and partly historical, and only a core of spatial relational items is strongly projectible.

If this is right, the question whether grammatical categories are natural kinds has no all-or-nothing answer; the interesting questions are gradable—how strong is the attractor, how projectible is the class, and how much of the class is core as opposed to periphery.

Three objections worry me. First, the attractor view may be unfalsifiable: if a category is widespread I credit its functional pull, if it is rare I call it a weak attractor, and if it is absent I say no attractor formed. What evidence or argument would discipline these attributions? Second, projectibility may be the wrong measure of naturalness, since institutional kinds—say, cuisines or legal statuses—are highly projectible without being natural kinds, while some natural kinds may be too local or too rare to ground confident induction. Third, the essay may overstate functional explanation: inherited categories can persist long after their original rationale has eroded, so the attractor story may redescribe historical accident rather than explain convergence.

I would especially welcome comments on whether the second objection can be met without abandoning projectibility, and on whether the core/periphery move does real work or merely postpones the problem.
