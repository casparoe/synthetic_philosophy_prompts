Below is a passage from Chris Olah and colleagues, "Zoom In: An Introduction to Circuits" (Distill, 2020). The authors state:

"Features are the fundamental unit of neural networks. They correspond to directions. They can be rigorously studied and understood."

In the gloss that follows, they write:

"We believe that neural networks consist of meaningful, understandable features. Early layers contain features like edge or curve detectors, while later layers have features like floppy ear detectors or wheel detectors."

Shortly afterward they note: "The community is divided on whether this is true."

Consider two readings of claims like these. Under the realist reading, a feature uncovered by interpretability methods is a genuine representational unit of the model: something the network itself codes for, such that calling a unit a "floppy ear detector" tracks a real property of the computation. Under the instrumentalist reading, "feature" is a useful post hoc description that an analyst imposes or projects onto a complex distributed computation, and the detector vocabulary carries no commitment to the network's having genuine representational structure. I want to know whether empirical work can genuinely pressure-test these readings apart, rather than merely produce visualizations that both sides can accept. Please propose the strongest concrete test you can — an experiment, intervention, or measurement — describe the result that would favor each reading, and then state as precisely as you can what even a clean positive result would leave philosophically unsettled. In doing so, separate the parts of this dispute that are well enough understood to be operationalized from the parts that remain genuinely contested among people who work on the topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Write your answer in American English.
