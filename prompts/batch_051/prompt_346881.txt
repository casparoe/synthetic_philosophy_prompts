Here's an asymmetry in my own epistemic practice that I can't get to cohere. Every emerald anyone has ever seen is both green and grue, where a thing is grue iff it has been examined before some future time t and is green, or has not been examined before t and is blue. The observed data fit "all emeralds are green" and "all emeralds are grue" exactly equally up to now. Yet I'd call you rational for expecting an emerald first examined right after t to be green and irrational (or at least half mad) for expecting it to be blue on grue grounds. What justifies treating these two predicates different? If you say green is simpler or more natural, tell me why that isn't just a fancy way of voicing the very preference it's meant to explain; if you say green is entrenched in our linguistic practice, explain why a predicate's having been projected a lot in the past is a reason to project it now—would that also justify projecting whatever predicates a biased or deluded community had long projected? And if you say grue is gerrymandered because it mentions a time, give me a precise rule that rules out grue without either ruling out ordinary scientific predicates or quietly assuming the answer. Is there a principle that generates both verdicts, or is the asymmetry just a fact about us rather than the evidence?
