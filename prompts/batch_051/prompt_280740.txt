I am a philosophy professor grading a midterm in an upper-level undergraduate metaphysics course on material objects and parthood. Below are the question as it appeared on the exam and one student’s answer to it. I would like you to grade this answer as I would, out of 20 points. In your reply, lead with the bottom line in the first sentence: state the numerical grade and a one-sentence overall verdict, then give the point-by-point comments that justify it. Do not preface the reply, and do not restate the question or reproduce the full answer. I want the assessment to identify what the student gets right, each error or omission with its severity, and what the correct or improved account would say.

Exam question:
“State the special composition question. Explain why it matters — in particular, why it is not a merely verbal dispute about how to use the word ‘object.’ Then explain how a sorites-style argument from vagueness is supposed to undermine moderate answers to the special composition question, and assess whether that argument succeeds.”

Student answer:
“The special composition question, introduced by Peter van Inwagen, asks: under what conditions do some things compose another thing? Nihilism answers ‘never’: composition never occurs, so strictly speaking there are no material objects at all. Universalism answers ‘always’: any plurality of objects composes a further object, no matter how scattered or gerrymandered the members. Moderate views fall in between. One moderate view is the contact view, on which some things compose an object when they are in contact. Van Inwagen’s own view is also moderate: some things compose an object exactly when their activity constitutes a life, which is why organisms exist while tables and chairs do not.

The argument from vagueness, developed by van Inwagen against the moderate views, runs as follows. If composition is restricted, then there is a sorites series running from cases in which composition definitely does not occur to cases in which it definitely does — for example, a series in which parts are gradually brought into contact. Composition is a vague matter, so there can be no sharp cut-off in the series; but restricted composition requires precisely such a cut-off. Therefore restricted composition is false, and the only options are nihilism and universalism. Van Inwagen concludes that universalism is true, since nihilism is absurd. In my view this shows that universalism is the only stable answer to the special composition question.”
