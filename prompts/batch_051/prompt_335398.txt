I keep getting caught in a disagreement with myself about state and identity in programming, and I would like someone to help me work out what would justify each possible resolution rather than to pick a side. The view I'm drawn to is a strong value-semantics picture: an expression should denote a value, values themselves never change, and what looks like an update is really a function from old values to new values. Its virtues are exactly the ones you'd expect: substitution is valid everywhere, equational reasoning becomes mechanical, aliasing bugs become impossible, concurrency becomes far easier to think about, and time almost drops out of the program—there is no 'before' and 'after' for a value, only older and newer values in a sequence. On this picture, mutation is not a primitive fact about the program's world; it is a modeling convenience, and a dangerous one, which may be exactly why mutation is so hard to think about.

But when I try to model an ordinary bank account this way, I hit a wall. Under the faithful value-semantics description, there is no single account that persists while its balance changes; there is only a series of distinct account-values, each produced from the last by an operation such as a deposit. And I cannot make myself believe that. The bank's own bookkeeping suggests otherwise: an account number names one thing over time, and a liability attaches to that one thing, not to a dated sequence of balance states. The intuition that there is one account—the same thing I opened years ago, whose balance has simply changed—is firmer for me than any commitment to a programming paradigm. I am not asking for a verdict. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes and for me to accept that the enduring account is a convenient fiction; what it would take for the value-semantics picture to be amended so the intuition survives, for instance by distinguishing an object's identity from its current state or by making time and change first-class rather than something the semantics hides; and whether there is a reading of 'the same account' on which the theory and the intuition are both true.
