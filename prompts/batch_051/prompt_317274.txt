I keep noticing a parallel between two things that are rarely discussed together, and I would like to know whether it is a real structural identity or just a loose analogy. In his late reflections on the brain, von Neumann emphasized that the nervous system achieves reliable behavior from neurons that are individually much less reliable than the components of a digital computer. The trick, on his account, is enormous parallelism and redundancy: many noisy units are combined so that the collective absorbs the failure of any one unit. This looks to me like the Condorcet jury theorem restated in hardware. In that theorem, if each voter is only slightly better than chance and votes independently, a majority of many such voters can be far more reliable than any single voter, even a comparatively expert one, and the group becomes arbitrarily reliable as its size grows. Both stories seem to say that a large collection of weakly reliable, quasi-independent elements can emulate a single highly reliable element.

Is that analogy deep or superficial? In Condorcet's theorem the units must be competent—biased toward the right answer—and their errors must be independent. What would the corresponding conditions be in von Neumann's picture of the brain, and do real neurons meet them? Conversely, the jury theorem's well-known failure under correlated errors looks as if it should predict a specific failure mode for neural redundancy. Does that transfer, or does it show the analogy breaks under pressure?

If I am misreading von Neumann or the theorem, or if the comparison trades on an equivocation between reliability as truth-tracking and reliability as computational error-tolerance, please say so outright rather than politely developing the analogy; if you think the right verdict is something other than deep or superficial, defend that.
