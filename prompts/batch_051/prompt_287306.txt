I have been rereading Hobbes's philosophy of mathematics and keep returning to his maker's-knowledge account of geometry: the reason geometry can yield demonstration rather than mere opinion is that we produce its objects ourselves — we draw and define the lines and figures, so we know the causes of the truths we prove about them. Nature, on this picture, stays conjectural precisely because it is not our artifact.

I want to test that account against the way mathematics has developed since Hobbes's time. Modern mathematics, especially after the axiomatic turn, is not in any obvious sense a matter of constructing figures: it stipulates axioms about structures — infinite sets, the real numbers, the power set operation, the axiom of choice — and it routinely proves the existence of objects that no one has constructed and that in some cases cannot be constructed by any finite procedure. Does Hobbes's maker's-knowledge account of mathematical knowledge survive this development, or did it depend on a picture of mathematics as literally producing its objects? I would like to know which exact premise of Hobbes's argument the development bears on, whether the argument can be repaired into a weaker claim that still captures why mathematics is knowable, and whether it turns out that construction was never doing the real epistemic work.
