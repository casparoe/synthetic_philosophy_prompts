I am a software engineer and the de facto lead for a platform team at a mid-sized company; I have no formal background in philosophy. Our team is responsible for deciding which automated components we can safely trust. We review every third-party or trained component that performs above our acceptance threshold, before each release cycle and whenever a component is moved into a new context. Each review gets roughly two engineer-days, the reviewer is usually a mid-level engineer without an ML background, and the final document is signed off by an engineering director who is not technical enough to re-derive the reasoning from scratch. The stakes are real but not exotic: wrong charges to customers, missed security flaws, unplanned outages, and the occasional safety-adjacent decision in field-service tooling.

We keep running into the same problem. A component passes our tests and looks impressive, but we cannot tell whether it is performing well because it actually has a grip on the task, or because it has picked up statistical correlations that happen to hold in our test set and will silently fail the moment the context shifts. I have heard an idea that, as I understand it, comes from putting Darwin and Turing side by side, and it seems exactly relevant, but I do not know how to turn it into practice. The idea is roughly this. Darwin showed how the appearance of design can be produced by a blind process of variation and selection, with no designer anywhere who understands the finished result. Turing argued that a machine could produce intelligent-looking behavior by mechanically following steps without understanding what it is doing. Taken together, they suggest an inversion of the usual assumption: competence—doing the task well on the cases you can observe—can be entirely detached from comprehension—grasping the criteria that make a behavior correct, being able to state why a particular output is right, and anticipating where the behavior will break. If that is right, then our test suite measures competence and, by itself, tells us almost nothing about comprehension.

Please write a reusable decision procedure my team can actually run on a component that is already performing above our acceptance threshold, to determine whether and where we may rely on that component. Ground the procedure in the Darwin/Turing distinction above, not in generic software risk management; I want a checklist that is a faithful engineering translation of the principle, not a set of security clichés. The procedure should include: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each question to proceed; what answer forces the review to stop with a no or with a downgrade; three or four terminal verdicts with their operational consequences; the points where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment and who owns that judgment; and an explicit statement of what the procedure cannot tell us. Then run the procedure on all three of the following cases and report the verdict for each, including—without softening it—any case where the procedure tells me something I do not want to hear.

1. A hand-coded rules engine, written four years ago by two engineers who are still on the team, that parses customer emails and classifies refund requests. It passes all its original tests and every regression test we have added. Its authors can state the rule behind each classification and can predict, for a newly phrased email, whether the engine will handle it correctly. We want to extend it to new phrasing patterns and new refund categories.

2. A computer-vision defect detector from an external vendor. It scores near-perfectly on the vendor benchmark and on our own 5,000 labeled images. However, its accuracy drops sharply when the lighting, camera batch, or lens cleanliness changes even mildly; its errors tend to be confident; and neither we nor the vendor can explain in terms of the manufacturing defect criteria why it flags some parts and not others. We want to deploy it on a new production line in a different plant.

3. An LLM-based code review assistant that agrees with our senior reviewers' accept-or-reject decision on roughly 9 out of 10 pull requests in our benchmark. We want to make it an automatic gate that blocks merges without human review for most routine changes, cutting review time roughly in half.

The procedure must be written so that a mid-level engineer who has never read Darwin or Turing can follow it and reach the same verdict that a careful reader of the principle would reach. Please write the answer in American English.
