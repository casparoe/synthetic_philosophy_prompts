I'm teaching a unit on epiphenomenalism and I want to open the class with a poem that makes the "paradox of phenomenal judgment" felt rather than just argued. Could you write a pantoum (the form where the second and fourth lines of each quatrain become the first and third lines of the next, and the final stanza circles back to the opening lines) about someone who is perfectly articulate about the redness of a tomato or the ache of a tooth, while another register of the poem describes the neural events running underneath. Here's the philosophical job I want the form to do: the first-person lines should keep returning as reports — vivid, certain, repeated — while the third-person lines keep returning as causes, the only things in the poem that actually move anything along. By the final stanza, I want the reader to feel the epiphenomenalist vertigo: the report of the redness is right there, but the redness itself never does any causal work, and the report would be exactly the same if the redness weren't there. It should be accurate enough that someone who knows the self-stultification worry (roughly: if qualia cause nothing, then our judgments about qualia can't be evidence for them) recognizes it, but still work as a poem for someone with no philosphy background.
