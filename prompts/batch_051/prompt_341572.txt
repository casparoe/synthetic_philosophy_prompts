In my retirement from the natural sciences I have turned to constitutional theory, and a question about interregna has been occupying me. Consider a written constitution that contains only the following succession clause: "Whenever the office of President falls vacant, the Prime Minister shall exercise the powers of the President for a period not exceeding sixty days, and a special presidential election shall be concluded within that period." Suppose the President dies nineteen months before the end of the term. On the same evening, a cyberattack destroys the Electoral Commission's national voter register and its only backup, and standing statute forbids holding a presidential election unless every elector has first been biometrically verified against that register. The Commission estimates that reconstruction and verification will take at least eight months. The Prime Minister announces that, because the sixty-day period is impossible to satisfy, she will continue to exercise presidential power until an election can be held. In the seventh month she signs a mutual-defence treaty and imposes an emergency levy; a suit challenges both acts as nullities on the ground that her authority lapsed on day sixty.

Work through what the clause requires in this case. Set out the answers yielded by (a) a strict textual reading, (b) a purposive reading, (c) an inquiry into what the drafters would have said had they been asked about a prolonged impossibility of elections, and (d) what a reasonable reader on day sixty would have taken the clause to mean. Then say which approach should govern, and why. I am especially interested in whether the sixty-day cap is better understood as a limit on the acting office-holder's powers or as a deadline imposed on the state's duty to hold an election, and whether anything like a doctrine of necessity may extend a time limit the text states as a maximum.
