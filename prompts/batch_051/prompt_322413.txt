I've talked myself into a position about insect pest control and I want to know how exposed it actually is. The position, roughly: the ordinary ways we kill insects — spraying crop fields, fogging for mosquitoes, killing aphids on ornamental roses — aren't justified by the reasons people actually give, and those reasons look a lot like the speciesist moves we already reject when they're made about vertebrates. Three load-bearing pieces. (1) The uncertainty about insect sentience cuts the other way from how it's usually used: if there's a real chance that a treated field produces millions of felt deaths, uncertainty is a reason for much more caution, not for writing the deaths off. (2) 'Pest' isn't a moral kind; it just means 'an organism whose interests conflict with ours,' which is exactly what's true of any animal we set out to kill — so the label cant itself do any justifying. (3) Size, lifespan, and alienness aren't obviously the right discount factors; a tiny short-lived subject can still have an interest in not being killed, even if the interest is very thin. So what would count against this? I want a map of the view's exposure: evidence, arguments, cases, or commitments that should move me, sorted from fatal or gutting down to merely awkward. Include what a defender of the view should themselves concede counts against it, not just what critics say. I also want to know where this view makes predictions or demands that a more conventional 'prudent pest management' view doesn't, so I can see where it actually risks being wrong. And tell me straight: has the uncertainty move insulated the view into unfalsifiability, and if it has, is that a genuine problem for a view of this kind?
