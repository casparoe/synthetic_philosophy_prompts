I have spent years reading rationalist-adjacent blogs and forums, and the past few months trying to get my privacy intuitions into reflective equilibrium. I have seen the standard moves — privacy as control, as secrecy, as property, as dignity — but when I actually record my reactions to concrete cases, none of them tracks the pattern cleanly. That suggests either I am missing the real principle or several of my verdicts are wrong; I would rather find the principle and let it correct the verdicts than keep adjusting on the fly.

Here are the cases and my current verdicts. Treat the verdicts as revisable, but I would like an account that preserves as many as possible and tells me plainly which ones it cannot.

1. A neighbor repeatedly flies a camera drone outside my second-floor window and films me reading in bed. Verdict: clearly wrong, and worse than merely rude — something close to trespass or voyeurism is going on.

2. My grocery store tracks my purchases through a loyalty card and sells the resulting profile to data brokers, who infer my health conditions and sell targeted ads. Verdict: wrong, but mild; a small persistent annoyance rather than an outrage.

3. A hospital strips my records of name and address and adds them to a de-identified dataset used to study treatment outcomes. No one asks me. Verdict: fine; I would be irritated if this required my consent and slowed the research down.

4. My employer reads my work email and Slack messages, including personal notes I write during my lunch break. Verdict: fine; it is their system and I behave on that assumption. But I would be angry if they circulated the embarrassing parts internally.

5. A friend tells a mutual acquaintance something I told them in confidence, a mildly embarrassing but harmless detail. Verdict: wrong, and I would think less of them.

6. A street camera logs my face as I walk to a cafe, and the city keeps the footage for thirty days. Verdict: fine.

7. A data broker combines public records — voter registration, property records, a divorce filing — to infer that I am pregnant, builds a profile, and sells it to an advertiser. Verdict: wrong, and creepier than the grocery-store case even though every input was publicly available.

8. Police retrieve my DNA from a coffee cup I threw into a public bin and run it against a cold-case database. Verdict: fine, to my own surprise; I would have predicted I would object, but I do not.

9. A stranger at a party photographs me mid-conversation and posts the photo publicly without asking. Verdict: mildly wrong, but less wrong than the grocery-store case.

10. My health insurer buys location data showing that I visited a weight-loss clinic and raises my premiums. Verdict: clearly wrong.

The tensions I cannot resolve are these. The grocery-store case feels wrong while the hospital-research case feels fine, even though both involve an organization collecting information about me in the course of a transaction and using it for purposes beyond that transaction without asking. The street-camera case feels fine while the public-records-inference case feels wrong, even though both involve only what is outwardly observable about me in public. The employer case and the friend case both involve people reading or repeating things I produced or said within a relationship, yet I trust the employer's access more than the friend's discretion; that feels as if it should be reversed, but I cannot say why it is not.

The closest I have come to a principle is something like: information about me is mine to control when it is generated inside a relationship whose implicit norms restrict further use, while observations made in public are fair game. But that does no work on the difference between the grocery store and the hospital, and it collapses on the difference between the street camera and the public-records broker. I can patch it by adding rider after rider — unless the observer creates a new sensitive fact about me, unless there is a large power asymmetry, unless the use affects my opportunities — but that feels like epicycles, not a principle.

Please give me the best one or two candidate principles you can, stated precisely enough that I could apply them to a new case without falling back on a vibe. For each, run it through all ten cases and tell me which verdicts it gets, which verdicts it forces me to drop, and how much simplification I buy by dropping each one. Then apply your preferred principle to the case I keep going back and forth on: a distant cousin uploads their DNA to a public genealogy site, and police use the partial match to identify me as a suspect in a decades-old cold case, even though I never uploaded anything and never contacted the site. I want the principle to tell me whether that is fine, wrong, or something in between, and why.
