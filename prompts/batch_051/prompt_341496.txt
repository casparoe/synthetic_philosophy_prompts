I've been trying to understand the argument that unfolded in Germany after 1945 over whether the German people as a whole were guilty for the crimes of the Nazi state. The episode I have in mind is the familiar one: the Allies prosecuted the most prominent perpetrators at Nuremberg; denazification questionnaires and tribunals reached millions of ordinary citizens; many Germans answered the charge of collective guilt by insisting they had not known, or had only obeyed orders, or had themselves suffered; and by the 1960s a younger generation had turned the accusation against its parents, while people born long afterward still argue about whether they inherit guilt or only responsibility. Karl Jaspers intervened in the early debate by distinguishing four kinds of guilt. Criminal guilt concerns violations of the law and is dealt with by courts through evidence and punishment. Political guilt is the liability citizens bear for what their state does in their name, even when they did not personally commit the acts; it is dealt with through the power of the victors and has consequences for the polity as a whole. Moral guilt concerns what an individual personally did, condoned, or failed to do, and is judged by conscience. Metaphysical guilt rests on a kind of solidarity among human beings: one can be guilty merely by having survived while others were murdered, or by failing to risk one's life to prevent it, even where no legal, political, or moral charge applies, and it is judged only, so to speak, before God.

Use Jaspers's fourfold distinction as the lens for analyzing this episode. I want a disciplined mapping of the postwar debate onto the categories — who was guilty in each sense, on what evidence, before what judge, and with what consequence — and equally an account of who the framework excludes and why. Then run the analysis in the other direction: does the distinction explain what actually took place in the German reckoning, and where does it fail or stay silent, for instance about the shame and identification passed down to descendants, the material inheritance of a state's crimes, or the political responsibility of later generations? In particular, address whether a nation can be guilty in anything but a metaphorical sense, and whether guilt as Jaspers construes it can be transmitted across generations or can only be replaced by something else. Conclude with an assessment of the categories themselves: which ones do genuine work in this case, which ones are unstable under pressure, and what the episode shows about the theory.
