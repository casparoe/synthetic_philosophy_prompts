Your position is a form of evidence-relative act consequentialism: an act is right if and only if it maximises expected value relative to the agent's evidence, and the agent's duty is to perform the right act rather than to trace actual long-run consequences. Here is my opening challenge. You say the right act is the one with the greatest expected value relative to the agent's evidence, so I will not accuse you of pretending to trace actual consequences. But consider an ordinary choice — whether to take one job rather than another, or whether to give to this charity rather than that one. The long-run effects branch through the people I will meet, the work I will do, and eventually through which particular people come to exist at all. You and I agree that most of those branches cannot be assigned calculable probabilities. If the probability distribution over far-future outcomes is fixed only within wide, horizon-dependent ranges, then the expected-value ranking of my available acts is fixed only within those ranges too: adjust one ungrounded prior and a different act comes out on top. Your criterion then seems to tell me that one and the same act is right under one assignment of credences and wrong under another, although nothing in my evidence discriminates between the two assignments. In that situation, is "choose the act with the greatest expected value" a genuine action-guiding standard, or is it a formality that borrows its authority from credences no one can justify?
