In our lab we build safety-critical software (avionics, medical devices, the works), and over the last few years the house standard has shifted hard. A correctness argument doesnt really count unless a proof assistant has formalized it and machine-checked every step, and people now brush off a hand proof as just a human review, the way youd dismiss an anecdote. The odd part is that those same people will happily lecture you about Gödel and say the incompleteness theorems show no consistent formal system can grind out all the truths of arithmetic. So I want you to reverse-engineer the norm for me. What is the machine-check-first standard presupposing about what a proof actually is, and about how knowledge gets justified when we do math or verify a system? How much of that is strictly required by the practice, and how much is just group culture? Is the practice compatible with several rival theories of mathematical justification, or does it quietly rule most of them out? And is there a real tension with what Gödel showed, or are we confusing the fact that a particular claim can be checked in a particular system with the claim that the system can decide everything? Last part: if the group instead thought of proof as mostly a social practice of convincing competent peers, what would stop mattering and what would start mattering in our standards? I'm not after a reading list, just a careful reconstruction.
