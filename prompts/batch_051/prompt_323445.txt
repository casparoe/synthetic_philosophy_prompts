I just came from a long argument with a friend about whether Bentham's felicific calculus really requires interpersonal comparisons of utility. I took the view that it can get by without them, because the procedure considers each person individually and then merely sums the resulting balances. My friend insisted this is insufficient: to add up balances across people, you need a common unit, and Bentham's own appeal to *extent* smuggles that in. We kept returning to the following passage from Chapter IV of Bentham's *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*, and I realize I am no longer sure exactly what it commits him to:

> IV. To a number of persons, with reference to each of whom to the value of a pleasure or a pain is considered, it will be greater or less, according to seven circumstances: to wit, the six preceding ones; viz.,
> - Its intensity.
> - Its duration.
> - Its certainty or uncertainty.
> - Its propinquity or remoteness.
> - Its fecundity.
> - Its purity.
> And one other; to wit:
> - Its extent; that is, the number of persons to whom it extends; or (in other words) who are affected by it.

Please help me work out what Bentham is actually claiming here. Does *extent* merely count the number of persons affected, or does it require the pleasure of one person to be measurable against the pleasure of another? More specifically, when Bentham later says to repeat the process for each person and then sum the numbers expressive of the degrees of good tendency, is he presupposing interpersonal comparability of intensities, or only that each individual-level tendency is a quantity that can be added once computed? What is the least theory-laden reading of the passage, and which of my friend's and my positions does it support? Keep the response to no more than 400 words.
