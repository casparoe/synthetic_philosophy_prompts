Consider two false statements about the same restaurant. The first is a newspaper report that the restaurant failed its health inspection last month, although no inspection ever took place. The second is a respected food critic's prediction that the restaurant "will not survive the year — that kitchen is a salmonella outbreak waiting to happen," written while the critic privately believes the kitchen is clean and knows it has passed every inspection. Each statement reaches roughly the same readers, each empties the dining room, and each closes the business. Defamation law in most common-law systems treats the first as actionable — it can ground a claim for damages — because it is a false statement of fact. The second is likely to be classed as an opinion, a forecast about the future, and is therefore protected; and even under the American refinement by which an opinion becomes actionable when it implies a false assertion of objective fact, predictions and evaluative ratings are routinely placed on the protected side of the line.

That asymmetry is the puzzle. The obvious rationales are that a prediction is not falsifiable at the moment it is made, that audiences take forecasts with a grain of salt, and that the law should not be in the business of punishing what may simply be a bad guess. But the critic's prediction is not being offered as a guess: it is presented as the output of expertise, it carries an implied assertion that the speaker has examined the evidence, and it causes at least as much damage as the false inspection report. The same difficulty arises with a credit-rating agency that downgrades a firm while its own analysts privately judge the firm sound, and with a securities analyst who publishes an "overweight" recommendation against the output of the analyst's own model.

Find the strongest principle that could justify treating knowingly unfounded forecasts and ratings differently from knowingly false factual claims. Test that principle against the cases above, and give an honest verdict on whether the distinction should be kept, narrowed, or abandoned.
