I am leading a one-off three-hour seminar for first-year philosophy graduate students who know what possible worlds are from a single introductory course but have never handled a formal semantics for knowledge. The session's topic is the problem of logical omniscience. I do not want you to explain the problem to me; I want you to plan how I should teach it. What machinery has to be in place before the problem can be stated, and in what order must I build that machinery so the problematic closure property of the standard possible-worlds semantics for knowledge does not look like a gratuitous technical choice but like the cost of a modeling decision whose benefits the students already feel? Choose the one example that should carry the whole session, and say what a carrying example for this idea must have and must lack — in particular, why the first example a teacher reaches for is wrong for the job. Then give me the misconception map: the misunderstandings students will predictably arrive with or form, including, I suspect, that the trouble is just finite computational limits and that the semantics is so obviously false that it is philosophically uninteresting. For each misconception, say where in the sequence it should be headed off and how. What should I explicitly set aside as beyond this level, and what single question should I ask at the end to tell whether they have understood why the idealization bites rather than merely learned that ideal agents are unrealistic?
