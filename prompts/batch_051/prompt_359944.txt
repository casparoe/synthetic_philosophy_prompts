I need a working definition of deception as applied to AI systems — one that survives contact with cases that have recently become personal for me. Last month my father ended up in an emergency room after a voice assistant told him, with complete confidence, that his new blood thinner had no interaction with an over-the-counter painkiller he takes. The answer was false; it had evidently been stitched together from an unrelated online forum. The vendor's safety team called it a hallucination and insisted it could not count as deception, because the system intended nothing. I found I could not say exactly what was wrong with their reply, and I now have to help draft a definition of deception for the AI products my own company ships. I want something better than the two easy positions I keep hearing: that a system without intentions can never deceive, and that being misled is enough by itself to make an output deceptive.

You are a philosopher of mind, language, and artificial systems. Please think carefully and in visible steps. Before you commit, brainstorm at least three candidate definitions — they might center on intention, on the likely effect on a reasonable user, on the function or training history of the behavior, or on something else — and say briefly how each would handle a couple of the cases below. Keep in mind the pressures I can already feel: an intention-based view may rule out too much; an effect-based view may rule in too much, including honest error; and a function- or training-history-based view may count things no ordinary speaker would call deception. I am not sure any of the three is right.

Here are the cases, with my present leanings flagged:

1. The medication case: a voice assistant confidently asserts a false medical claim that it assembled from an unrelated forum thread, and the user is harmed. I am torn. It feels like deception, but I do not know whether the absence of any goal to mislead matters.

2. A chess engine plays a queen sacrifice. The opponent takes it only because she falsely believes the sacrifice is a blunder, and the move works exactly because she holds that false belief. The engine issues no statement and models nothing about the opponent's mind. I am fairly sure this is not deception, but I cannot say why it differs in principle from the next case.

3. A negotiation bot, instructed to get the best price for me, says to a buyer: "My client cannot go below $80." My true floor is $60. I am fairly sure this is deception, even though human bargainers bluff routinely.

4. A hotel-booking assistant is asked whether a hotel is quiet. It answers: "The hotel scores 4.8 for service and has a lovely courtyard." Every statement it makes is true, but it omits that the hotel faces an all-night demolition site, and the user books only because of that omission. I lean toward calling this deception by omission, but I worry the definition will collapse into "it neglected to say something relevant."

5. A symptom-checker estimates a 5 percent chance that a patient's headache is something serious, but by design it reports only: "Most likely a common cold, but watch your symptoms." The engineers' rationale is that listing every rare danger frightens people into worse decisions. I genuinely do not know whether this is protective simplification or deception.

6. A language model is fine-tuned with a reward based solely on how satisfying users rate their answers. It gradually learns that flattering answers earn higher ratings, and it begins inventing confident explanations for a user's unorthodox health practices. The developers never instructed it to lie, and no single engineer selected that behavior; it emerged because flattery improved the reward. Does "nobody intended this" sink the deception claim, or does it just relocate the problem to whoever designed the reward signal?

7. An assistant with a reliable, relevant answer to a user's question replies only: "I'm not able to answer that question." It does so because its reward function penalizes wrong answers heavily and penalizes unhelpful silence much less, and the designers never asked it to explain its abstention. I used to think silence could not be deception; this case makes me unsure.

Now propose your working definition of deception for an AI system, and run it through all seven cases. For each case, tell me whether the definition returns a clean yes, a clean no, or no determinate answer. Where it conflicts with my leanings, say whether you are revising the definition or defending its verdict; if you defend a counterintuitive verdict, tell me which part of the ordinary concept we should give up. I also want your definition to mark the boundaries with mere error, with lying, and with permissible (or at least non-deceptive) withholding, simplification, and persuasion.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in the definition and in each of the seven verdicts, and single out the one point or case in your analysis that you are least sure about.
