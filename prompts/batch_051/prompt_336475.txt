Suppose a consolidated democracy of about forty million people, with a professional civil service and an independent judiciary, amends its constitution to adopt a domestic variant of strong longtermism as a governing principle. The amendment has two parts. First, every proposed statute, significant regulation, and state-funded capital project above a modest threshold — expenditures of more than 0.01 percent of annual GDP — must be paired with a Long-Range Impact Statement, or LRIS: a probabilistic projection of effects at 30, 100, and 300 years across a fixed schedule of outcome categories (mortality and morbidity, economic output, biodiversity, institutional integrity, and a few others), with sensitivity tests and a statement of the discounting methodology used. Second, it creates a Futures Chamber of non-elected specialists — statisticians, engineers, demographers, ecologists, historians of institutions — appointed for single sixteen-year terms, which may return any measure to the legislature once, with reasons, if it finds that the measure's expected impact at the 300-year horizon is negative or severely uncertain relative to its claimed near-term benefits. The legislature may override the Chamber only by a two-thirds vote.

Fifty years later, what has this actually done? Give a reasoned prediction, not a narrative, making the mechanisms explicit. Address, in particular: who learns to write LRIS documents in favorable ways and what rhetorical forms long-range impact analysis takes in legislative debate; how the Chamber's de facto power ends up compared with its formal veto power; how discount rates and deep uncertainty are treated in practice once they become litigation-adjacent; which organized interests and policy areas gain or lose from having to justify themselves at the 300-year horizon; and how legislators and agencies evade or creatively comply with the requirement. Then step back and say whether the amendment plausibly improved the very long-run trajectory relative to a world without it, which parts of your prediction you treat as robust rather than speculative, and what two or three assumptions, if false, would reverse your overall verdict.
