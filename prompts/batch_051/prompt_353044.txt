A friend has been pressing the conceivability argument against physicalism about consciousness, and I've come to think it overgenerates, but I can't find the exact point of failure. The argument:

(1) A 'zombie world' is coherently conceivable: a world with exactly our physical facts but no phenomenal consciousness at all — nothing looks or feels any way to anyone, no one is ever in pain.  
(2) What is coherently conceivable in that strong sense is metaphysically possible.  
(3) If a zombie world is metaphysically possible, then the physical facts do not necessitate the phenomenal facts, so physicalism about consciousness is false.  
(4) Therefore physicalism about consciousness is false.

The trouble is the bridge at (2). By the same test, it seems coherently conceivable that Hesperus is not Phosphorus, and that the clear drinkable liquid in our lakes is not H2O. Applied to those cases, (2) yields possibilities that conflict with what are, on standard assumptions, necessary identities — so the bridge would give false positives. The argument therefore either extends to absurd results or requires a principled restriction to phenomenal cases.

My friend wants to keep (1)–(4) and restrict (2) only for phenomenal conceivability: phenomenal concepts, he says, pick out their referents directly, unlike natural-kind or astronomical concepts. I'd like to keep the zombie conclusion if it can be earned, but I suspect the restriction simply re-states the conclusion in fancier terms. Which step should I reject, and what does that rejection cost? If I reject (2), is any version of the conceivability argument left standing — and if I restrict it instead, what account of the difference between phenomenal and non-phenomenal conceivability would make the restriction more than special pleading?
