I am a high school student who builds constructed languages as a hobby, and I have been trying to settle whether a usable language could simply lack indexicals — words like 'I,' 'you,' 'here,' and 'now,' whose referent shifts depending on who says them, where, or when. I wrote out several test languages and recorded my verdicts, but they do not yet add up to a single position, and I want to know what principle best captures them.

(1) A language with no first- or second-person pronouns, in which speakers refer to themselves by name and to others by name or kin term. My verdict: I cannot make this one fail. Saying 'Maro is hungry' in place of 'I am hungry' feels clunky, but everyone can tell who is meant from the person saying it, and I cannot identify a situation where the name loses information.

(2) A language with no word for 'now' and no tense, in which all times are given as clock times and dates. My verdict: this one does fail. Someone can know that the train leaves at 4:05 and still fail, as 4:05 approaches, to connect that fact to the present moment the way the word 'now' would.

(3) A language with no words for 'here' or 'there', in which all locations are given as addresses and directions from landmarks. My verdict: it fails in the same way as (2). A lost person can know that the station is at Fifth and Main and yet be unable to act on that fact; believing that the station is here seems to be a different kind of information.

(4) Not a language but a thought experiment I read: a shopper follows a trail of sugar across a supermarket floor, thinking that someone is making a mess, but never thinking that he himself is the one making it, until he notices that the trail is coming from his own cart. My verdict: there is a real difference between describing a person and locating oneself as that person, and the word 'I' is what makes the difference available.

(5) A language with exactly one demonstrative, 'ti,' which covers 'this,' 'that,' 'here,' 'there,' and 'now,' with gesture and context doing the rest. My verdict: this language has not escaped indexicals; it has only compressed them into a single word, so I do not count it as an indexical-free language.

The pair I cannot reconcile is (1) and (4). If names really could do all the work of 'I', why does the shopper need to reach the first-person thought before he starts cleaning up? Yet I am also reluctant to say that a name-user cannot refer to herself at all.

Is there a single principle about what indexicals contribute — something about being located in a situation rather than merely describing a person, time, or place — that captures all or most of these verdicts? Please state the principle precisely, explain how it handles each case, say which of my verdicts it forces me to revise, and apply it to the case I keep going back and forth on: a language with no second-person pronoun, in which speakers address one another with a demonstrative like 'this one' plus gesture or with names. Is such a language missing something that 'you' provides, or is second-person reference less deep than 'I', 'here', and 'now'?
