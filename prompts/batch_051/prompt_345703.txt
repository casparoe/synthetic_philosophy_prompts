I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but a subplot in my current book has turned into an argument about envy. My protagonist's old friend has become successful and the protagonist can't tell wether his bitterness is just ugly envy or a legitimate signal that the friend got unfair breaks. Somewhere along the way I read Rawls, and he has a passage in *A Theory of Justice* — I'm paraphrasing from memory, this isn't the exact wording — where he distinguishes envy from resentment: resentment is a moral feeling, envy is not, but envy can still be excusable when it's a response to inequalities that genuinely undermine self-respect. That passage has me stuck.

I built the following argument and now I don't know if it's sound or where it slips. (1) If Rawls is right, excusable envy is envy that correctly tracks an injustice or a real loss of self-respect. (2) When someone feels envy, they feel that the other person's advantage makes them lesser in some way. (3) That felt sense of being made lesser is evidence, from the inside, that the advantage does undermine their self-respect. (4) So at least for the person feeling it, felt envy is evidence of an injustice. (5) But then every loser in any competition gets to treat their envy as a moral signal, and every winner's advantage becomes suspect just because someone resents it. (6) That can't be right, because some envy really is just spite toward excellence, or wanting what someone has without any claim to it. (7) So either Rawls's distinction between excusable and bare envy does no work, or feeling envy can't by itself count as evidence. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I keep the Rawlsian thought that excusable envy tracks something real, I worry I've turned all resentment into a verdict about injustice, and if I keep the distinction I worry I need some external standard that just begs the question. I'd also like you to separate what's settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely contested, and not present one side as the consensus.
