I'm a software engineer and this pattern in how cities handle statues versus street names has been kinda bugging me. After the 2020 protests a bunch of places tore down Confederate statues or put them in storage, and Bristol fished the Colston statue out of the harbor and now it sits in a museum with a plaque explaining what he did. But those same cities still have loads of streets named after confederate generals or slave owners, and nobody's in a rush about those. Also when a statue gets moved to a museum instead of being destroyed, everyone says it's "preserving history" rather than "honoring" the guy — even though the statue is still there, same guy, same bronze, same public.

So my actual question: what does this set of choices presuppose about what a statue is doing, philosophically? Does putting someone on a pedestal in a public square mean something different from naming a street after them, and is that difference big enough to justify the inconsistency? Does moving the statue into a museum neutralize the honor while keeping the memory, or is that a distinction without a differnece? And if we went with the view that a statue is just history and not an endorsement, would we then have to be okay with putting up new statues of, like, actually terrible people? I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether the practice actually needs it or is compatible with a bunch of different views, and tell me what would have to change if we swapped in a different one.
