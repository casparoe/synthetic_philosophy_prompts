In my world religions class we have been studying early Islamic theology, and I am trying to work through a disagreement between two schools of thought. As I understand them, the Mu'tazila held that at least some moral truths—for example, the wrongness of lying or the goodness of showing gratitude—hold independently of God's will and can be grasped by unaided human reason; on their view, God commands what is good because it is good. The Ash'arites, by contrast, held that good and evil in human action are fixed solely by divine command, and that unaided reason cannot be trusted to arrive at moral obligations; on their view, an act is good because God commands it.

I would like to compare these two positions in relation to a dilemma about the foundation of morality: is an act good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good? Could you compare how a Mu'tazilite and an Ash'arite would each answer that question? In particular, what does each school have to say about whether God's will is constrained by any standard of goodness independent of God, and what does each school imply about the role of human reason in knowing right from wrong? Finally, which horn of the dilemma strikes you as more costly for each school to accept, and why?
