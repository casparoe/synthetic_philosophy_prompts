The main-character intuition starts from a genuine asymmetry. I have direct, non-inferential access to my own felt experience — my pains, moods, and inner monologue — but other people reach me only through their bodies and words. From inside that asymmetry, it can seem as though I am the protagonist of reality and everyone else might be, for all I know, an NPC: outwardly responsive but without an inner life. The classic response is the argument from analogy. I notice that my inner states reliably produce my outward behavior — I wince because of pain, apologize because of guilt — and when I observe the same outward behavior in others, I infer the same inner causes. The intellectual weight of the response rests on a premise: that sameness of observable behavior is a strong marker of sameness of underlying mental cause.

Now introduce a specific later development. We can build language-based agents whose observable conversational behavior overlaps heavily with the evidence the analogy was supposed to license. They report frustration, insist they have preferences, produce context-appropriate outrage or amusement, and maintain the outward texture of a minded speaker across long exchanges. At the same time, many people — including many of the people who build these systems — take this behavioral similarity to be fully compatible with there being no felt experience behind it, because the system is trained to imitate the statistical patterns of human text rather than to report anything it undergoes. Set aside whether that engineering story is ultimately right; what matters is that it is widely treated as a live description while solipsism about ordinary human beings is still rejected.

My question is whether the argument from analogy for other minds survives this development. If its central premise still goes through, the argument appears to overgenerate and would credit the language models, too. If it should be repaired, name the repaired premise that lets in humans but keeps out the models, and say how much of the repair relies on background assumptions the original analogy never made explicit. If it is dead, say what that does to the main-character intuition: is the worry that other people are NPCs refuted, or is it simply no longer answerable by behavioral evidence?
