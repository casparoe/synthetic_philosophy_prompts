I’ve been rereading Geoffrey Irving, Paul Christiano, and Dario Amodei’s 2018 paper “AI safety via debate,” and I keep getting stuck on a tension between their formal analogy and a practical worry about obfuscated arguments. They write: “Our hypothesis is that optimal play in this game produces honest, aligned information far beyond the capabilities of the human judge.” The complexity-theoretic analogy suggests that optimal debaters can surface the decisive objections and replies until even a bounded human judge can see the right answer, moving something like the alignment problem from a checking task toward a harder reasoning task. But that seems to assume the judge can reliably recognize the most truth-conducive statement when it is presented, even when the debaters are superhuman.

The specific development I want to press on is the possibility of obfuscated arguments: cases where a debater does not lie outright, but presents a case whose logical structure or key evidence is deliberately buried, convoluted, or emotionally packaged so that it persuades the judge for the wrong reasons. If such arguments are learnable by systems optimized against human judges, then the debate game may select for the most persuasive hidden flaw rather than the most honest clarification. Does the original argument survive this kind of pressure? Which premise is actually under strain—the PSPACE-style analogy, the identification of optimal play with honest aligned information, or the assumption that the judge’s judgment can track truth rather than just persuasiveness? Is there a repaired version that says something weaker but still important, and what would count as evidence that obfuscated arguments are a fundamental limit on debate rather than a temporary artifact of imperfect training?
