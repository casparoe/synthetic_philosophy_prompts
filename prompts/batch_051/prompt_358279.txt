I'm in a population ethics course and we've been working through Benatar's procreation asymmetry. I think I've mostly got the asymmetry itself, but a friend explained it to me in a way that left me unsure how much to trust. His version was: when deciding whether to create a person, the suffering the person would experience counts against creating them, and the absence of that suffering if you don't create them counts in favor of not creating them, even though there's no actual person around to enjoy that absence. Meanwhile the pleasure they would experience counts in favor of creating them, but the absence of that pleasure if you don't create them doesn't count against not creating them, because no one is being deprived. So absent pain does a kind of moral work that absent pleasure doesn't. He then said this is basically just the familiar idea that we have strong duties not to harm people but no parallel duties to benefit people, and that this is why Benatar thinks any life, however happy, is worse than never having existed. He also said philosophers generally reject the asymmetry because it treats nonexistent people as if they had interests. I don't know exactly where his summary goes wrong, or wether the last two moves are fair. Could you go through it and tell me which parts are accurate, which parts quietly change the argument, and how a defender of the asymmetry would respond to the objection that nonexistent people can't have interests? I'm inclined to think that objection is pretty decisive, but I want to check if that's too quick.
