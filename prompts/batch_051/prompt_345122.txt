I was reading Plato's Euthyphro and got stuck around 13c. The Perseus translation has this bit:

"Then holiness, since it is the art of attending to the gods, is a benefit to the gods, and makes them better? And you would agree that when you do a holy or pious act you are making one of the gods better?"

Euthyphro answers, "No, by Zeus, not I," and Socrates says he doesn't think that's what Euthyphro meant either. I cant quite see what Socrates is doing here. He seems to be pushing Euthyphro's idea that piety is a kind of service or "attending" to the gods, but then turns it into something weird: that our pious acts would improve the gods. Why does Euthyphro find that absurd? Is the problem that you can't benefit a god, or is it that "service" is being taken too literally, and if so what would the non-literal sense be? I also wonder how this connects to the more famous dilemma in the same dialogue—because if the gods can't be improved by our actions, I'm not sure what role their approval is even doing in making something pious. Help me unpack what exactly the text rules out and what it leaves open.
