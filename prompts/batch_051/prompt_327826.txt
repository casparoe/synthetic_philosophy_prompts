For most of the past year, a friend and I have been circling the same unresolved dispute. I had come across an old view, associated with the medieval thinker Abelard, that the sin lies in the consent of the will rather than in the outward act, so that someone who fully consents to a wrong but is prevented from carrying it out is no less at fault than someone who succeeds. I have come to defend something like that view — call it the consent-only position. My friend holds what I will call the deed-and-outcome position: he insists that the deed itself, and what actually results from it, must add something morally; otherwise, he says, we could not make sense of why we reproach someone more for a harm they actually caused than for an identical intention that failed through sheer luck. After a year of talking past each other, we want to stop and do the work properly.

Write the joint statement we should be able to put our names to if we sat down with a good facilitator for an adversarial collaboration. It should include: a numbered list of claims both of us can accept, stated non-vacuously; a table of our remaining disagreements, worded so that each of us would accept the description of his own position; a statement of the cruxes, by which I mean the sub-questions such that settling one of them would move at least one of us; and, for each crux, a concrete case or piece of evidence that both of us agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. I suspect the cruxes include whether a completed act contributes a kind of badness that the bare intention lacks, whether what happens after an intention is formed can change what the agent deserves, and whether the finished act is merely better evidence of the intention or something morally significant in itself — and I suspect an example like two shooters with identical murderous intent, one of whose bullets is deflected by a gust of wind, bears on more than one of those cruxes. But identify whatever cruxes the logic of our two positions actually requires. Keep both positions honest and evenly matched; neither of us should be recognizable as a strawman.
