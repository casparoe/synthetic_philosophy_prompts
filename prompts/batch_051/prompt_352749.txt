I am a philosopher who works on the theory of punishment, and through collaborations with AI-safety researchers I have become fluent in the vocabulary of reinforcement learning and control theory: agents, environments, policies, value functions, rewards, discounting, credit assignment, constraints, regret, reward hacking, terminal versus shaping rewards, and the like. I want you to use that vocabulary to do something precise, and then to tell me what the exercise breaks.

Restate the debate between retributivist and consequentialist theories of legal punishment in the vocabulary of reinforcement learning and control. On the consequentialist side, punishment will presumably be a policy lever justified by its expected effect on future state trajectories: general deterrence as shaping other agents' policies through observed penalties, incapacitation as restricting the offender's action space or dynamics, rehabilitation as updating the offender's own policy, and the whole apparatus as minimizing some discounted long-run social cost subject to constraints against excessive false positives. On the retributivist side the translation is less obvious: the penalty looks like a terminal cost imposed because of the violation, assigned to the particular agent whose policy produced it, scaled to the severity of that violation, and not justified by the future trajectories it prevents. Improve these translations where they are inaccurate, but keep the translation honest; do not smuggle control-theoretic assumptions in and then declare victory for consequentialism.

Then report what does not survive. In particular, test the following notions, distinguishing for each whether it translates cleanly, translates only under distortion, or has no counterpart at all: desert and moral blameworthiness; the backward-looking temporal direction of retributive reasons; proportionality understood as an intrinsic fit between penalty and offense rather than as a constraint that merely makes the overall policy tolerable; the communicative or expressive function of punishment; the categorical wrong of punishing an innocent person for its deterrent value; and the difference between an agent's being guilty and a system's needing to update its model of that agent.

Finally, give me a residue report with a definite verdict. I have a live hypothesis I want you to confront: that once retributivism is operationalized in these terms, it tends to collapse into the claim that the constraint against penalizing the innocent is lexically prior and nontradeable, rather than into a competing account of what is being optimized—which would mean the two theories differ over the structure of the optimization problem, not over the currency in which outcomes are evaluated. Tell me whether that hypothesis is right, and what remains untranslated either way; then say whether that remainder should be read as a defect in the target vocabulary or as evidence that retributivism's talk of desert is less theoretically clear than it looks.
