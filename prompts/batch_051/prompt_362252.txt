In common-law courts, a witness may normally report only what she herself perceived: if she says on the stand that someone else told her p, and the point of the claim is that p is true, the court excludes it as hearsay. The stated ground is that the real source of the claim is not under oath, cannot be cross-examined, and cannot be watched for manner and demeanor. Yet the same courts have long admitted broad exceptions — records kept in the ordinary course of business, statements blurted out while an event was still overwhelming the speaker, statements against the speaker's own interest, and a dying person's account of the cause of her death. Some of these exceptions seem to rest on the idea that certain circumstances make a statement reliable even without cross-examination; others seem to rest on sheer necessity, since the evidence cannot be had any other way. There is at least a surface tension here: the general rule appears to say that one person's word should not be accepted simply because she said it, while the exceptions appear to accept exactly that.

What epistemology of testimony does this practice presuppose? In particular, does the exclusionary rule presuppose that testimonial justification must be traceable to the hearer's own checking — cross-examination, observed correlations, perception of the speaker — while the exceptions presuppose that testimony can carry its own weight under the right circumstances? Reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined. Then tell me what courts would have to change if they took either a consistently reductionist or a consistently anti-reductionist view of testimony.
