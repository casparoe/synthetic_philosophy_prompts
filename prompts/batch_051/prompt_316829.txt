I've been trying to settle whether cultural forms — languages, traditions, art forms — have value over and above what they do for the people who hold them. Rather than argue from a slogan, I have been recording my verdicts on particular cases, and I would like someone to extract the principle behind them, if there is one. Here are the cases.

1. The last native speaker of a small language dies. The language had been thoroughly documented: a grammar, a dictionary, and many hours of recordings. I regard the death of the language as a real loss, not merely as something sad for that person. The archive preserves something, but not the thing itself.

2. A village collectively decides to shift to a national language because doing so will help their children. No one is coerced, and within a generation the old language is gone. I think something has been lost to the world, though I hesitate to say that anyone acted wrongly, and I would not force them to maintain it.

3. A centuries-old practice that inflicts serious harm on its initiates — say, a coming-of-age ordeal that leaves permanent injury — is finally abandoned. I feel no loss at all. The fact that it was old and elaborately maintained does not weigh in its favor; its end is simply good.

4. A dance tradition's last practitioners die, and it is afterwards kept going only by a professional company with no ancestral connection to it, performing for audiences. I find this better than nothing, but hollow. I would not say the tradition itself survives.

5. A constructed language invented by one person for an art project, learned by a small group of enthusiasts, loses its last fluent speaker. I am sad for the enthusiasts, but I cannot regard this as being as great a loss as the language in case 1.

The pair that troubles me is 1 and 3 taken together. I want to say that a language's disappearance remains a loss even after its community is gone, but I refuse to say that a harmful practice's disappearance is a loss. That seems to rule out both the view that cultural content has intrinsic value independent of anyone and the view that culture is valuable only as a good for the people currently participating in it.

The closest I have come is this: the value of a cultural form lies in a community's living use of it, and once that living use is gone only a second-best or archival value remains. But that principle seems to make 1 and 2 very different in kind, which I do not feel, and it seems to imply that 4 has the same kind of merely archival value as a document, which I am not sure is right.

Can you state a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures these verdicts as closely as possible, and tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to give up? Then apply the principle to two further cases I keep going back and forth on: (a) a liturgical language with no native speakers that is still used in weekly worship by a living religious community; (b) a tradition that survives only because a state pays people to perform it for tourists. Please make the principle precise enough that someone else could apply it, say which clause covers which case, and be explicit about the verdicts it cannot accommodate. Finally, say how confident you are in the account you give and which point you are least sure of.
