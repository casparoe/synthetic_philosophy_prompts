I am a retired field ecologist, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of the environment with a small group. The group has asked me to write down my working commitments about the value of nature so that the commitments can be probed before I present them. I would be grateful for an audit of the set rather than a lecture: I am not asking for references or a survey of the literature, only for a test of whether these positions can be held together.

Let me state the commitments as plainly as I can.

(a) Natural systems have considerable instrumental value — carbon storage, flood control, the support of fisheries — and that value is real and should count in decisions. But they also have value that does not reduce to any human use, preference, or appreciation; they matter in part for their own sake.

(b) I take that deeper value to be graded in part by how far a system's present character is the product of its own unassisted processes rather than human intention. That is why a remnant salt marsh, shaped over millennia by its own hydrology and ecology, is more valuable than a marsh created ten years ago that is now indistinguishable from it in species, structure, and function.

(c) In general, I am committed to the idea that value is fixed by present properties; if two things are alike in every present respect, I would not normally accept that their histories could make one more valuable than the other.

(d) I am also a committed restorationist. The difference in value between a drained field and the functioning marsh created on it is enormous, even if the created marsh never equals a remnant.

(e) Human management does not automatically cheapen a landscape. Some of the places I value most are mown fens, coppiced woods, and heaths burned on rotation, held in their characteristic state by centuries of human labor; I accord them intrinsic value, not merely cultural or historical interest.

(f) A sentient animal's capacity to suffer gives me a reason not to harm it that does not depend on the animal's origin. I therefore accept culls of invasive animals only as moral losses, and only when the realistic alternative is the destruction of a native assemblage that cannot be replaced.

(g) I trust the attachments that decades of fieldwork have given me to particular sites, and I would distrust any theory of value that treated those attachments as mere sentiment. At the same time, I do not think a place's value depends on my being attached to it, or on anyone's.

I would like you to go through the set and discriminate among the possible fault lines. Identify which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, which merely appear to, and rank the genuine tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, describe the least destructive modification that would remove it and say what would be lost in making that modification. Conclude by identifying the single change that would do the most to make the whole set coherent. Please write the entire response as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a careful letter.
