I've been going through Sidgwick's *The Methods of Ethics*, the chapter in Book I called Pleasure and Desire — not the most famous part of the book, but there's a bit where he discusses how the impulse towards pleasure, when it becomes too predominant, tends to defeat it's own aim. Roughly: the more you consciously target pleasure for its own sake, the less likely you are to actually get it, so the rational hedonist ends up having to not think like a hedonist. I tried to reconstruct the point as a numbered argument, but I can't tell whether it's valid or whether one step is doing something sneaky. Here's the argument:

(1) Egoistic hedonism says that the only thing that ultimately makes an action rational or right is how well it promotes the agent's own pleasure.

(2) The best way to get pleasure is not to aim directly at it, but to take up activities and relationships for their own sake, get absorbed in them, and let pleasure come as a by-product.

(3) So if you adopt egoistic hedonism as your practical guide, you will be led to aim directly at pleasure as your target.

(4) But if you aim directly at pleasure as your target, you will end up with less pleasure than if you aimed at the other things instead.

(5) So adopting egoistic hedonism as a practical guide tends to make you get less of the very thing it says you should maximize.

(6) A principle whose adoption as a practical guide tends to defeat its own aim is self-defeating.

(7) Therefore, egoistic hedonism is self-defeating as a practical principle.

I'm suspicious of step 3, because Sidgwick himself elsewhere seems to think a rational egoist doesn't have to keep pleasure constantly before his mind — he can adopt other ends as means, or just let his natural impulses run. But maybe that's consistent: maybe the paradox isn't that hedonism logically commits you to aiming directly at pleasure, only that people who try to be hedonists in practice end up doing worse. So I'm unsure whether the fault is in step 3, or in step 6, or whether the argument is actually valid and I just dislike the conclusion. Which step, if any, fails? And if you give the failing step the strongest reading that makes it true, does the rest of the argument still go through?
