I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, mostly bioethics and new tech, and I've landed on a policy I'm not sure I can defend. When I write about a morally loaded topic—like embryo gene editing, or who gets a scarce ICU bed—I check the empirical facts myself, but on the moral half I basically hand the wheel to whichever philosopher or bioethicist I'm interviewing, even when my own judgment pulls the other way. My rule is: they have thier training, I don't, and on the moral part I'm reporting on the best available reasoning, not acting as an independent moral judge. A philosopher friend told me I've reinvented the 'moral testimony' problem from the inside, and that my policy is way more controversial than I realize. Is this habit defensable? When is deferring to a moral expert a reasonable version of epistemic division of labor for a journalist, and when is it just me laundering my own uncertainty through someone with better credentials? I'd like the answer in American English.
