Analyze the present competition to develop frontier AI systems—understood as both the US-China rivalry and the race among leading commercial labs—through the rationalist framework James Fearon developed to explain why war occurs. In Fearon’s account, even when conflict is costly and a negotiated settlement would leave both sides better off, rational actors still end up fighting because of private information with incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, or issue indivisibilities. I want a disciplined mapping first: who are the players, what are the options, what are the payoffs, and which outcome in the AI race is supposed to be the analogue of war—rushed deployment of unsafe systems, a first strike against another actor’s compute, or actual military conflict? Then tell me where the mapping breaks. In what ways is an AI race not a dispute over a divisible pie with a bargaining range? Are the mechanisms genuinely the same, or is the race better described as a collective action problem, an arms race in which the destructive output is not a costly exception but the product of the competition itself, or something else? Finally, say what the episode does to Fearon’s framework: does it extend cleanly to this case, does it expose an assumption the framework never anticipated, or does much of the current AI-race discourse borrow the language of rationalist war theory without carrying over the conditions that made the theory work?
