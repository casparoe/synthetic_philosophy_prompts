Two friends of mine from the wildlife rescue got into it last Saturday and I still can't settle who was right, which is annoying because I've started handling everything from baby squirrels to a concussed deer and I think about this alot now. The argument went like this.

Maya kicked it off by saying brain size can't be morally relevant in itself. What matters is capacities: whether the animal can suffer, whether it has wants or a point of view. Those are functional facts, not facts about how much tissue it carries. A being with a huge brain but no experience would count for nothing, while a small-brained animal that suffers counts fully, so size by itself is doing no moral work.

Dan said "in itself" is doing all the heavy lifting there. Most of the time we can't directly inspect capacities, especially with an animal we don't know well. Absolute brain size is a cheap, measurable, rough-and-ready proxy for cognitive complexity, and when you have to decide on the spot — say, how much care to give some odd animal in a box — a bigger, more elaborate brain should push you toward more caution. That's a form of moral relevance, even if it isn't the deep kind.

Maya came back that the proxy is bad enough to mislead. Absolute brain size mostly tracks body size; sperm whales have brains way heavier than ours, and almost nobody thinks that fact alone shows whales outrank humans. Meanwhile birds pull off sophisticated cognition with brains a sliver the size of a cow's. So it's neither intrinsically relevant nor a good evidential shortcut — using it gives you weird priorities.

Dan said that's a strawman. He never said absolute size is the right way to rank species against each other. The cases where size matters are the ones where capacity is opaque: a newly discovered animal, an alien, an AI system. If you knew nothing except that one had a walnut-sized brain and the other had a truck-sized brain, you'd reasonably assign more moral uncertainty to the second. It's an epistemic tiebreaker, not a value — but since it can change what you should do, calling it morally relevant is fair.

Maya said that just proves her point about the label. If its only a tiebreaker for our ignorance, then "morally relevant" is misleading: a stopgap you should drop the moment you learn about neuron density, wiring, and behavior isn't a moral feature of the animal, it's a feature of us being uninformed. The phrase makes it sound like brain mass itself changes what a being is owed, when it only correlates with what actually does.
