I am a retired experimental physicist who now runs a small reading group for fellow retired scientists. We have been working through Kant’s *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*, and one sentence has become a sticking point. In T. K. Abbott’s public-domain translation at Project Gutenberg, Kant writes:

> Everyone must admit that if a law is to have moral force, i.e., to be the basis of an obligation, it must carry with it absolute necessity; that, for example, the precept, "Thou shalt not lie," is not valid for men alone, as if other rational beings had no need to observe it; and so with all the other moral laws properly so called; that, therefore, the basis of obligation must not be sought in the nature of man, or in the circumstances in the world in which he is placed, but a priori simply in the conception of pure reason.

I am struck by how much weight this passage places on excluding human nature and circumstances from the basis of obligation. Yet when we moved on to R. M. Hare and Peter Singer, the test of universalizability seemed to re-admit exactly those features through descriptions of preferences, interests, and consequences. I want a decision tree for my group that sorts us into positions on the logic of “what if everyone did that?” by starting from whether we accept, reject, or modify Kant’s quoted claim.

Please construct the tree as follows. The first node should ask whether the basis of obligation must be a priori in the sense Kant states, or may include facts about human nature and circumstances; allow a third answer: the procedure is a priori but its inputs are empirical. Then branch by distinctions that actually separate positions. Each branch node should be a concrete question or a plain claim, not a “do you accept Kant/Hare/Singer?” question. For example, ask whether a malicious maxim that can be universalized without contradiction is thereby morally permissible; whether a maxim that fails only the “could I will it” test is wrong; whether universalizing an act with negligible individual effect can generate an obligation when no one else acts; whether the relevant description for universalization is fixed by the agent’s subjective maxim or by the act’s predictable effects. Leaves should include at least: an orthodox Kantian position, a Hare-style universal prescriptivism, a Singer-style universalization by marginal effect and aggregative harm, a rule-consequentialist position that takes universal acceptance as the criterion, and a skeptical or particularist leaf that rejects the test. Flag what your tree cannot separate, and note any combinations of answers that are logically open but unoccupied, with a one-sentence diagnosis of whether each is incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored. Keep the whole instrument to no more than twelve leaf positions, and write each leaf as one or two sentences stating its core commitment and its most awkward consequence.
