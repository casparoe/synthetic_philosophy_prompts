I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm putting together a piece on the Nordic model — the combination of high taxes, a large welfare state, and the strangely high willingness of people in Denmark and Sweden to pay into it — and weather it can be exported to lower-trust countries. I want to do some of the reasoning myself before I start calling economists, and the way in for me is game theory. The standard building block is the public goods game: n players each get an endowment E, each chooses how much to put into a common pot, the pot is multiplied by m (with 1 < m < n), and then split equally. In the one-shot game free-riding is dominant; in the repeated game, cooperation can be sustained by reciprocity and punishment if players are patient enough. The Nordic case is odd because the main contribution isn't voluntary: about half of a household's income is taxed away before it gets to choose anything, and the state then spends that money on public goods everyone receives. So I want to vary the canonical game. Add a stage zero: each player's endowment is taxed at a flat rate t (0 ≤ t < 1), the tax revenue goes through the same multiplier m and is split equally among all n players, and only then does each player choose a voluntary top-up v_i from the remaining (1−t)E. Let t range from 0, the textbook game, up to a high level where the compulsory contribution approaches the full Pareto-optimal contribution. Work through how the standard analysis changes as t, m, n, and the discount factor δ move: for which parameter ranges is the compulsory stage simply crowding out a reciprocity-based equilibrium that would have emerged anyway, and for which ranges does it make cooperation possible where none existed before? Then add the part I actually care about: suppose a small fraction of the players are low-trust types, meaning they believe others will top up little and expect the scheme to unravel, so they behave myopically or punish noisily. Does a high compulsory t make the cooperative equilibrium more robust to them, or more fragile? And does the model say a high-t, high-trust equilibrium could be exported to a low-trust population — do the types converge or diverge? Please think like an economist who works on public economics and repeated games, and dont hand-wave the algebra; start by writing out the payoff function and the one-shot best response before you treat the repeated case.
