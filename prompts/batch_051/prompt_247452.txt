I have been turning over an argument that began while I was on holiday, and I think it opens onto a genuine philosophical question. A friend maintained, with complete confidence, that a visitor who fails to tip in a country with a tipping culture has done something not merely awkward but morally wrong; yet when I pressed her, she could not say why a convention she happened to have grown up with should bind someone who did not. The argument then moved through queue-jumping and jaywalking, and I noticed that all of us slid between describing these things as rudeness, breaches of etiquette and moral failures without much care about where one category ended and the next began. I would like a piece of writing that satirises that carelessness in order to test whether the confidence behind it is earned.

Please write a satirical piece of approximately 600 words in the form of a parody official document: a "Visitor's Code of Conduct" issued by a fictional residents' association for an otherwise ordinary street or town, which legislates its unwritten conventions — tipping, queuing, jaywalking and any cognate practices you think fit — as though they were strict moral laws binding on all visitors. Keep a deadpan, bureaucratic voice throughout; do not break character and do not wink at the reader. The humour should come from the content rather than from any ironic aside. Exaggerate real features of the position being satirised: the moralising of local habit, the unearned certainty, the asymmetry between insiders and outsiders, and the demand that visitors comply with rules nobody thought to explain to them. The target is the conflation of conventional practice with moral obligation, not any particular country, town or nationality and not any living person. By the end, an attentive reader should feel the force of the question behind the joke: if everyday conventions do impose obligations, what could make those obligations reach a visitor, and where is the boundary between being rude and being wrong?

Please proceed in two stages. First, in the role of a moral philosopher working on social conventions who also writes satire, provide a short planning section of a paragraph or two, which I will treat as scaffolding rather than as part of the piece: distinguish the strands of the target and sketch two or three ways the document could heighten them while remaining a fair exaggeration. Then choose the strongest strand and write the document in full. The document may use headings, numbered clauses, definitions and in-character annotations where they serve the satire, but it must remain internally consistent: every absurd clause should be a plausible extension of the view being mocked, not a free-standing joke at the view's expense.
