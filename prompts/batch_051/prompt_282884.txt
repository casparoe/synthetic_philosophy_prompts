I have spent the past year convening a working group in moral psychology, and two of its members have finally agreed to stop debating one question and instead write up where they stand as an adversarial-collaboration document. One of them, whom I will call the virtue-theorist, holds that envy and resentment are best understood as passions to be governed and largely distrusted: each includes an element of ill will or at least a wish that another be worse off, each tends to present another person's advantage or my disappointment as an injury done to me, and their characteristic action tendencies — spite, pulling down, retaliation — are not warranted by the inequalities that trigger them. On her account, when an inequality or wrong is real, the fitting response is indignation at the injustice or a demand for redress, not envy or resentment, and Rawls's treatment of envy as excusable under severe inequality marks a social condition that makes such feelings comprehensible without endorsing them. The other, the signal-theorist, holds that these emotions are costly, information-bearing responses to threats to a person's standing and to violations of legitimate expectation, and that they are especially informative for people who lack standing to voice the complaint through ordinary channels; their unpleasantness, he argues, no more impugns their informational content than the unpleasantness of fear impugns its capacity to indicate danger, and a culture that treats them as vices risks blaming the sensor for the condition it detects.

They want me to draft a document both could sign, in three parts. The first is a numbered list of agreed claims about envy and resentment that neither side finds vacuous: how the two emotions differ, what each tends to track, the conditions under which each misfires, and what each side is prepared to concede to the other. The second is a disagreement table: for each remaining point of dispute, a neutral statement of the issue and the two positions worded so that each side would accept the phrasing of its own view; wherever a disagreement appears to rest on a merely verbal boundary — for example, whether the emotion Rawls calls excusable is properly envy or is resentment at unjust inequality under another name — they want that made explicit and the boundary redrawn. The third is a set of at most four cruxes, sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side, each paired with a concrete case or empirical finding that both sides agree would bear on it and with each side's prediction recorded in advance. They suspect two cruxes in particular are worth pressure-testing: whether the epistemically reliable cases of envy can be fully redescribed as envy-free indignation at unearned advantage, and whether the familiar split between benign and malicious envy in the empirical literature separates a functional signal from a vice in a way that favors either camp's account of the emotion itself.
