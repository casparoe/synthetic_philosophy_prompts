I'm a novelist, and I sit on the board of a small foundation that underwrites a series of short biographies for teenagers — each one a life meant to make goodness feel imitable, very much in the old Plutarchan spirit of reading a life in order to become a certain kind of person. We have a manuscript in front of us now, a life of a mid-century labor organizer (the author's composite, though the book presents him as a single man): he really did hold a wildcat strike together and spent thirty years pulling workers out of company towns, but he was also, by any honest reading of his own letters and his family's accounts, a bully and a serial fabricator. The author has simply left all of that out. The book is good, the moral arc is neat, and the editor argues that's the genre — that a biography for fifteen-year-olds is a training weight rather than a documentary, and that Plutarch himself drew a clear line between writing lives and writing histories. Part of the board wants to reject it as hagiography: their line is that a partly-fictional exemplar doesn't train moral judgment, it only trains admiration, and the omissions are doing the real teaching. The other part says we aren't a fact-checking body, and that you start a young reader with the clean version and complicate it later.

Please draft the short written opinion I'd circulate before Thursdays meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame (publish as is, demand a revision, add a framing note, reject — whatever you think is right), and it should engage the strongest version of the losing side, not a straw man. I'd especialy like the memo to get into what a life is for when it's being used to teach: does the moral force of an exemplar depend on the truth of the actual life, or on the shape of the story a reader can carry away? And what breaks if we get that question wrong? Think it through properly before you commit — maybe sort out the two or three hardest pressure points first — and write as someone who works on the ethics of life-writing and moral education. Then start the memo itself directly: first sentence states the recommendation, no preamble, no "here is a draft," no closing pleasantries to me.
