I am a journalist who writes about the conceptual foundations of machine learning, and while preparing a piece on how far modern systems actually embody Occam's razor, I keep returning to a specific puzzle about the classic formal argument for it.

The classic argument, as I understand it, is roughly this. Kolmogorov complexity measures the length of the shortest program that produces a given object. Solomonoff induction assigns each computable hypothesis a prior probability that decays exponentially in its Kolmogorov complexity, and then predicts by mixing over all computable hypotheses. Simpler hypotheses therefore dominate the prior, and the resulting predictor is universal in a striking sense: for any computable data-generating process, its expected cumulative prediction error is bounded by a constant that depends only on that process's own complexity. On this picture, Occam's razor is not a mere heuristic; it is close to an optimal inductive strategy whose only unavoidable cost is the complexity of the true explanation itself.

The development that unsettles me is the now well-documented behaviour of heavily overparameterised neural networks. A network with far more parameters than training examples can be driven to fit the training set essentially perfectly, even when the labels have been randomised, yet on real data it frequently generalises well; in the so-called double descent phenomenon, test error often improves again precisely after the model has crossed the interpolation threshold, in the regime where it has enough capacity to memorise. Read naively, the Solomonoff picture would treat such a model as enormously complex, and a fitted model that memorises noise would be exactly the kind of hypothesis the simplicity prior should punish most severely. But the generalising network seems not to be punished; its training procedure appears to be recovering genuine structure in data that, by any count of parameters, it should have been too complex to capture. I would like to know whether the Solomonoff/Occam argument survives this development, and which specific premise is under real pressure. Is it the assumption that the prior over hypotheses is where simplicity lives; the assumption that a model's complexity, once it has fitted the training data, is a reliable guide to generalisation; the assumption that the effective hypothesis is the fitted network rather than the whole training pipeline; or something else? Is there a repaired version, perhaps one that relocates simplicity to the algorithm or the training procedure, or that treats the implicit regularisation of stochastic gradient descent as a successor to the simplicity prior, and if so, what exactly would that repaired claim assert? Or should the Solomonoff result be demoted to an idealisation that is formally true but practically disconnected from how learning works? Please answer in British English.
