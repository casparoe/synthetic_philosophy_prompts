I'm a philosophy professor and I've been rethinking Adam Smith's account of acquisitiveness in The Theory of Moral Sentiments, and I keep getting stuck on a passage from Part IV, Chapter I, where he's explaining why people value the arrangement of things more than the ends the arrangement serves:

A watch, in the same manner, that falls behind above two minutes in a day, is despised by one curious in watches. He sells it perhaps for a couple of guineas, and purchases another at fifty, which will not lose above a minute in a fortnight. The sole use of watches however, is to tell us what o’clock it is, and to hinder us from breaking any engagement, or suffering any other inconveniency by our ignorance in that particular point. But the person so nice with regard to this machine, will not always be found either more scrupulously punctual than other men, or more anxiously concerned upon any other account, to know precisely what time of day it is. What interests him is not so much the attainment of this piece of knowledge, as the perfection of the machine which serves to attain it.

I'm attracted to reading this as the core of a theory of greed: what we call acquisitiveness is largely a misdirected love of the elegance, order, and perfection of means, not a raw desire for the actual goods. That would explain why people pursue wealth far beyond comfort, why the miser's desire seems self-propelling, and why getting the thing is often less satisfying than wanting it. It's a tidy, unifying account, and it makes greed look less like a brute appetite and more like a crooked aesthetic impulse.

But I also have a firm intuition that something is missng from this. When I think of someone who acquires in order to dominate others, or whose desire for more is fundamentally a desire to be ahead, or who treats every exchange partner as an instrument, I don't just see a confused watch-lover. I see a vice with a different moral shape: something about status, power, or contempt. Smith's account seems to reduce greed to a cognitive or aesthetic error, and I can't tell whether that's a revelation or a distortion.

Should I let the intuition go and admit that the "something more" I'm sensing is just the love of order wearing a red face? Or should the theory be amended so it can take in the agonistic, status-driven, sometimes predatory side of acquisitiveness? Is there a reading of Smith here where the theory and my intuition are compatible after all? I'd want to know what would justify each move, not just which side to pick.
