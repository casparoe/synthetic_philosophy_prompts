Been reading philosophy on and off for years now, and I keep noticing a pattern in moral arguments I can't put a name to. A group is arguing about a principle in the abstract — say, we have no duty to rescue strangers when it costs us something real — and everyone roughly accepts it. Then somebody retells the very same case with one extra bit of concreteness: the victim gets a name, the water is described so you can feel the cold. By hypothosis nothing morally relevant has changed. But the verdicts flip. And the strange part is what comes after: from then on, the vivid version is treated as the 'real' intuition and the abstract one as the distortion, as if concreteness helped people see more clearly rather than just getting them worked up. I've seen it with lever-and-bystander cases, where adding the smell of smoke and the sound of screaming changes the answer, and in arguments about duties to distant strangers, where a drowning child goes from negligible to decisive once the child is given a red coat and a birthday. Assume the cases really are identical in structure. I'm not asking about any general effect of wording on judgment — I know psychology has names for that. I'm after this narrower thing: philosophy's habit of using concrete detail to draw out intuitions while treating the resulting vivid verdicts as especially authoritative, even though the added detail does no argumentative work. Does this have an established name? If not, what would you call it, and how would you seperate it from plain old respondent bias?
