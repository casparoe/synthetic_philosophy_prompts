I am drafting a paper on the relation between the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, and before I attribute a view to a colleague I want to see whether her recorded judgements support the attribution. I should like you to work through the following exercise in some detail.

The colleague, whom I shall call Professor A, works in formal epistemology, and over the years she has given me verdicts on a series of cases. In every case the question concerns rational acceptance: whether it is rational for an agent to take a proposition as a settled basis for further thought and action. These are her verdicts.

1. In a fair lottery with one million tickets and exactly one winner, the proposition that any given ticket loses has probability 0.999999. Professor A says it is rational to accept each individual ticket-loss proposition, and rational to accept that some ticket wins, but not rational to accept the conjunction of all one million ticket-loss propositions.

2. In the preface case, an author has written a long factual book, every claim of which has been carefully checked. Professor A says the author is rational to accept each individual claim in the book, rational to accept that at least one claim in the book is false, but not rational to accept the conjunction of all the claims.

3. In a fair ten-ticket lottery, the proposition that ticket 1 loses has probability 0.9. Professor A says it is rational to accept that proposition.

4. In a fair six-ticket lottery, the proposition that ticket 1 loses has probability 5/6. Professor A says it is not rational to accept that proposition.

5. Two independent propositions, p and q, each have probability 0.95, so their conjunction has probability 0.9025. Professor A says it is rational to accept p, rational to accept q, and rational to accept the conjunction of p and q.

Now consider a new case. In a fair twenty-ticket lottery with exactly one winner, the proposition that ticket 1 loses has probability 0.95. But the circumstances are high-stakes: a decision with irreversible consequences turns on whether ticket 1 loses, and accepting that proposition falsely would be gravely harmful. What would Professor A say about whether it is rational, in this case, to accept that ticket 1 loses?

I am less interested in a bare answer than in the following. Identify the candidate rule or rules that fit her recorded verdicts, including at least one rule that predicts acceptance in the new case and at least one that predicts rejection. Say which rule best explains her pattern as a whole, and give the reasons for preferring it. Then specify, precisely, a further case—with its probabilities and stakes stated—that would discriminate between any rules left open by the new case. Finally, state plainly any respects in which her record underdetermines the rule she is following.

Please write your analysis in British English.
