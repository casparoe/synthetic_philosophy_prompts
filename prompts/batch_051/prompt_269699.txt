I'm a philosophy PhD student working on conceptual engineering, and I also spend a fair amount of time writing code, so this parallel has been nagging me. Please act as a professional philosopher who knows both conceptual ethics and enough software engineering to follow the analogy. Think carefully, maybe brainstorm the mapping explicitly before assessing it, and then give me your honest read: I care more about whether the parallel is genuine than about being told it's clever.

The parallel is this. In software you can either document exactly what an API does today—quirks and all—or you can change the interface and ship a new major version. Semantic versioning gives a rough taxonomy: patch changes fix bugs without changing the contract, minor changes add functionality in a backward-compatible way, and major changes deliberately break the contract. Deprecation sits in between: the old behavior still works, but you're warned to stop relying on it because it will be removed. It seems to me the old contrast between conceptual analysis and ameliorative analysis maps onto this almost term-for-term. Conceptual analysis is writing docs for the current API: it tells you what the concept actually does in our practice, including the ugly parts. An ameliorative analysis in the Haslanger style is a breaking change: you keep the same identifier—"woman," "race"—but you change what it picks out because you judge that the old concept was doing harmful work. And conceptual ethics more generally is the decision procedure for whether to accept a proposed version bump: patch, minor, or major.

My question: is this a real identity of structure, or am I just pattern-matching on the word "change"? If its real, what would have to be true of natural-language concepts for the software results to transfer over? The obvious disanalogy is that a library has a maintainer who owns the name and can force a breaking change on downstream users, whereas a natural-language concept has no maintainer and the "users" massively outnumber any would-be engineer. But maybe that's a difference in implementation rather than in the structure of the analogy. Also, is there anything specific from software—the semver norm that you must not smuggle a breaking change into a minor or patch release, the way maintainers keep backward-compatibility shims around for years, the fact that some users stay on the old version forever because migration is costly—that predicts something nontrivial about conceptual engineering that my field has underappreciated? And conversely, where does the analogy break in a direction that would actively mislead someone doing conceptual ethics, not just fail to transfer?
