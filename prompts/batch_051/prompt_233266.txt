I've been turning over Locke's idea that you're the same person as someone in the past just in case your consciousness reaches back and takes ownership of that person's actions. The stock objection—I think from Reid?—is the brave officer: a boy steals an apple, the officer later remembers being that boy, and the old general remembers being the officer but can't remember the boy. By Locke's rule the general is the officer and the officer is the boy, but the general isn't the boy, so identity breaks at the joints.

But here's the part I want to push on. That whole debate seems to assume memory is a storehouse of fixed records, and the question is just which records you can still open. We now know memory is rebuilt every time from scraps and expectations, and people can be honestly certain about events that never happened. There are even experiments where people end up 'remembering' things that were implanted. So does Locke's criterion survive this shakier picture of memory, or does the brave-officer problem turn from a logic puzzle into a real dead end? Which premise is actually doing the work, and is there a repaired version of the idea that's still about persons and not just about having the same brain or the same body?
