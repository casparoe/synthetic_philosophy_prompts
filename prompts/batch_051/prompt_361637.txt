Here's an asymmetry in our thinking about curiosity and knowledge that I'd like you to pressure-test for me. We tend to think curiosity is a virtue, and that some knowledge is worth having even when it pays no practical dividend — say, knowing how the first stars lit up, or how a dead language handled its verbs, or what's under the ice on one of Jupiter's moons. But we also think, pretty firmly, that some knowledge is not worth having: few of us admire the person who memorizes the serial numbers on every dollar bill in his wallet, or who spends a year counting the exact number of cracks in the sidewalk on his block, or who just wants to know the precise number of hairs on a stranger's head. A hair count is a set of true beliefs, just as much as a chapter of cosmology is. So if truth is worth having for its own sake, why isn't every truth worth at least a little? And if your answer is that some topics are important, then what makes a topic important without pushing the question back one step?

So here's the asymmetry I want a principle for: we judge a person's curiosity as good, even excellent, when it aims at the first kind of thing, but we judge it as pointless or a bit pathological when it aims at the second, even though in both cases the person simply wants to know, and has no plan to use the knowledge. Give me the strongest principle you can find that draws the line where ordinary judgment seems to draw it, and test it against nearby cases before you commit. For instance: the exact number of stitches in the president's suit (about an important person, but trivial in itself); the crack pattern on a particular sidewalk (a trivial object, but it might reveal something general about how materials fail); or a friend's elementary-school attendance record (trivial, but about someone you care about). If the principle can't handle those, say so. And if you end up thinking the whole asymmetry is just a prestige prejudice about which topics are taken seriously, or that we should start admiring the hair-counter more than we do, I want you to say that and defend it.

Oh, and do the whole thing in American English.
