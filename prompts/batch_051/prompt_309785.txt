I need a carefully engineered teaching example for a course unit on decision-making under ignorance, and I want a single constructed case that cleanly separates three criteria students tend to run together: Rawls's maximin rule, the minimax regret criterion, and Harsanyi's impartial expected-utility reasoning, in which the agent treats each possible state as equiprobable and maximizes the straight average of the payoffs. The point of the example is to show that these three rules are extensionally distinct in a non-degenerate way, not to argue that any one of them is correct.

Build the case to the following specifications. It should be an ordinary-life decision problem, concretely described and not merely an abstract matrix, with exactly three feasible acts and exactly three mutually exclusive, jointly exhaustive states of nature. No empirical or objective probabilities may be available for the states; for the Harsanyi calculation, the equal weights are imposed as an impartiality assumption rather than read off the world. Express payoffs as cardinal utilities for the agent, as small integers between 0 and 20 inclusive. No act may weakly dominate another. Under maximin, exactly one act must come out best; under minimax regret, exactly one act must come out best; under equal-probability expected utility, exactly one act must come out best — and the three winning acts must be three different acts. The purpose of the example fixes what counts as cheating: the verdicts must be generated by the payoff structure, not stipulated, so a case that simply asserts the disagreement, or that achieves it only through ties or dominated acts, fails.

Then do the check in full. Present the decision table; compute each act's minimum payoff and state the maximin verdict; display the regret matrix and state the minimax regret verdict; compute the three equal-probability expected utilities and state that verdict. After the check, add two honest notes of the kind a constructed case should carry. First, isolate the structural feature of the payoff numbers that does the work — the pattern or contrast that produces the three-way divergence — and say what happens to at least one pairwise disagreement if that feature is weakened or removed. Second, suppose a defender of one of the three criteria objects that the case is rigged against their criterion: explain what their best objection would be and how you would answer it without altering any specification.
