You are a senior philosopher of action who works at the intersection of philosophy of mind, ethics, and behavioral economics, and you have a reputation for giving colleagues unsparing Q&A preparation before they present. I want exactly that treatment. Think carefully before you write anything: first reconstruct the genuine fault lines in my argument, then reconstruct how each person in the room would actually press those fault lines—in their own voice and vocabulary, not as strawmen—and only then write the prep sheet. Here is the context.

I am giving a one-hour departmental colloquium next month. My title: "Weakness of Will as Intrapersonal Preference Reversal." The thesis I defend is this: ordinary weakness of will is best explained not as a synchronic failure—an agent at a single moment acting against their own all-things-considered judgment—but as a diachronic, intrapersonal phenomenon. The akratic agent's preferences are quasi-hyperbolic, in the β-δ sense: near-term rewards receive an extra present-bias weight. As a reward moves from the future into the present, its discounted value overtakes that of a larger, later good, and the agent reverses an earlier reflective resolution. Weakness of will, I claim, is this predictable preference reversal between an earlier and a later self, not a single self being defeated by desire at the moment of choice.

The compressed argument I give in the talk, which you should treat as the target for objections, runs like this:

1. Typical akratic episodes have a recognizable temporal shape: a sincere resolution formed at t1, then violation at t2, with no new evidence and no change in the agent's settled values. I explicitly set aside compulsion, addiction, and self-control failures that lack this shape.

2. Quasi-hyperbolic discounting predicts exactly this pattern, because it generates preference reversals as the smaller, sooner reward becomes imminent. This is among the most replicated features of intertemporal choice experiments, and it is precisely the pattern behind procrastination, overeating, and undersaving.

3. The regret and self-binding behavior characteristic of akrasia make sense only if the earlier self's perspective is still felt as authoritative by the agent afterward. The model explains this better than a synchronic story on which desire simply wins at the moment of action, because the synchronic story leaves the continued authority of the earlier judgment unexplained.

4. Therefore, akrasia should be re-described as a product of the discounting structure over time. The philosophically interesting question then becomes why the long-run perspective is normatively privileged—not how a single judgment fails to move the agent.

The room I will face, in rough terms:

- Two or three philosophers of action and moral psychology. One is a Davidsonian inclined to say that strict akrasia is impossible and that apparent cases require re-description of what the agent really judged or chose. One works in a Holton-style framework and thinks weakness of will is a failure to stick to resolutions—an over-ready revision of intention—rather than action against an all-things-considered judgment. One works on addiction and self-control, is sympathetic to present-bias evidence, but worries about overgeneralizing from the lab to ordinary weakness.

- Two behavioral economists or decision modelers who know the β-δ discounting literature cold. They are not impressed by philosophical relabeling, and they will ask what the model adds to a straightforward empirical description of present bias, and whether the later self's choice can even be called irrational.

- A Kantian-leaning philosopher of agency who will worry that I have fragmented the self into time-slices, and will press me on which temporal perspective has the authority to call the other one "weak."

- Plus general faculty and graduate students, some of whom will have ordinary intuitions about struggle, temptation, and blame they want the model to respect.

I should be candid about the point I am most afraid of: I do not have a knockdown story about why the earlier self's long-run preferences are authoritative rather than merely earlier. The mechanics of reversal are clear; the normative story about which self to side with is not, and I suspect a good questioner can force that apart.
