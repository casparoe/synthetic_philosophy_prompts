Earlier this week I found myself in a long argument with a friend about how to evaluate risky choices. My friend insisted that the expected value of a gamble is the only yardstick that matters: if the expectation is positive, a rational agent should accept the gamble, and if someone who accepts it repeatedly ends up worse off, that is simply bad luck. I replied that this conflates two different sorts of average. Consider a gamble that, on each round, multiplies a person’s wealth by either 0.6 or 1.5 with equal probability. The expected multiplier is 1.05, so the arithmetic mean of wealth across many parallel players rises; yet a single player who repeats the gamble many times is very likely to become much poorer, because the sequence of multipliers compounds and the geometric mean is below one. My friend was unpersuaded, and I am not sure I made the point as sharply as I should have.

Could you explain the conceptual issue behind our disagreement? I want to understand, intuitively rather than formally, the difference between the ensemble average and the time average, why multiplicative payoffs break the straightforward link between a positive expectation and long-run improvement, and what this implies for the use of expected utility as a guide for an individual decision-maker over time. In particular, does the distinction show that expected utility theory is mistaken, or only that it answers a different question from the one an individual agent faces when deciding whether to repeat a risky opportunity?
