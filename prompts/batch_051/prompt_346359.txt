Here's a position I keep defending and I'd like you to take aim at it. I think Arrow's impossibility theorem is one of the most overread results around: people hear that any social welfare function satisfying a few innocuous-looking conditions is dictatorial, and conclude there's something deeply wrong with democracy or with majority rule. But that conclusion only goes through if the theorem's conditions track what we actually care about when we vote, and I don't think they do. In particular, independence of irrelevant alternatives looks to me like it's doing almost all the work; it's a much weirder and more restrictive condition than its name suggests, because it forces aggregation to proceed as if the only usable information is a profile of ordinal rankings. No actual democratic procedure is committed to that, so I read the theorem as a result about a certain formalization of social choice rather than a deep impossibility result about democracy. Tell me what would count against this. Which formal results—dropping independence of irrelevant alternatives, weakening it, or the Gibbard–Satterthwaite side of things—should make me worry that the impossibility is robust after all? Are there actual cases or features of real deliberation that should push me back toward the bad-news-for-democracy reading? And am I just making the cheap move of saying the conditions are badly choosen, in which case the real philosophical action is elsewhere? Give me a committed verdict, not a survey, and rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. I'd personally be most moved by a result that keeps something like the impossibility while relaxing independence of irrelevant alternatives, but tell me if that's the wrong thing to weight.
