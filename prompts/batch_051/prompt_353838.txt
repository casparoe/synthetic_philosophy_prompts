You are a professor of philosophy of biology who works on the adaptationism debate and has taught an upper-division course called Evolutionary Psychology: Science or Just-So Stories. I am setting the final exam for that course and need your help drafting one essay question, a model answer, and a marking scheme.

The students have already been tested on the basic positions, so the exam must not be answerable by reciting the standard defenses and critiques from the syllabus. I want the question built around the following case, which I wrote for the exam and do not want corrected or augmented:

A recent paper in an evolutionary psychology journal argues that dreaming is an adaptation rather than a by-product of sleep. Its central claim is that the brain uses dreams as a virtual rehearsal space for dangerous events, and that this threat-simulation function increased ancestral fitness by improving waking threat detection and escape. The authors offer three kinds of evidence: threatening events are overrepresented in dream reports relative to waking life; people in physically dangerous environments tend to report more threat-related dream content; and REM sleep activates perceptual and motor-planning systems of the kind used in waking threat response. A critic replies that the paper is a just-so story. The critic says the evidence is equally consistent with a by-product account, on which dreams merely consolidate waking memories and emotionally charged material resurfaces more often than neutral material. The critic adds that the paper reports no measurement of any fitness advantage associated with having more threatening dreams.

Write a single 90-minute essay question based on this case. The question should force students to do more than pin the label just-so story on the paper or defend it by saying that evolution can explain anything. It should make them address at least three things: what evidence would actually distinguish the threat-simulation hypothesis from the by-product hypothesis; whether the circularity charge has force even though the hypothesis can accommodate the existing findings; and whether the by-product account escapes the same criticism or merely relocates it. Then write a model answer of roughly 600 words at the level of a strong upper-division student, and a marking scheme of five must-hit points totaling 20 marks. For each point, give the marks and a one-line note on how weaker students typically mishandle that point. Also state which subset of the five points separates a merely competent answer from an excellent one. The exam is closed-book, and neither the question nor the model answer should ask for or depend on citations, bibliographies, or named readings.

Before drafting anything, plan your approach: first list the two or three most predictable shallow answers, then explain to yourself how the question will force a student past them. Use American English throughout.

End with an instructor's note, clearly separated from the exam materials, saying how confident you are in the model answer and the mark allocation overall, and singling out the single point in the marking scheme you are least sure about.
