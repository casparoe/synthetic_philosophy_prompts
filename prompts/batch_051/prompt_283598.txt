Next month I am presenting a working paper at a cross-disciplinary workshop on the economics of AI safety, and I want to go into the question period prepared. The paper's thesis is that the alignment tax, as usually discussed, conflates two distinct costs that should pull policy in opposite directions. The first is the irreducible engineering and design cost of building a system that is actually safe and does what its operators intend; I call this the safety cost. The second is the competitive disadvantage a firm incurs by paying that cost while its rivals do not; I call this the competitive penalty. My central claim is that only the first deserves the name alignment tax, since only it is a cost of alignment as such. The competitive penalty is a negative externality: under competitiveness constraints, a firm that unilaterally internalizes safety costs loses contracts, talent, and market share to firms that do not. Treating the sum of the two as a single quantity to be minimized pushes firms toward cutting the safety-relevant component, which is the one component we should be least willing to cut. I therefore argue that policy should aim at shrinking the competitive penalty through coordination mechanisms—auditing, liability, procurement standards, and possibly treaty-like agreements—while treating the residual safety cost as a legitimate condition of deployment rather than a defect to be optimized away.

The audience is mostly economists: two trade economists, several people from industrial organization, and a mechanism designer. They know the AI-safety literature mainly through high-level summaries and are suspicious of any concept called a tax that is not defined against a measurable counterfactual or within a Pigouvian framework. There will also be four or five safety researchers who have used the alignment-tax framing in their own work and may read the paper as an attack on a concept that has served them well. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased in the vocabulary of the person most likely to raise each one; for each, a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is weakest; what I should concede up front; what I should hold; and the question I am hoping nobody asks. I already anticipate the obvious counterfactual question and the charge that I have merely relabeled the problem as an externality—include those, but I am most interested in the objections I have not already guessed. Be committed throughout: rank the objections rather than listing them neutrally, say which ones have real force, and give definite replies rather than a menu of options.
