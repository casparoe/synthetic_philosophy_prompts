I teach an undergraduate course on the histrocal-critical method and the problem of reading scripture as both history and revelation, and every year Exodus 33 gives my students the same whiplash. In the NRSVUE, verse 11 says "Thus the Lord used to speak to Moses face to face, as one speaks to a friend." Then, nine verses later, Moses asks to see God's glory and is told: "you cannot see my face, for no one shall see me and live." A student always asks whether the final editor just nodded off, or whether we're supposed to read the two uses of "face" as doing quite different work.

So that's the question I want you to work through. Are these verses in genuine tension, or is the apparent conflict an artifact of reading them with the wrong expectations? Give me the main candidate reconciliations — for instance, that "face to face" in verse 11 is an idiom for directness of speech rather than literal sight, or that verses 12–23 belong to a later stratum that is deliberately toning down an earlier, more anthropomorphic tradition — and say which one a historical critic can accept without quietly ceasing to read the text as revelation. Which reading does the least violence to the text, and which ones are special pleading? Be explict about what would change your verdict: which consideration, if it turned out to be true, would push you to the other reading, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then reason it out, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
