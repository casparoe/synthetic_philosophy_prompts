One influential family of accounts of slurs runs roughly as follows. A slur's derogatory force is not something the word carries on its own; it is something a speaker does with it. The speaker expresses or endorses a derogatory attitude, propagates an ideology, or invites the audience to share a contemptuous perspective. On this picture, the wrong of a slur lies in the act and in the attitude behind it, not in the string of letters or sounds considered in isolation.

Now consider a specific recent development. Large language models produce fluent text, and they sometimes produce slurs. A model is not a speaker in the relevant sense: it has no attitudes, no ideology of its own, and no intention to degrade or persuade. Yet when a model emits text containing a slur, the text is ordinarily received as derogatory. Moreover, the offense does not simply disappear when the audience learns that no human authored it; people still object to the output, demand that it be blocked or removed, and sometimes hold the system's designers or operators responsible.

Does the attitude-based account survive this development, does it need repair, or is it effectively dead? I am interested in what happens to the argument itself, not in content moderation policy. Identify which premise the LLM case puts pressure on, explain whether the account can be repaired while preserving the idea that made it attractive, and state what a weaker but still significant version of the view would claim.
