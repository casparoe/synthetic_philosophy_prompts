I'm a PhD student working on the epistemology of mathematics, and I've been going back and forth with a friend in the CS department since a colloquium on formal verification. He works with proof assistants like Lean and keeps insisting that the old worry about computer-assisted proofs is obsolete now that we have fully formal, machine-checked proofs. But the more he talks, the more I suspect the original objection has just changed shape rather than died. The classic worry, back when the four-colour theorem used a computer to exhaust thousands of cases, was that a proof is supposed to give a priori knowledge by being surveyable: a competent mathematician can, in principle, go through the steps and see that the conclusion has to hold. Outsourcing part of a proof to a black box seemed to make our justification rest on the reliability of a particular machine, and that looked like empirical knowledge, not math. The standard reply was that the code and the combinatorial reduction were simple enough to check by hand, so the machine was only doing routine, non-insightful work.

The new situation feels different. With a fully verified proof in Lean, there is a proof object and a small kernel that checks it, and in one sense it's more rigourous than a normal journal proof. But nobody actually surveys the thing—the human almost never inspects the proof term directly, and the 'check the code' move no longer gives you surveyability; it just pushes the reliance onto a kernel, a compiler, and an operating system. So I'm trying to figure out whether the old surveyability objection survives this shift. If it does, which premise is doing the real work—that mathematical justification has to be a priori, or that it has to be surveyable by a single human mind? If it doesn't survive, is there a weakened version that still bites, maybe aimed at whether machine-checked proofs give mathematical understanding rather than bare knowledge-that? I don't care about the practical question of whether we should trust proof assistants; I want to know what happens to the argument itself.
