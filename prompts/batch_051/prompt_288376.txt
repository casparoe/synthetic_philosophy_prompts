I sit on a committee that decides whether our organization should pay for small pilot studies before committing to large programs, and our internal memos lean heavily on expected-value-of-information reasoning. Lately I have been reviewing the foundations of that reasoning, and I keep circling around the contrast between Good's theorem—which says that, for an ideal expected-utility maximizer, free information can never lower the maximum expected utility attainable in a decision problem—and Newcomb-style cases in which acquiring a piece of information seems to change what the world will already have done. A precise hybrid of the two has been troubling me, and I would like you to work it through carefully.

Take an otherwise standard Newcomb's problem. An opaque box contains $1,000,000 if a predictor has predicted that you will take only the opaque box, and $0 if it has predicted that you will take both boxes. A second, transparent box always contains $1,000. The predictor, whose accuracy in forecasting such final choices is q, has already filled the boxes: if your eventual act is one-boxing, it predicted one-boxing with probability q and two-boxing with probability 1−q; if your eventual act is two-boxing, it predicted two-boxing with probability q and one-boxing with probability 1−q. The modification is this. After the prediction has been made and the boxes have been filled, but before you make your final choice, you may pay $10 to inspect the opaque box's contents; after inspecting, or after declining, you choose exactly one box or both. The predictor knew in advance that this paid inspection would be offered to you, and it based its prediction on your entire decision procedure, including whether you would take the inspection. Please run through what evidential decision theory and causal decision theory each recommend at each choice point, and determine, for each theory, the range of q for which buying the inspection is or is not worthwhile. Then answer the conceptual question head-on: is the paid inspection a genuine counterexample to Good's theorem, or does the theorem's conclusion simply fail to apply because one of its assumptions is not met—and if the latter, which assumption is the exact point of failure?
