You're a professional philosopher working on social choice theory and liberal political thought. I'd like you to make an argument for a particular take on Sen's liberal paradox. Quick recap just to make sure we're on the same page: Sen showed that there is no social decision rule satisfying unrestricted domain, the weak Pareto principle, and minimal liberalism—where minimal liberalism means at least two people each get to be decisive over some pair of alternatives that is their own private business—assuming the usual consistency requirements on the social ranking. The standard toy case has Lewd and Prude and one copy of Lady Chatterley's Lover. Let x = Prude reads, y = Lewd reads, z = nobody reads. Lewd ranks x > y > z, and Prude ranks z > x > y. If Prude is decisive over (x,z) and Lewd is decisive over (y,z), then Pareto and the two rights give a cycle: z > x > y > z. Now please argue that the right response to this impossibility is not to abandon liberal rights, but to restict the Pareto principle so that it does not apply to meddlesome preferences—preferences about what other people do within their own private sphere. And since that is the controversial move, you should anticipate the obvious objections: that the fix is ad hoc, and that the notion of a meddlesome preference cannot be drawn in a non-circular way. Think through the issue step-by-step, maybe start by brainstorming a few candidate replies to those objections, then give the strongest version of the argument.
