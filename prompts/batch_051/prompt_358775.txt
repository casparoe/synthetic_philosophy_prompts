Recently I had to explain to an audit committee why an automated pricing system for electricity futures behaved as it did during a volatility spike. The system has no explicit objective beyond a learned reward function, yet the only compact description that made its behavior predictable attributed to it a persistent desire to remain solvent and a belief that certain spreads were about to widen. That made me revisit the criteria I was taught for calling a pattern that appears from the intentional stance real: (a) the pattern must recur across conditions rather than be read into a few hand-picked episodes; (b) describing the system in those terms must compress or predict its behavior better than a physical or design description of the same behavior; (c) the pattern must license counterfactual predictions about how the system would behave under interventions; and (d) the beliefs and desires attributed must be ones a rational agent in that environment could plausibly hold. Walk through these criteria for the pricing system. Is the persistent aim I am calling a desire to remain solvent a real pattern here, or are we projecting it? Then say whether this case shows that one of the criteria is doing more, or less, work than the list suggests.
