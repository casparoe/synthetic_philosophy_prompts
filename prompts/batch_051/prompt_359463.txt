I am a working novelist, comfortable in the vocabulary of literary craft and almost completely untrained in philosophy, and I need a careful translation done. There is a philosophical position I keep half-overhearing in discussions of artificial intelligence: roughly, that the more we lean on AI systems to do cognitive work we used to do ourselves—summarizing, outlining, retrieving, drafting, comparing, judging—the more we risk losing not just information or time but something philosophers call understanding: the sort of grasp that lets you say why something is so, or do a task from the inside, rather than merely present a competent-looking result. I want this position restated in the language I actually think in, which is the language of craft: voice, style, influence, apprenticeship, imitation, pastiche, ghostwriting, revision, facility, constraint, internalization, and the old distinction between a writer who has absorbed an influence and one who has only copied its surface.

Please act as a professional philosopher who is also a serious reader of literary culture, and who will not flatter my framework just because it is mine. Your assignment has two parts. First, restate the position as faithfully and precisely as you can within the vocabulary of literary craft: translate the claim that epistemic dependence on AI erodes understanding into claims about voice, apprenticeship, ghostwriting, internalization, and the rest. Second, and more importantly, give me a residue report. Tell me which parts of the position map cleanly onto my vocabulary, which parts you had to bend or fudge to force across, and which parts cannot be expressed in it at all. For each piece of residue, say whether the failure belongs to my framework, to the philosophical position, or to both. Before you write the polished version, please think carefully and begin by sketching your working glossary and the candidate mappings you tried and rejected; I would rather see the honest failures than a tidy translation that smuggles in assumptions and calls them mine.

Two things I want to hear about specifically. One: is the loss the philosophers describe the same loss a novelist fears when they suspect that relying on AI to draft passages will cost them their voice, or is it the older loss a tradition names when it distinguishes a writer who has truly absorbed an influence from one who pastiches it? Two: does the long literary experience of ghostwriters, apprentices, and imitators have anything to say back to the philosophers—for example, about when dependence on another's skill does and does not erode the dependent writer's own craft, and whether the whole worry is ever a form of moral panic rather than a principled point about understanding? Treat the residue as the interesting part, and do not flatten my vocabulary into mere metaphors for propositions.
