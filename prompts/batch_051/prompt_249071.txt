Constitutional precommitments are often defended on the model of Ulysses binding himself to the mast: a polity, foreseeing that it may later act against its own deeper commitments under pressure, ties its own hands in advance. Critics reply that the analogy breaks down, because Ulysses binds only himself, for a limited time, on terms he himself set, whereas constitutional rules bind future citizens indefinitely and were adopted by people whom those citizens never authorized.

Write an essay on whether constitutional precommitments are better understood as a legitimate exercise of collective self-government or as an objectionable dead-hand constraint on later citizens. To sharpen the issue, distinguish procedural precommitments, which entrench decision procedures such as supermajority requirements for certain legislation, from substantive precommitments, which entrench particular values or policies against amendment. A further pressure point: defenders tend to treat the polity as a single temporally extended agent whose present choices may bind its future phases, while critics treat later citizens as distinct agents who cannot be bound by someone else's choice. Weigh the strongest arguments on both sides for each kind of precommitment, and do not end on a non-committal note: defend a definite verdict about when, if ever, each kind can be justified to citizens who did not participate in adopting it.

Begin with a single sentence stating your conclusion. The essay must be no more than 900 words.
