I've been trying to think through a well-known argument from archival theory and the history of record-keeping. It runs something like this: archives are never neutral repositories. Because records have to be appraised, selected, arranged, and described by someone, the archive reflects the interests and power of the people and institutions who did that work; what survives is partial, and the silences in the sources are not accidental omissions but products of power. The argument rests on a background assumption: records are relatively scarce, storage is costly, and the decisive act of power is the decision about what to keep and what to destroy.

A recent development seems to me to dissolve that background assumption. Most of contemporary life now produces a record automatically — messages, payments, location traces, searches, sensor readings, surveillance footage — and the cost of retaining all of it has collapsed, so almost everything survives by default without anyone choosing to preserve it. The new bottleneck is retrieval. Search engines, database schemas, automatic classifiers, and recommendation systems determine which of that vast retained mass a historian, journalist, or ordinary person can actually find, and which of it is effectively invisible because it is unprocessed, poorly indexed, or ranked into oblivion. A document can be silently buried while being kept forever.

My question is about what happens to the argument, not about how archives should be run. Does the claim that archives are not neutral survive this shift intact? Does its force simply relocate from the moment of appraisal to the moment of retrieval — with algorithmic ranking playing the role that selection once did — or does something in the argument break once silence stops being a matter of destruction and becomes a matter of findability, ranking, and attention? If the argument does survive, is it the same argument or a rebuilt one?
