You are a professional philosopher who works on animal ethics and the ethics of consumption. Think carefully and start by brainstorming the principles you would use before you commit to a procedure, and be honest about which steps are real reasoning and which are just my values wearing a flowchart costume.

I run a small farm sanctuary — pigs, goats, a flock of rescued hens, a few sheep — and I have only been doing it a year, and the one thing noone warned me about is what to do with the stuff the animals produce. The hens lay unfertilized eggs whether we want them to or not. The sheep need to be sheared or they get flystrike and overheat. The goats sometimes come to us already lactating and need to be milked. The manure piles up. My board and volunteers are mostly vegan, and they split into two camps every time this comes up. One camp says using any of it is exploitation, full stop, because it treats the animals as resources and teaches visitors that animals are here to make things for us. The other camp says that is dogmatic: if an egg is going to rot or the wool is going to be composted, then eating the egg or selling the wool to pay for feed does not create demand, does not cause extra suffering, and is just freegan logic applied to sanctuary animals — using what would otherwise be wasted. They accuse each other of, respectively, sanctimony and wastefulness.

I need a reusable decision procedure my tiny staff and I can actually run each time a byproduct question comes up, which is basically weekly. We have no formal ethics training, maybe thirty minutes per decision, and whatever we decide has to be defensible to a board that leans abolitionist about animal use and to donors who will absolutely read a Facebook post about it in the worst possible light. I want it as a sequence of questions to work through in order, but please write the whole thing as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, just like you were writing me an email. For each question say what counts as a good enough answer, where the procedure should stop with a no, and where it should stop and say this is a values call a human has to make rather than a fact you can settle from the theory.

Then run the procedure on three actual decisions I have right now: whether the volunteers can eat the eggs, whether we can sell the wool to fund winter feed, and whether we can use the goat milk to bottle-feed orphaned kids we take in. And one more thing: if the procedure lands somewhere I do not want to hear — like telling the volunteers the eggs go to compost — do not soften it. I need to know where the argument actually goes.
