Say Adam Smith gets revived in 2025 and somebody sits him down with an iPhone and scrolls him through a big Twitter pile-on, or the comments under some influencer's apology video, and explains the basics: followers, likes, virality, the fact that most of the people attacking have never met thier target. I want a careful account of what he would make of this using the scaffolding from The Theory of Moral Sentiments, not just a vague 'he'd be horrified'. Specifically: how would he map the online mob onto his distinction between actual spectators and the impartial spectator—is the mob just a lot of partial spectators, or is it doing the impartial spectator's job badly, and what hinges on saying one rather than the other? How does his point that we want to be praiseworthy and not merely praised apply to someone curating a feed for likes, or workshopping an apology post? And does he have a way of explaining why being shamed by a thousand strangers feels different in kind from being shamed by the imaginary judge he says we set up inside ourselves, or is it the same machinery run amok? What Im really asking: how far can the sympathy/impartial-spectator apparatus be stretched to cover this stuff before it breaks, and where exactly does it break?
