I am a high school student, and I have come to a view about what should count as a real object in mathematics that I would like you to test in detail. The view is this: a mathematical object is legitimate only if it is given by a finite exact specification from which it could in principle be exhibited or computed, with straightedge-and-compass construction as the clearest model of what I mean. I am willing to extend legitimacy to curves described by polynomial equations with rational coefficients, since those are still finite exact rules, but I am inclined to deny it to transcendental curves such as the sine curve, the cycloid, and the quadratrix when their existence is justified by an appeal to limits or continuous motion rather than by a finite construction. I am also suspicious of existence proofs that cannot even in principle be turned into an explicit example.

I know this puts me near one end of a long dispute that runs from Greek compass-and-straightedge restrictions to the modern acceptance of transcendental curves as first-class objects, so I do not mainly want the usual story about how mathematics moved on. I want to know what would count against my position: what would have to be true for it to be false, which concrete cases, arguments, formal results, or developments in mathematical practice would most unsettle someone who holds it, and what the position commits one to or predicts that its rivals do not. Please rank the defeaters from decisive to merely awkward, and be specific about which of them a defender of the view should themselves concede as counting against it, rather than only what critics would say. I would also like an honest assessment of whether the view has been insulated by qualifications, for instance by quietly redefining legitimate to mean constructible in whatever sense is currently convenient, and whether that sort of insulation is a problem for a view of this kind. Please write your answer in American English.
