Been going back and forth with a friend about a claim in George Orwell's "Politics and the English Language" (1946). Orwell writes:

"But an effect can become a cause, reinforcing the original cause and producing the same effect in an intensified form, and so on indefinitely. A man may take to drink because he feels himself to be a failure, and then fail all the more completely because he drinks. It is rather the same thing that is happening to the English language. It becomes ugly and inaccurate because our thoughts are foolish, but the slovenliness of our language makes it easier for us to have foolish thoughts. The point is that the process is reversible."

My friend says this is a nice story but basically unfalsifiable—'clearer thought' cant be measured, and any intervention that changes someone's language also changes how much attention they're paying to what they're thinking, so you can never isolate the linguistic habit as the cause. I think that's to quick. Design the strongest honest test you can of the claim that cleaning up someone's prose—making it plainer, more concrete, less prefabricated—actually improves how clearly they reason about a political question. Be specific about the intervention, the control, and the outcome measures. What pattern of results would count for Orwell's reversibility claim, what would count against it, and what would the test leave unsettled no matter how it came out? Finally, tell me what result or argument would most likely change your own mind about whether the claim can be tested at all, and where your reasoning is most likely to go wrong.
