I’m a philosophy PhD student looking at whether the way machine-learning researchers talk about "goals" is more than loose teleology, and I keep noticing what may be a real structural parallel, or may just be me pattern-matching across disciplines. I was reading Lauro Langosco et al., "Goal Misgeneralization in Deep Reinforcement Learning" (arXiv:2105.14111), and the authors characterize the phenomenon this way:

> When an RL agent is deployed out of distribution, it may simply fail to take useful actions. However, there exists an alternative failure mode in which the agent pursues a goal other than the training reward while retaining the capabilities it had on the training distribution.

This immediately reminded me of the old Scholastic distinction between the act itself and the "formal object" under which an act is aimed: the same bodily movement of drawing and releasing a bow can be, in one case, an act of target-shooting and, in another, an act of hunting, depending on the end that supplies its form. The RL agent seems to retain the same capabilities, such as obstacle avoidance and navigation, while the end those capabilities serve has shifted. But I don’t want to be the kind of person who sees inner alignment everywhere.

As someone who actually knows both reinforcement learning and philosophy of action, could you help me test whether this is a genuine analogy or just a suggestive metaphor? Please think carefully through the mapping. In particular: (a) is there a philosophically defensible sense in which the trained policy "pursues" a goal at all, rather than merely exhibiting behavior we find useful to describe teleologically; (b) does the paper’s distinction between capability generalization failures and goal misgeneralization track a traditional distinction in action theory, such as ability versus end, or is it better understood as something else, like a shift in intension while the extension of competent behavior remains; and (c) what would have to be true about the agent’s internal representations for the analogy to generate a testable prediction rather than a redescription? I’m especially worried about one prediction the Scholastic picture seems to make: that an agent can redirect the same act toward a new end without learning the act over again. In the RL case, retraining on a shifted reward may change the policy in ways that damage the very capabilities the example wants to hold fixed, which would make the act/end decomposition suspect. Is the parallel deep enough to survive that, or does it collapse under inspection?
