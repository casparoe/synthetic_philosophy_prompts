I cover science and ideas for a national magazine, and I serve as the public member of my city's heritage commission. We must decide this month whether to approve a new historical marker in a working-class neighborhood. In 1936 a fire at the Bannock Chemical Works killed fourteen workers and spread into nearby homes. Neighborhood memory, sustained by families and an annual vigil, holds that the fire began because managers ordered crews to wash stored magnesium with water, and that the official story—blaming a cigarette dropped by a dead worker—was a cover-up. The neighborhood has submitted marker text reading: "Near this spot on June 4, 1936, fourteen workers died when the Bannock Chemical Company's routine practice of washing stored magnesium with water started a fire that spread into the surrounding homes. The official inquiry blamed the dead. Their families and neighbors kept the truth alive for three generations."

The documentary record complicates this. Company maintenance logs, a 1937 state safety inspection report with one appendix missing, and a labor historian's reconstruction indicate that the magnesium was stored in a different shed, that the fire more likely began among oiled rags in the paper-packaging shed, and that no washdown took place that afternoon. But much of that record was produced by the company itself or by a fire marshal who was the company president's brother-in-law, and the neighborhood's distrust of it is not unreasonable.

Our ordinance says markers should be historically accurate, but the commission has previously approved oral-history-based markers where the written record was silent or compromised.

You are a philosopher of public memory and commemoration advising the commission. Before drafting anything, brainstorm at least three distinct ways of understanding what such a marker is for, and what each implies for our decision. Then write the short opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend one concrete course of action—approve the proposed text, insist on a rewritten text, or reject the marker—and it should state what principle we would be adopting about when a community's memory may outweigh the documentary record. Engage seriously with the strongest argument on the losing side; do not merely restate the ordinance.
