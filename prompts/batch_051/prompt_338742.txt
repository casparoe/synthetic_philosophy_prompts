I'm grading this short-answer question for an early modern philosophy survey. Heres the question:

"Explain Descartes' position on whether nonhuman animals have minds. Reconstruct his main arguments, and be careful to say whether he allows animals any sensations, feelings, or forms of consciousness."

And here's the student's answer:

"Descartes denies that nonhuman animals have minds. On his view they are mere machines, or automata, whose behavior is explained entirely by the arrangement of their organs. His main argument comes from language: even the dullest or most deranged human beings can arrange words to express thoughts, but no animal can do this. Parrots and magpies produce words, but they never arrange them in a way that shows they understand what they are saying. Since this cannot be put down to defective vocal organs, Descartes concludes that animals don't merely have less reason than us, but none at all. He adds that animals sometimes outperform humans at particular tasks, yet fail at related ones, which shows they act from the disposition of their organs or from instinct, not from knowledge.

Descartes doesn't deny that animals see, hear, and feel pain. What he denies is that they reflect on these sensations or possess self-consciousness. After all, in the Second Meditation he lists sensory perception among the modes of thought, so the beast-machine doctrine has to be read as denying only higher-order thought while leaving first-order sensation in place. So on his account animals are conscious, just not self-conscious."
