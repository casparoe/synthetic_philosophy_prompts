In discussions about whether games can be art, I keep encountering one analogy that is made to do a lot of work: the claim that playing a well-designed game is a conversation between the designer and the player. The designer opens by building a situation — a space of possible moves, incentives, obstacles, a felt pressure toward one outcome rather than another — and the player answers by acting inside it. The game then returns a reply in the form of new situations shaped by the player's act, and out of this trading of moves something expressive supposedly emerges. The conclusion people draw from this is strong: games are not an inferior or compromised art form but a distinctive one, because the audience is inside the work and the player's agency is itself the medium; a game can make you feel the weight of a choice rather than merely depict someone else making one. I find this picture attractive, but I worry it is doing the argument's work for it, since in an actual conversation the other person can be surprised, can misunderstand you, can change their mind, and is answerable to you — whereas the designer is absent, and every answer the game can give was authored in advance.

I'd like you to audit this analogy rather than simply rule for or against it. Please organize the reply into four sections named exactly as follows. Correspondence table (short): lay out what is supposed to map onto what in a conversation — speaker, listener, utterance, turn, uptake, repair, shared context, and anything else you think belongs. Structural disanalogies (longest): detail the places where the mapping breaks, including at least the designer's absence during play, the fact that the player's replies are selected from a pre-scripted response surface, the asymmetry of repair, and the repeatability of a game across playthroughs; add any further disanalogies you find. Which disanalogies matter (medium): separate the breakdowns that genuinely threaten the conclusion — that games can carry artistic meaning through agency — from the ones that are true but irrelevant to that use. Bottom line (short): say whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are admitted, and if you think the conversation analogy should be replaced, offer a better analogy for how games mean.
