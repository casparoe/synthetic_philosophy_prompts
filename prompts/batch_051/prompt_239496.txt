I am a PhD student in technology policy, working on the governance of frontier AI systems. In the debates I follow, one analogy does a great deal of the work: the claim that advanced AI accelerators, and the very large training runs they make possible, are like fissile material and uranium enrichment. The comparison is used to license a fairly specific agenda—export controls on high-end chips, licensing regimes for large compute clusters, international monitoring of compute stocks and flows, quantitative thresholds for when a training run should trigger scrutiny or a pause, and, more broadly, the idea that advanced AI should be treated as a proliferation problem rather than as an ordinary commercial technology or an arms race to be won.

I would like you to pressure-test this analogy rather than simply affirm or mock it. First, give me the correspondence table: what is supposed to play the role of the fissile material, the feedstock, the enrichment cascade, the significant quantity, the weaponization step, the peaceful nuclear program, the safeguard, the inspector, and the facilities under monitoring. Then identify where the mapping breaks. I am especially interested in the disanalogies that matter for the intended policy use—for example, the fact that compute is not consumed and does not have to be diverted from a safeguarded stockpile, that software and trained weights can be copied at negligible cost, that algorithmic progress shifts the amount of compute needed for a given capability, that most chips and most training runs are unambiguously civilian, and that the relevant actors include private firms and diffuse open-source communities rather than only states with inspectable sites. For each disanalogy, say whether it is fatal to the policy conclusion being carried or merely limits the analogy's reach.

Finally, tell me whether the agenda the analogy is used to support—export controls, compute accounting, international verification, quantitative tripwires, and the case for pausing frontier training—actually survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and which parts of it were leaning on the nuclear comparison all along. If there is a better analogy available, say which one it is and what it would recommend that the nuclear analogy obscures.
