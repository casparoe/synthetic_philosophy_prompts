In my philosophy of disagreement course we've been doing conciliationism and steadfastness, and before seminar today I overheard two coursemates, Elena and Sam, properly going at it over a case our tutor mentioned. The tutor said he'd lowered his confidence in a controversial claim from about 80% to 60% after learning that a colleague he considers an epistemic peer held the opposite view. Elena thought this was right in spirit but too weak; Sam thought he shouldn't have lowered it at all. I didn't join in, but I remember the exchange pretty well.

Elena said: if two people with the same evidence and roughly equal ability land on opposite sides of a claim, they can't both be fully justified in staying where they are. Something's gone wrong somewhere, and neither has a neutral test to show it was the other person. So the only non-arbitrary thing to do is move your confidence substantially toward the peer, not just shave off a bit.

Sam said: that treats both sides as black boxes. I'm not choosing between two equally opaque judgements. I have the argument in front of me, I've gone through the steps, and I can see where they lead. My confidence isn't a bet on which of us is brighter; it's a response to the reasoning itself. If I can't find the flaw in my reasoning, I don't owe it to you to drop my confidence just because you couldn't either.

Elena came back: but "I've checked the argument" is exactly what the other side says too. In a genuine peer case, both people think their first-order reasoning is the good one. If you let someone defer to their own sense of the argument, the peer symmetry does no work at all, because by your lights your view is always the one the evidence supports.

Sam replied: there's a difference between always keeping your view and being permitted to keep it. Obviously if a peer disagrees I should re-check, slower. But after re-checking, sticking isn't arrogance. Your view makes disagreement a poison pill: on any contested philosophical or political question, I'd have to hold my view at near 50/50. That's not humility; its abandoning the thought that reasoning can settle something for me.

Elena said: that's a slippery slope, and I'm not that strong a conciliationist. I said move substantially toward the peer, not to the midpoint. And the symmetry only applies when both people share the same evidence and competence, so it doesn't force me to match some stranger with a different evidence base. But where it does apply, you've got two equally credible verdicts and no neutral scoreboard, so some lowering is rationally required.

Sam said: now you've got an escape hatch you reach for whenever the view has an absurd implication. And anyway, people we'd both call peers disagree about conciliationism itself, which is literally what we're doing now. So by your own rule you should drop your confidence in your rule, but then you can't very well demand that I follow it. If the rule only applies when it doesn't undermine its own application, I don't see how it's the fundamental principle you're making it out to be.

I came away thinking they were each right about some things and wrong about others, but I honestly couldn't structure it. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right about which specific point and what either of them is missing? Also, do you think it matters whether Sam learns that this particular person disagrees, versus merely learning that somewhere there exists some equally good reasoner with the same evidence who reached the opposite conclusion?
