I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology serving as a TA for a graduate course on conditionals. I have to grade a take-home exam answer. The exam question was:

State Adams' thesis about indicative conditionals, outline Lewis's first triviality result, and explain what the result shows about the prospects of identifying the probability of an indicative conditional with the corresponding conditional probability.

Here is the student's answer:

Adams' thesis says that the degree of acceptability of an indicative conditional "If A, then B" equals the conditional probability P(B|A) whenever P(A)>0. On the standard reading, this means the conditional has no truth conditions but is assessable by a probabilistic norm.

Lewis's triviality proof proceeds as follows. Take any A and B with P(B)>0. By total probability:

P(A→B) = P(A→B|B)P(B) + P(A→B|¬B)P(¬B).

Applying Adams' thesis to the conditionalized functions P(·|B) and P(·|¬B):

P(A→B|B) = P(B|A∧B) = 1, provided P(A∧B)>0;
P(A→B|¬B) = P(B|A∧¬B) = 0, provided P(A∧¬B)>0.

Substituting gives P(A→B) = P(B). By Adams' thesis once more, P(A→B) = P(B|A), so P(B|A) = P(B). Thus A and B are independent. Since A and B were arbitrary, every pair of events must be independent, which is possible only in the extreme probability functions assigning every event 0 or 1. So no nontrivial probability function can validate the thesis.

The proof does not refute Adams' thesis outright; it refutes the package that treats the conditional as expressing a proposition whose probability satisfies the thesis. Adams's own view is that conditionals do not express propositions, so they do not have probabilities that obey total probability, and the thesis instead governs the acceptability of conditionals. Thus the right conclusion is that conditionals cannot be ordinary events.
