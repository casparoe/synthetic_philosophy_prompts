In American defamation law, a false statement that asserts a checkable fact about a person is actionable, while a statement framed as evaluation or prediction is given far greater protection. A news outlet that reports that a surgeon performed an operation while under the influence has made a checkable claim about the past; if the claim is false and injures the surgeon's reputation, the surgeon can recover. But if the same outlet calls the surgeon the worst in the region, ranks the surgeon's hospital last in the state, or publishes a statistically precise forecast that the surgeon is likely to harm a patient within the year, the statement is likely to be classified as protected opinion or forward-looking judgment — unless it implies an undisclosed defamatory fact — even when it rests on careless reasoning, speaks with the trappings of objective measurement, and does the same professional damage. A reader may draw much the same conclusion from all three: do not trust this surgeon. Yet only the retroactive factual claim is treated as a false statement of fact.

This looks like a principled asymmetry, but it also looks like a loophole. The standard rationales are, first, epistemological: assertions about the past are verifiable at the time they are made, whereas predictions and assessments are partly subjective and cannot be refuted at the moment of utterance, so the law should not punish them as false. Second is a free-speech rationale: we want uninhibited evaluation and speculation, and audiences are expected to discount opinions and forecasts rather than treat them as fact. Please examine whether these rationales — or some better one — genuinely justify treating retrospective factual claims differently from ratings and forecasts. In doing so, stress-test the rationale against cases in which a rating or forecast is issued by an entity claiming expertise, expressed with numerical precision, and designed to guide the decisions of others. Should the asymmetry be kept, narrowed, or abandoned?
