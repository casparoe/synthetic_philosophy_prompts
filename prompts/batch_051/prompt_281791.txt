I have spent the past few years keeping informal notes on what a friend of mine, Marcus, finds funny, because his judgments are steadier than most people's and I want to extract the rule behind them. I have come across the usual suggestions that laughter comes from feeling superior, from incongruity, from released nervous tension, or from something being wrong in a harmless way, but I cannot tell which of those, if any, his record actually supports.

His recorded reactions are these.

A border collie at an obedience trial, asked to speak, produced a long and perfectly timed car-alarm noise while the judge's face stayed blank and the handler tried not to laugh. Nothing was harmed and nothing was broken. Marcus laughed hard.

A skating clip showed a skater strike a rail shin-first and scream, with a caption poking fun at the fall. Marcus did not laugh; he said it was pain, not comedy.

Our shy colleague, in a tense budget meeting, said the budget was not merely tight but was practically wearing a corset. Marcus laughed out loud.

A comedian's entire set was a mocking imitation of a foreign accent. Marcus sat quietly and said afterward that he could only hear the meanness.

A photograph showed a small dog sitting upright at a desk, wearing glasses and a necktie, presented as a bank manager turning down a loan. Marcus laughed and kept showing it to people.

At a wedding, the officiant accidentally called the groom by the bride's ex-boyfriend's name. The bride's face fell and the room went silent. Marcus did not laugh, and later said it was humiliation rather than humour.

Now I want to predict his reaction to a new situation. Marcus has a cousin who, at every family wedding, rises during the toasts, walks to the cake, and with no change of expression sets a single canned sardine, tin and all, on the top tier, then returns to her seat. She has done this at all five of the weddings Marcus has attended. Everyone, including the newlyweds, expects it and treats it as affectionate rather than hostile, and at the most recent wedding the room was already grinning before she had even stood up.

Would Marcus find the sardine ritual funny, the way he finds the dog at the desk funny, or would it strike him as too familiar to be funny? Which of his earlier reactions is the closest analogue? If his record genuinely underdetermines the answer, set out the two principles that both fit his past judgments but point in opposite directions here, say which one you would bet on and why, and name the further case that would most cleanly separate them.
