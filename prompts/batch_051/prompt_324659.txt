I ended up in a long, going-nowhere argument with a friend last night about English spelling reform, and he kept retreating to an analogy I couldn't quite break. His claim was that a writing system is basically a theory of a language: an alphabet is a theory of the phonology, and something like Chinese characters is more of a theory of the morphology. From there he argued that judging English by whether each letter maps to one sound is like rejecting a scientific theory because its surface predictions don't match every observation. English orthography, he says, is a morphophonemic theory — it deliberately keeps morphemes stable across pronunciation shifts (sign/signature, electric/electricity, the regular past tense -ed), and he thinks spelling reformers are like people who would junk a good theory over a few anomalies. I suspect the analogy is doing a lot of work for him but also smuggling in assumptions about what a theory is and what it's for. Can you lay out, in American English, how far the writing-system-as-theory analogy actually goes — what maps onto what, where the mapping breaks down, which features of "theory" get imported into writing systems without much justification, and whether his conclusion about spelling reform survive once the disanalogies are on the table? I'm not looking for a survey of orthographies; I want the analogy stress-tested and, if it fails, a sense of what a better analogy would be.
