Draft essay

We praise the judge who cannot be bought. We also suspect that there is something chilling about the person who cannot be moved at all. I want to distinguish two things that travel under the name incorruptibility and to argue that only the first, suitably located, deserves to be called a virtue.

The first is narrow incorruptibility: immunity to inducement, intimidation, flattery, and the other ways in which a decision-maker's judgement can be captured. The official who refuses a bribe, the civil servant who resists a minister's threat, the referee who ignores the crowd: all display it. In the appropriate roles it is an unqualified good. Where someone acts for a public trust or on behalf of others, her decisions should track the reasons proper to the role rather than her private advantage; being unbuyable is a condition of our justified reliance on her.

But incorruptible is sometimes inflated into a wider ideal: the wholly unbending person, uniformly indifferent to every particular attachment, favour, and pull of sympathy. On this picture, any departure from an impartial standard — giving the benefit of the doubt to a friend, showing mercy where the rule permits no exception, preferring one's own child's welfare — is treated as latent corruption. I want to argue that this wider ideal is not an ideal at all. A parent who weighs her child's bedtime with the perfect impartiality of a stranger is not a more excellent parent; she has failed at something her role requires. A friend who will not take his friend's side because taking sides is a form of bias has misunderstood friendship. Mercy in sentencing is not necessarily the corruption of justice; it can be the recognition that justice must sometimes answer to the particular person, not only to the rule.

The conclusion is that incorruptibility is best understood as a role-relative virtue: a disciplined suspension, within a defined office, of partialities that are legitimate elsewhere. The judge is professionally unmoved by the parties before her yet may rightly be moved by those same people after she leaves the bench. The aim, therefore, is not to become incapable of being moved but to learn what should move one, and when. A maximally incorruptible person in the wider sense — someone who could not be moved at all — would not be an ideal human being. He would be a machine for applying rules; the machine's emptiness, not its purity, would be the truth about it.
