I'm a law clerk at a constitutional court, and I've been assigned to draft the bench memo for a case that's giving me a headache. The constitution has a clause that reads: "The dignity of the human person is inviolable. All public authority is bound to respect and protect it." The drafters, writing just after a war, were plainly thinking about forced labor, degrading punishment, and the state using people as tools; there is no way they had our case in mind. The case is this. A stage actor, still alive and mentally sharp, sold the rights to all her letters, diaries, and voice recordings to a tech firm that will train a model to speak as her after she dies — answering letters from family members and giving interviews about her life. The cultural ministry blocked the contract, saying it turns her personhood into an object and offends the dignity clause. She is suing: the state is being paternalistic, dignity means respecting her choices, and this was her choice, made so her grandchildren inherit something. The court's president is a Kantian who keeps telling us dignity is the ground of all the other rights; one of my fellow clerks keeps muttering that dignity is just autonomy wearing a nicer outfit and adds nothing. I need the memo to work out what the clause actually does here: go through the interpretive approaches in turn — plain words, purpose, what the drafters would of said if asked, what a reasonable modern reader takes the clause to mean — and then say which one should govern and whether "dignity" is doing any work that "respect for autonomy" doesn't. Keep it useful for the judges, but don't hide where each reading gives out.
