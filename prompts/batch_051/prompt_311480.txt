I've been reading the Guide of the Perplexed for a seminar on medieval Jewish philosophy, and I keep circling back to Maimonides' much-discussed explanation of the sacrificial laws. One of the handouts our instructor distributed offers a compact account of his position, which I transcribe here from my notes:

"Maimonides viewed the Torah's system of animal sacrifice as a temporary concession, not a timeless ideal. Since the Israelites had been formed among the Egyptians and could not have conceived of a religion without offerings, an abrupt command to worship God entirely without them would have exceeded what human nature could bear; had the Torah tried, the people would simply have refused. God therefore retained sacrifice but deliberately narrowed it — a single sanctuary, a restricted altar, a limited list of species — and shifted the weight of religious life toward prayer and moral conduct, which Maimonides considered the true substance of Judaism. On this reading, the sacrificial laws have no intrinsic value: they were an emergency measure demanded by one historical moment, and Maimonides implies that in the messianic era the Temple will not need them."

The account feels too tidy to me, for two reasons. It clashes with something I remember from Maimonides' legal code, the Mishneh Torah, where he describes the messianic king rebuilding the Sanctuary and the offerings being reinstated as they once were; and the phrase "no intrinsic value" strikes me as stronger than what I can actually locate in the Guide, though I am not trained enough to say whether that is a real difference or just paraphrase. Could you go through the handout's paragraph sentence by sentence and sort out what the Guide plainly says, what is a common but contestable inference, and what is probably just wrong? I would also appreciate a brief explanation of why this reading became so widespread, since it cannot be pure invention. Please write the answer in American English.
