I'm running an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the metaphysics of quantum mechanics, and I'd like you to design a ninety-minute structured activity for fourteen students that makes the Born rule problem in the many-worlds interpretation something participants run into themselves rather than an objection they read off a slide. By the end, students should have felt the simultaneous pull of three claims — that every branch produced by a measurement is equally real, that a rational agent before the measurement should nonetheless care more about some branches than others, and that nothing in the branching structure itself seems to single out any branch as the one the agent will end up in — and should understand why this leaves Everettians owing an account of probability that neither branch-counting nor talk of a selected outcome can provide.

The centerpiece should be a branching wager with real, small stakes, such as coins or chocolate. One volunteer makes a financial commitment before a simulated measurement; the commitment must specify how a fixed payoff will be distributed among their future branch-selves, played by other students across two rooms. I'd like the setup to make the two branches differ along two dimensions at once: one branch contains many copies of the agent but carries a small stipulated amplitude-weight, while the other contains a single copy but carries a large weight. The rules, incentives, and payoff table should be arranged so that counting copies, treating branches as equally weighted, and weighting by the stipulated amplitude pull in three different directions, and so that the pre-branching volunteer discovers they cannot satisfy all their branch-selves at once. The facilitator should not pre-announce these tensions; they should emerge from the choices participants make. Please include complete roles and rules, the exact procedure for running the simulated measurement, the payoff structure, a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, and a debrief that maps moments in the exercise onto the actual dialectic: the claim that in the real theory the Born weights are stipulated amplitudes rather than branch frequencies, the Deutsch–Wallace decision-theoretic attempt to derive them from rational preferences across branches, and the self-locating-uncertainty replies defended by Sebens and Carroll.

End the debrief with an honest account of what the game cannot reproduce. The simulation has a facilitator outside the process who announces the weights and enforces the payouts; in the many-worlds interpretation there is no such external bookkeeper, and that absence is precisely what turns the Born rule from an instruction into a problem. The facilitator should say this plainly and ask the students whether the decision-theoretic moves that felt natural inside the game would survive the removal of the outside rule-giver. Also flag the most likely failure mode in advance — participants will quietly import the assumption that there is a single post-measurement self who receives one unique outcome — and explain how a facilitator should catch and correct it during play.
