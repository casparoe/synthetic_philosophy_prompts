I have been trying to settle a question about method in moral theory and would like your verdict on an argument I keep returning to. It runs:

(1) A moral theory's fit with recalcitrant intuitions counts as evidence for it only to the extent that the theory is not ad hoc.
(2) A moral theory is ad hoc to the extent that it accommodates intuitions by adding independent principles, exceptions, or priority rules for which it offers nothing beyond the fact that the addition saves the theory.
(3) Parsimony is precisely the constraint that forbids such additions; it is what keeps fit from degenerating into ad hoc curve-fitting.
(4) Therefore parsimony and fit are not two independent virtues that must be weighed against each other: parsimony is partly constitutive of fit's evidential force.
(5) Hence there is no genuine trade-off in which a more complex theory's superior fit with our intuitions must be weighed against a simpler theory's parsimony; a better-fitting but gerrymandered theory has no genuine epistemic advantage.

I suspect that (4) overreaches, but I cannot state the objection sharply. Is the argument valid? If not, which numbered step fails, and under what reading of its key terms? If it is valid, which premise should a defender of intuition-fitting pluralism reject, and on what grounds? In your answer, please distinguish what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people working on this topic, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
