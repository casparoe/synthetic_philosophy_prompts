I teach a unit on personal identity, and every year the students get comfortable saying “the self is a bundle” without really working out what that claim costs. Lately I’ve been chewing on the chariot passage from the Rhys Davids translation of The Questions of King Milinda (the Milindapañha), Book II, chapter 1. Here’s the bit I mean:

“Then if you came, Sire, in a carriage, explain to me what that is. Is it the pole that is the chariot?”
“I did not say that.”
“Is it the axle that is the chariot?”
“Certainly not.”
“Is it the wheels, or the framework, or the ropes, or the yoke, or the spokes of the wheels, or the goad, that are the chariot?”
And to all these he still answered no.
“Then is it all these parts of it that are the chariot?”
“No, Sir.”
“But is there anything outside them that is the chariot?”
And still he answered no.
“Then thus, ask as I may, I can discover no chariot. Chariot is a mere empty sound. What then is the chariot you say you came in? It is a falsehood that your Majesty has spoken, an untruth! There is no such thing as a chariot!”

I keep thinking this passage does two contrary things at once. It shows why “chariot” and “Nâgasena” can be useful designations over parts, sure, but the king’s original objection — who acts, who enjoys, who is reborn, whose kamma is it? — never quite get answered just by pointing at the parts. So for next week I’d like you to write a dialogue. Two characters: one defending the no-self reading of this passage (anatta, everything is just a conventional label over the aggregates), and the other arguing that the chariot analogy cheats, because a person isn’t a static heap of parts but something more like a causally continuous process, so the real issue is persistence over time rather than composition in the present moment. Each should take the other’s best points seriously, and neither gets to win by fiat. Push them until the anatta character has to say what the chariot analogy actually proves — does it show there is no self, or only that “self” is a conventional term? — and until the process/continuity character has to explain how a process can survive radical change without quietly smuggling a soul back in. Make it feel like two smart people actually listening to each other, not a staged debate.
