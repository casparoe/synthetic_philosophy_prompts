I'm a PhD student writing a chapter on the wrong kind of reasons problem — the problem of saying why some reasons for an attitude (admiration, fear, desire) make the attitude fitting, while others merely make it useful to have. I'm trying to firm up the object/state distinction, and my current criteria for a reason to hold attitude A toward X being the right kind are: (i) it is object-given — it cites a feature of X itself rather than the costs or benefits of holding A; and (ii) it is the kind of consideration that could make A fitting toward X (it could show X to be admirable, fearsome, lovable, and so on), not merely show that A would pay off. I take (i) and (ii) to be individually necessary and jointly sufficient. Standard demon cases are easy for me: a demon's threat is state-given and wouldn't make admiration fitting.

But here's a case I keep going in circles on. A therapist tells a man that his marriage is, for him, only sustainable if he keeps valuing it for its own sake; if he slips into valuing it mostly as a means — for the children, the house, the social standing — the marriage will predictably fall apart, and the therapist's track record is good enough that the man should believe this. The marriage is by any normal measure a genuinely good one, and the man wants it to survive. So he has a reason to value the marriage for its own sake: that if he does, a good marriage will survive. That reason looks state-given by (i), because it is explicitly about what the valuing would bring about. But it also seems to satisfy (ii), because a good marriage is exactly the sort of thing that makes valuing-for-its-own-sake fitting, and the reason points him toward that response. Does this reason count as the right kind under my two criteria? Go criterion by criterion, and where the criteria underdetermine the answer, say so. I'm also tryng to figure out whether this case shows the object/state distinction is less robust than I thought, or whether it just shows that a wrong-kind reason and a right-kind reason can be present simultaneously.
