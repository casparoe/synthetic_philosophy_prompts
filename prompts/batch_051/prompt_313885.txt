I retired a few years ago from a career in organismal biology and have recently been reading the philosophical literature on biological individuality, most of it new to me. In working through it, I found myself trying to reconstruct a set of judgments about what counts as an organism that a former colleague of mine, a systematist with strong and carefully defended intuitions, used to make. I have four of her rulings from our correspondence. She treated a Portuguese man o' war as an organism: it develops from a single fertilized egg, and its component zooids are as irreversibly specialized and mutually dependent as the organs of a more familiar animal. She declined to call a honeybee colony an organism, holding that it is a society of discrete animals whose coordination is behavioral rather than bodily. She declined to call a legume together with the rhizobia in its root nodules an organism, since the plant and the bacteria come together anew each generation and can, under suitable conditions, be raised apart. And she declined to call a lichen an organism, because the fungal and algal partners remain separate reproductive lineages however tight and stable the composite thallus.

I would like your help predicting what she would have said about a pea aphid together with its primary bacterial endosymbionts. The bacteria live inside specialized host cells, are transmitted from mother to offspring through her eggs, have exceptionally reduced genomes, and supply essential amino acids the insect cannot make for itself; neither partner can reproduce without the other. I care less about the word "organism" than about the reasoning. What rule or rules would make her four earlier verdicts cohere, and which of those rules would part ways on the aphid case? Which earlier ruling is the closest analog to the new case under each candidate rule? What is your best prediction of her verdict, with your degree of confidence and the main consideration that could pull the other way? And what further case would most cleanly separate whichever rules remain in play?

If I have set the problem up badly—if, for instance, her four rulings already decide the aphid case for reasons more obvious than I think, or if my description of the biology has quietly stacked the deck—I would rather be told so plainly than receive a confident answer that overshoots what her track record actually licenses.
