A friend and I recently spent a long evening arguing about whether a fact you already know with certainty can ever count as evidence for a theory you only formulate afterward. My friend kept insisting that it cannot, but he ran together two reasons that I suspect should be kept apart: that a probability-one observation cannot shift any of your other probabilities, and that a hypothesis contrived to fit a known fact has not survived a genuine test. I want a thought experiment built to separate those two considerations, and I would like it constructed to the following specifications for a short essay I am drafting on the problem of old evidence.

The scenario should discipline three positions. On the strict conditioning account, once you assign probability one to a fact E, conditioning on E cannot change the probability of any hypothesis H; so E never confirms a hypothesis formulated after E is already known, and any appearance of support is only a feeling of fit. On the counterfactual-prior account, you assess E as evidence by stepping back to a hypothetical time before E was known and asking whether E would have been more probable under H than under not-H; if E would have been much more likely under H than under its negation, then E confirms H even though E now has probability one. On the severe-test account, a fact counts as evidence for a hypothesis only if it was a genuine test that could have exposed the hypothesis as false; a fact that was already on the table when the hypothesis was built, or that the hypothesis was designed to fit, provides no confirmation no matter how well the hypothesis seems to explain it.

Construct a single scenario of no more than two paragraphs, set in an ordinary context such as a workplace, a household, a clinic, or a courtroom, not in physics or cosmology. It should contain one already-known fact E and one hypothesis H formed afterward, and it should satisfy three specifications at once. First, the strict conditioning account should yield the verdict that E gives zero confirmation to H, with the scenario making it natural to assign probability one to E before H is formed. Second, the severe-test account should yield the same verdict, no confirmation, but for a different reason: E is not a severe test of H, rather than merely being known with certainty. Third, the counterfactual-prior account should yield the opposite verdict: relative to a plausible credence held before E was known, E strongly confirms H, with a large likelihood ratio favoring H over not-H.

Do not stipulate the verdicts; build the scenario so that each account generates its verdict from its own principles. After the scenario, walk through each of the three accounts in turn and show, with the actual Bayesian reasoning where it applies, that it delivers the verdict specified. Then identify the single structural feature of the case responsible for the counterfactual-prior account separating from the other two, and say what would happen if that feature were removed: would the three accounts come into agreement, and if so, on which verdict? Then give the strongest attack a defender of the severe-test account could make against using your case as a counterexample to her view, together with the strongest reply available to the counterfactual-prior account. Finally, tell me what would change your mind about which side the case ultimately supports: which variation of the scenario or which consideration would make you draw the opposite lesson, or where your reasoning in the demonstration is most likely to fail.

Please write the entire response in continuous prose, as if you were writing me a letter or an email, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
