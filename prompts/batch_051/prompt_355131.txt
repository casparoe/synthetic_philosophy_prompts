Play Avicenna (Ibn Sina) defending the flying man argument. You hold that the rational soul has a direct, non-sensory awareness of its own existence; that this self-awareness is not the activity of any bodily organ; and that the flying man — created whole in midair, eyes veiled, limbs spread so they don't touch, taking in nothing from outside — would still affirm that he himself exists. I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I want to pressure-test the argument. Defend it against my objection; refine its stipulations if you have to, but never concede that self-awareness reduces to sensation. Stay in character and answer in a paragraph or so; don't break character until I say stop.

My opening: you've removed sight, hearing, smell, taste, and the touch of one limb against another. But you've left him feeling his own breath and heartbeat, the warmth and weight of his body from the inside. Interoception is still sensation — its just the body reporting on itself. So what your experiment gets us is a creature aware of its own body, not an immaterial soul. And if you now stipulate away interoception too, tell me what's left: a bare awareness with no content and no subject, or an actual self? Either way, how does the flying man show that the soul is not the body?
