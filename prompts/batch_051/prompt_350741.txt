I serve on the statistical advisory board of a biomedical journal, and for four years we have been unable to reach a stable policy on how far to push authors toward Bayesian methods. The frequentist members argue that null-hypothesis significance testing, confidence intervals, and preregistration give the journal an objective and replicable standard: an author either prespecified the analysis or did not, and a result either survives error control at the chosen level or does not. They see priors as researcher opinion dressed up as mathematics and warn that permitting authors to choose priors would simply move p-hacking one step upstream. The Bayesian members reply that p-values answer a question no scientist is actually asking—the probability of data at least this extreme under a null that is usually a straw man—whereas credible intervals and Bayes factors answer the question the scientist poses, namely how a particular study should shift a reader's degree of belief in competing hypotheses; on their view, the replication crisis is partly a product of reading p-values as if they were posterior probabilities. The two sides agree about the empirical facts of the crisis: low power, unreported analytical choices (forking paths), hypothesizing after the results are known (HARKing), publication bias, and garden-variety p-hacking are all real, and the board adopted preregistration and effect-size reporting without controversy. The stalemate crystallized for me last quarter, when a single manuscript drew two statistical referee reports: the frequentist referee asked for a prespecified p-value and a power analysis, while the Bayesian referee called the p-value uninformative and recommended a Bayes factor; the authors, reasonably, asked which standard the journal actually requires, and we could not tell them.

My working hypothesis is that the deadlock is not a dispute about the mathematics of inference and will not be settled by simulations or replicability statistics, but a disagreement about what a statistical analysis is for: long-run error control within a procedure, versus rational revision of degree of belief after a particular study. I can see at least two confounds, though: the two camps may simply be answering different questions under the single word 'inference', and there may be a hidden empirical bet about what will actually reduce non-replicable findings in editorial practice. I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict and rather than a tutorial on the two frameworks: what kind of disagreement is this, which parts are substantive as opposed to transposable into the other framework's terms, and, given that diagnosis, what would actually count as progress—a decision-theoretic result, an empirical study of reporting practices, a distinction, a change of question, or something else? If the honest answer is that no generally persuasive resolution should be expected, say why, and tell me what progress could still look like for a body like ours.
