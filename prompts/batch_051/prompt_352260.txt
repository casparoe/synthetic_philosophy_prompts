So I was reading about counterfeiting in the nineteenth century, and about how once bad notes got common enough people started treating even genuine notes with suspicion, and governments had to keep adding security features just to keep ordinary transactions moving. The thing that struck me is how much this resembles the line people use about deepfakes and video evidence. The thought is that recordings work as a kind of epistemic backstop for testimony: if a person claims something happened and there's a video, we tend to treat the video as the thing that settles it, without checking its provenance or chain of custody. Deepfakes attack exactly that default—the same way a counterfeiter attacks the fact that a shopkeeper only glances at a bill before passing it along.

The parallel I want to test is not just that both are fakes. It's that currency and recorded evidence are both network goods whose usefulness depends on widespread, low-effort acceptance, and both get undermined at the point of least inspection. If that structural part is right, does anything from monetary economics actually carry over? For instance, the old idea that bad money drives out good, or the tendency under counterfeiting pressure for people to move toward hard-to-fake tokens—would an epistemic version predict that we should start treating only cryptographically signed or otherwise heavily authenticated recordings as evidence, and that anonymous video becomes worth less and less? I'm not sure that's really happening, which makes me suspect the analogy snaps somewhere. What would have to be true of both sides for results to transfer, and where exactly does it come apart? Also, please seperate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who study this, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
