Machiavelli's chapter 25 of The Prince has this passage:

"Changes in estate also issue from this, for if, to one who governs himself with caution and patience, times and affairs converge in such a way that his administration is successful, his fortune is made; but if times and affairs change, he is ruined if he does not change his course of action. But a man is not often found sufficiently circumspect to know how to accommodate himself to the change, both because he cannot deviate from what nature inclines him to do, and also because, having always prospered by acting in one way, he cannot be persuaded that it is well to leave it; and, therefore, the cautious man, when it is time to turn adventurous, does not know how to do it, hence he is ruined; but had he changed his conduct with the times fortune would not have changed."

A friend sent me his reconstruction of this, and i can't tell whether it goes through or whether one step is doing something sly.

(1) In the passage, the cautious man is ruined when the times turn adventurous because he cannot deviate from what his nature inclines him to do.
(2) If someone cannot deviate from what his nature inclines him to do, then changing his conduct with the times is not something he is able to do.
(3) So the cautious man is unable to change his conduct with the times.
(4) Yet the passage says that, had he changed his conduct with the times, fortune would not have changed.
(5) A claim of the form "had he done X, Y would not have happened" implies that he could have done X.
(6) So the passage implies both that the cautious man could not change and that he could have changed, which makes Machiavelli's account of why princes fail inconsistent.

Is this argument valid? If not, which step fails and why? If it is valid, is there a reading of the passage that still avoids the contradiction? And if I'm setting the whole thing up wrong, just tell me straight instead of humoring me.
