I keep noticing what might be a real parallel and I can't tell if it's deep or just a pun. The tool-AI vs agent-AI debate in AI safety keeps mapping itself, point for point, onto the old distinction in agency law between acting through an instrument and acting through an agent. In the legal version: if I drive my car through your fence, I'm on the hook for my own act. If I instead send someone else in my car and tell them to just 'get it there by five', and they plow through your fence while trying, I might still be on the hook — but now it's because they were my agent and they were inside the scope of what I delegated, not because I personally steered. The line there is drawn by things like whether I control the means or only the ends, how much discretion the other person has, and whether they're acting on my behalf. In AI safety, the tool/agent line looks like the same thing: a tool sits inert between inputs, has a narrow action space, and can't take a multi-step path you didn't spell out; an agent gets a goal, can plan, can take actions you didn't enumerate, and can do damage 'within the scope of its authority' — and that phrase alone makes me suspect I'm not imagining the isomorphism.

The reason I care is that if the analogy is load-bearing, it might give AI people a borrowed set of concepts instead of reinventing them badly. So: is this a genuine identity of structure or am I just pattern-matching on the words 'tool' and 'agent'? If it's real, what transfers from agency law to thinking about AI systems — vicarious liability, scope of authority, apparent authority, the employee-vs-independent-contractor line, the old rule that a principal isn't liable when the errand-runner goes off on a personal detour? What would have to be true on both sides for those to carry over, and where does the mapping come apart (an AI isnt a legal person, doesn't form intentions the way an employee does, and generally can't be 'hired')? As a test case, the analogy seems to predict that a customer-service chatbot with too much apparent authority could bind the company that deployed it, and my gut says that's wrong — but I can't say why without leaning on 'corporations only act through people.' So I'd like the full diagnosis: the mapping stated precisely, the points where the analogy breaks, and one prediction the parallel makes that you think comes out false.
