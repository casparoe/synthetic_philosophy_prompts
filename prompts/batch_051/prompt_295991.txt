A friend has constructed an argument which, he says, shows that omissions never really cause anything, and I cannot find the exit. The argument runs:

(1) A cause must be something on which the effect counterfactually depends: had the cause not occurred, the effect would not have occurred.
(2) An omission is not an occurrence at all; it is the absence of one — the guard's failure to feed the prisoner is just the non-occurrence of a feeding, not some further event over and above it.
(3) Counterfactual dependence can hold only between occurrences, since evaluating "had X not occurred" requires removing something definite from the actual world, and an absence offers nothing definite to remove.
(4) Therefore omissions are not causes, and sentences such as "the guard's failure to feed caused the prisoner's death" are, strictly speaking, false, or at best shorthand for something non-causal.

I resist the conclusion because (4) seems to prove too much: it would make the difference between the negligent guard and a distant stranger — both of whom failed to save the prisoner — a difference only in our moral expectations rather than in anything genuinely causal. I am equally unwilling to deny that the engineer's failure to inspect caused the bridge collapse, or that a parent's neglect can cause real harm. Which premise should be rejected, and what does rejecting it commit one to? In particular, can one preserve the truth of ordinary causation-by-omission claims without having to count absences among the things that exist?
