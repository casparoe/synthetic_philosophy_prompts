I'm an undergraduate taking a course on rational choice and the foundations of economic decision theory. In one lecture, the instructor defended the idea that one person taking many independent risky choices over a lifetime is, for evaluative purposes, like a large population each taking one of those choices exactly once. He uses this analogy to license expected-value reasoning for individuals: since a population would on average come out ahead, a single person repeatedly rolling the same kind of risk over a lifetime is, in the relevant sense, coming out ahead on average too, and should therefore choose by the ensemble expected value.

A visiting speaker objected with a concrete case. Suppose a gamble multiplies your current wealth by 1.5 with probability 1/2 and by 0.6 with probability 1/2, and is repeated each period on whatever wealth you have accumulated. Across a large ensemble taking it once, the expected multiplier is 1.05, so average wealth rises. But a single player who stays fully invested and repeats the gamble indefinitely has a per-period geometric mean growth factor sqrt(1.5 × 0.6) = sqrt(0.9), which is below 1, and with probability one the player's wealth tends toward zero over time. The visitor concluded that the lifetime-as-population analogy is not merely rough but actively misleading, and that an individual maximizing long-run wellbeing should maximize a time-average growth rate, not expected value.

I don't yet trust either side's conclusion. Please audit the analogy itself. Give an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the population members, their simultaneous outcomes, the averaging across them, the time horizon, the individual's successive states or selves, and anything else that belongs in the mapping. Then identify where the mapping breaks down, separating the disanalogies that matter for the instructor's expected-value conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Which of the instructor's conclusions survive the disanalogies, and where was the analogy doing the real argumentative work? Finally, tell me where your own verdict is least secure, and what argument or consideration would most likely change your mind about it.
