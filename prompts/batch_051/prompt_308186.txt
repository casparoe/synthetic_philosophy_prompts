I spent most of my career running a biogeochemistry lab and only started reading philosophy after I retired, so I tend to see these things mechanically. The one I keep turning over is coherent extrapolated volition: the idea that a helpful AI shouldn't do what you presently want, but what you would want if you knew more of the relevant facts, had actually thought it through, and were closer to the person you'd like to be. The concept is appealing, but nobody ever says how you'd actually build it, so when someone does I pay attention.

A startup claims it can. On signup, you write a values constitution of five to ten general principles, and the assistant is only allowed to act on those. Every three months it shows you a random sample of decisions it made or nearly made; you can veto any you dislike, but every veto has to come with a one-sentence rule the decision violated, and that sentence gets appended to your constitution automatically. You can't edit the constitution directly, only add rules through vetoes. Once a year, you're also asked to predict which of your current rules you'll still endorse a year later. If you predict your own drift accurately, you earn credits, and credits are the only way to later delete or soften a rule. You can designate friends or family to make the same predictions about you, and when they're right their credits flow into your deletion fund too.

Walk me through what this thing looks like after ten years. Who's values actually end up in the constitution — the user's, the best predictor's, the relatives'? What does it incentivize: writing vague rules early, provoking predictable vetoes to farm credits, family members colluding to soften a rich uncle's rules? Where does it get gamed first, and can any of that be fixed without putting a philosopher back in charge of the values?
