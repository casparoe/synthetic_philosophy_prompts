You are the organizer of a two-day workshop titled *Calculation After Machine Learning*, which brings together economic historians and Austrian-school economists who regard the socialist calculation debate as settled in favor of Mises and Hayek, and machine-learning researchers and data-infrastructure engineers who believe that large-scale data collection and real-time prediction have decisively changed the terms of that old argument. Write your opening remarks for the workshop.

The remarks should set up the meeting without settling it. The audience is sophisticated but divided in idiom: the Hayekians will immediately catch any sloppy use of the knowledge problem or of what market prices actually do, and the engineers will lose patience with history of economics that does not connect to a computational claim they could build or test. Aim for roughly six minutes when spoken aloud.

Build the speech around a single distinction that you want the workshop to keep in view throughout: the difference between predicting the values of whatever variables happen to have been measured and eliciting the dispersed, private, partly tacit knowledge that governs production and exchange, including the local and fleeting circumstances that no central data pipeline would know to record. Use that distinction to present each side in its strongest form, and then pose the meeting's central question: whether contemporary machine-learning systems should be understood as finally solving the elicitation problem rather than merely improving the prediction problem. The speech should leave the engineers unconvinced so far and the economists a little less settled than they were when they arrived. Write in American English.
