I keep going back and forth on promising. On paper I'm basically Humean about it, and this passage is doing most of the work—David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature*, Book III, Part II, Section V:

That the rule of morality, which enjoins the performance of promises, is not natural, will sufficiently appear from these two propositions, which I proceed to prove, viz. that a promise wou'd not be intelligible, before human conventions had establish'd it; and that even if it were intelligible, it wou'd not be attended with any moral obligation.

What I like is that it doesn't posit a special moral faculty or a sui generis promissory obligation; promising becomes a piece of social technology. It explains why promising needs a background practice, how a few words can bind a stranger, why promises seem so tied to institutions, and why they don't bind people who couldn't understand the convention.

But there's the case that keeps me up. My sister is dying and asks me to promise to raise her son in our hometown, near her grave, even tho everyone involved thinks he'd do better in the city where I have a job. I promise, partly so she can die at peace. She dies. No one else knows about the promise, the kid is too young to know, and keeping it means turning down the job and probably making both our lives worse. There's no ongoing reliance, no practice I'd be undermining if I don't keep it, and no one left to release me. And yet I'm convinced the obligation is still there, and it's owed to her—not just to some useful convention. The Humean in me says this is exactly the sort of sentiment the account was designed to handle, but I can't make myself beleive the wrongness here is only the disvalue to the institution of promising.

Walk me through the strongest version of both sides. What would it take for the Humean view to absorb the case, and is there a reading of the passage on which it does? What would I have to accept to give the intuition up instead—and is the intuition tracking something the theory leaves out? I don't want you to just pick a side; I want to know what would have to be true for each move to be justified, and whether there's a third path where they don't actually collide. At the very end, after you've done the actual work, give me one final line in the form \answer{...} with one of these four: \answer{intuition yields}, \answer{theory yields}, \answer{compatible}, \answer{neither}.
