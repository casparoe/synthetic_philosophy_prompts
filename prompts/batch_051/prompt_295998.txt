You are a cognitive scientist who studies concepts, analogy, and computational models of cognition. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming two or three possible tests before settling on the one you judge strongest.

I want your help determining whether a certain claim is a substantive empirical claim or only a redescription. The claim concerns conceptual slippage in a deliberately tiny microdomain: letter-string analogy problems. A participant sees a change such as abc → abd and must apply "the same change" to a target string such as ijk, where the natural answer is ijl. The interesting cases arise when a literal rule gets blocked. If asked to apply abc → abd to xyz, for instance, a literal rule such as "replace the rightmost letter by its successor" comes under pressure: a wrap-around reading gives xya, a mirror reading gives wyz, and which answer counts as "the same change" shifts with framing and context.

One camp describes this behavior in terms of fluid concepts: when the concept successor is blocked, it slips into a nearby concept such as predecessor; when rightmost is blocked, it slips into leftmost; and the slipped concept then drives the answer. The rival camp says this is just a vivid way of describing an ordinary fixed mechanism: a stable repertoire of features and rules with parameters for direction, position, wrap-around, and boundary conditions. On that rival view, no distinct process of conceptual slippage exists.

I want to know whether the fluid-concepts story makes a difference that a test could detect. Design the strongest experiment or empirical study you can, within this microdomain, that would pull the two accounts apart. Specify the materials, the manipulation, and the exact pattern of results that would favor each side. Then say, with as much precision as you can, what a positive result would and would not show, and what residue no experiment of this kind could settle — for example, because any fixed-rule account can be extended post hoc to fit the data, or because the disagreement is ultimately about how to individuate mental representations.
