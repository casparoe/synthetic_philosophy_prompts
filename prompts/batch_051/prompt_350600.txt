I have been trying to understand Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles on my own, and I keep running into the same illustration. A popular philosophy book I read summarizes the whole issue like this:

'Leibniz held that no two distinct things can be exactly alike; he treated this as a foundational principle of his metaphysics. But suppose there is a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres, identical in size, composition, temperature, and color, hanging motionless two miles apart in otherwise empty space. These spheres share every property there is: each is made of iron, each is perfectly spherical, each is two miles from another iron sphere, and so forth. Yet they are plainly two spheres, not one. So Leibniz's principle is false. The only way to rescue it would be to posit some hidden difference between them, or to fall back on the bare fact that one is this sphere and the other is that sphere — and that is just the kind of empty metaphysics that science has no use for.'

This strikes me as persuasive, but I also suspect the summary is sliding between 'exactly alike' and 'sharing every property,' and I am not sure the imagined universe settles as much as the author claims. Could you go through this summary piece by piece and tell me which parts a careful defender of the principle would accept, which parts they would resist, and where the author is conflating distinct claims or drawing a stronger conclusion than the example supports? I am less interested in a final verdict on the principle than in knowing exactly how much of this criticism survives once the terms are kept straight.
