I've been arguing with a friend who writes electronic music about whether she can properly be said to create her pieces. She insists that when she finishes a track she has brought a new musical work into existence, and I want to agree with her. But I came across an argument (I'm reconstructing it in my own words) that seems to prove otherwise, and I haven't been able to find the flaw, though I half-suspect one of the early steps is doing too much work. It runs as follows:

(1) A musical work is a type: a repeatable structure whose tokens are particular performances, playings of recordings, and other sound-events.
(2) Types are abstract objects, like numbers, properties, or species: they are not located in space and time.
(3) No abstract object can be brought into existence by a human action, because making is a causal, spatiotemporal process, and an abstract object is not the sort of thing that could be the effect of such a process.
(4) To compose a musical work is to bring that work into existence: before the composition is completed the work does not exist, and when the composition is completed the work exists.
(5) If composing a work brought it into existence, then a human action would have brought an abstract object into existence, which (3) rules out.
(6) Therefore, no one ever composes a musical work; at most, a "composer" selects or discovers a structure that was already there.

I find (6) absurd, and it seems to misdescribe the creative act of composing. I'm torn between thinking the argument is valid but has a false premise, and thinking there is an equivocation somewhere—perhaps on "type," or on what it means for a work to "exist" after being composed. Please first tell me whether the argument is valid. Then commit to a diagnosis: which step, if any, should be rejected, and what is the strongest alternative account of a musical work that preserves the intuition in (1) that works can have many performances while still allowing composers to genuinely create them? Don't just lay out possible responses; I'd rather you take a definite position and defend it against the others.
