After retiring from a career in experimental neuroscience, I have been reading philosophy of mind and trying to work out what I actually believe about the limits of a finite mind. I would like you to audit the following set of commitments for internal consistency. I am not interested in whether you think each thesis is true; I want to know whether they can all be held together, and if not, which pairs strain hardest and which adjustments would cost least.

Here are the positions I find myself drawn to, stated as plainly as I can. First, the brain is a finite physical system; for any empirically accessible level of description, the number of possible total states available to a given brain over a normal life is finite, even if the number is astronomically large. Second, conscious experience is exhaustively fixed by the physical-functional state of the brain; adding any nonphysical ingredient would not add further experiential states beyond that finite state-space. Third, a finite physical system can nevertheless think about infinite domains, such as the natural numbers or the real line, because a finite representational system can deploy recursive or compositional rules whose content is infinite even though the vehicle is finite. Fourth, there is no finite list of experience-types such that a person who had worked through the list could then have no genuinely new experience; the space of lived experience is open-ended. Fifth, the set of basic, discriminably different sensory qualities available to a particular organism is finite, because the underlying receptor channels, discrimination thresholds, and comparison processes are finite in number and resolution. Sixth, if two individuals had exactly the same physical-functional history over a whole life, there would be no difference in what their lives were like for them. Seventh, when people say that experience contains an actual infinity of distinct qualities, they are confusing potential unboundedness through indefinitely many recombination steps with completed actual infinity.

I suspect that several of these are only superficially in tension, and that one or two may be in deeper trouble than I can see. Please go through the set in an audit. For each pair that is genuinely in tension, state what each side requires and how severe the conflict is, then say what the cheapest revision would be. Also identify any apparent tensions that dissolve once the terms are sharpened, so I can distinguish real problems from merely verbal ones. Finally, tell me which single revision would do the most to make the whole set hang together, and what that revision would cost me.

Do not present this as a general lecture on the topic; treat it as a consistency check on my stated position. And please write your response in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a letter.
