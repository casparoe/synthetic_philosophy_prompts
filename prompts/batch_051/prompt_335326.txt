I have been going back and forth with two colleagues about whether mathematics is discovered or invented, and each time I press them the debate seems to shift between claims of very different strength. The strong claim is that numbers, sets, and other mathematical objects exist independently of human minds and practices, roughly as real but nonphysical things. The weak claim is only that, once a system of axioms and rules is fixed, what follows from it is not up to us: any competent reasoner who worked long enough would reach the same theorems, whether or not those theorems are about an independently existing realm. I am not satisfied with the common reply that the whole dispute is purely verbal, because the two claims seem to me to be of different kinds, and I suspect evidence might bear on one even if it cannot bear on the other.

I would like you to design, as rigorously as you can, a test or observational program for the claim that mathematics is discovered rather than invented. Be concrete about the data you would collect. Candidate sources of evidence might include simultaneous independent proofs of the same theorem, the surprising applicability of pure mathematics to physics, patterns of cross-cultural convergence and divergence in mathematical development, or mathematicians’ own reports that certain results felt forced rather than chosen. For each line of evidence you propose, specify what result would count in favor of discovery over invention, and then say as precisely as possible what a favorable result would and would not establish. In particular, I want to know whether any conceivable result could support the strong mind-independence claim, or whether positive evidence can at most support the weaker claim that mathematical truth is compulsory relative to accepted rules. If the strong claim turns out to be untestable, say whether it should be regarded as a different kind of claim rather than merely a bad one.
