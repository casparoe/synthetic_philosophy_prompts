I've been turning over an argument about belief and inquiry, and I can't decide whether its skeptical conclusion follows or whether the machinery is suspect somewhere. (1) A method of fixing belief is adequate only if it can settle disagreement and survive the question whether the beliefs it yields are true. (2) The method of tenacity — holding fast to whatever one already believes — fails this test, because against a rival view it can only beg the question. (3) The method of authority — letting institutions dictate doctrine — fails, because no authority can suppress every source of contrary opinion, and once contrary opinions leak through, the doctrine is destabilized. (4) The a priori method — letting what is agreeable to reason settle the matter — fails, because what is agreeable to reason shifts with fashion and offers no external check. (5) The method of science — testing belief against the resistance of experience — alone passes, since it lets something independent of any inquirer constrain the answer. (6) But the method of science passes only because it presupposes that there is a reality independent of inquiry and that inquiry will in the long run converge upon it. (7) That presupposition cannot be secured by the method of science itself without circularity, since the scientific method is the very thing whose credentials are at issue. (8) Therefore the scientific method rests, no less than the others, on a preference or leap of faith rather than on the world. I suspect the mischief is in (6) or (7) — perhaps they demand an external guarantee where a fallibilist would accept a revisable hypothesis — but I can't make the diagnosis stick. Say exactly which step or steps fail, if any, and supply the correct version of the argument.
