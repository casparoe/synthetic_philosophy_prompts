I am a novelist at work on a book whose protagonist is a historian of mathematics preparing a new commentary on Euclid's Elements, and the intellectual drama of the novel turns on a real quarrel in the historiography of mathematics over how to read Book II of the Elements. For much of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries a standard reading treated Book II as "geometric algebra": the proposition that a line cut at random yields, in modern notation, (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b² was taken to show that Euclid was really doing algebra in geometric costume, solving what we would now call equations before symbolic algebra was available. From around the 1970s onward, a rival school rejected this as anachronistic, insisting that Greek mathematics had no unknowns, no equations, and no algebra-like operations on magnitudes, and that translating Euclid into x's and y's imposes a later conceptual scheme on him.

I want to make this dispute dramatically intelligible rather than merely reproduce its slogans. Analyze this historiographical quarrel as a case study in the problem of anachronism in reading old mathematics and science. Specifically: what exactly is the anachronism the critics attribute to the "geometric algebra" reading, and is it a single error or several distinct ones—linguistic, conceptual, teleological? Can the modernizing reading be defended as a non-anachronistic translation, and under what conditions? Then run the analysis in the other direction: what does this episode reveal about the concept of anachronism itself—for example, where the charge of anachronism does genuine historical work and where it merely vetoes useful notation? This matters to me because it will govern how my protagonist writes his commentary: every time he places an algebraic paraphrase beside Euclid's geometry, I need to know precisely what he is claiming and what he is not.
