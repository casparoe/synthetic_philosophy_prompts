I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about fakes and originals, and my gut keeps giving me answers that don't fit any single principle I can state. Here are the cases. (1) A newly "discovered" Vermeer hangs in a museum for years and everyone finds it gorgeous; then it's exposed as a 1930s forgery. I think the revaluation is right, not just a market panic—the object really is a different artwork now, even though the pigments didn't change. (2) But I don't think I've read a "lesser" novel when I read a cheap modern paperback rather than a first edition; the text is the work. (3) If I learned a love letter from my partner was copied word-for-word from a novel, I'd be hurt, even if the words said exactly what I needed; yet I can't say why the copied words are worse when the feeling is the same. (4) A game-used bat is worth more to me than an identical bat that sat in a warehouse, but I suspect that's just the appeal of association and may not belong in the same account. (5) If a perfect copy of the Mona Lisa were made down to the last craqueleure, I'd still save the original from a fire, but I can't articulate what I'm saving beyond a particular piece of wood. (6) A staged photograph of a battle isn't a document anymore, but it might still be a good photograph; I'd value it less as news and maybe the same as art—I go back and forth. Is there one principle, or a small set, that captures these verdicts? If some of them have to go, tell me which and why. And what does the principle say about a case I can't settle: a conservator rebuilds a lost painting from photographs so exactly that no one can tell it from the original—is that a restoration, a copy, or a forgery, and what value does it have?
