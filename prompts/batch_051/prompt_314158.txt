In a seminar on the political economy of hunger, a classmate offered his take on Amartya Sen's work on famines, which he said he had absorbed from a widely circulated popular explainer. He was summarizing from memory rather than quoting, but the gist was this. Sen's central claim is that famines are not fundamentally natural disasters; they are failures of particular groups to command food. The decisive case is the Bengal famine of 1943, where the harvest had not catastrophically failed, yet at least a couple of million people died because wartime inflation, speculation, and hoarding pushed rice beyond what rural laborers could pay, while the colonial administration did far too little. The useful idea is "entitlement," which in practice comes down to purchasing power: when grain prices outrun rural wages, exchange entitlements collapse and people starve even while warehouses are full. The Irish famine, by contrast, was a genuine natural disaster—the potato blight destroyed the crop the poor lived on, and there simply was not enough food on the island, so Sen's framework must concede that some famines really are acts of nature. The Great Leap Forward famine is the clearest politically caused case: collectivization, falsified harvest reports, and brutal grain procurement created the famine, which is best described as a political crime. This is why Sen argued that famines do not occur in functioning democracies; elections and a free press give rulers an incentive to prevent mass hunger, so famine is essentially a symptom of authoritarianism. The practical lesson is that once a country has democratic institutions and a free press, famines will not happen, and when one does occur we should look for culpable political decisions rather than weather or crop failure.

I find this broadly persuasive, but two things nag at me. The treatment of the Irish famine as the natural exception feels too tidy, given that the blight destroyed a crop but not necessarily the entitlement structure, and the closing claim about democracies sounds less like an empirical pattern and more like a law of history. I have not read Sen myself, though, so I cannot tell whether my reservations track real distinctions or only my own priors. Please audit the summary. Which parts would Sen or a careful scholar in his tradition accept? Which are common simplifications or overstatements? What would a defender of the view push back on? And do the conclusions about democracy and political culpability survive once the accurate parts are separated from the stretched ones? Be precise about the distinctions among absolute food shortage, a decline in aggregate food availability, and a collapse of exchange or transfer entitlements, and about the difference between an empirical regularity and a guarantee.
