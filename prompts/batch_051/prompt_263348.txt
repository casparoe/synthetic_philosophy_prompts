I spent my working life in the natural sciences, mostly building simplified models of systems too complicated to study whole, and I have only recently begun reading philosophy in earnest. One resemblance keeps nagging at me, and I would like help deciding whether it is structural or merely verbal.

In scientific modeling, idealization is not a failure to be cleaned away. The frictionless surface, the point mass, the ideal gas: the model asserts something known to be false about a particular system—no real surface is frictionless, no real gas is ideal—in order to make a true general pattern visible. The falsehood is controlled and local, and in the better cases there is a procedure for restoring the omitted detail and estimating what it costs.

I keep seeing the same logic at work in historical fiction and biopic screenwriting. A writer compresses a decade into a season, merges two historical people into one character, moves a death to a more legible moment, or puts sentences in a real person's mouth that no document records. The defense offered is the one I used to give: the alteration is not the point; it clears away clutter so that the deeper truth about the person or the period can show through.

Please help me check whether this parallel survives scrutiny. I am not asking for a literature review; I want the comparison itself tested. State the most defensible mapping between the two practices—what in the scientific case corresponds to the documentary record, to the real person, to the law or theory, to the audience, and to the justified falsehood—and then say what would have to be true on both sides for a justification to transfer from one domain to the other. I can already see two possible fault lines. First, in physics the justification of an idealization is roughly quantitative: one argues that the omitted factor is small, or causally inert, or irrelevant at the scale in question, and one can in principle put it back. A composite character or an invented scene has no analogue of that error estimate, and often cannot be corrected without dissolving the story. Second, a gas molecule has no interests and no descendants, and is not the object of anyone's grief or trust; the dead are different. Are these fatal to the parallel, or only the places where an analogy naturally changes register? Also name at least one concrete thing the parallel would predict about why some biopics succeed or fail that turns out to be false in practice.

If the entire comparison is confused from the outset—for instance because I have packed too much into the word "truth," or because scientific idealization and narrative invention falsify in opposite directions—I would rather be told that plainly than receive a more elegant version of the analogy.
