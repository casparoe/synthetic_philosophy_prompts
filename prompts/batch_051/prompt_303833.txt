I'm working through Spinoza's political writings for a seminar and I keep getting stuck on how he actually thinks about democracy, because two places seem to pull in different directions. In the Theological-Political Treatise, around chapter 16, he basically says democracy is the most natural kind of state, since when everyone transfers their natural right they hand it over to the whole community rather than to some single person, and so people remain as free and equal as they were in the state of nature. That sounds like a pretty inclusive picture. But then in the unfinished Political Treatise, chapter 11, he goes out of his way to argue that women should have no share in ruling, and from what I remember he doesn't just say it's a bad idea under the circumstances; he seems to say women are by nature subject to men, or at least that they are naturally unfit to govern, and that this justifies excluding them from the assembly.

So my question is whether these two views are genuinely in tension. On one reading he's contradicting himself, or just blind about who counts as everyone. On another, his defintion of democracy is purely formal, about where sovereignty is placed, and it doesn't promise that every human being will be counted as a citizen. Or maybe he changed his mind, or maybe he thinks equality in natural right is compatible with excluding some people from participating in actual government. What I'd like is a careful account of how these two passages can or can't be reconciled, what it forces you to give up about the usual picture of Spinoza as a democrat, and whether the reconciliations that make him consistent do so only by making his earlier democracy pretty thin. Also, if you think there is a real tension, say which of the two positions better represents his considered view and why.
