I've been trying to work out what I think about who owns a thing when the owner is gone, and I want a principle that fits my gut calls. Here's where I land on some cases:

- A guy dies in 1965 with no heirs and no estate. His nephew later finds a box of his photographs in an attic and posts the good ones online with a note saying "my uncle took these." Fine.
- A publisher folded in the 70s and no one knows who holds the rights to a certain out-of-print novel. A library scans it and puts it online. Fine.
- A shareware DOS game from 1993: the coder is dead, the company is long gone, no one can find a rights holder. Someone archives the binary on a retro site. Fine, even if the archiver is taking donations to keep the site up.
- Same game, but a company buys the rights from a cousin who claims to have inherited them, slaps a new logo on it, and sells it. I feel like that's not fine, or at least the company shouldn't be able to stop the free archive from before.
- An open-source repo with no license file. The maintainer vanished five years ago. Forking it seems fine; changing it to a closed-source, paid product seems not fine. But if a fork becomes popular and starts charging for support, I can't put my finger on why that's different from the paid closed version and whether it should be allowed.
- A living photographer's out-of-print book is scanned and posted by strangers without asking. Not fine, even though it's not for sale anymore.
- A diary of a woman who died 60 years ago with no children or known relatives; a scholar publishes big excerpts in an academic book. I think it's fine, though something about publishing it without anyone ever having asked her bothers me a little.
- A dead jazz musician's unreleased live tapes, with no known estate. Fans trade them; I think fine. A label issues them for sale under the musician's name; I think less fine, but maybe only because I don't trust the label not to sit on further recordings.

Is there a single principle that gets all of these, or nearly? In particular, what does your best principle say about this one I keep flip-flopping on: a company builds a "lost media" database, does a genuinely dilligent search, and then claims the legal right to license commercial use of anything it has designated abandoned or orphaned, whether the original creator is found later or not. Should it get to do that? I'm not asking for the actual legal answer or citations; I want the principle you'd defend. And please tell me which of my listed verdicts, if any, your principle forces me to overturn.
