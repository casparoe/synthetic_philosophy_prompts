I'm a PhD student in philosophy of logic, and I've been going round in circles on Lewis Carroll's tortoise piece — you know, Achilles and the tortoise and the rule that refuses to be a premise. I keep ending up with an argument that seems to prove no-one has ever been rationally compelled by a deductive argument, which is obviously mad. Here's the chain as I'd write it on a board:

(1) To be rationally compelled to accept q on the basis of p and if-p-then-q, you have to accept modus ponens; the two premises alone don't force the move.
(2) Accepting modus ponens is just taking another claim to be true — the claim that any inference of that shape preserves truth.
(3) So the real premises of the inference are p, if-p-then-q, and that further claim.
(4) But getting q from these three premises needs another rule, and by the same reasoning that rule is a further premise, and so on.
(5) So either you need an infinite stack of premises and never reach q, or at some point you make the move without first accepting a rule for it — which is exactly the un-compelled leap we were trying to rule out.
(6) Therefore no deductive argument ever rationally compels anyone; every inference bottoms out in something logic doesn't justify.
(7) But then proof, maths, and most of reasoning are no more rational than habit, which can't be right.

I'd most like to keep (1), but maybe that's the one that has to go. Tell me which step to reject, why it looked fine, and what rejecting it costs. I can see at least three escape routes — rules are permissions rather than premises, accepting a rule is know-how rather than belief, the regress is benign — but I can't work out whether any of them actually saves (1) or just relocates the problem, and what each one gives up elsewhere. And please separate what's actually settled about this from what's still genuinely contested among people who work on the normative force of logic; don't present a contested resolution as the consensus.
