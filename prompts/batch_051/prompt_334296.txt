Act as a specialist in philosophical logic and constructive mathematics, equally at home with classical proof theory and with the Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov interpretation of the connectives. Think carefully and show your work: begin by brainstorming two or three candidate existence claims and saying briefly why each does or does not satisfy the constraints below, then choose one and develop it.

Construct a thought experiment in the form of a specific mathematical dispute between a classical mathematician, C, and an intuitionistic mathematician, I, over a single existence claim. The scenario itself should be written in one paragraph and must satisfy all of the following:

1. The existence claim is simple to state and uses no more than real numbers, natural-number sequences, the rationality or irrationality of a number, and the notion of a decidable property. No exotic objects, no set-theoretic pathologies, no appeal to any choice principle.
2. C proves the claim using a genuinely classical step, such as a case split on whether a number of the relevant sort is rational or irrational, or an application of the law of excluded middle to a proposition not known to be decidable. C's proof must be mathematically correct by classical standards.
3. I must not be made to assert the negation of the existence claim. I may at most say that C's proof does not establish the claim, and that without a construction the claim is not available to I. If a separate constructive proof of the same claim exists, mention it only to note that it is irrelevant to the dispute about C's proof.
4. The disagreement must not depend on either side misunderstanding the other's standard. Within or immediately after the scenario, have C correctly state I's rule that an existence proof must supply a construction or an effective method, and have I correctly state C's rule that a proof may proceed by treating each proposition as determinately true or false.
5. The two sides should part company over whether C's proof is valid as a proof of existence, not over a minor gap that could be filled by a small constructive repair.

After the scenario, do the following:

(a) Present C's proof and mark the exact inference I refuses to accept, naming the logical principle it relies on.
(b) Give I's objection in Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov terms: state precisely what I requires in order to accept an existence proof, and why C's inference fails to supply it.
(c) Give each party's verdict on the original existence claim as licensed by C's proof: C asserts the claim; I withholds it and does not assert its negation.
(d) Identify the structural feature of the case doing the work, for example an undecidable predicate, a double-negated existence statement, or a case split that cannot be eliminated within the proof as given.
(e) Explain what happens if that feature is removed: does the proof become constructively acceptable, and does the disagreement collapse?

Finally, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested. The settled part should include the formal facts: that C's proof is classically valid, that I's objection is internally consistent with intuitionistic proof theory, and that I's rejection of C's proof does not intuitionistically license the negation of the existence claim. The contested part should include the philosophical interpretation of existence in mathematics: whether a classically valid existence proof already amounts to a proof of existence in the full-blooded sense, or merely to a proof that the claim cannot fail without a witness being supplied. Do not present either interpretation as the consensus; instead, say how a defender of the classical truth-conditional reading would reply to I's objection, and how an intuitionist would answer that reply.
