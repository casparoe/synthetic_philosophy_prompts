I edit a column on how science and philosophy handle concept formation, and I'd like your analysis of a proposal that has come across my desk. A consortium of journals and research funders is considering a rule, built around the Bongard problems familiar from concept-learning research, to govern when a new scientific category may be used in funded work and in print. For readers who haven't seen them: a Bongard problem shows a small set of examples that fit an unstated rule and a small set that violate it, and the task is to infer the rule from the contrast. Under the proposed rule, any team wanting to introduce a category—a new cell type, a personality profile, a climate mode, a genre of online behavior—must file twelve examples: six the team says fall inside the category and six the team says fall outside, chosen to sit as close to the boundary as possible and presented with their labels. Three outside evaluators see the twelve examples but not the team's rule. Each evaluator writes down the one-sentence rule they believe the examples were chosen to illustrate. The file passes only if at least two evaluators converge on the same rule, as judged by a separate reader who sees the two formulations placed side by side and must judge them equivalent without knowing their authors. A failed file may be refiled once with fresh examples, but the team may not simply rename the category or rotate the boundary examples. A passed file licenses the category for one funding cycle or two review rounds, after which the team must file again.

Treat the proposal as a piece of institutional design, not as a referendum on whether categories are real. Walk through how it would operate. What sorts of categories would survive the filter, and which would be quietly dropped? Where would it be gamed first, and by whom? What would it do to the examples scientists choose to publish, to how they talk about their categories before filing, and to how collaborations manage internal disagreement? Which failure modes could be fixed by adjusting the rule, and which seem intrinsic to the idea that a category should be teachable from a few labeled examples? I'd like the analysis as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
