A colleague is drafting a thought experiment about whether officially recognized social categories can be made to behave as administrative kinds — revised and redistributed by the people classified under them — rather than as natural kinds the state merely discovers. She has proposed the following mechanism and wants it stress-tested rather than judged in the abstract.

The setting is a mid-sized jurisdiction replacing an inherited set of official racial classifications. Under the new scheme, each resident must annually register exactly one category from a closed list maintained by the state. The registration is used for every purpose the old classifications served: school-funding weights, hiring and admissions preferences, anti-discrimination enforcement, public-health statistics, and the drawing of electoral districts. Individual declarations are sealed; only the aggregate distribution is published each year. Switching categories is costless, requires no documentation, and there is no requirement that a person's declaration be consistent from one year to the next. Every ten years a Classification Commission may add, merge, or remove categories by majority vote; its members are selected by lot from the previous year's declarations, in proportion to each category's share of the population, with no other qualification for service.

Walk through the next thirty years. Who switches category, when, and for what reasons? What happens to the statistical and social content of the categories as the state's uses respond to the shifting distribution? Does the system end up measuring the inherited categories, altering them, or dissolving them into something else? Where does it get gamed first, and how do the games interact with the commission's power to revise the list? Finally, say whether the failure modes are fixable by adjustments within the same basic rules, or whether they trace back to the fact that the categories are being made to do real administrative work.
