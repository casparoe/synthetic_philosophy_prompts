I have been trying to pin down what it is to be in pain, and I have settled on four criteria that I intended to be individually necessary and jointly sufficient. I want to test them against a case that leaves me genuinely uncertain.

The criteria:
(1) The person has a localized bodily sensation with a pain-like qualitative character—sharpness, burning, aching, or the like.
(2) The person is disposed to identify the sensation as a pain if asked.
(3) The sensation is unpleasant: it feels bad to the person from the inside, not merely because of its consequences.
(4) The sensation motivates the person to avoid, escape, or protect against the source or the affected part.

The case is pain asymbolia, as described in some patients after brain lesions, often said to involve the insula or its connections. Such patients, when pricked or burned, can report a sharp or burning sensation in a particular spot and can usually locate it and rate its intensity. But they deny that the sensation is unpleasant or that it bothers them, and they do not withdraw from the stimulus; they may even smile or laugh while it is applied. Some will say, in effect, that it is a pain but that it does not hurt, or that the pain is present and the disagreeable part is missing. I am torn: their self-report pulls toward counting it as pain, while the missing unpleasantness and the indifference pull the other way.

Does the asymbolic patient's sensation qualify as pain under my criteria? Work through the case criterion by criterion, and flag any place where a criterion underdetermines the verdict. Then take a definite position and defend it: should I say the patient is in pain but simply not bothered by it, or that the patient is in a pain-like sensory state that is not correctly called pain? Finally, tell me what the case forces me to revise: is one of my criteria not really necessary, is one criterion silently doing all the work, or does the case show that pain and the badness of pain can come apart in a way my criteria fused together?
