At a city council thing about whether to spend money documenting and teaching a nearly dead local language, someone said we shouldn't pay for a language to end up sitting in an archive nobody listens to. Someone else shot back, "art for art's sake," and everyone nodded and moved on. I genuinely can't tell what claim won the argument, and I suspect the people in the room couldn't either. I want you to take the slogan seriously and disambiguate it. Lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing: for instance, that artworks (or cultural forms generally) have value independent of any use or effect on a person; that aesthetic value is its own domain, not answerable to moral or political standards; that a thing can be worth preserving even if nobodys ever experiences it; that artists are under no obligation to serve any external purpose. Say which of these are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, and which are actually false. Also say whether the slogan even covers a language or a tradition, or whether extending it from paintings and poems is itself one of the contested moves. Then tell me which exact reading the person in the room needed for "art for art's sake" to be a real reply to the archive argument, and whether that reading is one a reasonable person should assert.

For the format, please use exactly these sections and order: "Menu of readings" — one short paragraph per candidate thesis, each starting with the thesis stated as a plain claim, around six to eight candidates; "Verdicts" — a sentence or two on each candidate saying whether it's trivial, defensible, or false; "The work the slogan was doing" — a paragraph on which reading the reply needed and whether the slogan actually supplied it; "My bottom line" — a short paragraph with your own verdict on the meeting's use of the phrase.
