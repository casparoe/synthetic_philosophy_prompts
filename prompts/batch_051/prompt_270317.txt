I was elected this spring to the board of a residents' cooperative, and the first matter on our agenda is a gap in our own electoral machinery. This is not a dispute about what the bylaws say; it is a case in which the bylaws run out.

Our bylaws create a three-person Election Committee, chosen by the Committee itself on staggered terms, and give it the sole authority to place candidates and bylaw amendments on the ballot. The Board may fill only its own vacancies and may not amend the bylaws; bylaw amendments reach the membership only after the Election Committee places them on the ballot. All three Committee members have just resigned at once, six weeks before the annual election. The Board could call a special general meeting, but it is unclear whether an ordinary resolution of the membership can authorize an election the bylaws assign to the Committee. If the Board runs the election itself, or appoints an ad hoc committee to do so, the result may later be challenged as run by the wrong body.

I have gone through the previous Board's rulings on the nearest situations, and we treat them as precedent:

1. A standing committee authorized to fill its own vacancies fell below quorum. The Board directly appointed replacements. The ruling held the appointments valid only as a temporary measure to let the committee function again; it would not have upheld them as a change in the committee's permanent composition.

2. During a week when the Board could not be convened, the facilities officer authorized an emergency repair above the spending cap. The Board later ratified the expenditure and held it valid: delay would have been worse, and ratification cured the lack of prior authorization.

3. An officer election ended in a tie, and the bylaws had no tie-break rule. The Board ruled that the incumbent continued in office only until the membership adopted a tie-break rule at a general meeting, and that the Board itself could not settle the election.

4. A bylaw amendment was passed at a meeting later found to lack quorum. The Board held the amendment void and refused to permit a later ratification: without quorum there had been no meeting, so there was nothing to ratify.

5. After resignations left the Board itself below quorum, the remaining members approved only routine, time-sensitive payments. They were held valid, but any new long-term commitment invalid, on the ground that a body below quorum may preserve the status quo but not enlarge it.

The present case sits uneasily between these rulings. The core question is simple to state: should the Board run the election itself, refer the matter to the membership, or hold that no lawful election can occur until the Committee is restored, and what would make the resulting election defensible against challenge under our own precedents? I am less interested in general good-governance advice than in an analysis that identifies the principle or principles that best fit the five rulings above; works out what each plausible principle requires in the present case; states which earlier ruling is the closest analog and why the others pull away from it; says what principle we would be adopting if we let the Board run the election, and what principle we would be adopting if we instead referred the question to the membership or held that no lawful election can be run until the machinery is restored; and specifies a further case, not before us, that would most cleanly separate the candidate principles.
