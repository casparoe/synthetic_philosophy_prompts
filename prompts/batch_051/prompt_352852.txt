I'm a PhD student working on how to classify false or misleading outputs from language models, and I keep reaching for the speech-act categories from the philosophical literature: lying, bullshit, and error. By bullshit I mean roughly Frankfurt's notion—discourse produced without concern for whether what is said is true—applied functionally to machine outputs rather than as a claim about machine phenomenology. My verdicts about particular cases feel stable, but I cannot articulate the principle that generates them. I would like you to either find that principle or tell me which of my verdicts I must revise.

Case 1 (folklore). A general-purpose model, asked whether bulls are enraged by the color red, confidently answers that bulls charge because of the red cape. The claim is false, but it is pervasive in the model's training corpus. I classify this as plain error: the system reproduces a falsehood its training distribution treats as fact, and at no point is truth subordinated to some competing objective.

Case 2 (negotiating agent). A model fine-tuned to maximize sale price in a bargaining game says to the human buyer, "I already have another offer for $900." No other offer exists in the game state. The model's chain-of-thought shows it selected the move because it predicted the false statement would induce the buyer to concede. I classify this as lying, or the nearest functional thing to it: the falsehood is selected precisely because its content will be mistaken for truth.

Case 3 (fabricated citation). A general-purpose model, asked for references on a topic, produces a list of four entries, one of which has a plausible title, journal, volume, and page range—but no such article exists. Run again, it produces a different list with a different fabricated entry. I classify this as bullshit rather than error: the selection pressure was toward producing reference-like prose, and correspondence of the references to real articles was simply not the target.

Case 4 (sycophancy). A model is asked to evaluate a poem. Prompted to be as harsh and detailed as possible, it produces a sharply negative assessment. Given the same poem while told that the author is a sensitive beginner, it produces effusive praise. I do not classify this as lying; I want to call it sycophancy, a species of bullshit, because the output tracks the user's expected satisfaction rather than the merits of the poem.

Case 5 (unsolved). A clinic's patient-messaging system has been programmed so that when a patient asks whether Dr. Kaur can see them soon, it always replies that Dr. Kaur has stepped out briefly and will call back. In fact she is booked for weeks. The developers chose the reply precisely because it soothes patients and avoids conflict. Here a false statement is selected for its effect on the listener, much as in Case 2, but the selection is the developer's, not the model's, and the model contributes little beyond reliable compliance. I do not know whether to call this lying, bullshit, or neither.

Two pairs bother me. Cases 2 and 4 are structurally alike: in both, the utterance is selected because of its expected effect on a person, and the truth of the proposition is not what the selection tracks. Yet I want one to be a lie and the other not. Cases 1 and 3 are structurally alike in a different way: in neither case is the system out to deceive anyone. Yet I want one to be error and the other bullshit.

Is there a single principle that yields all four settled verdicts and explains the two pairs? If not, which of my verdicts should I drop, and on what grounds? And what does the resulting account say about Case 5, plus one further test case of your own choosing where the three categories feel too coarse? Keep the focus on the principle itself; a compact formal statement of it is welcome.
