Two people in my evidence-law course got into it after class about naked statistical evidence, and I honestly couldn't tell who was right. Its a weird corner of the law I'd never thought about, and their both sharp enough that I got lost in the back-and-forth. Dan said: suppose a cyclist is hit by a bus at dusk, and the only thing we know is that 80 percent of the buses in that town are run by Blue Transit and 20 percent by Red Transit. If the civil standard really is just more likely than not, then the cyclist should win against Blue Transit: given the evidence, the probability that a Blue Transit bus hit him is 0.8, and 0.8 is comfortably past 0.5. Maria replied that courts don't actually let a bare base rate do that work. The plaintiff has to tie the defendant to the accident, not just to the class of buses. The statistic tells you about Blue Transit's overall share of traffic, not about this bus; that's why people call it naked. Dan came back that all evidence ends up statistical anyway. A DNA random-match probability is just a frequency in a population, and a witness saying it looked like a Blue Transit bus is also probabilistic because witnesses are fallible; if a witness were known to be 80 percent reliable under those lighting conditions, no one would call that naked. So why is the base rate different? Maria said because the witness's reliability bears on whether this bus was blue, whereas the base rate exists independently of the accident. And she brought up the gatecrasher problem: if a rodeo has 1,000 people in the stands but only 499 paid, then each attendee is 50.1 percent likely to have gatecrashed. If you allowed bare statistics, the operator could sue every single attendee and win every case, even though you know from the outset that 499 of them paid. That can't be what preponderance means. Dan said that's not a reductio: it just shows the standard tolerates false positives. If you try 100 people and each is 51 percent likely to have done it, you expect about 49 mistakes. The rodeo is the same, just aggregated. It feels weird, but no individual verdict is wrong, so the objection is aesthetic rather than logical. Maria then asked: which statistic? You said 80 percent of buses in town are Blue Transit, but I could say 30 percent of buses that pass that exact curve at dusk on a Tuesday are Blue Transit, or 90 percent of buses with recently repainted panels are Red Transit. You can shop for reference classes until you get whatever number you want; without an event-specific fact selecting the class, the statistic is unanchored. At least a witness singles out this bus. Dan said that's an argument for choosing reference classes more carefully, not for banning statistical evidence. And the alternative is worse: you reward parties who can produce a plausible-sounding witness and punish those who just have a frequency, even when the frequency is more accurate. Individualized evidence sounds virtuous but its often just more vivid noise.

I couldn't referee them, beyond sensing they might be talking past each other.
