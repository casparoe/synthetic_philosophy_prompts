I have a position on the ethics of blackmail that I have been developing for a law-and-philosophy workshop, and I would like a consistency check before I present it. The following is the package I think I accept, stated as plainly as I can:

(1) It is generally permissible to reveal a true, intimate fact about a person to someone with a serious interest in it, even against that person's wishes, when withholding it would leave the interested party importantly misled — for example, telling a friend that their spouse is having an affair.

(2) Absent any duty to disclose the information or any duty to keep it confidential, it is permissible to offer to remain silent about an embarrassing truth in exchange for payment.

(3) Blackmail — threatening to disclose such information unless the target pays — is seriously wrong, and its prohibition is justified.

(4) If two actions are each permissible on their own, then a plan that offers to perform one of them for payment and the other in the event of refusal is also permissible, unless the plan contains some wrong-making feature beyond the two component actions themselves.

(5) I also hold ordinary privacy convictions: revealing intimate personal facts against someone's wishes is normally objectionable unless it serves a compelling interest of the person to whom the facts are revealed.

(6) An act is wrong only in virtue of the harms or risks it imposes on others; there is no further ground of wrongness in the bare fact that something is treated as a commodity that should not be, or in a bad motive that has no further effect on anyone.

Please work through this set and distinguish the genuine tensions from the merely apparent ones. Rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say what the cheapest modification would be and what it would cost. End by identifying the single change to the package that would do the most to restore coherence, and what that change would sacrifice.
