Two engineers on my team got into an argument about a specific part of Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," the section on learning machines. I have read the paper, but I don't have formal training in philosophy, so I could not referee it. The paragraph in question is the one where Turing says that instead of trying to program an adult mind directly, we should build a child machine with a large capacity to learn and then educate it. He compares the process to teaching a child, says the machine would need sense organs, and suggests that its early instruction could use rewards and punishments rather than only explicit symbolic rules.

Dan took Turing to be making a blank-slate argument. He said the whole point of the passage is that you get intelligent behavior through learning from experience, not by putting knowledge into the machine at the start. For our current project, he argued, this means we should stop adding domain-specific hand-designed rules and instead keep the model as unconstrained as possible and let the training data do the work. If a capability can be learned, building it in is a shortcut that masks the real phenomenon.

Priya said Dan was overreading. She said Turing does not say the initial machine has no structure; he tells you to design a child brain with enough organization that it can be trained at all, and he treats the brain as the product of evolution and inheritance. She said Turing explicitly allows a built-in initial state and built-in learning mechanisms, and what he rejects is trying to produce adult-level competence in one step. So the passage is an engineering point, not a rule against innate structure. She also said Dan's reading sits poorly with Turing's later remark that rewards and punishments alone transmit very little information, which implies that the machine must begin with the right structure to take advantage of instruction.

Dan replied that Priya was getting the engineering point backwards: if Turing thought the initial structure mattered for the resulting competence, the child-machine strategy would not be an alternative to programming the adult mind; it would just move the programming problem to the child. He said Turing's emphasis on training, reward, and punishment only makes sense if learning, not built-in structure, is doing the explanatory work.

They asked me to settle whether Turing's argument supports a general presumption against built-in competence, or whether it leaves open that the right design includes substantial innate structure. Can you go through their exchange and say who is right about which specific point, and what both of them might be missing?
