In my graduate seminar on distributive justice, I keep returning to the fact that disputes about meritocracy and desert depend on causal attributions that no one can actually observe: how much of an outcome is a person's own doing, and how much is native endowment, social position, or brute luck. Let me pose the counterfactual I set my students, and ask you to reason through it further than a seminar discussion allows.

Suppose that in 2035, across the wealthy democracies, a cheap, non-invasive instrument—call it the responsibility spectrometer—comes into general use. For any adult and any specified outcome (earnings, educational credentials, health, even the success of a marriage), it produces a well-validated causal decomposition into four components: fixed native endowment, unchosen social circumstances, brute luck, and a residual "responsibility share" attributable to the person's own prior choices and sustained effort. Independent replication confirms the instrument's reliability, and firms, courts, and welfare agencies begin to accept its outputs. The instrument does not settle whether the responsibility share has whatever further properties moral desert requires; it only makes the causal facts public, precise, and cheap.

Predict, by tracing mechanisms rather than offering a narrative, how this changes labor markets, hiring and admissions, taxation and transfer policy, criminal and civil liability, parenting and education, and people's self-conceptions over the following two decades. Since no historical society has possessed such information, build the prediction from general facts about incentives, institutions, and human psychology, and for each major strand distinguish what follows from robust incentives or stable psychological tendencies from what is merely plausible. In particular, say whether you expect meritocratic justifications of inequality to be strengthened or eroded, and what would have to be true of human beings or their institutions for your central predictions to be wrong.
