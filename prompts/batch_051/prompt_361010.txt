I teach a seminar on the ethics of character, and this week we spent a whole session on Plutarch’s Life of Aemilius Paulus. The opening has a remark I can’t stop thinking about. Plutarch says, more or less, that he started the Lives for other people’s benefit, but the project gradually became something he did for his own sake too, because he used those lives as a mirror in which he tried to arrange and correct his own life toward the virtues of the men he described. My students wanted to know what that means in practice, and I realized the conceptual issue is really a ranking problem. Biographies can be said to work on us in many ways, but they can’t all be equally central to Plutarch’s own picture of moral learning.

Here are six candidate functions a life might serve for moral learning:

1. Supplying imitable patterns of virtuous action
2. Making abstract virtues intelligible by attaching them to concrete decisions and costs
3. Training emotional responses like admiration and emulation
4. Showing that character is a whole-life phenomenon, displayed across repeated choices rather than single acts
5. Revealing how fortune, circumstances, and small cumulative choices shape the virtues and vices we end up with
6. Functioning as an instrument of self-examination, more like a mirror for the reader than a model to copy

Could you rank these from most to least central to what Plutarch seems to be claiming in that passage, give each one a 0–10 score for fit, and then tell me which one you’d actually cut from a ten-week syllabus if you had to make room? I care about the reasons for the ranking more than the exact numbers. Please be explicit about what the mirror metaphor does and doesn’t commit Plutarch to, because I suspect half my students are importing modern ideas about autonomy and self-knowledge into a text where something closer to character-formation is at stake.
