For the past two years I have been running a reading group for philosophy PhD students, mostly in epistemology and social philosophy, who have no formal training in economics. Next month I am leading a two-hour session advertised as "What do markets know?" I want to build the entire session around the no-trade theorem—the result that, under conditions that include a common prior, Bayesian updating from private information, common knowledge of rationality, and risk aversion, private information alone cannot make it common knowledge that both sides of a proposed trade expect to gain from trading an asset. Most of the students have encountered the Hayekian idea that prices condense dispersed information, but none has worked with information partitions, common knowledge, or Bayesian updating. I want them to encounter the theorem not as a piece of technical economics but as a limit theorem in the epistemology of markets: a claim about what private information can and cannot do once everyone is reasoning about everyone else's reasoning.

Plan the session rather than explaining the theorem to me. Give me the order in which the component ideas must be introduced and why each depends on the previous one, and say which formal machinery I should introduce and which I should keep informal. Choose one concrete running example that should carry the whole session, and say what features it must have and what it must avoid. Then map the misconceptions the students are likely to form and where in the sequence each should be headed off; my own guesses are "a common prior means everyone already has the same beliefs at every moment," "the theorem is an empirical prediction that speculative trade never actually happens," and "if a market aggregates dispersed information, then informed traders should be able to profit from it," but I would like you to correct and extend that list. Finish with one end-of-session question or small exercise that would show me whether they have understood the result rather than learned to repeat it, and state explicitly which adjacent topics and which technical qualifications I should leave out at this level and which single assumption someone should be encouraged to reject if they want to resist the conclusion.
