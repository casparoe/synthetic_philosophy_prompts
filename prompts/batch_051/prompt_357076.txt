You are an expert philosopher of mind and language who works on memory, attention, and how ideas spread. Please think carefully, begin by brainstorming two or three candidate criteria, and then settle on a single definition.

I've been trying to get a precise grip on what I'll call an 'antimeme': an idea or piece of information that resists being remembered or passed on — not simply because nobody ever comes across it or because it strikes everyone as useless, but because something about the idea itself tends to undermine its own retention or transmission. The trouble is that this description sweeps in a lot of different-looking cases, and I want to know where the core of the concept really is.

Here are some cases to test your definition against. For each one, say whether your definition counts it as an antimeme, rules it out, or leaves it undecided, and explain briefly:

1. A random 16-digit number I generate once, never write down, and have forgotten by the next day.
2. The PIN to my bank card, which I remember perfectly but never share with anyone.
3. A true and even useful mathematical theorem that almost nobody can remember a week after understanding it.
4. A joke that is hilarious the first time but already stale the second, so almost no one retells it.
5. An instruction not to think about a white bear, which spreads very easily once heard but reliably makes people fail at exactly what it tells them to do.
6. A vivid dream that dissolves on waking and cannot be reconstructed no matter how hard I try.
7. A safety warning above a machine that workers have walked past so often they no longer notice it is there.

I want the definition to draw a principled line rather than merely sorting the cases, and I want you to be explicit about which feature is doing the work at each step — content, memory, attention, or social transmission. If the definition returns a surprising verdict on a case, defend that verdict rather than automatically revising the definition.
