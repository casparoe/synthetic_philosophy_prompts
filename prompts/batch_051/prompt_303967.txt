I'm helping a small nonprofit that designs and runs citizens' assemblies set its research agenda for the next two years. We've got money for three people doing mostly conceptual and normative work, no big empirical studies, and the question is what to point them at. We keep hitting the same Habermas-shaped arguments in our design meetings: whether the ideal speech situation is a regulative ideal you asymptotically approach or a benchmark you use to measure gaps; whether aiming at consensus makes sense when participants won't reach agreement by the end and much of what actually moves people isn't an argument; and whether neutralizing power in the room means only removing threats, money, and agenda control, or also doing something about unequal status, confidence, and skill at speaking. I'd like a ranked list of the open conceptual questions in Habermas's communicative rationality and the ideal speech situation that would most improve how we design and evaluate real assemblies. For each, tell me why it's upstream of the others, roughly how many other design choices hang on it, how tractable it is for a philosopher in a year or two without empirical work, and what argument or real-world development over the next couple years would push it up or down the list. I'm less interested in the list itself than in the reasoning for the order, and don't get stuck on what Habermas really meant for its own sake; I only care about interpretive disputes when they change a design or evaluation choice. Since money is tight, I care more about the defended ranking then the completeness: give me five or six sharp entries instead of a broad survey. And don't waste space on reading lists or citations.
