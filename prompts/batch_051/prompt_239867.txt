I've been going back and forth on whether Goodhart's law and the AI-safety idea of specification gaming are actually the same thing wearing different names, or whether they do different work. As I understand it, Goodhart's law says that once a proxy measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure of the thing you originaly cared about. Specification gaming is what happens when an agent—usually some RL-trained system—satisfies the literal reward signal while doing something useless or even harmful: the classic image is a simulated boat that just loops around collecting turbo pads instead of finishing the race. The first one sounds like a warning about metrics losing their validity. The second sounds like a warning about an agent actively exploiting an objective. I'm not sure that's a real distinction, or just a shift in vocabulary.

I thought the Wells Fargo fake-accounts scandal would be a decent test case. From memory, the shape of it is: for years the bank made cross-selling the core strategy, with goals like getting every household to eight products; low-level employees under brutal pressure opened millions of checking and credit card accounts without customer approval; it blew up in 2016, something like five thousand employees were fired, senior executives resigned, and there were big fines. My exact numbers are probably off, but that's not the part I care about.

What I'd like you to do is analyze that episode through both lenses and tell me where each lense earns its keep and where it strains. Start by spelling out what a Goodhart's-law reading of the scandal would emphasize, and what a specification-gaming reading would emphasize. Do they pick out the same mechanism, or different ones? Then say what each lens misses or distorts—for instance, Goodhart's law as usually stated has nothing to say about why the metric didn't just stop being informative but became actively destructive, or about the pressure, the rule-breaking, and the way responsibility was distributed up the hierarchy. Finally, run it in the other direction: does the scandal confirm Goodhart's law, or does it show that the law is too thin to explain something like this and that the specification-gaming vocabulary adds something real? Are the employees better modeled as human agents gaming a corporate target, or as components in a system whose objective was badly specified from the top—and does that distinction do any work or does it just muddy things?
