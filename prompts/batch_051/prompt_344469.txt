I spent my working life in the natural sciences, and the part of philosophy I have found easiest to enter is the moment when an old doctrine is restated inside a formal framework and the framework exposes exactly where the doctrine is committed. Lately I have been reading the classical doctrine of divine simplicity, and I would like you to think through whether one of its central arguments survives a development the doctrine's original defenders did not have. Please do not answer with a survey of what various authors have said; I want the argument itself worked through.

The doctrine, as I understand it, is that God has no parts of any kind: no physical components, no composition of matter and form, no distinction between essence and existence, and no distinction between God and God's attributes or between God and God's acts. The argument for it runs roughly as follows. A thing composed of parts requires some explanation of how its parts are combined, so a composite cannot be the uncaused cause of everything; therefore the first cause must be entirely simple. But the cost is immediate. If God is identical with each of his attributes and with his willing, then God's willing to create is not an episode added to God; it just is God. And God is standardly taken to exist necessarily, not as one being among others but in every possible world.

The development that interests me is the precise semantics for necessity worked out in the last century: a proposition is necessary just in case it is true in every possible world. When divine simplicity is restated inside that semantics, an old objection takes a much sharper form. Since God's act of willing is identical with God, and God exists in every possible world, God's act of willing exists in every possible world and cannot vary between worlds. If the content of that act includes the existence of this created world, then this world seems to be present in every possible world as well. Contingency disappears, creation becomes necessary, and God could not have done otherwise. I understand this is often called the modal collapse.

Does the argument for divine simplicity survive this tightening, or does the tightening expose a flaw that the older, looser language was able to hide? In particular, which premise is actually under pressure once the doctrine is expressed in possible-worlds terms, and is there a repaired version that preserves what the doctrine was protecting, namely a first cause with no composition, without buying the collapse? For example, can one distinguish God's act from the object or content of that act without reintroducing a distinction inside God, or is the right lesson that possible-worlds semantics is simply the wrong instrument for a being with no distinct states? And if one weakens full simplicity to something less than identity between God, his attributes, and his acts, which part of the original doctrine was doing the real work? Please answer in continuous prose, as you would write a letter or an email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
