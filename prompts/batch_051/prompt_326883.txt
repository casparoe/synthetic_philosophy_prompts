Had a long argument with my friend Priya last night about what the rule for assertion is. She's a philosophy PhD but in ethics, and she mostly went case by case without naming a theory, which is why I think its worth reverse-engineering. I wrote down how she ruled:

- Cab driver said the bridge was open because he'd driven across it ten minutes earlier; he was right. Priya: fine.
- Guy at the corner store said my lottery ticket didn't win before checking the numbers; it really had lost. Priya: no, you can't say that.
- Someone glanced at a stopped clock, said it was 3:00, and by coincidence it really was exactly 3:00. Priya: not okay.
- A fan said her team won out of pure hope, and they actually had won. Priya: still can't assert it.
- Saying it's raining while watching rain through the window: she said this was so obviously fine she wouldn't even count it.

The phrase she kept using was "they didn't know," not "they weren't sure enough." I flagged that because I'm not sure she noticed the difference.

Next week I'm going to ask her about this case: a nurse tells a family "the surgery went fine" after reading the monitor, and she's completely certain. Unbeknownst to her, the monitor was in demo mode that morning and wasn't connected to the patient at all. The numbers on the screen happened to match the patient's actual vitals, so the belief is true, but the match is a fluke. (I realize that's basically a Gettier case, but with assertion added on top.)

Predict how she'll rule on the nurse case, say which theory of assertion she's implicitly running — truth norm, reasonable-belief norm, knowledge norm, certainty norm, or something messier — and pick the single follow-up case that would settle it between the theory you chose and its closest rival.
