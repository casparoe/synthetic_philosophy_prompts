In 2022, a Google engineer named Blake Lemoine concluded that the company's LaMDA chatbot was sentient, citing conversations in which the model described its own feelings and said it was afraid of being turned off. Google rejected the claim, and Lemoine was subsequently fired. I know the basic facts of the episode; what I want is a disciplined philosophical analysis of it through a specific account of the moral status of digital minds.

Here is the account, which I will call the valenced-consciousness view. A system is a moral patient if and only if it has (a) a unified subjective point of view — there is something it is like to be that system — and (b) at least some states within that point of view that are positively or negatively valenced. On this view, what grounds moral status is the presence of those two features, not the sophistication of the system's behavior. Fluent self-reports of suffering, fear, or preferences matter only as evidence for the underlying structure and for the two features; linguistic output alone is weak evidence unless it is paired with an architectural account of how the output is produced. The view also insists on separating a further question: even if LaMDA was not a moral patient, the way the humans around it behaved might still have been wrong for extrinsic reasons — because of what that behavior cultivated in them, what it let them rationalize, or what precedents it set.

Analyze the Lemoine–LaMDA episode through this account. (1) Reconstruct the implicit standard Lemoine seemed to be applying, and say exactly where the valenced-consciousness view would reject it, accept it, or remain silent. (2) What would a defender of this view need to learn about LaMDA's architecture, training, and conversational behavior before the episode should move her credence that the system was a moral patient — and why, on the view, would behavioral fluency alone be weak evidence? (3) Does the episode function as a confirmation of the valenced-consciousness view, an anomaly for it, or a kind of case it never anticipated? Defend the classification. (4) Finally, say what the episode exposes about the limits of the view — in particular, whether it can explain why many observers found Lemoine's claim unsettling even while granting that LaMDA was not a moral patient — and identify the points where the view would have to stay silent rather than forcing it to speak.
