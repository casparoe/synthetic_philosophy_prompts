I cover science and ideas as a journalist, and I am preparing a recorded long-form interview with a political philosopher who defends a demanding version of the expressive theory of voting. On their view, an individual vote in a large election has essentially no chance of deciding the outcome, so the moral assessment of voting must run through what a vote expresses — endorsement, values, solidarity, self-presentation — rather than what it causally brings about. From this they conclude that voting for a third-party candidate in a close two-party race can be not merely permissible but sometimes more honest than lesser-evil voting, and that insisting on the lesser evil often reflects a flattering misreading of how little one's own ballot does.

I do not want the interview to stop at a restatement of the position. Please write a sequence of eight to ten interview questions, ordered so that each question tightens a constraint the previous answer appeared to leave slack. For each question, give (a) the answer the guest is most likely to give given the position stated, and (b) the follow-up you would put immediately after that answer. The sequence must include: the secret-ballot problem, pressed as a question about whether an act can genuinely express anything when there is by design no audience for the individual performance; whether the account licenses self-indulgent protest votes when the defeat of one candidate would foreseeably harm vulnerable people; and how the guest proposes to distinguish honest expression from moral self-licensing. Let the final question ask what concrete case or evidence would get them to abandon the view.
