Predict, as rigorously as the evidence allows, how John Muir would have responded to the constructivist critique of wilderness that arose in environmental history after his death: the argument that wilderness, understood as pristine nature untouched by human beings, is largely a cultural projection, one that imposes a falsified baseline on landscapes like Yosemite and erases the long-standing presence and land stewardship of Indigenous peoples. Muir is among the main sources of that projected ideal, yet he died before the critique was formulated. Begin from what is well documented about his outlook—his sense that wild nature is sacred, restorative, and morally commanding, and his defense of the Hetch Hetchy Valley against even practical proposals to dam it—and reconstruct the strongest Muirian reply to the constructivist position. Then mark the boundary between moves that are licensed by what he actually wrote and said and moves that require speculative extension from his principles. Finally, say whether the constructivist critique, if accepted, damages only Muir's rhetoric or also the substance of his preservationist case, and what your reconstruction implies about whether the value of wild places is best captured as intrinsic, relational, or instrumental.
