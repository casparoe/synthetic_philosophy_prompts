I've been chewing on something that keeps showing up in rat/forecasting-adjacent spaces and I can't remember if there's a standard name for the thing, or whether people shove a few different things under one label.

Here's the pattern. Take a fact that's already known with certainty—an anomaly in some data, a historical event, whatever. A theory comes along later that seems built specifically to explain that fact. In ordinary epistemic life we slam that: explaining the last five years of a stock in hindsight is curve-fitting, not evidence; tell me what happens next time, or your theory gets no credit. But the history of science is full of celebrated cases with the same shape. The stock example is Einstein explaining the anomalous precession of Mercury's orbit. The precession was measured and known to be weird before General Relativity existed. It was not a novel prediction; it was an old loose end. Yet it's routinely treated as a major confirmation of GR, not as post hoc curve-fitting.

So can an already-known fact genuinely confirm a theory that was built after the fact? If yes, what makes the Einstein case different from the stock-market hindsight case? Is there an established name for this question/dilemma, and if so, how far does that name actually reach? I want you to carve the boundaries: how much of what I've described is a problem about probability (the evidence already had credence 1 so conditioning on it can't move anything), how much is a problem about how much credit a theory deserves for retrodiction vs prediction, and how much is just masked by the word "overfitting." If the label only covers part of this, say which part and name the other parts if they have names.
