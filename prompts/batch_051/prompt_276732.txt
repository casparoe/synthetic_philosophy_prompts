I'm putting together a discussion for an IR theory reading group I help run, and I want a thought experiment that stops people treating the declaratory/constitutive distinction as if it were a two-position slugfest. Quick backdrop in case it matters: the usual factual criteria for statehood — permanent population, a defined territory, a government, and the capacity to enter into relations with other states — either settle the matter on their own (the declaratory line) or still leave something missing until other states recognize the entity (the constitutive line). I want a case where a third view gets daylight.

Here's the build-to-order part. Construct a peacetime case about a breakaway territory, call it Caldara, that meets all four factual criteria throughout and never fights a war, changes government, or alters its borders; the only thing that changes is what other states say about it. Design it so that (a) the declaratory theorist says Caldara is a state the whole time; (b) the constitutive theorist, reading recognition as a social practice of being treated as an equal member of the club, says Caldara is a state early on but by the end is no longer one — even though the total number of states that have said they recognize it is higher at the end; and (c) a third view I'm calling the speech-act view, on which different kinds of recognition utterance create different graded statuses rather than an all-or-nothing membership, says Caldara's status genuinely changed type — from full sovereign state to something lower like de facto authority — with no fact on the ground changing. So: no factual variation, one variable (the form, content, and pattern of recognition acts), three genuinely different verdicts.

After the scenario, walk through the reasoning for each position in turn and then tell me which single feature of the case makes the third view peel off — I'd guess it's something about the recognitions being hedged rather than full, but show me. Then run the collapse test: change only that feature (say, make every recognition full and unhedged) and say whether the case falls back into the usual declarative-vs-constitutive arguement and what each view says then. Finally, give the best attack a constitutive theorist could mount against the speech-act view's verdict — where exactly does the speech-act view have to gerrymander what counts as a recognition? If I've misread the declarative/constitutive debate, or these specs secretly can't all be satisfied at once, say that up front rather than forcing the case.

Format: start with a one-sentence Bottom line on what the case shows (under 40 words), then The scenario (150–250 words), then What each view says (80–120 words per view, in this order: declaratory, constitutive, speech-act), then The load-bearing feature (60–100 words), then Collapse test (60–100 words), then The best objection to the speech-act view (60–100 words).
