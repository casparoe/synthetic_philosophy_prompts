I'm in a political theory course and we're going through Hobbes's Leviathan, and one thing keeps bugging me. In the state of nature he says everyone has a right to everything — he even seems to extend it to each other's bodies. But if I have a right to an apple and you also have a right to that same apple, then the theory seems to be committed to two incompatible things being true at once. Isn't that already a contradiction sitting at the bottom of the whole state of nature? And if the pre-political condition is logically broken in that way, I dont see how a social contract is supposed to help — it seems like we'd be contracting away rights that couldn't coherently exist in the first place. I have to write a short paper on this and I want to make sure I'm not just attacking a strawman. Could you spell out how a Hobbesian should answer this apparent contradiction, and in particular whether the social contract depends on our natural rights already being consistent? Also, since I'll have to defend my reading in class: tell me where the Hobbesian response is weakest, or what consideration would lead you to change your mind about it. Please write in American English.
