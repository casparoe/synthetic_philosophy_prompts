I help run an epistemology reading group whose members are split on a question I want the students to sort themselves on, rather than just argue about in the abstract. The question is whether rationality — the kind of thing people try to improve by doing calibration drills, base-rate exercises, pre-mortems, and explicit expected-value estimates — can be trained the way a martial art is: through repeated practice of decontextualized forms ("katas"), in the hope that the trained movements later transfer to live "sparring" in messy, real-world decisions. The live disagreement in the room is not really about whether practice helps at all; it is about where any transfer comes from and how far it reaches, and about what exactly is being trained when someone gets better at a drill.

Please design a decision-tree handout for this group: a classification instrument that moves from a neutral opening question to a named position at each leaf. The tree should have roughly eight to twelve branch nodes. Each branch node should present a concrete case or a separable claim — for example, whether someone who becomes excellent at updating probabilities in textbook word problems also makes better hiring predictions, or whether someone who can name every bias in the abstract still falls for them under time pressure — so that respondents sort by what they think happens in cases rather than by which label they prefer. Build the tree from logical space rather than from a list of names: specify the two or three splitting axes that do the real work (I suggest something like near-versus-far transfer, and whether failures of rationality are mostly knowledge deficits or performance deficits, but you should improve on these if needed), show which combinations of answers are occupied by an actual position and which are empty, and briefly say whether each empty cell is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

The leaves should include at least these positions: the strong general-skill view (domain-general rationality is trainable, and far transfer is real); the weak-transfer view (training works, but only for near-transfer, drill-like tasks); the situated-cognition view (there is no context-free skill to transfer; apparent rationality is a bundle of domain-specific expert habits); the virtue/character view (rationality is best modeled as a cultivated trait or epistemic virtue rather than a transferable procedure); and the fixed-ability view (training mostly selects or measures ability rather than building it). Add further leaves if the logical space calls for them. For each leaf, give a one-sentence statement of that position’s most awkward commitment. End by flagging any pairs of leaves your tree cannot really separate with the questions it uses, and offer one further diagnostic question that might split them.
