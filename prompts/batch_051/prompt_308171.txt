A friend and I recently spent a long evening arguing about whether it is ever acceptable to create and use digital reconstructions of the dead—chatbots or voice models trained on a person’s text messages, letters, recordings, and other traces—and, if so, under what conditions. We did not converge. Since then I have written out my own verdicts on a set of concrete cases, and I would like to know what principle, if any, captures them, what that principle says about cases I have not considered, and which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon. I would rather end with a spare, honest principle than a patchwork that preserves every verdict.

Here are the cases, with my verdicts.

1. A widow’s husband dies suddenly. She has kept texting his old phone number, and a service uses the messages he sent her during their marriage to build a private text-based reconstruction of him. She now writes to it a few times a week about their children’s small milestones, in place of the number she had been texting. Verdict: permissible.

2. A terminally ill woman spends her final months recording hours of video in which she explains who she is, and arranges for these recordings to be made into a reconstruction left to her young daughter. Verdict: permissible, and no different in kind from leaving behind letters or a diary for a child.

3. A man builds a reconstruction of his late wife from their archived correspondence and repeatedly asks it to forgive him for an affair he never disclosed to her while she lived. Verdict: impermissible, even though it is private. The machine is being made to issue a pardon only she could have issued.

4. A father builds a reconstruction of his dead teenage son and begins adding screenshots of his exchanges with it to the family album, shown to visitors next to photographs of the living children. Verdict: impermissible, though I recognize that it is close to Case 1.

5. A company scrapes the public social media accounts of recently dead people and sends their families unsolicited remembrance bots, with a paid tier that unlocks more of the deceased’s messages. Verdict: impermissible.

6. A daughter builds a reconstruction of her late father from voicemails and emails so that her young children, who never met him, can ask it questions about his life and come to feel that they know him. Verdict: permissible.

7. A young man whose closest friend died by suicide builds a reconstruction of the friend and repeatedly asks it why, hoping to extract from his messages an answer the friend never left. Verdict: impermissible—not because the friend would have refused, but because the practice feeds rumination and pretends the model holds knowledge it cannot have.

8. A dying man explicitly tells his wife that he does not want any simulation made of him. After his death she builds one anyway, using their own correspondence, and speaks with it in private. Verdict: impermissible.

The pair that troubles me most is Cases 1 and 4. In both, a surviving partner or parent has fitted a private reconstruction into daily family life; my verdicts diverge, and I cannot yet state what separates them. I am also unsure whether Case 3 should be forbidden by something specific to forgiveness, or by a more general rule about what one may properly ask a reconstruction to do.

My friend’s proposal, which attracted me until it began to strain, was roughly this: a reconstruction is permissible when it is built from traces the dead person shared with the user, employed in private mourning, and not offered to others as a substitute for the living person; it is impermissible when commercial, non-consensual, or exhibited socially as if the person still lived. That rule handles Cases 2, 5, 6, and 8 reasonably well, and it might separate Case 1 from Case 4 by means of the “exhibited socially” clause. But I am not sure why that clause should bear as much weight as it is being asked to bear, and the rule seems to treat consent, purpose, and publicity as three separate tests in a way that may not be principled. It also says little about what one may ask a reconstruction to do once one has built it.

Please give me the strongest principle, or small set of candidate principles, that best fits my verdicts. For each principle, state its clauses precisely, identify the case that motivates each clause, apply it to all eight cases, and say clearly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why. Then apply the principle or principles to a few further cases of your own construction, including at least one in which the reconstruction is used by an institution—a school, a museum, a church, or a memorial site—rather than by an individual mourner. Finally, tell me whether the distinction I drew between Cases 1 and 4 is one a coherent principle can draw, or whether I should give up one of those two verdicts.
