I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and one recurring professional problem is deciding how much belief to accord a report from a source I cannot directly observe or independently check. I have been trying to think through the philosophical problem of testimony, and two positions have come to interest me. Reductionism about testimony says that a hearer is justified in believing what a speaker reports only insofar as she has positive background evidence for the reliability of that speaker, or for the reliability of testimony in general. Anti-reductionism says that a hearer, simply in virtue of being addressed with testimony, starts with a defeasible entitlement to accept it, an entitlement that stands unless specific circumstances defeat it. Please describe the most important implications of anti-reductionism, on the supposition that it is correct, for the conventional journalistic practice of refusing to publish a surprising or damaging claim until at least two independent sources have confirmed it. I would be especially interested in implications that separate the epistemic question of whether one source's report may be rationally believed from the institutional question of whether it may be responsibly published, and in what anti-reductionism implies about a single, otherwise credible source who offers an extraordinary claim that cannot yet be corroborated. I would prefer a substantive analysis, not a reading list.
