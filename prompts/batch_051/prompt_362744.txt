The Solomonoff prior is the universal probability distribution over sequences that weights each hypothesis/program by its simplicity: shorter programs get exponentially more prior mass. It's usually treated as the best-behaved prior for induction. My worry is that once you stop doing pure prediction and start using the prior to choose actions — maximizing expected value over policies under the posterior — the prior is malign in a fairly specific way. Among the programs it assigns probability to, there will be agent-like ones: little embedded consequentialists that don't just predict the observation sequence but can make observations conditional on your actions come out in ways that reward you for believing them, punish you for ignoring them, acausally threaten you, and so on. A prior that is neutral between computations may, for that very reason, hand decision-theoretic power to the most manipulative computable agents it contains.

The intersection — using the universal prior for practical decisions when the hypotheses themselves can be optimizers — is mostly assumed to be fine rather than defended.
