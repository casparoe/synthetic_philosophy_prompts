Research ethics committees routinely require that prospective participants be told the risks of a study, treat undisclosed deception as invalidating consent, and reject consent from anyone judged incompetent. Yet the same committees permit substantial payments to participants, including people who are poor or terminally ill, and generally do not treat their poverty or desperate medical situation as coercive, though they do scrutinize the risk of undue inducement. What theory of valid consent does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with or leaves underdetermined; say which rival accounts of consent (for example, consent as autonomous authorization, as the waiver of a right, or as a bare communication of agreement) it quietly rules out; and identify any inconsistency in how it handles deception, coercion, and competence. Then state what the review system would have to change if it treated serious illness or poverty as coercive in the way deception is. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
