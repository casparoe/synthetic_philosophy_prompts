Hey — I'm an ethics PhD student and I keep coming back to an argument a friend runs against consequentialism. It sounds airtight when he lays it out, but the conclusion is so strong I suspect it's proving too much, and I can't find the exit. It goes like this:

(1) Consequentialism says an act is right just in case it produces the best outcome available (ties aside).
(2) So if honesty, promise-keeping, gratitude, and the virtues matter at all, they matter only because and to the extent that they tend to produce the best outcomes.
(3) If something matters only because and to the extent that it tends to produce the best outcomes, then it's a mere heuristic: on any particular occasion where following it wouldn't produce the best outcome, there's no real reason to follow it.
(4) Mere heuristics can't ground robust requirements; they're just rules of thumb for when you can't do the full calculation.
(5) But honesty, promise-keeping, and the virtues are, in ordinary moral thought anyway, robust requirements — e.g., there's some reason not to lie or break a promise even when it would produce slightly more good in the particlar case.
(6) So consequentialism can't make room for honesty, promise-keeping, or the virtues as robust requirements; it demotes them all to rules of thumb, with no independent normative force.
(7) That can't be right. A view that says breaking your promise is fine the moment it produces marginally better consequences has, I want to say, stopped talking about promise-keeping at all. So either consequentialism is false, or ordinary thought is massively confused about promises, honesty, and virtue.

I'd rather keep (5) than accept (7), but I have a nagging feeling that something between (2) and (4) moves faster than it should. Every time I try to pin it down, though, it just sounds like I'm insisting that a heuristic can be more than a heuristic. Which step do I actually give up, and what does it cost?
