Our company has an old calibration standard, rule 4.2, that reads: "A forecaster's current subjective probability for a proposition shall equal the expected value of the forecaster's own subjective probability for that proposition at any specified future time." It was written years ago, when the only forecasters were human analysts estimating things like churn or hardware failure.

I now maintain a system in which a large language model produces the probability judgments. After each round of judgments, the model is fine-tuned on a batch of user feedback that may be unrepresentative or even adversarially selected. I can evaluate the model at time t0 and get P(A) = 0.7. I can also prompt the model to simulate its own future judgment after the next fine-tuning round, and I find that its expected future P(A) is 0.9. The rule’s plain text seems to say the current value should have been 0.9, but the future value is partly the product of training that hasn’t happened yet and that I control.

Does rule 4.2 apply to this case? If it applies, does it apply to the model, to the training process, to me, or to the overall forecasting system? Walk through the plain text, the purpose of the rule, and what its authors would have said if asked about systems that can predict their own fine-tuning. Say which approach should win and why. Then tell me what this implies about whether van Fraassen’s reflection principle is a genuine constraint on rational agents or only a consistency condition on a single agent that isn’t being deliberately changed. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this.
