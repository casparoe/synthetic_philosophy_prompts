I have been trying to get a clearer grip on the difference between money, liquidity, and real wealth by working through a thought experiment, and I would welcome a careful, step-by-step treatment of it rather than a one-line conclusion.

Suppose a small open economy of about ten million people, otherwise similar to a developed country today, amends its constitutional law so that every month the central bank creates new base money equal to one per cent of the current stock of broad money and credits it in equal per-capita amounts directly to every adult's transaction account. The new balances are never sterilised, and no fiscal or central-bank operation is permitted to mop them up at the aggregate level afterwards; commercial banks, however, may still create credit, set interest rates, and hold reserves exactly as before. The country's trading partners continue to accept its currency, and nothing else changes at the outset.

I would like your reasoned prediction of where this economy stands after thirty years, with the causal chain made explicit rather than a simple judgement that prices will rise. Walk me through the early stages when the transfer is unexpected, then the transition to a regime in which the monthly injection is fully anticipated: spending, asset prices, wages, debts, the exchange rate, and the distribution of gains and losses. In particular, try to separate what the money creation actually creates in the way of real resources from what it merely re-denominates or redistributes, and identify the points at which the story depends on assumptions about expectations, price and wage stickiness, financial structure, or political response. Which predictions are you most confident in, and what would have to hold for the outcome to be a durable increase in real prosperity rather than a durable inflation?
