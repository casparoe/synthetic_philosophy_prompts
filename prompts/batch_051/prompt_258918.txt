For a seminar I teach on science, determinism, and the sources of moral seriousness, I have been re-reading Albert Einstein's essay "Religion and Science," first published in the New York Times Magazine on November 9, 1930. Einstein there distinguishes the religion of fear from moral religion and then introduces what he calls cosmic religious feeling. The passage I keep coming back to is this:

"The individual feels the futility of human desires and aims and the sublimity and marvelous order which reveal themselves both in nature and in the world of thought. Individual existence impresses him as a sort of prison and he wants to experience the universe as a single significant whole."

He claims that this feeling "knows no dogma and no God conceived in man's image," so that no church can center its teaching on it, that its representatives have often been taken for atheists, and that the most important function of art and science is to awaken it. In other writings he also denies human freedom in the philosophical sense, while writing with unmistakable moral seriousness about how we should live.

I would like you to write an essay on the following controversy. Does cosmic religious feeling, as Einstein characterizes it, actually have the resources to do the work of a religion—to orient a life and carry moral weight—once a personal God, dogma, and a church are gone? And does his determinism undermine that moral seriousness or, as he seemed to think, make it more tolerant and clear-eyed? One side regards the position as atheistic awe dressed up in religious language; the other regards it as the only intellectually honest form religion can still take after philosophical and scientific criticism. Weigh these positions and reach a conclusion, justifying it.
