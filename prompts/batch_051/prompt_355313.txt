I spend a significant amount of time in an online multiplayer game in which players trade, build, and sometimes scam or destroy one another's work. Over time I have adopted a rule for how I form beliefs about the people I meet there: I weigh what a person does inside the game exactly as heavily as I would weigh the same kind of act in ordinary life. If a player swindles a stranger out of an item that took the stranger a month to obtain, I conclude that the swindler is a thief, rather than that they merely acted wrongly within a game. If someone spends hours helping me build something, I treat that as evidence of their character just as strongly as if they had helped me move furniture. My older brother says this is a category mistake: the stolen item never existed as a real object, and the victim did not really lose anything, so my rule is bound to produce false beliefs about people. I would like to know whether my rule is defensible as an epistemic policy, or whether it systematically over-weights virtual evidence. Under what conditions, if any, should virtual behavior count as heavily as offline behavior in judgments about a person's character and about wrongdoing? Does it matter whether the virtual object was bought with real money, whether the victim's loss was chiefly time and skill, or whether the community around the game treats the act as a serious wrong? If my rule as stated is too strong, what would the defensible version of it be?
