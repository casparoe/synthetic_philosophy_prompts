I am a retired evolutionary biologist who has only lately begun reading philosophy of language, so I would welcome a rigorous audit of an analogy I keep meeting. Popular histories of the Romance languages often say that Latin did not die but turned into Italian, and into the other Romance languages, much as an ancestral species turns into descendant species; historical linguists sometimes reach for the same comparison to dissolve the question when Latin became Italian as no better posed than when one species became another. The intended conclusion is deflationary: there is no non-arbitrary date, only a gradual accumulation of changes, with any boundary drawn after the fact.

I want the analogy examined properly, not waved through. Lay out the mapping explicitly: what is supposed to correspond to organisms, populations, reproduction, inheritance, isolation, speciation, the fossil record, and extinction. Then identify where the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that threaten the intended conclusion from the ones that are merely superficial. Three points worry me in particular. First, languages do not reproduce and their transmission is not particulate, though it is not obvious whether this undermines the analogy or simply redefines it. Second, the analogy quietly imports a branching-only tree and natural boundaries between varieties, whereas borrowing and dialect continua look more like hybridisation and horizontal transfer in biology. Third, Classical Latin persisted for centuries as a deliberately maintained written standard alongside the spoken varieties that were becoming Italian, and I do not know what biological counterpart to adduce for that. Assess whether the deflationary conclusion survives once these disanalogies are on the table, or whether the question is being dissolved too cheaply. Finally, say what a better comparison would be if this one fails, and what the exercise shows about the identity conditions of languages in general.
