I have been reading about the disputed Florida vote count in the 2000 U.S. presidential election, and I keep coming back to a question the coverage never quite answers. On the ground, the problem looked very concrete: punch-card ballots with hanging or dimpled chads, machine counts that gave different totals when the same ballots were run through twice, county officials disagreeing about whether a ballot with an indentation but no detached paper counted, and a legal standard that floated back and forth between requiring a mechanically clean vote and asking what the voter had probably meant to do. What bothers me is that all the arguments about recount procedures seemed to presuppose that there was a real fact of the matter, ballot by ballot, about which ones were votes — suppose I am skeptical. I don't have any academic background in philosophy, but I have recently read a bit about two rival accounts of vagueness: epistemicism, which claims that vague words have sharp boundaries that we simply cannot know precisely, and supervaluationism, which claims that in borderline cases there is no single correct answer until we sharpen the term one way or another. I would like you to act as a philosopher of language and treat the Florida recount as a case study through that lens.
