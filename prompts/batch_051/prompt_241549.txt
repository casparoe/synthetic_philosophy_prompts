Here's a set of gut verdicts I've got about composition — about when a bunch of things together make up a further thing — and I'd like to know whether one principle sat behind all of them. The grains in a heap of sand: they compose a heap. The iron head and the wooden handle of a hammer, once they're fitted together properly: they compose a hammer. A trout in Montana and a turkey in Iowa: they don't compose anything at all. The thirteen hearts scattered through a shuffled deck: they don't compose anything either. The cells in a living cat: they compose a cat. Two strangers shaking hands at a conference: they don't compose a third object, not even for a moment. Tell me what rule, if any, gets all six of those verdicts, and be explicit about which verdict or verdicts you'd have to give up if you preferred a simpler rule. Then apply your rule to two further cases I keep going backwards and forwards on: two flagstones propped against each other to make a little arch, and the body of a cat that has just died.
