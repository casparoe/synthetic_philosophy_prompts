In philosophical debates about the role of religious arguments in democratic politics, a particular standoff has lasted since at least the 1990s and neither side seems to move. On one side, political liberals defend a constraint similar to John Rawls's proviso in *The Idea of Public Reason Revisited*: citizens may introduce their comprehensive doctrines, religious or otherwise, into public political discussion at any time, provided that in due course they present what Rawls calls properly political reasons supporting what they propose. On the other side, critics of public reason, some coming from religious ethics and some from free-exercise liberalism, insist that this gives religious citizens a second-class status. They ask why the shared or secular language of political reasons should have a veto over the reasons that actually move believers, and whether requiring believers to translate their convictions into a different idiom is itself an exclusionary act. Both sides accept that coercion must be justified, that democratic citizens owe one another some kind of justification, and that religious citizens are full members of the political community. Yet neither side has been much persuaded by the other in three decades.

I suspect the impasse is not really about whether religious reasons may be spoken, because the proviso already concedes that they may. It is about what happens when the translation requirement bites. Is this a factual disagreement about what reasons are actually shareable in pluralistic societies? A normative disagreement about what justification to a religious citizen requires? A verbal disagreement about what counts as a public reason? Or a deeper disagreement about the aim of political philosophy, one side modelling an ideal of legitimacy and the other describing the lived ethics of actual democratic argument? Diagnose what kind of disagreement this really is, and say what, if anything, could count as progress given that diagnosis. Please answer in British English.
