I run a small reading group that has spent way too long on whole-brain emulation, and we've split into two camps that keep talking past each other. The Pattern Camp says a mind is its information-processing structure: if you capture enough of that structure and run it, you get the same mind back, whether it runs on neurons, silicon, or a simulation, and regardless of whether the original brain was destroyed, kept alive, or copied twice. The Continuity Camp says pattern transfer is not enough; for it to be the same mind there has to be the right kind of causal and temporal continuity between the old mind and the new one, so a scan-and-instantiate at best makes a very convincing duplicate with your memories, and at worst destroys you and builds someone else. We've decided to do an adversarial collaboration, and I'm the one stuck drafting the document.

Write the joint statement we'd produce. It should start with a numbered list of claims both camps can sign — including what they agree on about feasibility and fidelity, since that turned out to be less contested then we thought. Then give the precise residual disagreement, worded so each camp would accept the phrasing of its own position, followed by a table of cruxes. For each crux, give a concrete case or argument both camps agree bears on it, and write down in advance what each side predicts about that case. The cases we kept returning to were gradual neuron-by-neuron replacement, a destructive scan whose emulation first runs a week later, a non-destructive scan where the original goes on living, and a single emulation forked into two copies — use those unless you have a better one. If you conclude part of the dispute is merely verbal, say which part and why. And please mark clearly what is actually settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this stuff; don't present either camp as the consensus. No citations or reading lists — we want it to stand on the arguments, not on authority.
