I am writing a seminar paper on the nature and value of privacy, and I have decided I will not adopt any of the standing theories until I have the specification of what a theory is supposed to do in front of me. The literature as I read it proceeds by counterexample: control-based views are refuted with cases in which a person suffers a privacy loss without ever having exercised, or had the chance to exercise, control over the relevant information; access-based views are refuted with cases in which someone has access to a person or her records and yet we hesitate to call it a privacy violation; contextual-integrity views get asked how we criticize a context's own norms from the outside if the wrongness of a data flow just is its departure from those norms. I find these moves illuminating but piecemeal, and I want the criteria made explicit before I take sides.

Assume the theory is meant to be good enough to guide judgment about cases where nothing was ever hidden in the traditional sense: a retailer's model infers that a shopper is pregnant from her purchases without any employee's having looked at her; a face-recognition system follows a person across public space; a researcher reuses geolocation logs for a purpose no one was told about; a neighborhood is profiled from aggregate data even though no individual is re-identified. Write the desiderata. I want the cases an adequate theory must classify correctly and the consideration behind each, the distinctions it cannot collapse (secrecy, anonymity, confidentiality, and solitude; a privacy loss versus a wrong; individual-level and group-level privacy), and the facts it must accommodate (for example, that privacy is context-relative, and that surveillance can wrong someone even when it produces no tangible harm and she never learns of it). For each requirement, mark it as non-negotiable or as the preference of a particular camp, and flag any pairs you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. Then tell me which single desideratum does the most work to eliminate the leading candidates — control, access, contextual-integrity, and intimacy accounts — and briefly why; I will do the full scorecard myself.
