I am a professor of philosophy, and for more than a decade most of my own work has been carried out through a research simulation platform I maintain with a small team. As a result, I have become genuinely fluent in the working vocabulary of software maintenance: commit messages and repository archaeology, comments that drift away from the code they describe, regression tests that pin behavior whose original purpose no one on the team can now state, refactoring, technical debt, legacy modules, and the standing distinction between what a system actually does and what its documentation says it is for.

There is a position in philosophy, sometimes discussed under the label free-floating rationales, that an artifact, practice, or organism can have a rationale even when no agent anywhere holds that rationale. The reason a feature exists, or a practice has the shape it has, is real, explanatory, and in principle discoverable, but it is not represented in the mind of any designer, user, or beneficiary: the rationale lives in the structure, the history, and the selective pressures that shaped it, not in anyone's explicit grasp of it. These are reasons without reasoners.

Restate that position as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I have just described — the vocabulary of a legacy codebase whose original authors have left. I want a systematic, element-by-element translation. Where does a rationale with no current reasoner map onto a commit message whose author and context are lost? Onto a test that enforces a behavior nobody can now justify? Onto a module whose design encodes constraints inherited from a dead platform, or onto the way one reconstructs, through repository archaeology, reasons that no living engineer actually entertained at the time? Be exact about what translates cleanly and what has to be bent to fit.

Then give me the residue report: the parts of the free-floating-rationale view that did not survive the translation, the places where you had to fudge, and the places where the codebase vocabulary itself resists the philosophical claim. Use the residue to adjudicate my working suspicion, which is this: the view is roughly equivalent to the observation that a codebase can outlive the reasons of its authors, so that reasons become opaque, distributed, and available only through archaeological reconstruction — and anything the position says beyond that is inflationary. Tell me whether that suspicion is right, or whether it quietly smuggles an original reasoner back into the picture and thereby misses something central to the view.
