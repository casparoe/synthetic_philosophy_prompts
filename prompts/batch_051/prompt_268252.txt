Stephen Wolfram's A New Kind of Science (2002) advances the Principle of Computational Equivalence. Roughly speaking, the principle holds that once a process passes a rather low threshold of sophistication, it can carry out computations just as sophisticated as those of any other process, including processes usually regarded as especially complex, such as the human brain or the universe as a whole. Wolfram takes this to have broad scientific consequences: it undermines, in his view, the assumption that complex natural behavior requires complex underlying rules, and it motivates studying simple programs such as certain cellular automata as models for physics and biology.

Below are five readings of what the principle might amount to as a scientific claim. Please rank them from most to least defensible as a contribution to natural science, and explain in one or two sentences for each why it occupies that position. In evaluating them, weigh empirical content, explanatory power, and susceptibility to the charge of being either trivially true or unfalsifiable.

1. A metaphysical reading: all processes that are not obviously simple are computationally equivalent in sophistication, and complex natural systems literally carry out universal computations.

2. A boundary reading: the space of possible computational behavior has a sharp threshold, with only a small set of systems lying below it, and essentially everything beyond that threshold sits at the same maximal level of computational sophistication.

3. An epistemic-limit reading: the principle is best understood as the claim that the behavior of computationally irreducible systems cannot be predicted by any computation substantially shorter than running the system itself, so no finite observer can systematically outrun such systems.

4. A methodological reading: scientists should treat simple programs such as cellular automata as a legitimate and often preferable basis for modeling natural systems, because conventional models that encode complex rules merely import the complexity they are meant to explain.

5. A deflationary reading: because universal computation is easy to implement, almost any physical substrate can be described as a universal computer, so the principle merely restates Turing's results and stretches the notion of computation too far to make contact with the world.

Present your answer as an ordered list, with a short justification for each ranking. Your answer should be written in British English.
