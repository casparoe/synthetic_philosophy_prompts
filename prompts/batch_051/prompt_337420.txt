I've been arguing with my advisor, who is a moral particularist: she thinks there are no true, exceptionless moral principles of the form lying is wrong or causing pain is wrong, and that whether a given feature counts for or against an act can shift from case to case with nothing general underneath. I'm more of a generalist—I assume their are at least a few principles worth keeping. Over the weekend I tried to steelman her, and I ended up with an argument that seems to show her view undermines itself. Now I'm not sure whether I've found a real problem or just pulled a fast one on myself.

(1) If particularism is true, then there are no true general moral principles like lying is wrong or causing pain is wrong.

(2) If there are no true general moral principles, then there is no general story about why a feature counts the way it does; the fact that an act is a lie can count against it here, count for it there, and nothing general explains the difference.

(3) If nothing general explains why a feature counts the way it does, then our verdicts in particular cases aren't answerable to reasons in a way that can be communicated or taught—they're answerable to our reactions to the specifics.

(4) If our verdicts aren't answerable to communicable, teachable reasons, then two people who disagree about a case need not be making a mistake; they just have different reactions, and talk of someone making a moral mistake in a particular case becomes an illusion.

(5) That can't be right. People really do make moral mistakes in particular cases, and moral disagreement is usually about something, not just a clash of tastes.

(6) Therefore particularism is false.

Each premise sounds right when I read it alone, but the conclusion says my advisor has to be wrong, and she isn't dense. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd trade almost any of them to keep (5), but I want to know what I'm actually paying before I walk into her office and announce that her view refutes itself.
