Me and a mate spent most of Saturday evening arguing about whether obesity should count as a disease. He kept saying we'd be medicalising normal variation, and I kept saying it's no different in principle from mild hypertension, which everyone calls a disease even when the person feels fine. By the end neither of us could give a rule that didn't have obvious counterexamples, and it's been nagging at me since. So I wrote down my own verdicts on a bunch of cases, and I want to know whether there's actually a coherent principal behind them or whether my instincts are just a muddle.

Here are the verdicts. Untreated essential hypertension in a forty-five-year-old with no symptoms: a disease, full stop, even though the harm is only probabilistic. Menopause: not a disease, even though it brings symptoms and raises some risks, because it's a normal life stage. Severe grief six months after a bereavement, where the person can't work: not a disease; a depressive episode with much the same presentation is, and the difference seems to be something about how it came on and its shape, though I can't say exactly what. Dental caries: definitely a disease, and the fact it's caused by bacteria and sugar doesn't matter—an external cause doesn't stop something being a disease. Infertility in someone who desperately wants children: a disease. The very same infertility in someone who has never wanted children: I still want to call it a disease, but it feels less like one, which bothers me because the patient's values seem to be doing some of the work. Short-sightedness: a disease, whilst it is hugely common and glasses correct it. Male-pattern baldness: not a disease, even when the man is genuinely pained by it—and I cannot explain why myopia is a disease and that isn't. A gene test showing someone will get dementia in their fifties, before any symptoms: already a disease now, not merely a future one. A person whose internal organs are all mirror-reversed but work perfectly: not a disease, despite being a departure from the species-typical arrangement. Chronic low back pain where every scan is normal: I'd call it a disease, or at least a disorder, even though I can't point to which part is broken. Pregnancy: obviously not a disease, despite nausea, pain and real risk, because the reproductive system is doing exactly what it's for.

My working theory was that a disease is a part or process failing to do what it's biologically meant to do, where the 'meant' comes from normal human biology. That handles hypertension, myopia, caries, the dementia gene and pregnancy neatly, but it gets wobbly with chronic pain, and it doesn't explain the baldness–myopia pair. There's also the historical point: homosexuality used to be classed as a disease and then was declassified, and that change looks like it was driven by shifting values rather than by a new discovery about biology, which makes me suspect values are in the mix somewhere. So what I'm after is a precise principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible. State it clearly enough that I can apply it to fresh cases: obesity, say, or age-related hearing loss, mild cognitive slowing in old age, or being very short. Tell me which of my verdicts it can't accommodate, and whether it forces me to drop one or two of them. And be honest: if the only way to keep every verdict is a baroque principle with a seperate clause for each case, I'd sooner have the simpler principle and find out which intuition I have to abandon—and I'd like to know which one it is.
