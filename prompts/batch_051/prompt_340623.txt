Here's an inconsistency I keep finding in my own political views, and I want someone to either dissolve it or tell me which way to jump.

Take a person who is, by ordinary standards, a consistent democrat: they think hereditary legislators are an embarrassment, a hereditary judge would be absurd, and a hereditary CEO or a hereditary seat on a hospital board would be an obvious bit of inherited privilege. But they're entirely fine with, or even fond of, the monarchy in the UK, Norway, Sweden and Japan — provided the monarch has no real power. The usual defence is that it doesn't matter because the role is ceremonial: heredity is bad when it allocates power, but harmless when it allocates ribbon-cutting. That has never quite worked on me. We don't normally think an anti-democratic principle becomes fine when you attach it to something decorative. Imagine a town whose mayor is always drawn from the same family, and the job is only opening fetes, visiting schools and standing in silence at memorials. I'm pretty sure the same democrat would not be fine with that; they'd ask why on earth the job shouldn't be elected. So what's doing the work in the royal case: the symbolism, the continuity, the non-political figurehead, the belief that an elected president would be worse, or just that its old and familiar and nobody wants to reopen it? Is the asymmetry defensible, or should a consistent democrat be a republican (in the abolish-the-monarchy sense) even in places that seem broadly happy with their royals? If you defend the asymmetry, defend it specifically against the hereditary fete-mayor and the hereditary hospital-board member, and say whether you'd keep it, reverse it, or give it up. Please write the answer in British English.
