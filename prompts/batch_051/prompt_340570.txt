Here is an argument I have been turning over, and I cannot decide whether it is a sound piece of reasoning or a conjuring trick. Please help me assess it.

(1) On the Everettian, or many-worlds, interpretation of quantum mechanics, whenever a quantum event has several possible outcomes, the universe branches and every outcome is realised in some branch; no branch is any more real than another.

(2) Suppose a person uses a quantum suicide device: a measurement whose outcome determines whether a lethal mechanism fires. On the Everettian interpretation, there is a branch in which the mechanism does not fire and the person survives, and a branch in which it fires and the person dies.

(3) A stream of experience can continue only in a branch in which the brain that supports it keeps functioning. In a branch where the brain is destroyed, there is no further experience to be had from that person's point of view.

(4) So, for any given run of the device, there is always at least one branch in which the person's stream of experience continues: the survival branch. The death branch contributes no further first-person experience.

(5) Therefore, from the person's own point of view, the only future experiences there can be are ones in which they have survived. The person should therefore expect to keep finding themselves alive after each run, however many times the device is used.

(6) Hence, under the many-worlds interpretation, a person who runs the device repeatedly should expect to be subjectively immortal.

I half-suspect that step (5) slides from a statement about there being a branch to a statement about which branch I shall find myself in, but I cannot articulate the mistake precisely enough to withstand my friend's replies. Please say whether the argument is valid. If it is not, identify the precise step at which it first fails, explain the nature of the failure in terms of what is being quantified over or equivocated on, and state what assumption about personal identity across branching would be needed to make the conclusion follow. If it is valid, state which premise a many-worlds theorist must accept and why. Please answer in British English.
