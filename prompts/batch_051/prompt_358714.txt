I'm an undergraduate taking an upper-level seminar in international relations on the role of historical analogy in foreign-policy decision-making, and we've been circling a problem I can't stop thinking about. In a recent session, the instructor handed everyone the same one-page description of a looming foreign-policy crisis and split us into three groups to advise the president. The groups reached almost opposite recommendations, and in retrospect the difference tracked the analogies people happened to reach for. One group kept invoking Munich and the danger of appeasing a rising aggressor, another kept invoking Vietnam and the danger of slipping into a quagmire, and a third framed the situation as a Thucydides-trap rivalry between a rising power and an established power. What unsettles me is that the facts of the case barely seemed to drive anyone's judgment; the analogy each group brought to the table did most of the work before the evidence was seriously discussed.

I'd like you to design a runnable classroom activity for this same seminar, with 14 students and 80 minutes available, that makes participants feel that problem from the inside rather than hear it asserted in a debrief. It should be a structured game, simulation, or role-play with roles or rules, a timeline, real choices or small stakes, and a debrief. Arrange the rules or materials so that participants run into the way a chosen analogy pre-selects the conclusion before the facilitator names it, for example by varying which historical precedent is made salient to otherwise identical groups, or by forcing participants to decide under an analogy they did not choose. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the idea that historical analogies can mislead, say what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run and how to handle it, and be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce about real policy-making, such as high stakes, incomplete information, or having lived through the original episode, and therefore what the facilitator must supply. Participants should leave with a felt sense that analogies are arguments rather than data, and that the mature response is not to stop using analogy but to audit it.
