Consider two ordinary judgments. A country that depends heavily on imports for its staple food, energy, or critical components is generally treated as dangerously exposed, and state support for domestic production is widely accepted as prudent even at considerable economic cost. Yet a person who aimed at full self-sufficiency—growing all of his own food, supplying his own power, and relying on no one else's specialized labor—would be regarded not as prudent but as something between an eccentric and a survivalist, someone who has thrown away the gains from the division of labor. Dependence on outsiders is thus treated as a sensible default for a person and as a strategic vulnerability for a state, and the ideal of autarky is treated as a serious goal for nations but as a suspect one for individuals. Is there a defensible principle that makes the very same dependence rational for an individual to accept and dangerous for a nation to fear? Or is at least one of these attitudes a mistake? If you defend the asymmetry, test it against the case of an individual who has few realistic alternatives to a single employer or supplier, and the case of a small state that can buy freely from many competing sellers; do not let the defense rest on unit size alone. If you reject the asymmetry, say which side should be revised and what the practical consequences would be. Please answer in American English.
