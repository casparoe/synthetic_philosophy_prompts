I am a third-year undergraduate taking a philosophy of science course, and we have just finished the unit on underdetermination: Duhem's point that a prediction always tests a whole network of theory plus auxiliary assumptions, so a failed test does not tell you which element to blame; Quine's stronger claim that, with enough adjustment elsewhere in the web of belief, any hypothesis can be preserved in the face of any evidence; and the usual distinction between a transient underdetermination that further evidence can break and a permanent underdetermination that no possible evidence could break. I want to stress-test these ideas against a real scientific controversy rather than the stock examples.

Take the debate over the cause of the end-Cretaceous mass extinction, the K-Pg boundary about 66 million years ago. The broad facts are these. One camp, following the Alvarez group, proposed a bolide impact: an iridium-rich asteroid striking near what is now Chicxulub, Mexico, producing the global iridium layer, shocked quartz, tektites, soot from wildfires, and a dust cloud that shut down photosynthesis. The main rival proposal was that the massive Deccan Traps flood volcanism in India, erupting over roughly the same interval, released enough carbon dioxide, sulphur dioxide and other material to drive the extinction, with some of its advocates arguing that volcanism could also account for the iridium anomaly. Over the following decades the Chicxulub crater was identified and dated to the boundary, and the timing of the Deccan eruptions relative to the extinction was refined; most, though not all, palaeontologists and geologists now favour the impact as the primary cause, with the contribution of the Deccan Traps still under argument.

Analyse this episode through the lens of underdetermination. In particular: (a) During the 1980s and early 1990s, did the two hypotheses truly share the same body of evidence, or did each camp have its own favoured evidence and its own auxiliary assumptions about what that evidence showed? (b) Where did each side adjust auxiliary hypotheses to keep its core idea alive, and were any of those adjustments ad hoc in a way the underdetermination literature would recognise, or were they ordinary scientific refinements? (c) Did Chicxulub and the improved dating genuinely break the underdetermination, or did they merely make one hypothesis far more consilient and the other more cumbersome to defend? (d) What does the episode show about the underdetermination thesis itself: does it support the weak version, the strong version, or neither, and does it suggest that underdetermination is a real feature of scientific inference or mostly a philosopher's possibility?

Please give me a committed analysis: take a definite position on whether this was a case of genuine underdetermination and which version of the thesis it supports, argue for that position, and say explicitly where a reasonable philosopher of science would push back, rather than laying out all readings and leaving me to choose.
