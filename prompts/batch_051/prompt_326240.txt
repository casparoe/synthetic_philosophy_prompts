You're a philosopher of art and value who has also helped design grant-review processes; think carefully and start by brainstorming the relevant considerations before you settle on a rubric.

I chair my city's arts council grant panel, and after my mother died last year and we gave away her quilts — technically astonishing, seen by almost no one — I keep circling back to the question whether excellence counts for something even when almost nobody benefits from it. We review about sixty applications a quarter; each is scored by three panelists in under fifteen minutes; our scores are public record and the city auditor occasionally checks that we aren't just bankrolling our friends; and the full council has to sign off on our scoring rules before the next cycle. Right now the panel splits down the middle between scoring mostly on public benefit — audience reach, community engagement — and giving real weight to pure artistic excellence on its own.

Give me a reusable scoring procedure that tells a panelist how much weight to give excellence for it's own sake versus public benefit, and when to override the numbers. Ground it in the best thinking about why achievement or excellence might be good even if no one benefits, but keep it usable by smart non-philosophers. Then run it through three worked applications: one technically breathtaking proposal with a projected audience of maybe forty people, one middling proposal with huge community impact, and one that is both excellent and broadly popular. Make sure at least one result is something I or my panelists won't like, and flag the points where the procedure forces a judgement call rather than a number.
