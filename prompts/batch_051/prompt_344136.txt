Okay so here's a view I've ended up defending, and I want to know what would actually count against it—not just the usual objections. I think the independence axiom in expected utility theory is best understood not as a requirement of rationality but as a bookeeping convention: a rule for how outcomes and states get individuated once you've decided how to describe the decision problem. On this picture, the Allais paradox doesn't show people are irrational. In the classic pair people take a certain million over a gamble that's 89% at a million, 10% at five million, and 1% at zero, but then take a 10% shot at five million over an 11% shot at a million. I read that as misdescribed outcomes: the person in the first pair is choosing to avoid the particular regret or self-reproach of having a sure fortune and then losing it, while the second pair has no such safe baseline, so the two choices aren't actually inconsistent. If you enrich the outcome descriptions—regret, disappointment, the story the chooser tells about themselves—their preferences can satisfy independence after all, by re-individuating what counts as a final outcome. My worry is that this move seems available for basically any pattern of choice, which would make independence stop making predictions and make my view unfalsifiable. So what would genuinely count against it? I want the observations, arguments, formal results, or carefully built choice problems that should move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be, and including things defenders of this view would themselves have to admit are awkward. I'd be most moved by an Allais-type problem where the outcome descriptions are fixed in advance and the participants themselves sign off on them as complete, and the usual violation still shows up. Also tell me what my view predicts that the 'people just violate independence' view doesn't. And give me an honest verdict on whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if it has, whether that's actually a problem for a view of this kind.
