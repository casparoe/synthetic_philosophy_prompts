Draft the joint statement that a legal proceduralist and a Lockean prerogative theorist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on emergency measures. The collaboration is anchored by a close reading of this passage from John Locke, *Second Treatise of Government*, chapter XIV, section 160:

“This power to act according to discretion, for the public good, without the prescription of the law, and sometimes even against it, is that which is called prerogative: for since in some governments the lawmaking power is not always in being, and is usually too numerous, and so too slow, for the dispatch requisite to execution; and because also it is impossible to foresee, and so by laws to provide for, all accidents and necessities that may concern the public, or to make such laws as will do no harm, if they are executed with an inflexible rigour, on all occasions, and upon all persons that may come in their way; therefore there is a latitude left to the executive power, to do many things of choice which the laws do not prescribe.”

The proceduralist position: the rule of law requires that every exercise of state coercion be traceable to an existing legal authorization, even in an emergency; if the authorization is too narrow, the remedy is to amend the law or invoke explicit constitutional emergency clauses, not to act outside it. The Lockean position: in a genuine and unforeseen necessity, an executive may legitimately act for the public good without prior legal prescription, and sometimes even against an existing statute, provided the action is limited, accountable after the fact, and aimed only at preserving the community.

Produce the document such a collaboration should produce, with these components: (1) a numbered list of claims both parties can sign, including claims about what the passage says, what it does not say, what an emergency is, and what both sides would count as an abuse of emergency power; (2) a disagreements table with columns for the precise point at issue, the proceduralist wording they would accept, and the Lockean wording they would accept; (3) an identification of the cruxes—sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one side; (4) for each crux, a concrete case or test both sides agree bears on it, with each side’s prediction recorded in advance. Use three test cases: closing a border to prevent the spread of a novel pathogen when no statutory provision expressly authorizes it; a lockdown imposed by executive decree while the legislature is able to convene; and a quarantine order applied to a single infected individual in defiance of a statute that requires a court warrant. Indicate where the disagreement may be partly verbal—e.g., over the meaning of “legal” or “legitimate”—and where it is substantive. Do not resolve the debate; the deliverable is the joint statement, with residual disagreement made exact.
