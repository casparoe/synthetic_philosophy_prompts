I've been teaching a Sunday school class on hard biblical texts, and over the last year I've settled into a reading policy and I want to know whether its defensible. My rule is this: I read a passage literally unless a literal reading would conflict with something I already accept as factually or morally true, and then I read it as allegory, typology, or parable. So the six days of Genesis 1 I read figuratively, because they conflict with what I accept about cosmology; the commands to wipe out the Amalekites and the conquest narratives I read as something other than literal divine orders, because they conflict with my moral convictions; but I read the resurrection narratives literally, since nothing I already believe rules them out.

A friend who grew up fundamentalist says this is just rigging the game: the Bible can never correct me, because my prior beliefs are the test a passage has to pass, and I can't tell the difference between the rule and simply ignoring whatever I dislike. Another friend who has actually read Origen says my rule is not only wrong but the reverse of his. As she describes it, Origen didn't allegorize a passage because it conflicted with his preexisting opinions; he went looking for the spiritual sense when the literal sense was internally impossible, incoherent, or unworthy of God on its own terms, and the failure of the letter was supposed to push the reader deeper. On her account, my criterion is external to the text in a way his wasn't. My defense is that no one reads without prior assumptions, I'm just making mine explicit, and I set the bar lower than she does: I only need the literal sense of a story to be historically and morally plausible before I'll credit it.

So, is my reading policy defensible as a way of forming beliefs about these texts? Or is it a bias dressed up as a hermeneutic? If it isn't defensible, what's the closest policy that is, and where exactly do the two come apart?
