A friend of mine is deep into wild animal welfare stuff and has been pushing the same argument on me for months, and I hate it but can't actually locate the mistake. I'm hoping someone can do better than I can. Here's the cleaned-up version:

(1) Most wild animals are r-strategists — a single frog or insect lays hundreds or thousands of eggs, and the typical life ends within days or weeks, in starvation, being eaten alive, disease, or cold. So the average wild animal life is plausibly more pain than pleasure, and in expectation it's a net-negative life.

(2) Destroying a habitat — draining a marsh, clearing scrub, paving it over — prevents an enormous number of those short, mostly miserable lives from ever beginning.

(3) Preventing a being from coming into existence doesn't harm that being; there's no subject for the harm. (I find the usual assymetry between a life not started and a life cut short persuasive.)

(4) The animals actually killed by the destruction are comparatively few, and most of them would have died soon and painfully anyway, so the harm of killing them is small next to the suffering prevented.

(5) If an act prevents vastly more net suffering than it causes and doesn't wrong anyone in a way that outweighs that, then, all else equal, we should do it — or at least we shouldn't bemoan it happening.

(6) Therefore, all else equal, destroying a rich wild habitat is good, and preserving it is bad.

Now this is the kind of conclusion that makes me want to throw the whole argument away, and my friend says that's exactly status quo bias doing its thing. I'd like to keep (3) and I'd also like to keep the view that habitat loss is a moral catastrophe, not a moral achievement. So: which step is the weakest, and what do I have to give up if I reject it?
