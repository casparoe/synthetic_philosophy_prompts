I've been trying to get straight what I actually think about weighing one person's happiness or suffering against another's, because my brother-in-law and I keep going in circles about it and I want my commitments written down so I can see where the cracks are. I'm not trained in any of this, so be generous about the wording. Here's my list. (1) Numbers matter additively: curing ten people of equally bad headaches is ten times as good as curing one person. (2) But a world with a huge number of lives that are only barely net positive does not automatically seem better to me than a smaller world where everyone flourishes; that conclusion feels wrong. (3) Within a single life, I don't think any value is infinite: for any finite bad event, there's some sufficiently large amount of good within that same life that could compensate for it, even if I wouldn't want to actually make the trade. (4) Across different lives, though, I think some values are infinite-ish: one person being tortured can't be made okay by giving any number of other people any amount of benefits. (5) I think of the moral weight of a being's happiness as something like degree of consciousness times felt happiness: a being with half the awareness counts about half as much, so two of them balance one fully aware being, and with enough numbers many barely conscious happy beings should outweigh one more conscious one. (6) But I also think some goods are different in kind and don't belong on that additive scale at all—being humiliated or losing your dignity isn't just some negative number of pleasant moments, even though in practice I still have to trade them off somehow.

Go through that and tell me which commitments (or clusters of them) are actually in tension, which pairs only look like they're in tension, and rank the real tensions by how severe they are, with the cheapest fix for each and what it costs me. And please tell me what would change your mind about the audit: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
