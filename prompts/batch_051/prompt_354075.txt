The selected-effects (etiological) account of biological function says that the function of a trait is whatever effect it was selected for in its evolutionary history: the heart is for pumping blood because hearts were selected for pumping blood. A standard argument for this account can be reconstructed as follows. (1) Any trait has indefinitely many effects—the heart pumps blood, makes a thumping sound, casts a shadow on an X-ray—so the phrase 'the function of the trait' presupposes some principle that marks one effect as privileged. (2) The only principle that can do this is a history of selection: the privileged effect is the one the trait was selected for, while the rest are mere side-effects. (3) Moreover, only such a history can ground the distinction between functioning properly and malfunctioning, since a trait can fail to produce its function while still possessing it. (4) Therefore the selected-effects account is the correct account of biological function, and the rival causal-role account—on which a function is simply a component's contribution to some capacity of a containing system—is deficient because it makes function relative to the analyst's chosen system and cannot by itself sustain the norm of malfunction.

Now consider a development the argument did not anticipate. Synthetic biologists design and construct biological components—de novo proteins whose sequences are fixed computationally, gene circuits assembled from standard parts, increasingly whole synthetic cells—that have canonical functions (sensing a metabolite, catalysing a reaction) but no history of natural selection, and, in the case of purely rational design, no history of differential reproduction of variants at all. Does the argument survive this development, and if not, which premise bears the weight? Is the right conclusion that the selected-effects account must simply deny functions to engineered components; that it should broaden its notion of 'selection' so that design counts as fixing a function; or that the causal-role account regains the advantage it was thought to have lost? I am interested in the fate of the philosophical argument itself, not in how biologists should speak about synthetic biology. Please answer in British English.
