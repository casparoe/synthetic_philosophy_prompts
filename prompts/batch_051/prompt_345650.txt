I work on entity representations in machine learning, and recently I trained a language model on a corpus in which the names Hesperus and Phosphorus occurred in systematically overlapping contexts. By the end of training, the model had assigned the two names nearly the same internal vector, and for it the sentence Hesperus is Phosphorus produced no representational or behavioral change that Hesperus is Hesperus did not. That has made Frege's puzzle personally urgent for me, and I want to reason through what the puzzle demands and whether its argument survives this kind of system.

Frege's argument, as I understand it, is this. If the meaning of a name consisted only in the object it designated, then a true identity sentence such as Hesperus is Phosphorus would express the same thought as the trivial Hesperus is Hesperus, since both would say only that one and the same object is identical with itself. But the two sentences differ in cognitive value: the first can be an empirical discovery, while the second is knowable a priori and extends no one's knowledge. Frege concluded that a name must carry something beyond its referent—a mode of presentation, or sense—and that an identity sentence is informative only when its two names present the same object under different senses. The argument assumes a thinker for whom the two terms are associated with distinct modes of presentation, so that recognizing their coreference does real cognitive work.

Now consider the development that concerns me: a system that represents coreference directly, in a single fused representation. As far as its internal states reveal, there are no two senses; the identity is not informative; yet the system is behaviorally competent with both names across a wide range of contexts. Does Frege's argument survive this development, or does the system show that the sense–reference distinction is optional rather than necessary? Which premise is under pressure—the assumption that understanding two names requires distinct modes of presentation, the assumption that differences in informativeness track something semantic rather than merely the agent's changed information state, or some other premise? Is there a repaired version of Frege's argument that still says something true and important once direct coreference representation is possible, and if so, what would it have to say about such a system?
