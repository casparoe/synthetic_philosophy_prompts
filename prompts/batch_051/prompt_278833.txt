I've been asked to submit a two-page proposal for a funded summer workshop on pain, affect, and clinical phenomenology, and I'd like to use the slot to settle a question I've been turning over. The question is what pain asymbolia actually shows about the badness of pain. Patients with the condition can identify and localize a noxious stimulus and report that it hurts — they can even rate its intensity — but they don't find it aversive: they don't withdraw, guard the affected part, or describe the state as something they want to end. The standard philosophical lesson drawn from this is that pain's badness cannot be identical with its sensory quality, because the sensory quality is present while the badness is absent; the badness must therefore be a further evaluative or motivational ingredient that can fail to attach. I suspect this inference moves too quickly. Both sides of the debate tend to treat the badness of pain as a single thing, when it might split into three components: felt intensity, motivational pull (the urge to end the state or protect the body), and a normative-seeming gloss (the impression that the state is bad for its subject). Pain asymbolia might cleanly dissociate only one of these, and which one it dissociates changes what the condition actually establishes.

What I'd like from you is the proposal itself, written in my voice for the workshop committee. It should state a precise question with a determinate answer — perhaps whether what pain asymbolia removes is best characterized as felt unpleasantness, motivational pull, or the normative gloss on the sensation. It should explain why the existing positions leave the question open, chiefly that they inherit an undifferentiated notion of badness from the way the cases are standardly described. It should set out the approach and why it's better than the obvious alternative of doing everything by introspection: I imagine working through the clinical descriptions, building a taxonomy of the three candidate notions, and then using the taxonomy to reconstruct the strongest version of the standard argument, testing which dissociations would have to obtain for each conclusion to follow. Finally, it should say what a successful deliverable looks like (I'm imagining a short diagnostic paper: if the condition dissociates X from Y and Z, the standard lesson survives; if it dissociates only Y, the lesson needs revision), what would count as the project failing, and a realistic schedule for the six-week duration of the workshop. Don't include a bibliography; I'll add references myself.
