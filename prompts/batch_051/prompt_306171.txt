I coordinate the grading for an undergraduate history of science course. Each term, sixty students write a short paper in which they reconstruct an argument from an older scientific or mathematical text in their own words, and three teaching assistants each grade about twenty of those papers over a weekend. The grades have to be consistent enough to survive students comparing papers and appealing them. The recurring disagreement among us is not about mechanics but about anachronism. One teaching assistant lets a student say Aristotle had a theory of inertia or that Euclid used algebra as shorthand, treating these as harmless modern dress. Another wants to flag any use of current notation or concepts as a distortion of what the historical author could have meant. We need a single reusable procedure, not a list of examples, that a graduate teaching assistant with no special expertise in the period can run in ten minutes on a paper before assigning a grade. It should say, in order, what to check; which substitutions and modernizations are safe and which are not; what to do in the grey area; and when the procedure must stop and send the case to me rather than pretending the checklist has settled it.

Please ground the procedure in the actual distinctions the history and philosophy of science has developed around anachronism, presentism, and rational reconstruction, but translate those distinctions into questions a grader can answer without reading the primary text in the original language. Include the hard cases: rendering an old result in modern notation; attributing a modern concept or problem to a text that runs on different notions; evaluating an old claim against later experimental or mathematical standards; and reading the historical importance of a result back into the author's stated aims. Then apply the finished procedure to three worked examples, at least one where it requires marking down a paper a teaching assistant found acceptable and one where it requires accepting a paper that initially looked suspiciously modern. End by saying what the procedure cannot determine: which judgments of anachronism rest on a historiographical or philosophical commitment that no checklist can settle, how the procedure should flag that for me, and how the grader should phrase that honestly to the student.
