In my undergraduate philosophy of language course, a student recently argued that J. L. Austin's distinction between constative and performative utterances collapses, because, as she put it, "saying is doing." Her reasoning was that since every utterance is itself an action, the contrast between utterances that describe or state facts and utterances that do something in being said cannot be a distinction of kind, and the notion of a performative utterance therefore carries no explanatory weight. I would like a structured map of the possible readings of her slogan before I take this up in lecture.

Please separate the distinct precise theses that "saying is doing" could be used to assert. At minimum, consider the following readings: (i) the merely physical thesis that uttering involves movement, sound production, and other ordinary bodily action; (ii) the Austinian thesis that in saying something one performs an illocutionary act, such as asserting, warning, or promising; (iii) the stronger thesis that constative utterances are themselves a species of performative, since stating or describing is an act one performs in speaking; (iv) the still stronger thesis that the descriptive/performative contrast is theoretically empty because all utterances do things and many apparently descriptive statements are themselves subject to felicity conditions; and (v) any radical reading according to which there is no fact-stating at all that is independent of action. For each reading, indicate whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false or misleading as stated. Then tell me which reading or readings would actually support her dismissal of the constative/performative distinction, and which would not. I am less interested in a single correct interpretation than in a menu of readings with verdicts attached, so that I can distinguish in class between what the slogan shows and what it merely gestures at.
