Amartya Sen's liberal paradox is often presented as a clash between two social-choice commitments. The first is the Pareto principle: if every individual ranks one alternative above another, the social ranking should do the same. The second is a minimal-liberty condition: for each individual, there is at least one pair of alternatives, typically involving that person's private life, over which the individual's preference alone should determine the social ranking. Now consider Sen's two-person case. There are three alternatives: a, person P (the prude) reads a controversial novel; b, person L (the lewd) reads it; c, no one reads it. P's preference ordering is c over a over b. L's ordering is a over b over c. P is decisive over the pair a and c, while L is decisive over the pair b and c. Show in detail how these three requirements—P's decisiveness, L's decisiveness, and the Pareto principle—generate a cyclical social ranking. Then survey the main ways of dissolving the paradox: modeling rights as constraints on outcomes rather than as preferences to be aggregated, restricting the domain of admissible preference profiles, weakening one of the two principles, or denying that the minimal-liberty condition captures what a right actually is. For each proposal, identify exactly what it preserves and what it must surrender. Is there a reconciliation that keeps both the Pareto intuition and the thought that some choices belong to the individual, or does the paradox show that one of the two commitments has to go? Which resolutions amount to special pleading rather than genuine solutions?
