So last night me and my friend got into this long, kind of heated argument about whether she had a duty to keep doubting this health thing her mom sent her, and whether my habit of pushing back on everything anyone says makes me a good friend or just a jerk. After two hours we realized we weren't even disagreeing about the same thing, because we'd never pinned down what we each actually think about doubt as a moral matter. I want to turn the argument into a handout for my philosophy meetup — smart adults who haven't taken a class on this — that sorts people into the position they actually hold.

Write me a decision-tree-style quiz: six or seven yes/no branch questions, around eight leaves, each leaf a named (or "unnamed") position with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Build it from the logical space of the answers, not from a list of famous views, and make each branch question a concrete case or claim someone can answer without already knowing the labels.

Here's what constrains it.

The branch questions should probe distinctions we kept getting stuck on: (a) whether forming beliefs is even the kind of thing you can have moral duties about, as opposed to being merely rational or irrational; (b) whether the duty, if there is one, is universal or asymetrical — for example, whether the powerful and comfortable owe more self-doubt than the already-marginalized; (c) whether doubt is best thought of as a rule about evidence, a virtue of character, or a social practice that can be weaponized; (d) whether practical stakes change how much evidence you need before believing becomes permissible.

Use at least these cases as branch questions: a friend who shares a viral post about a new diet study and says she's just asking questions while refusing to read the actual paper; a juror who wants to convict because the defendant's story doesn't feel right; a person leaving a tight-knit religious or political group who starts doubting beliefs the community treats as a condition of belonging; and an activist group deciding whether to air its internal criticisms publicly right before a big campaign.

The leaves have to include the Clifford-style view that believing on insufficient evidence is always a moral wrong; the James-style pragmatic view that when the evidence can't decide a forced, momentous question you're allowed to go with your passional side; a virtue view on which you're not under a rule to doubt but trying to become the kind of person who doubts at the right times; a stakes view on which the evidential bar rises and falls with what's on the line; a socialized view on which the demand to doubt is unevenly distributed and can be used against people with less power; and a loyalty-or-fideist view on which trust and fidelity to a community can make persistent doubt the wrong choice.

Wherever the tree implies a combination nobody actually holds, label the leaf unoccupied and say in a few words whether it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And be honest about which pairs of leaves the questions can't really separate — the pragmatic view and the stakes view, for instance, may hand down the same verdict a lot of the time even though they explain it differently.

No citations or reading list, I just want the structure. Keep it conversational, American English.
