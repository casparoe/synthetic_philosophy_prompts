I'm a novelist, and I'm drafting a near-future novel in which a consumer implant can directly stimulate the brain's reward circuitry. My plan was to write the heavy users as cautionary figures—people who have wireheaded themselves, tampering with their own reward machinery in a way the narration treats as self-undermining. But a careful reader pushed back: all goal-directed behavior, they said, is the brain steering toward reward; the implant is just a more direct route to what everyone is already pursuing, so treating it as tampering begs the question against an efficient means to a universal end. I can feel the force of that, and I don't want the novel's moral weight to rest on a confusion.

I need this settled before I write the scenes. In the AI case, as I understand it, wireheading and reward tampering are problems because a system is gaming a reward signal that was only ever meant as a proxy for something else—there is a designer, a specified objective, and a channel that can be corrupted without the objective being achieved. Does an individual human using direct reward stimulation fit that structure, or is the framework being stretched into a different claim when applied to people? More sharply: is there a principled difference between a person who uses the implant, and an AI that tampers with its reward signal—or is the only difference engineering, and my cautionary instinct is just prejudice against a shortcut? I want a committed answer, not a survey of options: take a definite position, give the reasons, and tell me directly whether my cautionary framing is defensible or whether I need a different ground for treating a life spent on the implant as a failure.
