I’m trying to get clear on something before writing a paper, and I’d like you to write the specification rather than the argument: what should a theory of canonical choice—of when a labeling, representative, or construction is harmless and when it carries information—actually have to get right? The central case, for me, is the roots. For x² − 2 over the rationals, there are two roots, and no field-theoretic description with rational parameters can tell them apart, because the nontrivial automorphism of the splitting field swaps them; but as soon as we sit inside the reals and bring in the ordering, one of them is positive and the label √2 is forced. With x² + 1, by contrast, the roots are i and −i, and complex conjugation is an automorphism of the complex numbers fixing the reals pointwise, so nothing expressible in the field language with real parameters picks one out—yet we standardly designate one of them and call the other its negation, and nothing breaks. And x³ − 2 over the rationals is subtler still: its three roots are fully symmetric over the rationals, since the Galois group is the full symmetric group, so field theory over the rationals cannot label any of them; only relative to the reals is the single real root ∛2 privileged, while the two complex roots remain swapped by conjugation. Off the polynomial setting, the same issues surface in quotients, where a function on cosets is well-defined exactly when it does not depend on the chosen representative, and in choices of representatives that exist but encode no structure.

Write me the desiderata for a theory that sorts these cases: which choices are canonical, which are arbitrary but harmless, and which are mistakes. For each requirement, say what case or consideration forces it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as the taste of a particular camp (for instance, the demand that every existence claim come with a canonical witness, or the opposite demand that arbitrary choice be purged from mathematics entirely). The theory should at minimum respect distinctions like these: unique, versus unique up to unique isomorphism, versus unique up to non-unique isomorphism, versus not even that; well-definedness as independence from a representative, versus canonicity as invariance under the symmetries of the given structure; and a symmetry that fixes all the background structure, the way conjugation fixes the reals, versus a choice that no relevant symmetry leaves put. If some of these requirements cannot be jointly satisfied—for example, if respecting every symmetry would force you to conclude that √2 is no more canonical than i, against ordinary mathematical practice—that collision is exactly what I want identified.
