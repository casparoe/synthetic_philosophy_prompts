I just spent a long evening caught between two friends arguing about whether Daniel Dennett’s Chase and Sanborn example really shows that qualia are an incoherent posit. For background: Dennett imagines two coffee tasters, Chase and Sanborn, who once liked a blend and now dislike it. Chase says the coffee tastes exactly the same as it always did, but his standards have changed. Sanborn says his standards are unchanged, but the coffee now tastes different to him. You can fill in the causal details so that every publicly observable fact about their behavior, discriminations, and reports is held fixed. My friends disputed what follows from this.

Clara said the example is a reductio. If two complete histories can be identical at every functional and behavioral level while differing only in the alleged quale, then the quale is doing no work. The claim that Chase’s experience is one way rather than another is untestable even in principle. That makes talk of qualia empty, not just hard to verify.

Rafael replied that this mistakes underdetermination for absence. We can imagine two waveforms that make all the same observable predictions, yet no one says the wavefunction is incoherent. From the inside, a subject can be directly acquainted with a phenomenal change while being unsure whether to describe it as a change in the object or a change in the attitude. Introspection need not be infallible for there to be a fact of the matter.

Clara answered that this analogy backfires. A wavefunction earns its keep by unifying third-person predictions; a quale is supposed to be known directly. The striking thing about the Chase and Sanborn case is not that an outside observer cannot tell the difference, but that even the subject cannot say whether his experience changed or his standards changed. If the phenomenal character is directly given, how could that question be opaque?

Rafael said it is opaque only because we are being asked to classify a total experience under one of two functional descriptions. I can be directly acquainted with the overall character of my current coffee experience while genuinely not knowing whether to call the difference a change in the taste or a change in my reaction. In fact, that kind of classificatory difficulty is exactly what one would expect if qualia are intrinsic, nonrelational properties rather than dispositions.

Clara said that is just relabeling the problem. If a quale is intrinsic and nonrelational, then it can float free of every disposition to compare, react, remember, or judge. A difference that makes no difference to any disposition is a difference that no one—including the subject—can detect. Dennett’s point is not merely epistemic; it is that the posit has no content.

Rafael said that is too strong. Beliefs can also change gradually in ways the believer cannot date or describe precisely, but we do not reject belief as incoherent. Vagueness and introspective uncertainty are pervasive features of mental life. The demand that qualia be either sharply knowable or nonexistent imposes a standard we do not apply to other mental states.

Clara replied that beliefs are functional states, so vagueness there is harmless. A quale is supposed to be a definite, immediate, intrinsic quality. Different standards apply. The Chase and Sanborn case is designed to show that the notion of a pure phenomenal residue cannot be pinned down even by the person who allegedly has it.

Rafael came back that qualia can be vague too. Consider the subjective transition from orange to red: there is no precise boundary, yet the endpoints feel different. Vagueness does not refute phenomenal character. The real issue is whether we can make sense of an intrinsic fact that outruns all our evidence and all our dispositions, and I think we can—refusing to accept that is an expression of verificationism.

They asked me to referee, and I honestly could not. Please go through their exchange step by step and say who is right about which specific points, what either of them is missing, and who has the stronger overall case.
