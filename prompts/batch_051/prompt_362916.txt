Last week two students in my philosophy of language seminar — call them Dana and Marco — stayed after class arguing about a version of the sorites puzzle. Dana defends epistemicism about vagueness: the view that vague predicates like "heap" really do have sharp boundaries, but we cannot know where those boundaries are. Marco defends a supervaluationist picture: a vague predicate is only made precise relative to admissible sharpenings ("precisifications"), and a sentence is true outright only if it holds on all of them. They went back and forth four times, then asked me to referee, and I could not decide.

Dana began: "The classical data force a sharp cutoff. If each sentence 'n grains make a heap' is plainly true or plainly false, then somewhere there is a last true case before the first false one. The only way to deny such a cutoff is to abandon bivalence, and that costs us ordinary reasoning. So borderline cases are just cases where we are ignorant of the hidden boundary."

Marco replied: "Too quick. I keep classical logic where it matters, at the level of supertruth: every classical theorem and every instance of excluded middle holds on all precisifications. What I reject is bivalence for borderline sentences like 'this case is a heap,' which are neither true nor false. Your cutoff sentence is true on each precisification but has no single witness across them, so no particular boundary is part of the meaning of 'heap.' That lets me avoid your epistemic miracle — a boundary no speaker could ever learn — without gutting ordinary reasoning."

Dana shot back: "You haven't removed the boundary; you've pluralized it. Each precisification still draws a sharp line, so your semantics is quietly committed to the very cutoffs you call impossible; you just write 'not determinately' over them afterward. And you owe me a story about higher-order vagueness — the vagueness of 'admissible precisification' itself — which looks like an infinite hierarchy of meta-languages. My view handles that uniformly: higher-order vagueness is just higher-order ignorance about the same one hidden boundary."

Marco ended: "A line inside a precisification is a modeling artifact, not a claim about the world or the word. Precisifications are admissible ways of sharpening an incomplete meaning, not candidates for the hidden extension. Higher-order vagueness cuts both ways: your view posits an infinite tower of unknowable cutoffs, which strikes me as more occult than my hierarchy. And my view explains an asymmetry yours cannot — why borderline cases feel different. On your account a competent speaker is trying and failing to track an inaccessible fact; on mine the cases resist assertion because the semantic basis for assertoric commitment runs out."

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move. Put your answer in exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion. In items 1 through 4, rule on each move in order: whether it is correct, partly right but misleading, or mistaken, and why. In item 5, say who is more right overall and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
