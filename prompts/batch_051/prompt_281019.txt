I teach political philosophy and philosophy of technology, and I've landed on a view that sounds a bit off at first. The rationalist story about war says that since war is costly for both sides, there's always some bargain both would prefer to fighting — so you only get war from private information with incentives to misrepresent, from commitment problems, or from indivisible stakes. Lately I've become convinced this is also the right lens on AI races. The labs and governments involved aren't stupid or oblivious to the risk; they know a race to the bottom could hurt everyone, yet they still can't strike a bargain. On my view, the AI race is mostly a commitment problem wearing a technology costume, not a case of collective irrationality. What would actually count against this view? I want the observations, arguments, possible cases, and formal or empirical results that should move me — not just the obvious objection that firms sometimes behave irrationally. Where does the rationalist framework break down when you move it from states to firms and from war to technology competition? Does the view make predictions about, say, the conditions under which a treaty or verification regime could stabalize, and has it been insulated until no failure can refute it? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones someone committed to the view would concede as genuine costs.
