I am a third-year undergraduate in a philosophy of mind course, and the position that dominates our discussion of consciousness is that epiphenomenalism is refuted by the paradox of phenomenal judgment. The standard line, which I have defended in one essay, is that the view is self-stultifying: if experiences are causally inert by-products of neural activity, then a judgment that one is in pain is not caused by the pain itself but only by the underlying neural state. A duplicate with the same neural causes but no phenomenal experience at all would produce the very same report. So introspective judgments turn out to be causally insensitive to the phenomenal facts they seem to report, and the first-person evidence that motivates epiphenomenalism in the first place would be unreliable under the theory.

I want to avoid simply rehearsing this objection. Treat the standard conclusion as a claim under test and ask what would have to be true for it to be wrong, that is, for epiphenomenalism to escape the paradox. Identify the load-bearing assumptions in the argument: about whether introspection is a causal transfer of information, about what it takes for a judgment to reliably concern its subject matter, about whether the physical causes of a phenomenal judgment can also fix its content and its truth, about whether knowledge requires sensitivity to the fact known rather than mere reliability in the actual world, and about how the theory's motivation depends on introspective warrant. For each assumption, specify what our cognitive architecture or the psychophysical laws would need to look like for it to fail. Then sort the escape routes into those that are live positions with genuine resources in the current debate and those that are merely logical possibilities no one could seriously occupy. Distinguish considerations that would merely embarrass the standard view from those that would overturn it. Finally, give an honest overall assessment: how exposed is the claim that epiphenomenalism is self-stultifying, and does the view risk anything at all once these replies are conceded, or has it been insulated in a way that is itself a problem? I would personally find it most persuasive if someone could show that the strongest version of the objection can be run without relying on any special theory of introspection, so that the epiphenomenalist cannot buy safety simply by rejecting a causal theory of self-knowledge.
