I am a novelist. In the book I am drafting, a character makes a burdensome promise to a dying friend — to finish the friend's unfinished work and see it into print, or to raise the friend's child. After the friend dies, she considers breaking the promise: performing would cost her years of her life, the person to whom she made the promise is gone, no one else expected anything of her, and no one would be harmed. Another character reproaches her with a familiar analogy: a promise is a debt. You borrowed trust, he tells her, and the death of the lender does not extinguish what you owe; you still have to pay. I want the novel to take this thought seriously rather than use it as a slogan, so I need to know how much weight the analogy can actually bear. Please audit the promise-as-debt analogy for me. Lay out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of creditor, debtor, principal, interest, default, discharge, renegotiation, third-party enforcement, and so on. Then identify the points where the mapping breaks down. Separate the disanalogies that matter for this character's situation from those that are merely cosmetic. Finally, evaluate the conclusion the analogy is being used to support: that a promise, once made, creates an obligation that survives the promisee's death and cannot simply be set aside because performance has become costly. If the debt picture breaks exactly where it is load-bearing, say so, and offer a better analogy or a more precise statement of what, if anything, still binds her.
