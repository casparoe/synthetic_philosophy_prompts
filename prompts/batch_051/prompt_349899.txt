I'm a political theory PhD student and I've been going back through Dewey, and something keeps bugging me. His experimentalism says democracy is a mode of collective inquiry: policies are working hypotheses, their consequences are the data, and a public is just the group that forms around indirect consequences that need regulating. And I can't decide wether the resemblance to reinforcement learning is deep or just a metaphor doing all the work.

In RL you have an agent with a policy that acts on an environment, receives delayed and noisy reward, and updates. There's an explore/exploit tradeoff: keep trying things that might fail vs. stick with what currently works. Dewey sounds structurally similar: a public notices a problem, tries a policy, observes what it does to people, revises or abandons it. So the mapping would be: policies map to policies, consequences to the reward signal, publics and legislatures to the learning algorithm, and norms like free inquiry and revisability are supposed to be the conditions that keep this learning process from just drifting. My question is whether any of the machinery transfers. E.g., RL treats credit assignment as a serious problem when reward is delayed and many actions entangle to produce one outcome—does that say something precise about why Dewey thought publics fail to form around diffuse, long-run consequences? Or does the analogy break because in RL the objective is fixed and given, while in Dewey the "value function" is itself up for grabs and revised through the same inquiry it's supposed to guide? I want to know where the mapping holds and where it comes apart, what the democratic side would have to be like for convergence or sample-efficiency results to carry over, and what the analogy predicts that's obviously false.
