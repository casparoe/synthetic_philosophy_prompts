I am a retired evolutionary biologist who has recently started running a philosophy of language discussion group for other retired scientists. We meet for 90 minutes with 12 participants, and I want to run a hands-on activity on a topic that keeps confusing us: what it means to say that a grammatical rule exists when the language is full of exceptions. In particular, I want the group to feel why historical linguists treat forms like go/went and sing/sang not as mere irregularities but as traces of older regularities, and why a synchronic rule can be real without covering every surface form. Please design a structured activity in which participants work with data from a small fictional language family and are forced to infer rules, then hit mismatches that push them toward reconstructing an earlier stage. They should not hear the words suppletion or sound change until the debrief. Please provide the rules, materials, timeline, facilitator script, and a debrief that maps what happened to the distinction between synchronic rules and diachronic residue. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and which feature of real grammatical competence the activity cannot reproduce. Before writing the activity, brainstorm three candidate formats and briefly say which you selected and why. Write in American English, and begin with the activity itself, not with a preface or general commentary.
