You are a professional philosopher specializing in metaethics and the philosophy of language, and you have been asked to audit an analogy that does a great deal of work in the expressivism debate. In many introductory treatments, expressivism about moral language is motivated by an analogy between moral utterances and simple expressions of emotion or approval: calling an act wrong is said to be more like shouting "boo" at a villain or "hooray" at a hero than like describing the act as having a certain property. On this cheer/boo picture, a moral sentence expresses a non-cognitive attitude toward its object rather than stating a fact about it. The same analogy is then used by critics to refute expressivism. The objection runs roughly as follows: if moral utterances were just cheers and boos, then embedded moral sentences would make no sense; a conditional such as "if lying is wrong, then getting your brother to lie is wrong" cannot be read as a conditional whose antecedent is a boo, because the speaker is plainly not booing lying in the antecedent, and the whole conditional seems to express a pattern of attitudes rather than any single attitude. Thus, the critic concludes, expressivism cannot account for the way moral language composes in conditionals, negations, questions, and attitude reports.

Your task is to audit this cheer/boo analogy, not to settle expressivism by fiat. Work carefully and systematically. Start by brainstorming the different argumentative jobs the analogy is made to do in the debate, and reconstruct the strongest version of each side's use of it before criticizing anything. Then build an explicit correspondence table: what in moral discourse is supposed to correspond to what in the cheer/boo picture — the utterance, the attitude expressed, the content, the speaker, the audience, disagreement, inference, and so on. Then identify the points where the mapping breaks down, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the Frege–Geach objection — compositionality, unasserted contexts, mixed descriptive–normative sentences, ascriptions of attitudes, logical relations between attitudes — from the disanalogies that are superficial and carry no argumentative weight. Finally, deliver a verdict: does the Frege–Geach objection refute expressivism, or only the cheer/boo caricature of it? What would a better analogy be, if any? And can any single analogy bear the explanatory weight that this one is asked to bear, or does the expressivist need a compositional theory of attitudes rather than an analogy? Be candid about the places where the analogy, even charitably repaired, still leaves the expressivist with a hard problem.
