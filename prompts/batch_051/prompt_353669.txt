I've been arguing with friends who run a small open-source project about where to draw the line on derivative works. Copyleft seems to collapse into "static linking bad, dynamic linking maybe bad, network services fine until someone changes their mind," so nobody can predict in advance what's allowed. One of them proposed a license built around a machine-computable derivation score. The rule: if your program talks to our code across a process boundary — no shared address space, only serialized messages through a documented API — you're always safe. If you link against us (statically or dynamically), we compute a score with three parts. (1) What fraction of your program's symbols resolve against our library; over 35% trips the flag. (2) If every call to us were deleted, would a reasonable user still recognize your program as the same program, or is it just a shell? (3) How many of our public entry points do you call, weighted by how deep they sit in our internal call graph — the deepest quartile counts double. Trip any two flags and your program is derivative; it has to be released under our license. There's also a public registry where you upload a build manifest and get a preliminary score before you ship, plus an appeals panel whose decisions get cached and treated as precedent for everyone else.

I don't want a verdict on whether the license is good or bad in the abstract. I want to know how this behaves once people adapt to it. Who benefits, and who gets squeezed? Where does it get gamed first — what do the first five years look like? What kinds of software or architecture will the score systematically misclassify? And can the failure modes be fixed by adjusting thresholds and weights, or is this whole approach just drawing a crisp line through something that'll always be fuzzy?
