I have been working through Hempel’s essay “Studies in the Logic of Confirmation,” and I keep returning to a passage early in the paper where he lays out two constraints on confirmation and shows how they interact. I am working from memory, so I will paraphrase rather than quote. The passage first records a criterion he attributes to Nicod: a universal generalization of the form All F are G is confirmed by an observed F that is also G, and disconfirmed by an observed F that is not G. It then records what Hempel calls the equivalence condition: if two statements are logically equivalent, any evidence that confirms one confirms the other. Hempel then constructs a line of reasoning that strikes me as proving far too much. Reconstructed in my own terms, it runs as follows.

(1) By Nicod’s criterion, the generalization All non-black things are non-ravens is confirmed by the observation of a non-black thing that is also a non-raven, such as a white shoe.
(2) The generalization All ravens are black is logically equivalent to All non-black things are non-ravens.
(3) By the equivalence condition, whatever confirms All non-black things are non-ravens confirms All ravens are black.
(4) Therefore, a white shoe confirms All ravens are black.
(5) The reasoning does not depend on the particular example; a red herring, a green leaf, or any other non-black non-raven confirms All ravens are black just as much as a black raven does.
(6) But that conclusion is absurd: it would make almost any observation, however irrelevant, count as evidence for any true universal generalization, and it would erase the distinction between genuine evidence and background noise.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I would most like to keep the equivalence condition, since it seems to be a purely logical constraint on confirmation, and I would also like to keep the claim that black ravens confirm All ravens are black. But I cannot bring myself to accept that a white shoe says anything about ravens. Tell me which premise or step to reject, and what rejecting it costs for a theory of confirmation. If the right response is instead to accept the conclusion and explain away its air of paradox, say exactly what feature of ordinary inductive practice produces that air, and whether a satisfactory theory of confirmation should be answerable to it.
