I was at a family dinner last weekend and two relatives got into an argument I'd love a second opinion on. My brother-in-law, a veterinarian, and my cousin, a robotics engineer, had both seen the same "robotic dog" exhibit at the science museum — a four-legged robot covered in synthetic fur that wags, barks, fetches, and pants. My cousin said it is obviously a dog in the only sense that matters: it looks like a dog, acts like a dog, and if you grew up around dogs you'd match it to all the particular dogs you remember seeing, so refusing to call it a dog is just snobbery. My brother-in-law said that's a heuristic, not the category: a dog has to come from dogs — be born of dogs, with the right lineage and internal biology — and a pile of servos can't be one. He added that a three-legged, hairless animal born to two dogs is more a dog than any animatronic could be. My cousin shot back that people managed to sort animals into dogs and non-dogs for thousands of years before anyone knew about DNA, on the basis of looks and behavior, and that the vet's view makes ordinary word use mysterious. The vet replied that ordinary people always had a rough biological theory — parents, birth, breeding — so origin was in the picture from the start, and when similarity and origin pull apart, people defer to origin. My cousin then said that if the vet were right, compounds like "robot dog" and "toy gun" would be contradictions, but they're perfectly fine, so something's off. The vet said compounds just mark the noun as borrowed or figurative, not the real category, becuase nobody thinks a toy gun is a gun. He also said a perfect imitation that shares no origin with dogs would be an imitation, not a real dog, no matter how good it is. That's when they started talking past each other and I couldn't follow who'd won any particular step.

I'd like you to adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each substantive claim, say whether it's right, wrong, or partly right, and why. Then say which overall position is stronger, and whether there's a third option (for example, different uses or senses of the word, or a role for both similarity and background theory) that resolves the fight better than either of them allowed.
