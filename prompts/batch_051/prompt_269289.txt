I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am preparing a long-form essay on impartiality and the moral weight of family ties in classical Chinese thought. I would like you to produce a rigorous conceptual comparison of two positions: the Mohist doctrine of impartial care, and the Confucian doctrine of graded love, sometimes rendered as love with distinctions. Rather than a general survey of the two schools, focus the comparison on a single contested question: whether the Confucian appeal to the family as the place where moral dispositions are first formed gives graded love a justificatory advantage over the Mohist demand that regard for others not track closeness to the agent. A recurring argument in the Mohist texts is worth engaging here: impartial care is defended partly on the grounds that it is the surest way for a devoted person to secure good treatment for their own parents, since others will then be disposed to care for those parents in return. A Confucian response would plausibly reply that this misdescribes filial devotion, because it turns a parent's welfare into an outcome to be produced rather than the direct object of a relational commitment. With this exchange in mind, compare the two positions on the following issue: are the two sides disagreeing primarily about the psychology of moral motivation, about the structure of moral justification, or about both? Then identify the strongest consideration available to each side, and offer a reasoned judgment about which position better withstands the other side's strongest criticism.
