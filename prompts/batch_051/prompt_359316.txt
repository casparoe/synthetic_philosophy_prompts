I'm in a philosophy of quantum mechanics course and the unit on psi-ontic versus psi-epistemic views has been bugging me. As I understand it (and I might be mangling this): a psi-ontic person thinks the wavefunction is something real in the world, part of the furniture, so if the state goes from (|L> + |R>)/√2 to |R>, that's a genuine change in the world. A psi-epistemic person thinks the wavefunction is more like a bookkeeping entry for what an observer knows, so updating the state after getting new information doesn't have to be a physical change at all. My lecturer says the two camps are maddeningly hard to separate if you only look at what gets measured, and I want to see them come apart on the question of whether a physical change happened, because that feels like the heart of the disagreement.

So here's the thought experiment I'd like you to build, with these specs. It should use a single particle in a superposition of two locations and a null measurement: I set up a detector in one location, it does not fire, and exactly that non-event is what forces the state to update. Keep it to ordinary nonrelativistic QM as its actually used in a lab — no many-worlds, no conscious observers causing collapse, and no hidden variable shortcuts that change the empirical predictions just to dissolve the problem. I want a psi-onticist and a psi-epistemicist who agree on every later measurement probability but disagree about whether anything physical changed in the particle when the state was updated. Then walk me through what each of them says happened, which feature of the case is doing the work, what would happen to the disagreement if the detector had actually fired, and how the psi-onticist should best answer the psi-epistemicist instead of just digging in. At the end, flag which parts of this are just settled formalism and which parts are genuinely contested among people who actually work on foundations.
