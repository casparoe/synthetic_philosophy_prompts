One influential argument in the study of science learning runs as follows. Students' errors about the physical world are not random: a pupil who says that a heavier object falls faster, or that a battery is used up by lighting a bulb, tends to make the same style of error across many superficially different problems. The best explanation of this systematicity, the argument says, is that the learner possesses a stable, organised, theory-like alternative conception — a misconception in the strong sense — which generates predictions and resists piecemeal correction. From this the argument draws an instructional conclusion: because the error is underwritten by something theory-shaped, teaching cannot remove it by supplying correct facts; it must induce conceptual change, replacing or restructuring the whole alternative framework.

Over the past few decades, however, a rival programme has argued from close-grained interviews that much of what looks theory-like is in fact a loose collection of small, context-sensitive intuitions — knowledge-in-pieces, or phenomenological primitives — that learners assemble on the spot in response to the surface features of a problem. On this rival view, the apparent systematicity is partly an artefact of the questions researchers ask: the very same learner shifts between incompatible explanations when the wording or context changes, which is difficult to reconcile with the claim that she holds a single coherent theory.

Suppose, for the sake of argument, that the piecemeal findings are right for a substantial share of the best-documented misconceptions. Does the misconceptions-as-theories argument survive this development? I want to know exactly where the pressure lands: on the premise that systematicity is evidence of an underlying theory, on the premise that stable errors require a stable internal representation, or somewhere else. Is there a repaired version of the argument that keeps its pedagogical core — that instruction must change how a learner thinks rather than merely what she says — while abandoning the claim that the change is always theory replacement? Or does the argument collapse into a much weaker claim that leaves the conceptual-change programme without a rationale?
