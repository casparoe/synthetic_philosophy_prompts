I've been reading Descartes' Discourse on Method lately, specifically part five, because my daughter has reached the age where she is absolutely convinced the family cat has thoughts, and she keeps asking me what I think, and I realized I didn't actually remember the details of the animal-machine argument. So I reread it. Around the same time I've been working with large language models and reading the debates about whether they understand anything. And I noticed something that keeps nagging me.

Descartes' move, as I remember it from the text, is that animals have no language, and that their inability to use words to express thoughts shows they have no thought at all. He says something like their cries and movements are just mechanical reactions, and he treats the absence of language as decisive evidence against animal minds. Then in the LLM debates, people often say the opposite in a weird way: a system can produce language fluently while still understanding nothing, so the presence of language is not evidence of a mind. So Descartes uses the lack of language to deny thought, and contemporary AI skeptics use the presence of language to deny thought. The structure looks inverted but maybe the underlying move is the same: both treat language as the thing that would settle the question, and both use it to reject an interior mental life where someone is tempted to project one.

What I'm trying to figure out is whether this is a genuine structural parallel or just a superficial pattern-match. If it is deep, does anything transfer? For example, does the LLM critique show that Descartes' test was never sufficient even for animals, because a machine can pass a language test without understanding? Or does Descartes' argument show that the LLM skeptics need something more than just "it's only predicting the next word," since he at least required a specific capacity that animals lacked?
