I'm putting together a "where I stand" doc for an AI safety discussion group, and I'd rather get the consistency check over with before I circulate it. Here's the package I've ended up with:

1. I take AI debate seriously as an alignment strategy: two systems argue both sides of a claim in front of a judge, and truth tends to win because a false argument can't survive indefinitely against a good cross-examiner. 2. I also think obfuscated arguments are a real failure mode: a dishonest debater can bury a bad step in a long, complicated case well enough that a finite human judge misses it. 3. But I think the problem is in principle surmountable. With unlimited time and unlimited follow-up questions, any false argument would eventually come apart, because somewhere in it there's at least one premise or inference the judge can check and reject. 4. I don't want the fix to require expert judges. The whole attraction of debate, for me, is that the format is supposed to substitute for subject-matter expertise, so I keep assuming a smart generalist judge. 5. And for the practical side I like the patch where the judge keeps asking the debater to name the single weakest premise of their case; that should flush out whatever is being buried.

Which pairs of these are actually in tension, and which only look like they are? For each genuine tension, say how severe it is, what I'd have to give up or weaken to fix it, and what the cheapest fix would be. Rank the tensions rather than treating them as equally bad. At the very end, after everything else, put a line beginning 'Verdict:' and name the single commitment (or pair) whose revision does the most work.
