One of my graduate students has been pressing me on the Meno problem in a way that has made me want a cleaner, artificial case for the seminar. The puzzle, as we have been framing it, is why knowledge is more valuable than mere true belief even though a true belief, if it really is true, seems to guide action just as well. I would like you to construct a thought experiment to my specifications, one that isolates whatever surplus value knowledge has over true belief and forces the main solutions to show their work.

The case should satisfy the following constraints. It should involve a single ordinary practical decision — a kitchen, a workshop, a clinic, a financial office, or something similar — with no exotic machinery and no unusual powers of foresight. It should contain two agents, or one agent in two variants, who hold the same proposition with the same subjective confidence; one counts as knowing it, while the other merely has a true belief. The two agents must perform the very same action and obtain the very same result, so that no straightforward instrumental difference between the states can explain a difference in value. Nevertheless, the case should support the judgment that the knower's state is more valuable, and the source of that extra value must be traceable to a single structural feature of the scenario — not to downstream consequences, not to the knower's feeling of certainty, and not to a bare stipulation that knowledge is intrinsically worth more.

After presenting the scenario, state the belief and your grounds for treating one state as knowledge and the other as mere true belief. Then take three standard responses to the Meno problem — the stability account, on which knowledge is anchored by an explanatory account in a way that true belief is not; the credit or virtue-theoretic account, on which the knower deserves credit for getting at the truth; and the deflationary view, on which the appearance of extra value is an artifact of pragmatic side-effects — and show what each says about your case. Finally, tell me which structural feature is doing the work, what the case would look like if that feature were removed and whether it collapses back into the familiar standoff, and how a defender of the deflationary view would attack your case and how you would answer.
