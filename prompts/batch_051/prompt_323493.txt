For a metaethics seminar I'm in, we've been going back and forth on whether morality is a matter of principles or of case-by-case sensitivity, and I got asked to write up my actual commitments so the others can stress-test them. I keep saying different things depending on the week, but here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) I'm a particularist about rightness: I don't think there are any true, finitely statable moral principles that sort all cases, and what's right depends on the whole configuration of the situation.
(2) I think holism about reasons is true: the very same feature (say, that some remark would be a lie) can be a reason against doing it in one context, a reason in favor in another, or no reason at all in a third.
(3) I think (2) is basically the strongest argument for (1): if features flip valence like that, fixed principles that mention the feature seem like a nonstarter.
(4) I think moral education is a real thing and that it mostly works by passing along rough generalizations like "don't lie" and "keep your promises," even though nobody treats them as exceptionless.
(5) I'm drawn to a Ross-style picture of prima facie duties as a pretty good summary of how the morally relevant features stack up, as long as we don't absolutize them.
(6) I think reflective equilibrium is the right method for fixing moral views, including meta-level ones like these.
(7) I think there are moral experts, and part of what an expert has is more and better generalizations about how situations tend to go, morally speaking.

Do these hang together? I genuinely can't locate the strain, and I'd rather you tell me than nudge me. Please go through the pairs and discriminate: which commitments are in real tension, which only look tense, and which are fine. For each real tension, say how severe it is, which views clash, and what the least costly revision would be — whether a particularist has a clean reply, or whether something else in my list has to give. Then tell me which single revision of the list would buy the most coherence, and what it costs.

For the format, use these sections in this order: "The audit" (about a third: a table or compact list, one verdict per pair — stable, apparent tension, or real tension), "Real tensions, ranked" (about 40%: severity, the clash, what would have to give and at what cost), "Apparent tensions" (about 15%: pairs that look bad but dissolve, and why), and "The cheapest fix" (the remainder: one revision and its price).
