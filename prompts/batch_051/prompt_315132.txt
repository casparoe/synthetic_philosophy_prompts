In my Philosophy of Religion course, our professor repeatedly settles debates about whether the lack of evidence for God's existence counts against theism by reciting "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence" as though it closes the question. Last week I argued that, given how much searching and theological reflection humanity has done, the fact that we have found no positive evidence for God should itself count as at least weak evidence that there is no God, and that the burden of proof therefore shifts to the theist. The professor replied only with that slogan and moved on.

I want to know what precise claims could be packed into that sentence. Is it the empty platitude that a particular absence of evidence is not itself a particular piece of evidence, or is it the substantive principle that a failed search never changes any probabilities, or is it something in between? Lay out each plausible reading, from the trivial to the radical, and assess each: which are platitudinous, which are substantive but defensible, and which are false. In particular, distinguish readings on which the slogan makes a claim about evidential support from readings on which it is a norm about who must go first in a dispute, and say which reading, if any, would actually license the professor's dismissal of my argument — and which readings would leave my argument standing.
