In a reading group I attend, the dispute over Quinn's self-torturer has stalled in a way I find instructive but frustrating, and I would like a diagnosis of the stall rather than a verdict on the example itself.

The case, at least as we have been using it, runs as follows. A person is fitted with a device that has many settings, numbered from 0 upward. The difference in pain between one setting and the next is so small that the agent cannot perceive it at all; each move upward brings a small but genuine sum of money. At setting 0 he is comfortable, and at the highest setting the pain is severe and unremitting. Presented with the whole sequence as a single offer, he would refuse the highest setting in exchange for the accumulated money. Yet at each individual step it seems rational for him to accept the reward and move one setting higher: the increase in pain is imperceptible, the payment is real, and the next setting feels exactly like the one before. Thus there is a chain of pairwise rational choices whose endpoint he would reject if offered directly.

Everyone in the group accepts this description, and everyone agrees that the agent should not end up at the highest setting. The disagreement is entirely about what lesson to draw, and neither camp has moved for several months.

One camp reads the example as a counterexample to the transitivity of rational preference. Strictly speaking, it cannot matter to the agent whether he is at one setting or the next, so no adjacent preference can be faulted; yet he prefers setting 0 to the highest setting; therefore the relation of rational preference need not be transitive, and the usual money-pump and representation-theorem arguments must be resisted or reinterpreted. The other camp replies that the case is misdescribed. The agent's inability to tell adjacent settings apart is a fact about perceptual discrimination, not about what he prefers; he may be indifferent between neighbours, or he may be failing to choose a plan over a scattered sequence, but nothing here shows that a coherent set of preferences can be intransitive. On their view, transitivity remains a conceptual constraint on a single preference ordering, and the example teaches something about sorites-like reasoning rather than about rationality.

Each side can predict the other's replies, and neither can say what would force it to revise its position. My working hypothesis is that the two camps are answering different questions under one heading: one is taking 'preference' to be whatever pairwise choice behaviour reveals, while the other is taking it to be a global comparative attitude that imposes coherence by definition. But I am not confident, and I would like the impasse examined from the outside.

What kind of disagreement is this, really? Is it substantive, verbal, or a collision of two different questions that happen to share a vocabulary? Is a hidden empirical question about discriminability or a hidden formal question about the representation of preference doing more work than either side acknowledges? Given your diagnosis, would anything — a distinction, a formal result, a differently constructed case — count as progress, or is this a stalemate we should expect to persist?
