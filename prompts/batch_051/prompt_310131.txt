I am a novelist, and while researching a book set around the turn of the last century, I have kept noticing a pattern in the way kinds of people are classified, and I want to know whether it has a name and, more importantly, how a philosopher would draw its boundaries. A category comes into use for some group — the juvenile delinquent, the gifted child, the hysteric — and the officials or experts who use it believe they are simply describing people who were already there, waiting to be correctly or incorrectly picked out. But the people who get picked out are not inert. They read the description, or feel its effects, and they answer back: they lean into the label, resist it, exaggerate it, negotiate its terms, or organize around it.

Within a generation the category's criteria have drifted, because the kind of person it was meant to pick out has changed partly because that very picking-out happened. The classifiers then revise the definition to catch up, which gives the newly classified people something new to respond to, and the whole thing starts again. I can see that this is adjacent to a self-fulfilling prophecy and to the familiar idea that a belief about a situation can become true in its consequences, but those are one-way stories: a false belief gets made true, and there the matter rests.

What I am after is the ongoing, two-way revision — the classified and the classifiers adjusting to each other through successive rounds — and what separates that process from a mere self-fulfilling prophecy on one side, and from an ordinary drift in the meaning of a word on the other. Does it require that the people know they are being classified, or can it operate through institutions they never see directly? I care less about acquiring the right label than about knowing exactly where the edges of the phenomenon are: what belongs to it, what is only adjacent to it, and why.
