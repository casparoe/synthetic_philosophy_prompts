I cover academic philosophy and the sciences for a general-interest magazine, and last month I sat in on a two-day colloquium about Augustine's treatment of time in Book XI of the Confessions. One session was built around a passage late in that discussion: Augustine argues, in rough paraphrase, that it is in the mind rather than in the motion of the heavens that he measures times; what he measures is the impression or affection that passing things leave behind, which remains after the things themselves have passed; and time is ultimately a stretching-out or distension of the mind itself, as the mind expects the future, attends to the present, and remembers the past. During a break I overheard two participants argue about what that passage actually commits Augustine to. One was a philosopher of mind who works on contemporary debates about time; the other was a scholar of late antique Latin thought.

The scholar said the passage is about the measurement of time, not the existence of time. Augustine begins by rejecting the idea that time just is the motion of celestial bodies, because motion can be fast or slow and needs a time in which to occur. His positive claim is that when we judge a length, we are judging an affection left in memory, not the moving body itself. That is a psychological or epistemological thesis. He never denies that creation has a real before-and-after order independent of our measuring it.

The philosopher of mind disagreed. He said Augustine spends the earlier chapters arguing that the past is no longer and the future is not yet, and when he finally locates time in the mind, he is answering the metaphysical question. The past and future exist only as the mind's present act of remembering and expecting, so time itself—which includes past and future—has its being from the mind. Without a mind there would be no time, only perhaps the bare present instant or eternity. That is a metaphysical claim, and it is what makes Confessions XI interesting to contemporary philosophy.

The scholar answered that treating the phrase about the past and future as a metaphysical doctrine is anachronistic. Augustine is working within a long skeptical tradition that says past and future cannot be measured because they do not exist. His solution is that they are present in the mind, so we can measure them. But the sequence of events in creation remains real. The mind does not create the sequence; it distends in order to grasp it.

The philosopher of mind replied that the scholar was softening a stronger text. Augustine says, still in my paraphrase, that the three times exist in the soul and nowhere else, and he calls time itself a distension of the mind. If time itself is a distension of the mind, then the mind is not merely measuring an outer sequence; it is constituting the temporal order. The philosopher then asked what, on the scholar's reading, the term time refers to once we subtract the mind's activity.

The scholar came back with Augustine's example of the sound of a syllable: the sound is real, it begins and ends, and the mind measures it by holding its beginning and end together. The measurement is mental, but the boundary between before and after is not. Augustine's target is our experience of duration, not the existence of temporal relations in the world.

I found myself unsure which of them was reading the text more fairly, and I suspect both were partly right and partly overreading. Before you settle on a verdict, think through the passage and each stage of the argument slowly. First reconstruct what the passage establishes with relative certainty and what it leaves open. Then go through the exchange move by move, identifying where each speaker is on solid textual or philosophical ground, where each is importing modern assumptions or overextending the evidence, and where they are talking past one another. Finally say which reading is on balance better supported, and whether the more accurate statement of Augustine's position is something neither speaker quite formulated.
