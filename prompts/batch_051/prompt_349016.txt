I serve on the board of a small civic forum whose meeting hall is governed by a constitution drafted in 1951. Article IV, Section 2, which has never been amended, reads: "No member shall be denied the floor at any meeting, and no organization shall be refused the use of the hall, because the Society or its officers disapprove of the opinions to be expressed at that meeting. This guarantee extends to unpopular, dissenting, and minority opinion, and shall be read broadly." The provision was adopted after a period in which the hall's previous management had barred speakers for their politics, and everyone understands it as the centerpiece of the institution's openness.

A newly formed organization has now applied to use the hall for a public organizing meeting. Its published program is to win control of the town council and then repeal Article IV, Section 2 in favor of a policy allowing the Society to exclude organizations it deems divisive; it is candid that it wants our hall specifically as a platform from which to advocate abolishing our open-hall guarantee. In the meantime, its organizers have announced that they will publish the home addresses of board members who publicly oppose them, so that supporters can register their views in person. Several members of our board, including me, would almost certainly be on that list. The group insists that its application cannot be refused under the clause, and that it is exactly the sort of unpopular opinion the clause was written to protect.

The board is split. One faction says the words are absolute: a political opinion, however destructive of the Society's rules, is still an opinion, and the 1951 drafters deliberately said "unpopular, dissenting, and minority opinion." Another faction says the clause was meant to shield dissenters from censorship, not to require the Society to hand its only platform to a campaign whose goal is to end that openness and whose methods include targeted visits to members' homes—a combination the drafters plainly were not imagining. We need to decide, not merely whether the clause technically reaches this group, but what the main approaches to interpretation each require here: the plain words, the purpose of the provision, what the drafters would have said had they been asked about a group organized around repealing the guarantee itself, and what a reasonable member of the Society today would expect the clause to mean. Which of those should control, and why? Is there a way to deny the room to this group without collapsing the guarantee for genuinely unpopular opinion in general? And if a faithful reading licenses a campaign to abolish the very provision it rests on, what does that tell us about what an unconditional open-platform rule can coherently promise—and how the paradox of tolerance should be handled by an institution that wants to remain open without becoming an instrument for those who would close it?
