I'm giving a talk next month at a workshop my department co-runs with the law school, on digital preservation, and I'm defending a position I've been developing on abandoned software. The thesis, roughly: when a piece of software's rightsholder can't be located after a documented good-faith search—public notice, registries, contacting companies that bought or licensed it, asking the community—archives, libraries, and preservationists should get a safe harbor to make and share preservation copies, offer noncommercial access, migrate or repair the code, and bypass DRM where there's no other way; if the owner later turns up, they can collect a reasonable license fee but not statutory infringement damages, and the software gets listed in a public registry as orphaned so no one has to redo the search. My argument is that copyright is supposed to either incentivize creation or eventually feed the public domain, and ownerless software does neither; that physical property law has abandonment and adverse possession for exactly this kind of stale claim; and that software is uniquely perishable—bit rot, obsolete formats, dead compilers—so the cost of doing nothing isn't just lost access but lost artifacts.

The audience is about two dozen people: mostly law school copyright scholars and IP practitioners, two software preservation archivists, one philosopher who works on the ethics of ownership and abandonment, and a few grad students. They're not hostile to preservation, but they care a lot about procedure, property stability, and incentives, and several are skeptical that not enforcing equals not owning. I've run the idea passed a colleague who does open-source licensing and she said I'm reinventing the orphan works problem with extra steps, which I think is fair but also kind of the point.
