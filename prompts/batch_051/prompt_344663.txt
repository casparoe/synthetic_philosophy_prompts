I sit on the ordination committee of a Protestant denomination whose governing documents affirm that the church stands on an apostolic foundation, but whose formal ordination standards are silent on whether the extraordinary spiritual gifts — prophecy, tongues, and healing — continue after the apostolic age. The denomination’s practice has nonetheless been quietly cessationist for generations. We must now fill a single seminary teaching post and have narrowed the pool to two finalists. One is a self-described cessationist who teaches that miraculous gifts ceased at the close of the apostolic era and that contemporary claims to them are at best category errors and at worst self-deception or fraud. The other is a self-described continuationist who reports having prophesied and prayed for healing in congregational settings, and says he would continue to do so if appointed. Half the committee argues that appointing the continuationist would repudiate established practice and expose the denomination to unaccountable charismatic claims; the other half argues that making cessationism a condition of office would turn a contested historical and exegetical position into a test of fellowship the denomination has never formally adopted. Please draft the short written memo I will circulate before next month’s vote. It should recommend a concrete action — appoint the cessationist only, the continuationist only, both, or defer the appointment pending a study commission — with reasons, a timeline we can execute against while the post remains vacant, and criteria future committees can apply. It should also make explicit how much of this disagreement is actually about history, biblical interpretation, the epistemology of testimony, or pastoral prudence, and it should engage the strongest consideration on the losing side.
