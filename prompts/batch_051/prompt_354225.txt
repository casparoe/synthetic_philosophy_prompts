I'm in a political theory course on Greek and Roman constitutional thought, and we've spent the last couple of seminars on Polybius, Histories book 6. The anacyclosis part keeps bugging me. Earlier he distinguishes three sound forms of government—kingship, aristocracy, democracy—and three corrupt forms—tyranny, oligarchy, mob-rule—and says they succeed one another in a regular sequence. Then, near the end of that chapter, he writes (this is the old Shuckburgh translation we're using):

"This is the regular cycle of constitutional revolutions, and the natural order in which constitutions change, are transformed, and return again to their original stage. If a man have a clear grasp of these principles he may perhaps make a mistake as to the dates at which this or that will happen to a particular constitution; but he will rarely be entirely mistaken as to the stage of growth or decay at which it has arrived, or as to the point at which it will undergo some revolutionary change. However, it is in the case of the Roman constitution that this method of inquiry will most fully teach us its formation, its growth, and zenith, as well as the changes awaiting it in the future; for this, if any constitution ever did, owed, as I said just now, its original foundation and growth to natural causes, and to natural causes will owe its decay."

What I'm stuck on is this. He says this is "the natural order," and that someone who grasps these principles will rarely be wrong about the stage or the next revolutionary change. If I take him at his word, doesn't that mean the only serious Polybian way to talk about a present-day democracy is to classify where it sits in the loop and then wait for its predictable degradation into mob-rule and finally one-man rule? It sounds like he's saying reform is beside the point, not because the forces are too strong but because the decay just is the natural course of the constitution. I know he goes on to praise Rome's mixed constitution as something special, but the passage says Rome owed its foundation, growth, and decay to natural causes too. So am I supposed to read this as a universal predictive cycle I should be applying to the politics around me, or is there something in his view of simple versus mixed constitutions that should stop me from using him that way? I honestly can't tell if I'm overreading him or if my professor is soft-pedaling how deterministic Polybius really is.
