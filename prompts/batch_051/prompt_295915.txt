I want to get clear about a standard of success in one area before I commit to a position in it. The area is hinge epistemology: the family of views inspired by Wittgenstein's On Certainty, according to which some commitments function not as ordinary, evidence-responsive beliefs but as the more or less fixed points against which evidence, doubt, and justification get their sense. Treat this as a specification exercise: rather than arguing for one account, set out the requirements that any adequate theory of hinge commitments must meet.

Please produce the full desiderata list. For each requirement, give the cases or phenomena that force it, explain why it is a requirement rather than a negotiating position, and indicate which requirements you regard as non-negotiable and which might be artifacts of a particular reading of On Certainty or of one philosophical camp's agenda. At least work through the following dimensions, though the list need not stop there:

- Role/content switching. The same content — for instance, the presence of one's own hands, or the claim that the earth has existed for a very long time — can apparently function as an ordinary empirical claim in some contexts and as a hinge in others. A theory must explain how this shift works, or else deny the appearance and say what is actually going on.
- Groundlessness without arbitrariness. Hinges are supposed to be held without being justified by evidence, but not simply chosen or arbitrary. A theory must characterize this middle status.
- Change and loss. People lose commitments that once functioned as hinges, through conversion, personal upheaval, or the slow transformation of a community's world-picture. If hinges are not answerable to evidence, the theory must explain how change is possible and what kind of process it is.
- Cross-world-picture disagreement. Different communal or personal world-pictures seem to contain incompatible hinges — for instance, religious and secular pictures of the world and of human life. A theory must say how two parties can both be rational, or in what way at least one fails, without collapsing into dogmatism or into an "anything goes" relativism.
- The social and developmental character of hinges. Hinges appear to be acquired through upbringing and embedded in practices, institutions, or forms of life rather than derived individually from evidence; a theory must account for this inheritance and for the difference between pervasive hinges and practice-local ones.
- Animal versus propositional certainty. On Certainty suggests a distinction between an enacted, animal certainty and a certainty that is articulated in propositions. A theory must say which is more basic, and which side of this distinction the term "hinge commitment" properly belongs to.
- The relation to knowledge. Many things treated as hinges are things we would ordinarily say we know: one's own name, the existence of the external world, the great age of the earth. A theory must explain either why they are not known in the ordinary sense, or how the philosophical category and ordinary knowledge claims fit together.
- The anti-skeptical burden. These notions are usually meant to diagnose radical skeptical doubt as idle, incoherent, or self-undermining. A theory must do that work without also ruling out ordinary, local doubt inside empirical inquiry.

After the list, please say which single requirement does the most work in excluding the standard ways of elaborating hinges — for example, treating them as a kind of non-evidential entitlement, as non-propositional animal certainty, as a form of practical trust, or as ordinary empirical beliefs — and say why. Then flag any subset of the requirements that looks jointly unsatisfiable: if a tension exists between two or more of them, state it precisely rather than dissolving it by fiat. A table is fine.
