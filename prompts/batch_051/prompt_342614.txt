I work as a software engineer and have no formal background in philosophy, but I have been reading John Locke's Second Treatise of Government because I am trying to settle a question about my own work. In Chapter V, Section 30, Locke writes:

Thus this law of reason makes the deer that Indian's who hath killed it; it is allowed to be his goods, who hath bestowed his labour upon it, though before it was the common right of every one. And amongst those who are counted the civilized part of mankind, who have made and multiplied positive laws to determine property, this original law of nature, for the beginning of property, in what was before common, still takes place; and by virtue thereof, what fish any one catches in the ocean, that great and still remaining common of mankind; or what ambergrise any one takes up here, is by the labour that removes it out of that common state nature left it in, made his property, who takes that pains about it. And even amongst us, the hare that any one is hunting, is thought his who pursues her during the chase: for being a beast that is still looked upon as common, and no man's private possession; whoever has employed so much labour about any thing of that kind, as to find and pursue her, has thereby removed her from the state of nature, wherein she was common, and hath begun a property.

The case I want to test this against is one Locke could not have imagined. I have built a system that continuously crawls publicly accessible web pages, extracts their plain text, removes near-duplicate passages, and uses the resulting corpus to train a language model. The pages are not behind paywalls or login walls, and my copying leaves the originals untouched and freely available to anyone else. I am now considering licensing the trained model and the curated dataset as my property. On one hand, I have invested substantial labour in selecting pages, writing the cleaning pipeline, and training the model; the model and dataset would not exist without that work. On the other hand, the object I copied was not, strictly speaking, removed from the common in the way Locke describes: every other person can still read the same pages, and a rival could easily collect them. The passage makes removal from the common state seem central, but the purpose of Locke's argument seems to be that a person should own what their labour has made useful. I am unsure which of those readings, if any, supports property in the model weights or the dataset.

I would like you to explain what the passage, read in its own terms, requires or permits in this case. In particular, give me the verdicts of the text-first reading, the purpose-first reading, and the reading that asks what Locke would probably have said if the case had been described to him, and then tell me which of those readings is most faithful to Locke's actual argument and whether it yields property in the model, the dataset, neither, or both.
