I want to work through a tension I've never been able to resolve cleanly. On one side, I'm drawn to a view I first met in Edmund Burke's Reflections on the Revolution in France, understood not as a political program but as a claim about where knowledge lives. The passage that arrested me says, in effect, that we should be wary of letting each person act on his own small private reserve of judgment, because that reserve is inadequate; he suggests instead that individuals draw on a common accumulated capital of reflection that nations and ages have stored up. That idea exerts a strong pull on me as a software engineer. I've watched a production service survive a novel failure mode because of an old check nobody on the team could explain; the original author was gone, the docs were stale, but the check encoded a real incident. It was Chesterton's fence as a deploy gate: don't tear out a safeguard until you know what it was guarding. Generalizing from this, it seems plausible that inherited rules and institutions are a kind of compressed, distributed memory—a capital of stored justifications no single maintainer can reconstruct from first principles. That is the theory I find compelling.

What clashes with it is a firm intuition I also cannot shake: an explanation is not the same as an after-the-fact rationalization, and a rule's having been useful to someone at some point is not enough to keep it. In software, requirement drift is real. A module can encode a decision that was correct for one user population, one scaling assumption, one failure profile, and be wrong now. I have a visceral sense that if nobody can state why a rule exists, the right move is to treat it as suspect—demand a reason or remove it—not to defer to it. Otherwise "the past is a bank" becomes unfalsifiable: every arbitrary rule, every piece of cargo-cult ceremony, every embarrassing legacy constraint gets defense by default. That seems to flip the burden of proof in a way I find both intellectually and practically dangerous.

So the question is which side should give way. Is my engineering intuition just a product of working in a domain where environments change fast and feedback is measurable, and does it therefore fail to transfer to social institutions? Or is Burke's rhetoric about a general capital of ages a way of laundering path dependence and raw inheritance into a false halo of wisdom? Give me the strongest version of each position, then give me a negotiated verdict—what each side would have to concede, and whether there is a reading on which they are compatible. I'm less interested in which side you'd choose than in what would justify each possible move.
