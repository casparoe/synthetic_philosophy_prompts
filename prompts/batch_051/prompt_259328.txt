I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am working on a piece about whether the distinction between original and derived intentionality has any empirical content. The distinction, as I usually see it drawn, is this: the words in a book, the symbols inside a computer, and the colors on a stop sign mean something only because someone interprets them or assigns them a meaning, whereas a human being's beliefs and desires are supposed to mean something in their own right, not in virtue of anyone else's interpretation. I have read enough of the philosophical back-and-forth to suspect that both sides are mostly drawing lines on paper, and I want to know how much of the claim could be made into something testable. Please treat it for the moment as a factual claim about the causal structure of a system rather than as a piece of metaphysics: a system has original intentionality just in case at least some of its representational states get their content from the system's own activity rather than from the interpretive or design choices of some other agent. Now design the strongest empirical test you can of whether a given system is original in this sense, as opposed to merely carrying derived content. Be concrete about the setup, the comparison condition, and the measurement. Say which outcome would count as evidence for a real original–derived divide, which outcome would count against it, and then say precisely what even the cleanest result could not settle, for example whether a system that passes your test might merely be mining distinctions already latent in its training data or its evolutionary history, and whether the word original would still earn its keep after the test is run. I am less interested in a final verdict than in a clear picture of what a test could and could not do here. Write your answer as continuous prose, the way you would write a careful email or letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
