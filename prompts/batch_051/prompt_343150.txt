I'm trying to work out what I actually think about loyalty, whistleblowing, and the ethics of betrayal by going case by case, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, my intuitions add up to. A nurse finds that a colleague has been charting medications as given when they were not, and she reports it through the hospital's internal safety system; I think she did exactly right and would have been wrong to keep quiet. An employee discovers her company has been concealing a serious safety defect in a product, tries internal channels until they are exhausted, and then gives the documents to a reporter; I think she did the right thing even though she broke her confidentiality agreement, and I admire her for it. A junior staffer leaks classified documents about a surveillance program he believes is illegal, going straight to the press without trying any internal avenue; I'm genuinely torn, but I lean toward saying he was justified if the program really was illegal, while remaining uneasy that he alone got to make that call. A campaign aide who signed a nondisclosure agreement publishes a tell-all revealing the candidate's ugly private remarks from strategy meetings; I think that was a betrayal, even though voters would have wanted to know. A soldier reports his unit for abusing detainees; I think he was obligated to, though it feels like betraying comrades.

On the more personal side: a friend discovers that another friend is having an affair and tells the betrayed spouse; I think she should not have done it, it wasn't her place, and it wrecked the friendship. A husband discovers his wife has hidden a gambling addiction that has drained their shared savings, and he tells her parents so the family can stage an intervention; I think he did the right thing, because the secrecy was itself part of the harm and the disclosure was aimed at helping her. An employee, without confronting him first, reports a coworker to HR for padding expense reports by small amounts; I think that was officious and wrong, though I can't quite say why it differs from the nurse's case. A defense-contractor engineer discovers systematic overbilling and files a sealed lawsuit through the legal whistleblower channel that lets him share in the recovery; I think that's fine, even admirable, because the real victim was the public and there was no other realistic way to stop it.

The principle I keep reaching for is roughly this: breaking a confidence is permitted to the degree that the hidden matter involves substantial, ongoing harm to identifiable victims, the disclosure is aimed primarily at stopping that harm or helping those victims, and the person has no reasonable, less damaging route, and it is least permitted when the disclosure mainly serves to punish, expose for its own sake, or settle a private score. But that principle doesn't obviously deliver my verdicts: it would seem to license the friend who told the betrayed spouse, and it would probably condemn the campaign aide less harshly than I do. I'd like you to state the best principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures these verdicts, say precisely which clause does which work, tell me which of my verdicts, if any, it forces me to drop, and then apply it to a case I keep going back and forth on: a journalist who has been protecting a confidential source's identity for years discovers that the source fabricated evidence in an unrelated story long ago, and must decide whether to keep protecting that source, who is currently supplying true information about a corrupt public official.
