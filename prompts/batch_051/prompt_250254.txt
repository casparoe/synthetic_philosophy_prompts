I am a novelist, not a philosopher. I am writing a novel about a woman who has to decide whether to protect a colleague in a small professional community, and I have been using a distinction I invented to keep the moral texture of my characters precise. Before I let it quietly structure the book, I want a professional assessment of it.

I call it the mutuality condition on loyalty. My claim is this: partiality toward a group counts as loyalty — rather than as mere bias, favoritism, or tribalism — only when it is embedded in a scheme of mutual partiality. More exactly, an agent A's special concern for a group G is loyalty only if two conditions hold. First, reciprocity: the members of G, or G as an organized body, would extend comparable special concern to A in relevantly similar circumstances. Second, endorsement: A would regard that reversed partiality as appropriate, something A could accept from the other side, rather than as an imposition or a corrupt favor.

On my account, the difference between loyalty and bias is not the intensity, duration, or sincerity of the feeling, and not whether the agent benefits. It is whether the partiality is bilateral in this specific way. Loyalty is mutual partiality; bias is unilateral partiality. The account is meant only to settle whether an instance of partiality deserves the name "loyalty" with its positive connotations, not whether the act is right all things considered: two people can be loyal to each other in covering up an embezzlement and still be doing something wrong on other grounds.

An example from the novel: a senior editor quietly routes a manuscript contract toward a friend who has been out of work. The condition asks whether the friend would do the same for her, and whether she would accept the friend's doing it. If yes, I write it as loyalty, possibly misguided but structurally different from cronyism. If no — if the flow of special concern is one-way, or she would resent the reversal — I write it as favoritism wearing a loyalty costume. I also find myself wanting to draw a corollary: loyalty to large, diffuse collectives such as a nation or a party is usually bias rather than loyalty, because an abstract collective cannot stand in a scheme of mutual partiality with an individual; the reciprocity is at best symbolic. I suspect that corollary is where the view gets into trouble.

What I want is an honest assessment. Is the mutuality condition coherent as stated? Does it already exist in the literature under another name? I know the broad debates over partiality, special obligations, and associative duties, but I do not know whether this exact reciprocity condition has been worked out. What does it get right, and where does it break? Please give me the strongest objections you can, and say which, if any, are fatal to the distinction rather than merely uncomfortable for it.

One further request about method. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this topic — for example, between what moral philosophers largely agree on, what social psychologists have robustly established about ingroup favoritism, and what remains a live dispute. Do not present a contested reading as the consensus, and flag any place where my view leans on a contested premise.

Please approach this as a professional moral philosopher who works on loyalty, partiality, and special obligations, and who also knows the empirical literature on ingroup bias well enough to keep the two registers apart. Before you evaluate, reconstruct the strongest version of my account and name the nearest existing positions; then turn to the objections. Think carefully about the exact wording of the two conditions, and if my statement of them is ambiguous, say which reading you are assessing.
