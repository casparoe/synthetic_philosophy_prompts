I write fiction for a living, and in arguments about public funding for the arts I keep hearing my own craft defended with an analogy I have never quite believed. The analogy is that reading literary fiction is like training in a flight simulator. The novel puts a person through difficult social and moral situations — a marriage failing, a character deciding whether to betray a friend, a stranger whose motives must be read from small signals — without making them pay the price of the real thing. The further claim is that, just as simulator hours make a pilot safer in an engine failure that has not yet happened to them, hours in novels make a person more practiced at noticing what other minds are doing, more fluent in imagining how a situation looks from inside, and readier to act sensibly when something analogous arrives in their own life. From this the conclusion is drawn that literature is a kind of social training infrastructure: a public good whose benefits are diffuse, slow, and hard to measure, and therefore exactly the kind of thing the state should fund the way it funds other safety-critical training.

I would like you to audit this analogy rather than restate it. Give me the mapping explicitly: what plays the role of the simulator, the aircraft, the weather, the instructor, the reset button, the crash, and the certification — that is, what in reading is supposed to provide the controlled danger, the feedback, the repetition, and the assessment. Then show where the mapping breaks. Be systematic about which features of a flight simulator are being quietly imported — validation against a known standard, a graded sequence of difficulty, someone correcting the trainee, a measurable transfer to real performance — and whether literary fiction actually supplies them. In particular, I want you to separate two jobs the analogy might be doing: training a perceptual skill, and changing what a person values or wishes to do; a simulator does the first, but many defenders of literature want the second as well. When you find disanalogies, distinguish the ones that threaten the intended conclusion from the ones that are cosmetic. Finish by telling me how much of the public-funding conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether some other analogy would carry the argument more honestly, or whether the argument would be stronger with the analogy dropped.
