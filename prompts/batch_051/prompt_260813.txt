A proposal I keep seeing floated for the deepfake problem, and I want someone to actually take it apart: the pitch is that it restores the old backstop where a recording could settle an argument. Every camera and microphone ships with a signing key burned in at the factory, and footage gets signed at the moment of capture with the hash, the device ID, and a rough timestamp (GPS only if you opt in). As soon as the file is edited, cropped, re-encoded, or moved through a lossy pipeline, it loses it's signature and becomes "unverified." Platforms have to keep hosting unverified media -- no takedowns -- but it gets a grey label and gets ranked behind signed stuff, while signed media gets a green "as-captured" badge. Courts mostly follow: signed footage gets a presumption of authenticity, unsigned footage doesn't unless the party offering it can lay extra foundation. Treat this as a machine to be gamed, not a position to cheer or boo. Walk me through how it would actually behave: who breaks it first and how, what it does to newsrooms, platforms, courts, protesters, and an authoritarian government trying to either exploit it or kill it, whether the green badge ends up meaning "true" rather than just "came out of a sensor," and which failure modes are structural rather than patchable. Give me the three most likely exploits inside the first year, and end with a one-sentence upshot on its own line as the very last line.
