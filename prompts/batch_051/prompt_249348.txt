I'm writing up my views on decision-making under risk and ignorance for a discussion group, and I want a consistency check before I present them. The position I've landed on is roughly this: (1) whenever there is a well-defined probability distribution, I'm a textbook expected-utility maximizer and I accept the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms and the independence axiom without much unease; (2) under complete ignorance, where the problem itself licenses no probabilities, I favor maximin over the principle of insufficient reason, because equal probabilities over states one knows nothing about look to me like manufactured information rather than real neutrality; (3) I think Harsanyi's impartial-observer argument is correct and important — if one does assign equal probability to occupying each person's position and then maximizes expected utility, one is pushed toward average utilitarianism, and that does tell us something real about impartial social choice; (4) nevertheless, I think Rawls was right that the original position should not assign equal probabilities to one's place in society, and the difference principle's concern for the worst-off seems better grounded than maximizing average utility under a uniform prior the veil never actually supplies; (5) in low-stakes practical choices under ignorance, I informally use minimax regret, because maximin ignores the shape of the non-worst outcomes and that feels wrong when the worst case is thoroughly bearable.

Which of these are genuinely in tension, and which only look that way? I'd like the real tensions ranked by severity, with the cheapest repair identified for each, and a closing judgment on which single revision would do the most to make the whole package cohere.
