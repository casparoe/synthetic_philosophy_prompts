The question I set was: Explain the difference between endurantism and perdurantism about persistence through time, and discuss which view gives a better account of the problem of temporary intrinsics.

Here's the student's answer:

Endurantism and perdurantism are two rival accounts of how things manage to exist over time. The endurantist says an object persists by being wholly present at every moment at which it exists. The person who showed up to last year's family reunion and the person reading this sentence are strictly one and the same object, with no temporal parts. The perdurantist denies this: objects are four-dimensional space-time worms made up of temporal parts, and at any particular time you only ever encounter a temporal stage of an object, not the whole object. Perdurantism clearly does better with the problem of temporary intrinsics. Suppose I am bent at noon and straight at six. The endurantist has to say that the very same object is both bent and straight, which are incompatible intrinsic properties, so the view lands in overt contradiction. The perdurantist avoids this cleanly: my noon-stage is bent, my six-o'clock-stage is straight, and since these are different objects, no contradiction arises. The endurantist can try to wriggle out by saying I am bent-at-noon and straight-at-six, but this is really just an ad hoc trick that turns straightforward intrinsic properties into relations to times, so it amounts to giving up genuine intrinsic shapes. On top of that, perdurantism is the natural partner of eternalism: if all times are equally real, then objects really are spread out through time the way they are spread out through space, which is just what perdurantism says. Endurantism, by contrast, only makes sense if presentism is true.
