In preparing a seminar on the ethics of scarcity, I have been trying to get clear about what I actually believe in a family of lifeboat cases, and I find that I cannot state the principle behind my own verdicts. I would value an audit rather than a lecture: treat each of the following as a fixed but revisable datum, and tell me which principle, or which small set of candidate principles, fits them best, which of them each candidate has to abandon, and which of my apparent conflicts with myself are not real conflicts once the principle is stated.

The cases and my verdicts are these.

1. A launch designed to carry eight is taking on water with twelve aboard and will sink unless four leave it. The passengers draw lots, and the four who draw short slips are made to enter the sea; those four die, and the remaining eight survive. I judge this permissible, provided the lottery was conducted fairly.

2. In the same situation, the passengers put the four weakest overboard instead of drawing lots, reasoning that the frailest are least likely to survive anyway and that the stronger rowers should remain. I judge this impermissible.

3. A lifeboat is at its safe limit of ten people when a swimmer from the wreck reaches it and grips the gunwale. Hauling him aboard would capsize the boat. One of the occupants pries the swimmer's fingers loose so that the boat can pull away; the swimmer drowns and the ten aboard survive. I judge the occupant's action impermissible.

4. In the same situation, no one touches the swimmer; the occupants simply do not take him aboard and row away. The swimmer drowns and the ten aboard survive. I judge their conduct permissible, though a source of lasting regret.

5. A hospital has one ventilator. It is in use by a patient whose chance of surviving the illness with the ventilator is about twenty percent; a second patient is then admitted whose chance with the ventilator is about eighty percent. The physician discontinues the first patient and gives the ventilator to the second. I judge this the right thing to do, notwithstanding that the first patient arrived earlier and was without fault.

6. Two patients need the last dose of an antidote, and either would have the same chance of surviving if given it. One of the two is the reckless person whose illegal disregard of safety rules caused the accident in which both were injured; the other is an innocent bystander. The physician gives the dose to the bystander because the bystander is innocent. I judge this wrong: the dose should have been allocated without regard to fault, by a fair tie-break such as a lottery.

7. Return to the overcrowded launch of case 1, but now the damage that is sinking the launch was caused by one passenger's reckless mishandling of the stove. When it becomes clear that four must leave, the other passengers put that passenger into the sea before drawing lots for the remaining three places. I judge this permissible; indeed, I think it the fair thing to do.

I do not know whether one principle can deliver all seven verdicts, or whether one or two of them must be revised. Please do three things. First, state each candidate principle precisely and say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate except by an ad hoc qualification; be candid about whether a distinction is doing independent work or is introduced only to save the verdicts. Second, separate the real tensions in this set from the merely apparent ones. Third, apply the principle to a variant on which I have no settled view: the swimmer at the gunwale in case 3 is the passenger whose reckless mishandling of the stove caused the launch to take on water. What does the principle say about prying his fingers loose, and does that prediction force any revision among my stated verdicts?
