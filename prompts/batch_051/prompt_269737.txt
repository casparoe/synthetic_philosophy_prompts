I spent a decade in philosophy of science working on the realism debate, and I am thoroughly fluent in its conceptual machinery: the observable/unobservable distinction, constructive empiricism, entity and structural realism, underdetermination, inference to the best explanation, and the standing disagreement about whether explanation outruns description. I now work in AI and follow the interpretability literature closely. I keep noticing that researchers there divide into realists and instrumentalists about the features, circuits, and directions they recover from trained networks, and that the two sides talk past each other in ways that feel familiar from my old field but do not map cleanly onto it.

Restate, in the vocabulary of scientific realism, the debate over whether trained neural networks genuinely have representations. Keep the translation honest: when the vocabulary forces a position into a mold it did not choose, say so rather than silently rounding it off.

Translate at least these positions separately. The realist side holds that some recovered components are causal constituents of the network's computation: they support counterfactual claims under activation patching (intervening on a unit's activity and tracking the downstream change), predict behavior on held-out or interpolated inputs, and transfer across contexts, and this is enough to call them real regardless of how their human-readable labels fare. The instrumentalist or eliminativist side holds that these components are artifacts of the methods used to find them: probes and autoencoder-based decompositions find directions because they are optimized to find them; polysemanticity and superposition (many concepts stored in a shared, lower-dimensional arrangement) suggest there are no clean representational joints; and different defensible methods yield different decompositions of the same network. On this view, the recovered structures are useful summaries for prediction and control, not facts about what the network computes.

For each side, give its best translation into scientific-realism terms — entity realism, structural realism, constructive empiricism, underdetermination, inference to the best explanation, or a mixture — and say whether the translation is faithful or whether something in the ML position gets distorted to fit.

Then produce the residue report. Which commitments translate cleanly, which translate only with fudging, and which have no expression at all in the scientific-realism vocabulary? In particular, diagnose whether the ML dispute is just a special case of the realism debate, a different debate that happens to use the same words, or a debate whose real crux concerns something the realism vocabulary never touches — for example, what it means to explain a system we built and trained rather than one we discovered, or whether a network's internals, which are in one sense fully observed, can still be present to us only through interpretability methods. End with a verdict on the residue: does it show that the ML side is missing distinctions philosophy of science has already worked out, or that the philosophy-of-science vocabulary is missing something specific to artifacts whose internals are both known and illegible?
