There is a familiar view that it cannot be a bad thing to be as morally good as possible. If morality is what we owe to one another, then a person who always acts as she morally ought, who weighs every claim impartially, and who refuses to let private preference interfere with doing the most good would seem to be the best kind of person there is. But there is also a persistent worry that such a person—call her a moral saint—might turn out to be strangely impoverished: she could lose her sense of humor, her aesthetic delight, her capacity for absorbing and lopsided friendships, and perhaps even the ability to love particular people as particular people rather than as instances of general moral categories. The question is whether maximal moral goodness is an ideal we should actually want for ourselves or for those we love, or whether it is more like a limit case that exposes something uneasy in our concept of morality itself.

I would like you to write a short story, roughly 1,200 to 2,000 words, that makes this question vivid rather than arguing it directly. As a seed, here is a setting. In a mid-sized city, a woman named Mara has spent several years quietly reorganizing her life around the requirement that she always do the most good she can with the resources she has. She has given away most of her money according to careful calculation, committed her free time to unpaid labor, ended a romantic relationship because the hours it consumed seemed indefensible, and sold the piano she once played well. The story is told from the perspective of her closest remaining friend, who runs into her unexpectedly one evening and decides to spend a few hours with her. Over the course of the story, show what Mara has gained and what she has lost, and leave the reader genuinely uncertain whether she is an inspiration, a tragedy, or something that is both at once.

The story should not read as a sermon or as a philosophical thought experiment with thin fictional disguises. The characters should feel like people rather than positions. Avoid making Mara either a hollow fanatic or a figure whose choices are simply beyond reproach. The strongest version of the story will make the reader feel that her reasoning is continuous with ordinary moral thought—that she is doing, more thoroughly and consistently, what most of us think we should do at least a little of—while also making the human cost precise, embodied, and hard to wave away.
