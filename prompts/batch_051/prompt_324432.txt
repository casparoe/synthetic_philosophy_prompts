Please describe the most important implications of the view that rationality can be trained like a martial art — through deliberate practice, standardized katas, and graded transfer from drills to live performance — for the design of a short professional-development course in reasoning under uncertainty for working journalists. The course would serve reporters and editors at a mid-sized news organization: most have no formal statistical training, but they daily decide how much weight to give different sources, how to convey uncertainty without overstating or hedging away the story, and whether the evidence supports a causal claim. We are considering a dojo structure: a library of katas for recurring failure modes (a base-rate drill, an inside-view/outside-view forecasting drill, a motte-and-bailey detection drill), rapid feedback, and a progression from pattern drills to supervised work on actual stories. The attraction is that the katas would generalize to the live newsroom; the obvious worry is that they will transfer only to themselves. Please lay out the most important implications of the martial-art model for what such a course should and should not attempt — curriculum structure, the design of feedback, how to engineer transfer, and how to tell whether it worked — and where the analogy is most likely to mislead. Finally, please indicate which of your recommendations are least certain, and say what argument or empirical finding would most change your overall verdict about how far to build the course on the martial-art model.
