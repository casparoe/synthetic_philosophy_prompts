I've been co-running a responsibility reading group for about two years and we have a standoff that's now survived more changes of membership than I can count. The compatibilist camp says: imagine we discovered tomorrow that determinism is true. Nothing would or should change—we'd still resent a friend who lies to us, still feel indignation at a landlord who rents out a flooded apartment, still take pride in having kept a hard promise. The hard incompatibilist (she's read a lot of Pereboom) doesn't dispute any of that prediction; she actually grants that we couldn't just switch the reactions off. Her point is that this shows how deep the illusion runs: gratitude, resentment, guilt, all of it presupposes that people are the originators of what they do, and if determinism took that away the attitudes would be left unjustified even though we can't stop feeling them. What's strange is that both sides agree on every empirical fact we can think of, and neither has moved in months.

My working theory is that the disagreement isn't about what determinism would do to our emotions but about what the reactive attitudes are: the compatibilist treats them as a practice with its own internal standards, while the incompatibilist treats them as a theory of agency written into our emotions, which can then be falsified like any other theory. Is that the right diagnosis? Or is this a hidden dispute about the word 'justified,' a bedrock difference about whether the participant's perspective can be overruled by the detached perpsective, or something else? And given whatever the diagnosis is, what would actually count as progress—a distinction, a new case, a form of argument—or should we expect none?
