I'm on the editorial committee of a philosophy journal and we're stuck on a submission. Two external referees, both first-rate people working squarely in the paper's area, have sent reports that flatly contradict one another. Both saw the same version of the manuscript and the author's same reply. Referee A recommends accept with minor revisions: the paper's central distinction, he says, dissolves a problem that's been open for years. Referee B recommends reject: the distinction is empty, because the two properties it tries to keep apart coincide in exactly the range of cases the argument needs them to come apart. The author's reply to B argues that the alleged coincidence only follows if you read a key term in a way the paper explicitly rules out. I've now read the paper, both reports, and the reply twice. My honest hunch is that A is right, but I'm not a specialist in this subfield, and I can't shake the worry that I'm mainly responding to A's elegant writing and the fact that I enjoyed the paper, rather than to the technical merits. Two other committee members want us to simply get a third referee, on the grounds that we should'nt be making an expert call ourselves. This isn't mainly a policy question for me; it's about how much my own positive reaction should count when two people better placed than me disagree.

I need to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's meeting recommending a concrete course of action with a time frame: accept with minor revisions, reject, get a third referee, or something else if I can justify it. It should be something the committee can actually act on, and it should engage the strongest reason against whatever I recommend rather than just restating the obvious. Draft the opinion I should send round.
