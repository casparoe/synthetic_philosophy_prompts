I am a doctoral student in philosophy of medicine, and a proposal that keeps surfacing in our journal club strikes me as a useful test case for how a regulatory definition behaves once people adapt to it. The proposal concerns how a public health system should distinguish therapy from enhancement for purposes of reimbursement. Rather than comparing a patient's functioning to a population-level standard of normality, the payer would use a personal baseline. For each patient, it would establish the highest level of functioning the patient demonstrably maintained for at least six consecutive months during the previous five years, scored separately for domains such as mobility, organ function, and cognition. An intervention would be funded as therapy if its expected outcome returns the patient to within a small fixed tolerance band above that personal baseline; if it is expected to leave the patient clearly above the baseline — stronger, faster, longer-lived, or otherwise more capable than their own recent best — it would be classified as an enhancement and denied public coverage, though patients could pay out of pocket. A patient could appeal their assigned baseline with documentation, and the regulator would revisit the tolerance band and the five-year window every three years.

I am less interested in whether this is the right normative view than in how it would operate as a mechanism. What would it actually incentivize, and who would adapt first? I would like a careful analysis of how patients, clinicians, pharmaceutical firms, and appeals committees would behave under the rule, including which forms of gaming — in measurement, in the timing of treatment, or in the documentation offered at appeal — would appear earliest. What would the rule do to patients whose baseline was set during an illness or a period of poor access to care, and to patients with unusually high baselines reached through luck or privilege? Does it quietly reclassify normal aging, prevention, or the management of chronic disease as therapy or enhancement in ways the designers did not intend? And which of these failure modes, if any, can be repaired by adjusting the six-month criterion, the tolerance band, the five-year window, or the appeal process without thereby reintroducing a population-level standard of normal function?
