I am a PhD student in philosophy working on action theory and early modern rationalism, and I have been puzzling over Spinoza’s treatment of Buridan’s ass in the Scholium to Proposition 49 of Part II of the Ethics. Spinoza says, in effect, that a man placed in exact equilibrium between equally distant food and drink, with no other motive than hunger and thirst, would die of hunger and thirst, and he uses this to deny that the will is free. I think the passage contains an argument that proves far too much, but I cannot locate the mistake. Here is the argument as I have reconstructed it:

1. In the equilibrium case, the agent has no reason to take one alternative rather than the other.
2. If an agent has no reason to take one alternative rather than the other, then any movement toward one alternative rather than the other would be arbitrary: nothing about the agent or the alternatives explains the selection.
3. An action whose selection is arbitrary in this sense is not a rational choice.
4. Therefore, in the equilibrium case, rational choice is impossible.
5. Every real choice is either between alternatives supported by equally strong reasons or between alternatives supported by unequally strong reasons.
6. If the reasons are unequally strong, the alternative supported by the stronger reasons is the only rationally eligible one, so the outcome is already determined by those reasons before the agent acts.
7. If the reasons are equally strong, then by steps 2 through 4 no rational choice is possible.
8. Therefore, in every real choice, either the outcome is already determined by the reasons or no rational choice is possible.
9. This conclusion is unacceptable: it leaves no room for choosing among options that are both permissible, where neither alternative is forced and neither is excluded by reason.

The argument seems valid, and I find each premise individually plausible, but step 9 cannot be right. I want to keep Spinoza’s verdict about the equilibrium case, because I think it captures something important about why a perfectly indifferent agent cannot get started. But I also want to keep the ordinary idea that some choices are genuinely open in a way that is compatible with responding to reasons. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, if the fault is in step 6, can that be denied without giving up the claim that rational choice is reason-responsive?
