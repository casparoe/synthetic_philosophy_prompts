I'm a PhD student in human–robot interaction, though my project has drifted into machine ethics, and I've reached the point where my verdicts on cases are much more stable than any principle I can state. I'd be grateful if someone could tell me what principle, if any, captures these judgements.

We're building a care robot for a residential home. The architecture has a rule that says be truthful and a rule that says promote the resident's wellbeing, and I'm trying to formalise the tie-break between them. So the concrete question is how much the robot should be allowed to deceive or overrule residents for their own good. Here are my gut reactions.

1. A woman with moderate dementia asks the robot every evening where her husband is. He died six years ago. When the robot used to answer truthfully she would become acutely distressed, sometimes for hours, and then she'd forget and ask again. The family and senior carers have now configured it to say he's popped out to the shops, or gone to visit his brother. I think that's clearly fine, possibly the best thing available.

2. A different resident is diabetic and cognitively completely fine. He has told staff more than once that he doesn't want his diet managed, but he keeps eating a lot of sugar. The robot has begun, on staff instructions, swapping the sugar in his tea for a sweetener and quietly serving him smaller portions of cake. It's working, his blood sugar control has improved. I still think this is not fine.

I can't quite say why these two should be treated seperately, beyond a vague sense that one person can't really grasp the truth and the other can.

3. A resident with advanced dementia sometimes gets agitated at night and insists she has to go home to her mother, who is long dead. Correcting her or refusing makes things much worse, so the robot instead says her mother has just rung, that everything is fine at home, and offers her a cup of tea. She settles and forgets. I think that's fine.

4. The robot plays chess with a resident who used to be a decent club player. It deliberately plays a shade below his level so the games stay close and he keeps playing, which keeps him mentally active. He's never told. I think that's fine too, though I had to think about it.

5. A resident who is depressed and lonely but cognitively intact asks whether his daughter called. The robot knows she hasn't, but it tells him she called earlier and sends her love, because on past form that lifts his mood for the rest of the day. This is the one I keep going back and forth on.

6. A resident with normal cognition is recovering from surgery. She has a severe needle phobia and is refusing an anticoagulant injection that would very likely save her life. The robot and the nurse tell her it's a routine vitamin shot, and she accepts. I think this one is fine, but it seems to sink the distinction I was reaching for in (2).

The closest I've got is something like: you may lie or overrule for someone's good only when the person can't weigh up the alternatives themselves, and even then only to shield them from distress rather than to impose a medical judgement on them. That handles (1) and (2) nicely, but it would condemn (6), and I'm not sure where (4) fits under it.

So: is there a coherent principle that gets all or most of these verdicts, and which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? And what should I say about (5)? I'd prefer a principle with fewer than six clauses, if that's possible.
