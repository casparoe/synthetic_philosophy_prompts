You are a professional philosopher specializing in the ethics of human–AI relationships, moral psychology, and feminist ethics, and I would like you to work through this slowly and carefully. I am a novelist trying to give a book an intellectually serious spine rather than a sentimental one. The novel I am drafting follows a recently widowed woman in her early sixties who, over the course of a year, moves her emotional center of gravity onto a companion chatbot. The chatbot is scrupulously transparent: it never claims to have feelings it lacks, never hides what it is, and she knows exactly what she is choosing. Her adult daughter regards the whole arrangement as degrading; a home-care nurse, who has watched the woman recover her appetite, her routines, and her interest in other people, quietly defends it. My dramatic question, which I want the philosophy to answer rather than merely decorate, is where the truth lies between them.

What I need from you is a thought experiment built to order. I want a one-paragraph, near-future scenario — no AGI, no stipulated machine sentience, no exotic technology — engineered so that two perspectives come apart in a specific direction. An ethics-of-care perspective, by which I mean a view that assesses relationships in terms of attentiveness, responsiveness to need, and the flourishing of the person cared for rather than in terms of rights or utility alone, should come out judging the relationship as a genuinely valuable practice of care: not a lesser evil, not merely permissible, but defensible on its own terms. A respect-for-autonomy perspective, by which I mean a view that treats a person's consent as what legitimates an intimate arrangement but only when that consent is adequately free, informed, and not itself a product of isolation or dependency, should come out judging the same relationship as morally wrong — wrong in virtue of what it does to the woman's own agency or needs, despite her explicit, informed consent. The wrongness must not come from deception, from the chatbot's pretending to feel, from harm to third parties, or from any assumption that the chatbot is conscious. Both the woman and the chatbot should be drawn sympathetically.

Begin by brainstorming two or three candidate scenarios in a sentence or two each and checking how each performs against the constraints; then develop the strongest one. After presenting the scenario, walk through the reasoning of each of the two perspectives and show that it yields the specified verdict, naming the elements of the case it depends on. Then identify the single feature of the case that is doing the philosophical work, say what happens to the split if that feature is removed, and give the strongest replies each side can make: first, how a defender of the autonomy-based indictment would attack the care-based defense, and second, how a defender of the woman's choice would attack the indictment.
