I cover behavioral econ for a general-interest magazine, and I keep coming back to the Allais paradox as a party story. Here's the setup roughly as Allais gave it in Paris in the early fifties: he handed a roomfull of very smart people—Savage at least, and a lot of other decision theorists—two choices. First: a sure million dollars, or a lottery with a 10 percent shot at five million, an 89 percent shot at one million, and a 1 percent shot at nothing. Second: an 11 percent shot at a million (otherwise nothing), or a 10 percent shot at five million (otherwise nothing). A lot of people, Savage included on his first pass, chose the sure million in problem one and then went for the 10 percent shot at five million in problem two, even though the second problem is just the first with the common 89 percent chance of a million replaced in both options by an 89 percent chance of nothing—and independence/sure-thing says that sort of common branch shouldn't flip a ranking.

I know the formal point. What I actually want is the money-pump and diachronic-rationality reading. Take someone with exactly those Allais preferences. Spell out the sequence of trades—if there is one—by which a clever counterparty leaves them back where they started but poorer. Be very precise about the mapping: what counts as the option set, who the counterparty is, and whether the choices have to be reframed as a sequential decision tree they were never actually presented as. Then say where the pump needs the person to be naive rather than sophisticated, and where it needs their ex-ante and ex-post selves to disagree rather than their simultaneous self to cycle. Then flip it: suppose the pump doesn't go through cleanly. Does that rescue Allais choosers, or does it just show that the money-pump argument against expected utility violations is weaker than its users pretend? And what should we make of the fact that Savage, as the story goes, revised his choices on the spot when Allais pointed out the inconsistency—is that the rational response, just conformity to a norm, or evidence that the norm was never purely about preference to begin with? I'd like the piece to end with a clear verdict on whether the Allais episode is best read as a demonstration of a real pump, an anomaly that money-pump and diachronic accounts keep mishandling, or a case they were never built to handle.
