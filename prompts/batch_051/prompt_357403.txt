I spent most of my career running a molecular biology lab, and since I retired I've been working through philosophy of science in a more careful way than back when I just read the pop summaries. The thing I keep circling back to is the replication crisis, because I watched a lot of p-values under .05 in my old field that never turned into anything you could build the next experiment on, and I still can't decide wheter the problem is that people use frequentist tools badly or that the tools themselves are doing something wrong. Every time I think I've found a clean frequentist/Bayesian disagreement, it turns out to depend on p-hacking, optional stopping, or a straw-man prior that no real Bayesian would use.

So I'd like you to construct a thought experiment to order for me. I want one realistic scenario (a clinical trial, a psychology experiment, an ecology field study — something that could actually happen, not an urn or a perfectly rational agent) in which a correct, by-the-book frequentist analysis and a correct, by-the-book Bayesian analysis give opposite verdicts about whether a pre-registered replication attempt succeeded. The frequentist gets fixed alpha (say .05), no peeking, no HARKing (hypothesizing after the results are known), no multiple-comparison games, and adequate power by conventional standards, say around 80%. The Bayesian gets a defensible, moderately skeptical prior that respects a realistic base rate for the effect. Neither side does anything obviously stupid. I want it set up so that after both analyses are run correctly, the frequentist rejects the null and treats the replication as a success, while the Bayesian, looking at the same numbers, concludes the posterior probability of the effect is still too low to be confident. Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph, with illustrative numbers if you like, though I care more about the conceptual structure than about getting the exact Bayes factor right.

Then walk me through what each analysis says and why, in enough detail that I can see the machinery doing the work. After that, be honest about which single feature of the case is load-bearing — I suspect it will have something to do with how much prior information each framework lets into the verdict — and tell me what would happen if you removed just that feature while holding everything else fixed. Finally, tell me how a committed frequentist would attack the case and whether you think the attack is fair. I'm going to use this in my reading group, so the case has to generate the verdicts from the statistical logic rather than just stipulating them.
