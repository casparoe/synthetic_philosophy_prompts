I have been sitting in on a philosophy-of-linguistics reading group, and for months we have circled the same debate without anyone giving ground: whether the familiar grammatical categories — noun, verb, adjective, case, preposition — are natural kinds that languages carve at the joints, or convenient labels that grammarians impose and that fit some languages better than others. The defenders of the categories as real keep returning to a cluster of facts: nearly every language that has been described seems to distinguish participants from events in one way or another, even when the marking is syntactic rather than morphological; children acquiring unrelated languages sort object-words and action-words differently from very early; and the same broad functional slots reappear across family after family with suspicious regularity. The other side replies by reading the same cross-linguistic record the other way: in some languages what we translate as adjectives behave like stative verbs, in others they pattern as a subclass of nouns; what one grammar calls a preposition shows up elsewhere as a postposition, a case affix, or a relational noun built on a body-part term; and case systems notoriously conflate roles that other languages keep scrupulously distinct. Both camps accept the data, both camps grant the edge cases, and neither moves. It has begun to feel as though the argument is less about the facts than about what the facts are being asked to show.

My working hypothesis is that the real disagreement concerns what it would be for a grammatical category to be a natural kind in the first place — a universal cognitive primitive, a feature projected by the language faculty, or merely a comparative grid the analyst lays over languages for descriptive convenience. But I can also imagine the impasse being a disguised empirical bet about what a broader sample of languages or better acquisition studies would reveal, or a merely verbal quarrel over the word 'category.' I want a diagnosis of the impasse with reasons, not a survey of the two positions. Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a formal criterion of categoryhood, a sharper distinction between descriptive and comparative categories, a new kind of evidence, or an agreement to stop treating grammatical categories as natural kinds at all?
