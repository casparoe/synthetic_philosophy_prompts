Next month I'm giving a talk at a small workshop on photography and perception, and I need a Q&A prep sheet. The audience is mostly philosophers: a couple of them work on depiction and think talk of 'seeing through' a photograph is a metaphor that should be dropped, one senior person has spent years defending the transparency thesis (photographs are like telescopes or mirrors — we literally see the photographed object through them), and there are two philosophers of perception who study vision through instruments and will care a lot about the precise optical and causal chain. There are also a few art photographers who will roll their eyes at anything that ignores editing and curation.

My thesis is that transparency is not all-or-nothing, but the degree of transparency isnt really about how much a photo has been retouched. Its about whether the image is a continuous trace of the light from the scene or has been discretized and reconstructed: a film photograph is highly transparent even if its blurry or badly exposed, a normal digital photograph is only weakly transparent because the sensor samples and the processor rebuilds the image, and a photorealistic AI image is transparent to nothing even if it looks identical. In other words, digital capture quietly broke the thing Walton thought made photographs special, and we should say we see through most photos alot less than we used to.

My rough argument: genuine seeing-through requires the object to be the source of the visual state in the right way. Film preserves a continuous photochemical chain from object to print. Digital capture breaks that chain into sampled values that get reassembled, so even an unedited RAW file is more like an accurate depiction than a window. Therefore transparency-talk should be reined in for digital images, not because they are manipulated but because they are reconstructed.

I want the eight or so objections I'll actually get, in the order they are likely to come and roughly ordered by how much damage they do, phrased the way these people would put them. For each, give me a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. And dont give me objections from a random internet commenter — give me the ones from this crowd, especially the senior transparency person, who will either say my view collapses into hers or that I've just defined transparency so narrowly it only fits a chemical fetish.
