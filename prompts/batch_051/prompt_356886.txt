I want a specification of what a theory of mathematical explanation in science has to deliver. By "mathematical explanation in science" I mean cases in which a mathematical fact is claimed to explain an empirical or biological phenomenon — to do explanatory work itself — rather than merely to represent, quantify, or enable a causal explanation. I want the desiderata first, with the case or consideration behind each, not a survey of authors.

The theory should be required to classify or accommodate each of the following fixed points. It is allowed to deny that one of them is a genuine case of mathematical explanation, but only through a general criterion, not an ad hoc exception.

1. The cicada case. Magicicada broods emerge every 13 or 17 years; both periods are prime. The standard argument holds that a prime period minimizes how often a brood coincides with predators or competing broods that have shorter integer periods, because two cycles of periods n and m coincide every lcm(n, m) years, and a prime n shares no divisor with any smaller positive integer. The theory must say what, if anything, the prime period explains about the evolved trait, and must separate the arithmetic fact from the selective history that presumably realized it.

2. The honeycomb case. Among partitions of the plane into regions of equal area, a regular hexagonal tiling minimizes the total perimeter per region. The proposed explanation is that bees build hexagonal cells because this design minimizes the wax needed to enclose a given area. The theory must say whether and how an optimization theorem about ideal shapes can explain the structure produced by an actual biological process.

3. An asymmetry case. A Fourier series represents a sound wave without explaining it, and the formula T = 2π√(L/g) gives a pendulum's small-angle period without itself being taken to explain the pendulum's motion; these are indispensable, mathematically rich descriptions where the mathematics is intuitively not doing the explanatory work. The theory must account for the asymmetry between cases of this kind and the previous two, without stipulating it.

The theory must also confront the following structural constraints. First, many canonical accounts of scientific explanation require citing causes, but mathematical objects are standardly taken to be causally inert; so the theory must either show how mathematical explanation can survive within a causal framework or supply a non-causal account that does not over-generate. Second, it must distinguish mathematical explanation in science from intra-mathematical explanation, and from merely having a mathematical model of a phenomenon. Third, it must take a position, or make room for one, in the enhanced indispensability debate: if mathematics is genuinely explanatory in science, does the mathematics involved have to be true, and does that give scientific reason to believe in the relevant mathematical objects?

Finally, after stating the desiderata: identify which are non-negotiable and which are merely the preferences of particular camps (causalists, nominalists, structuralists, and the like); say whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, and if so which; and identify the single requirement that would do the most work in eliminating currently standard accounts. I am more interested in the justification of each requirement than in a final ranking of theories.
