I am a novelist writing a book whose protagonist becomes convinced that her life, and everyone else's, is sphexish. At the novel's turning point she lays out the following argument in her own defense:

(1) The digger wasp Sphex performs a fixed sequence: sting a cricket, drag it to the burrow, leave it at the entrance, inspect the burrow. If the cricket is moved a few inches while she inspects, she does not simply drag it in; she repeats the drag-and-inspect cycle. Her behavior is mechanical in a precise sense: a cue-triggered routine, insensitive to a changed state of affairs.
(2) Human conduct is likewise organized into cue-triggered routines. Interrupted mid-routine, people too sometimes restart the sequence from the beginning rather than revising the plan.
(3) The only difference is quantitative: humans have more routines, longer routines, and routines for switching between routines. It is a difference of degree, not of kind.
(4) Conduct that is the output of fixed, cue-triggered routines insensitive to changed circumstances is not free.
(5) Therefore human conduct is not free in any respect that differs in kind from the wasp's behavior; our flexibility is a larger loop, not an exit from the loop.

Is this argument valid? If it is valid, is it sound? If it fails, identify exactly which premise is false or which step is an invalid inference — in particular, whether step (3) or step (4) is doing the real work, and what the strongest defensible version of the argument would be.
