so i'm a novelist, and i've been messing around with Bongard problems lately—those pattern puzzles where you get six little boxes on the left and six on the right and you have to name the rule that puts each box on its side. a square inside a circle goes left, a circle inside a square goes right, whatever. the more i do them, the more they look like the same problem as writing a character: i put five scenes on the page, the reader induces a concept of the person from those scenes, and then they use that concept to predict how she'd behave in a scene i never wrote.

the view i keep getting pulled toward is deflationary. the examples never determine the concept; finitely many examples are compatible with infinately many rules, so 'the' rule is just whichever compression our heads happen to find cheapest, not something the puzzle already had in it. this view has real virtues. it's one tidy story for why reasonable people find different 'right' answers to the same puzzle, why different reviewers can read the same six scenes as flat, contradictory people, and why the next scene can always overturn whatever concept i'd formed. it also hands me a clear policy: stop asking which reading is true and start asking which reading is cheapest for a given reader.

but as the person who actually builds the character, i can't shake the firm conviction that some readings are wrong and one is right, and that the difference isn't just simplicity or taste. when the rule for a puzzle finally clicks, it feels found, not chosen. when a reader gets my character, they can predict things i never put on the page, and when a reviewer fits every scene i wrote to a reading that isn't her, i don't think 'well, that's just another compression'—i think they got it wrong. i also don't think the next five scenes will vindicate them.

i don't want to be told which side wins. i want to know what each move costs: what would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what would it take for the theory to be amended or abandoned, and whether there's a reading on which both survive. is an authored character different from an un-authored puzzle because my intention grounds a fact the six boxes don't have, or is that special pleading for authors over readers? give me the strongest version of each side and say which you'd give up, or how you'd keep both.
