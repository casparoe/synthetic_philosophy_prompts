I'm a journalist who covers the science of consciousness, and I keep running into a tension in the way the field handles first-person reports. On one side, there is the method called heterophenomenology: it says the science of consciousness should proceed from the third-person point of view, treating a subject's verbal reports — that she sees a red afterimage, that the pain has moved to her left arm — as data about what the subject believes or says, while remaining neutral about whether the states those reports seem to describe really exist. This has real virtues: it makes the study of experience publicly checkable, it avoids giving any individual special authority over the data, and it lets consciousness itself be a theoretical construct inferred from a stream of reports. On the other side, I have an intuition I cannot shake: when I report that I am in pain, I am not merely producing one more piece of evidence for an outside theorist to interpret. I seem to know the pain immediately, and my report is authoritative about it in a way that no third-person measurement could override. My question is whether heterophenomenology actually conflicts with that intuition, or whether it merely brackets it for the purposes of building a public science while leaving the intuition untouched elsewhere. And if the conflict is real, which should give way — the methodological scruple or the first-person authority — and what would each concession cost? Please answer in American English.
