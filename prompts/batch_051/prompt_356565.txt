Write the joint statement that a time-average decision theorist in the ergodicity-economics tradition and a committed expected-utility theorist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on how a single agent should evaluate gambles whose outcomes compound over time. Address it to a reader who knows elementary probability but has no prior commitment to either camp, and treat both positions as live research programs rather than a correct view and a strawman.

Begin from this shared ground, which both parties accept. An agent starts with wealth W0 and chooses, for each of N independent rounds, what fraction f of current wealth to stake on a gamble with i.i.d. net return X, so wealth evolves as W_{t+1} = W_t (1 + f X_t), assuming for all fractions under consideration that 1 + f X_t stays positive. For this multiplicative process the ensemble average of final wealth, computed over many parallel copies, satisfies E[W_N] = W0 (E[1 + f X])^N. The time-average growth rate, by contrast, is g(f) = E[ln(1 + f X)], and the strong law of large numbers implies that (1/N) ln W_N converges to g(f) almost surely. Whenever E[1 + f X] > exp(g(f)), the process is non-ergodic: the ensemble mean grows at a rate the typical single trajectory does not realize. The two camps agree on this mathematics and disagree about its normative import. The time-average camp holds that the quantity a single agent should optimize is g(f), because a decision-maker lives along one realized path rather than across an ensemble, and that expected values of non-ergodic observables systematically misstate what a typical agent can expect to happen. The expected-utility camp holds that the rational criterion remains maximizing expected utility of final wealth for an increasing, concave utility function expressing the agent's attitude to risk; on this view the growth-rate rule is simply expected-utility theory with logarithmic utility rather than a rival to it, and the ensemble average is a coherent ex ante expectation over possible states of the world, not an empirical description of one trajectory.

Produce the document the collaboration would yield, in this format.

First, a numbered agreed-claims section. The claims should be substantive rather than truistic, and must include: the exact condition under which a time average and an ensemble average coincide for an observable of this process, and the exact way they diverge for wealth itself; what the compounding-gamble example does and does not establish; and any concessions or reformulations each side makes so the final wording is acceptable to both. Wherever you judge that part of the two camps' dispute was verbal or definitional, say which part and give a wording both can sign.

Second, a disagreements table with one row per remaining issue. Use columns: the issue stated in neutral language; the time-average camp's own wording of its claim; the expected-utility camp's own wording of its claim; whether the issue is a crux and, if so, the precise sub-question whose answer would move at least one side; the case, argument, or empirical result both sides agree would bear on that crux; and each side's prediction recorded in advance. The table must include rows on at least: whether the ensemble average is the right quantity for a genuinely one-shot non-ergodic gamble; whether logarithmic utility is forced by the dynamics or is merely one permissible utility function; whether the time-average criterion extends beyond multiplicative wealth to arbitrary non-ergodic observables in a principled way; and what each criterion prescribes for the repeated gamble when the agent's goal is to reach a target wealth by a deadline rather than to maximize terminal wealth. Add further rows for any other cruxes you identify.

Third, a closing section of three paragraphs: where the residual disagreement is most substantive and consequential; where it is merely verbal or definitional; and what each side states it would have to be shown before it would revise its own position.
