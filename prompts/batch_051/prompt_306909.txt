Over the past few months I have been keeping a file of ideas that strike me as genuinely important—distinctions, arguments, the kinds of thoughts that feel as though they reorder everything around them. Recently I noticed something unsettling: the entries I most want to retain keep vanishing, not from the file but from my attention. I read the title, remember that I once thought the idea mattered, but I no longer feel the force that originally made me write it down. When I manage to reconstruct it by working through my old notes, it changes how I think for a day or two and then goes flat again. Friends tell me I am simply bad at reviewing my notes, but I suspect something more specific is happening: some ideas resist being remembered because they undermine the mental cues that would normally retrieve them.

I have begun drafting a short essay around this suspicion, and I would like comments on the draft before showing it to anyone. The working thesis is that a certain class of ideas are antimemes—resisting their own propagation—for a cognitive reason rather than a social one. On this account, an idea is retrieved by cues that share some of its structure; an idea that dissolves or redescribes the very structure it exemplifies therefore destroys the cues that would bring it back to mind. My first example is the thought that insight is a function of framing rather than content. Once that thought is fully absorbed, the difference between a deep idea and a trivial one comes to seem like an artifact of framing, the feeling of importance fades, and nothing prompts me to revisit the entry that recorded it. My second, still-gestural example is the thought that salience is systematically biased against self-undermining ideas, so that any list of interesting ideas is subject to a selection effect. From this I draw a deliberately perverse practical conclusion: a note system should devote more review time to entries that feel dead, precisely because the absence of a sense of recognition may signal that an idea has done its work. I am unsure about three things: whether the cognitive mechanism can be stated without circularity, whether the two examples genuinely exhibit the phenomenon rather than ordinary forgetting, and whether the practical advice is something a real person could follow. Could you comment on those three points, and also indicate where you think the essay would face the strongest objections?
