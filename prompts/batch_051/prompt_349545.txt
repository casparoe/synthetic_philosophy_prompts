Gaunilo’s island objection is a reductio. If Anselm can prove that the greatest conceivable being exists just by appealing to the concept, then the same logic proves the greatest conceivable island exists. Gaunilo asks us to imagine an island more excellent than all other islands, the lost island. If it existed only in the understanding, it would be less great than an island that exists in reality. So, by Anselm’s own principle, the lost island must exist in reality. But this is absurd—no island can be inferred into existence from a description. So Anselm’s argument is invalid, because it proves too much. Anselm’s reply is that the island case is not parallel because an island is a finite, material thing and its qualities are not perfections in the absolute sense. God’s perfections—omnipotence, omniscience, and necessary existence—are not the kind of thing that can be increased by degrees. Existence is not a real predicate you can add to a finite thing’s description; it only belongs to God’s essence. Therefore Gaunilo’s parody fails. I think Anselm’s reply succeeds, because the argument is not meant to be a general machine for generating existing objects out of definitions; it only applies to the unique concept of God.
