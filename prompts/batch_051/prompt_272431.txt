I have been trying to formalize what I call the Re-Derivation Constraint for machine ethics, and I want an honest assessment of it. The guiding thought is that a machine is doing ethical reasoning — as opposed to executing an ethical policy — only if it could arrive at some of its moral conclusions from a specification that does not already state those conclusions. If an engineer writes "minimize harm," "respect autonomy," or "never use race as an input" into the system's rules or objective, then the ethics was done by the engineer before runtime, and the machine is only applying a decision procedure. The Re-Derivation Constraint therefore says: a system counts as an ethical reasoner only if it can regenerate at least some of its moral commitments from inputs that are not themselves moralized — full non-normative descriptions of the situation plus a formal characterization of what it would be for an output to be an ethical judgment, but no supplied list of wrong-making properties. A triage system that concludes that a patient's social status is irrelevant passes if it derives that irrelevance from something like an impartiality condition on reasons; it fails if "do not discriminate by social status" is already in the code. I can already feel the objection coming: the characterization of "an ethical judgment" is doing the real work, and moral content is being smuggled in at that very point. The constraint might be too strong, or circular. What I want to know is whether this is a genuine distinction between doing ethics and applying a decision procedure, or just an architectural preference — for bottom-up systems that learn norms over top-down systems that execute handed-down norms — presented as a conceptual claim; whether something like it already exists in the machine-ethics literature; and where it breaks, in particular whether any realistic system could satisfy it without smuggling moral content in through the choice of features, the success criterion, or the definition of what counts as ethical. Please reconstruct the constraint in its strongest defensible form before criticizing it, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people working on machine ethics, and do not present a contested position as though it were the consensus.
