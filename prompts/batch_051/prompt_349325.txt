In the debate over what governs assertion, I keep seeing the same standoff. The knowledge-norm view says that one may properly assert that p only if one knows p; its main selling point is that it explains both the absurdity of Moore-paradoxical statements (asserting that something is so while adding that you don't believe it is so) and the impropriety of flat-out asserting that your lottery ticket lost, however overwhelmingly likely that is. The rival views put truth, belief, or justified belief in the governing role; their favorite counterexample is selfless assertion, in which someone with strong epistemic grounds asserts that p while personally rejecting it because a role requires it, and many judge the assertion perfectly proper. A further camp says there is no distinctive norm of assertion at all, only general conversational pragmatics. The sides have traded these same cases for decades without convergence. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a verdict. Is this mainly a dispute about which case judgments count as data, about what kind of thing a norm of assertion even is, about whether the question presupposes a single norm where there are several doing different jobs, or something else? My own hunch is that the phrase norm of assertion bundles together distinct normative jobs, but I want that hunch tested rather than confirmed.
