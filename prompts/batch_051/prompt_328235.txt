Just got back from a long argument with a friend about what it means to understand a proof, and he left me with an argument I can't get out of my head. It goes like this:

(1) You understand a proof only if you can justify every step in it.

(2) A step that rests on a theorem isn't justified unless you can justify that theorem too; otherwise your just taking the textbook's word for it.

(3) So by (1) and (2), understanding a proof requires you to justify its supporting theorems, and the theorems behind those, and so on.

(4) For any nontrivial proof, no one actually runs that chain all the way back.

(5) And in principle you can't finish it, because at some point you hit axioms and definitions, and there's nothing further to justify them -- you just accept them.

(6) But accepting an axiom is exactly the kind of taking-someone's-word-for-it that (2) rules out.

(7) So no one understands any nontrivial proof.

(8) But that's absurd: mathematicians obviously understand proofs they couldn't reconstruct from axioms.

I'd like to keep (8), and I'd also like to keep (1), but something has to go. Where does this argument go wrong, and what does it cost to reject the premise you pick? If (1) goes, what's left of the difference between understanding a proof and just checking each step mechanically?
