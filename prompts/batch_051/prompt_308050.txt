I'm auditing a political theory seminar that has been going through Isaiah Berlin's distinction between positive and negative liberty, and this week a classmate brought in the republican critique of the negative view. In discussion, one of the grad students kept falling back on a slogan to shut things down: "freedom just means not being interfered with." He used it to dismiss a point I made about a worker whose manager has a huge amount of arbitrary power over her schedule and could fire her for almost any reason, but in fact never interferes because he's a decent guy. On my classmate's view the worker is free, full stop, since no one is actually blocking her. That seems too quick to me, but when I push I realize I dont have a clean statement of why.

What are the different precise things that slogan could actually be claiming? I can see a bare empirical claim about when people call themselves unfree, a conceptual definition of liberty, a normative claim about what kinds of unfreedom the state ought to prevent, and maybe something else entirely. Lay the readings out as a list from the trivial to the radical, and say which ones are defensible and which are just a debating move. Then tell me which reading my classmate's argument in the boss case depends on, and whether it actually supports dismissing the worry about arbitrary power. I especially want to know whether the slogan is compatible with saying the worker has a real freedom problem in the republican sense of non-domination or the positive sense of being her own master, or whether accepting it rules those out by definition.
