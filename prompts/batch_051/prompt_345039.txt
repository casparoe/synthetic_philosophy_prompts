I've been chewing on something and I'd like you to write it up for me. I've been reading Sen on famines, the entitlement approach: the idea that people don't starve because a country runs out of food, they starve because their claim on food collapses, their wages, their land, their rations, whatever. But the way aid agencies and famine early-warning systems talk about crises still splits them by trigger: "drought-induced" famine gets treated as a natural disaster, while "man-made" or "conflict-induced" famine gets treated as closer to a crime or a policy failure. I keep going back and forth on whether that split is a gears-level causal distinction or just donor-facing shorthand.

What I want is an essay that reconstructs what theory of causation, and maybe responsibility, that classification practice presupposes. Does it presuppose that a famine can have a single natural cause, that a drought can starve people on its own with the social arrangements just being background conditions? Is that coherent once you look at the usual cases people fight about, Ireland, Bengal, China, where the blight or the harvest or the war was only part of it and the rest was export policy, procurement targets, rationing rules, and so on? And then tell me what a famine early-warning system built on the entitlement view would actually look like. What would it measure, what would its "cause" field say, and would the natural-versus-political distinction survive in any form? End with a verdict on whether the distinction is doing real work or should be dropped.

Please write it as continuous prose only, the way you'd write an email or a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
