Here's an argument I keep almost believing, and it seems to prove way to much, so I'm trying to find the slide. I'll number it.

(1) If animal suffering matters morally, the right way to compare diets is by the expected total animal suffering each one produces, counting every animal affected, not just the obvious ones.
(2) Per meal, factory-farmed chicken requires far more individual animals raised and killed than factory-farmed beef: a single steer yields a lot of meals, while a single broiler yields only a few.
(3) So if you're choosing between meats, chicken is morally worse than beef — a welfare-conscious meat eater should eat cows, not birds.
(4) But growing plants kills wild animals too: rodents and birds caught up in harvest machinery, insects killed by pesticides, animals displaced when land is cleared.
(5) These deaths are unavoidable in almost any real diet, and the numbers are enormous. Small differences in how a crop is farmed — more or less pesticide, one grain versus another — can plausibly change the body count by more than the whole steer-versus-chicken difference.
(6) So the same expected-suffering calculus that delivered (3) now says the beef-versus-chicken comparison was just noise: the deaths that actually decide whether a diet is better are mostly insects in crop fields, and we can't estimate those numbers.
(7) And a principle that can't tell us which diet is better can't guide any diet choice — veganism included, since veganism also depends on crop fields.
(8) Therefore the principle that started with animal suffering ends up proving that no food choice can be justified, which is absurd enough that something must have gone wrong.

I'd like to keep (1); the lesson shouldn't be that animals barely matter. But if the argument is valid, tell me which premise is cheapest to give up, and what I'm committed to once I reject it. If it's not valid, tell me exactly which step slides and why it looked okay.
