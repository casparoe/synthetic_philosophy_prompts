I am one of the assigned respondents at a workshop on population ethics with infinitely many lives, and each of us must circulate a written statement of our commitments before we meet. I expect the statement to be attacked line by line, so I want an independent audit of mine before I send it. The list is as follows.

1. Welfarism. The value of a world supervenes on the multiset of real-valued lifetime welfare levels of its inhabitants. Two worlds with the same multiset are exactly equal in value; identity, temporal order, spatial position, and the order in which lives are enumerated carry no intrinsic value.

2. Finite totalism. For any world with finitely many inhabitants, its value is the arithmetic sum of their lifetime welfare levels, with nonexistence contributing zero.

3. Weak Pareto. For any two worlds with exactly the same inhabitants: if every inhabitant is at least as well off in the first as in the second, and at least one inhabitant is strictly better off, then the first world is better.

4. Infinite comparability. It is false that all infinite worlds are equal in value, and false that all pairs of infinite worlds are incomparable; at least some pairs of infinite worlds admit a genuine betterness comparison.

5. Cumulative divergence. Suppose two infinite worlds are represented side by side as paired sequences of welfare levels, (a1, a2, a3, ...) and (b1, b2, b3, ...). Let D(n) be (a1 + ... + an) − (b1 + ... + bn). If D(n) tends to positive infinity, the first world is better; if D(n) tends to negative infinity, the second is better; otherwise this criterion says nothing about the pair.

6. Finite swamping. If two infinite worlds differ only in the welfare levels of finitely many inhabitants, and each difference is finite, then the two worlds are equally good; a finite patch of welfare cannot matter against an infinite background.

Please go through all fifteen pairs of commitments and separate the genuine tensions from the merely apparent ones. For each genuine tension, say how severe it is and what the cheapest repair would be, and what that repair costs philosophically. Finally, tell me which single revision to the list would remove the most trouble for the least philosophical loss. Each item on the list was forced on me by some case or principle I trust, and I cannot tell which of them are doing the damage, so please be specific about where the pressure is located.
