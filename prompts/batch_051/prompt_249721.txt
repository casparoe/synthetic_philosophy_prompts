My city is setting up a "citizens' chamber": 120 people drawn by lot from the voter rolls, with the draw adjusted so the group matches the city on age, gender, neighborhood, and income. The chamber meets for two years, hears expert testimony, and can veto any ordinance the elected council passes, by simple majority; the council can override only by a two-thirds vote. The chamber cannot propose anything of its own. Supporters say this makes lawmaking more democratic because the chamber actually looks like the city; opponents say it's less democratic because no one in it was chosen by voters and no one can be voted out. I'm trying to work out what view of political representation this design is committed to. Does it treat a representative as a mirror of the governed, a delegate, a trustee, or something else? Does it presuppose that ordinary citizens' judgment is enough to check elected officials, and if so, what becomes of expertise? And the part I can't figure out: is "can only veto, never propose" a coherent implication of the underlying view, or a fudge between two rival views of democratic legitimacy? In particular, where is the design forced by a genuine philosophical commitment, and where is it just compatible with several different ones?
