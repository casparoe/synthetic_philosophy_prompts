I've been trying to work out this idea about markets and character and I keep failing to kill it, so I want someone smarter than my roommate to have a go. I'm calling it virtue liquidity. The claim is that the whole "does the market corrupt or civilize us" debate is badly posed, because markets don't make people better or worse in general — they change the price of being virtuous. Some virtues are liquid: you can cash them in through market transactions without depleting them, and often the more you exercise them the more they yield. Punctuality and reliability are like this; a freelance developer who always delivers on time turns that reliability into reputation, repeat clients, higher rates. Honesty is liquid when your track record is legible (seller ratings, audited accounts) but weirdly illiquid in a one-shot used-car deal, where being candid about the car's faults just lowers the price. Other virtues are basically illiquid: indiscriminate generosity, or forgiving someone in a transaction where you'll never meet again, tend to cost you and don't convert into anything the market will pay you back for. So my view isn't that markets corrupt, and isn't that they civilize: they reprice virtues, making some cheap to practice and others costly, and the real question is which traits a given market setup makes liquid. And that, I claim, is a question about law, information, repeat play, all that — not about "capitalism" in the abstract.

I know there's a long doux-commerce-ish tradition that trade softens manners and make people honest, and I also know the standard corruption line. What I like about my version is that it's a single mechanism explaining both the civilizing effects and the corrupting ones, depending on the setup, instead of just picking a side. But I'm aware this could just be a finance-flavored metaphor with no actual mechanism behind it, or it could already exist under some proper name I've never heard. So: is this a real distinction or a restatement dressed up in accounting language? What's the nearest existing view — and where does my version actually break? I want the strongest counterexample, some trait or market where my liquidity story predicts the character effects backwards, and a verdict on whether any of the core idea survives that.
