I'm trying to figure out what principle, if any, underlies my judgments about when it is legitimate to treat two pieces of evidence, two events, or two tests as independent. I have a set of cases, all of which feel right to me individually, but I cannot yet see the rule that generates them. Two friends who do not know each other independently recommend the same obscure restaurant; I treat the second report as strong additional evidence and my confidence jumps accordingly. But if I learn they both read the same review, the second report stops adding much, and I treat the two recommendations as one piece of evidence. Two laboratories in different countries replicate the same surprising experimental result; I treat this as far stronger than one positive study. But if I learn both labs copied their protocol from the same methods paper and used the same commercial assay under suspicion of systematic bias, I discount the replication almost entirely. A single coin is tossed ten times; I do not hesitate to multiply the probabilities of the individual outcomes, even though all ten tosses share the same coin, the same hand, and the same table. Three witnesses to a crash give matching details; their agreement impresses me until I discover they spoke to each other before giving statements, after which it impresses me far less. A DNA database match on two markers, each with a one-in-a-hundred random-match frequency, makes me want to multiply to one in ten thousand; a statistician warns me that this is wrong if the suspect and the true source are drawn from a structured population in which marker frequencies are correlated, and my intuition says to defer. And, coming at the same theme from the opposite side: if two genuinely independent causes each make some effect more likely, then learning that the effect occurred and that one cause was present seems to make the other cause less likely, as if conditioning on a shared effect can create a kind of negative dependence where none existed before.

My provisional principle is that you may treat items as independent only when you have a causal model in which there is no open path between them — that the heuristic "presume independence" is really a claim about conditional independence given the right set of shared causes. That formulation handles the friends, the labs, the witnesses, and the DNA cases quite well, but it seems to handle the coin tosses badly, and I am unsure whether the last case belongs to the same principle or is merely adjacent to it. So I want the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely: what clause is motivated by which case, what it says about cases I have not listed, and an honest account of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. If the coin-toss judgment has to be given up, or quarantined as a different kind of default, say so.
