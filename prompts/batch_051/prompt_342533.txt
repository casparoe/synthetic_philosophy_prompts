So I've been going down a rabbit hole about Clever Hans, the horse in early 1900s Germany that everyone thought could do arithmetic. It would tap its hoof to answer questions, even stuff like what's 4 plus 4, and it seemed to get them right way more than luck. Then a psychologist named Pfungst tested it more carefully and found the horse was picking up on tiny involuntary movements from whoever was asking — the person would tense up or hold their breath as Hans got close to the right number of taps, and the horse would just stop at the cue. When the questioner didn't know the answer or was hidden behind a screen, the horse couldn't do it anymore. So it was never mathematics, just reading people.

I keep pairing this in my head with the Turing test and the bigger idea that the behavior is what matters — like, if something acts smart enough for long enough, we should treat it as having a mind. I can't find anyone online who lays out the matchup the way I want, and I don't have any academic background, so I'm asking you.

Walk me through what a behavioral criterion of mind would have said about Hans before the cues were found, and then after. Then flip it around: what does the Hans story do to the behavioral view? Does it just show the original test was badly controlled and a proper behavioral test would still work, or does it show something deeper — that behavior can pass the test while the inner stuff we actually mean by mind is missing? And here's the part that realy keeps me up: if the cues were doing the work, couldn't you say the mind was never in the horse alone but in the whole horse-plus-trainer-plus-crowd system? Would a Turing-style judge testing the whole ensemble, instead of the horse alone, have to say the system as a whole understands arithmetic? I'm less interested in the history trivia and more in what the case forces us to admit about behavior as a test for mind.
