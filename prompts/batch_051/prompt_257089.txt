A reading group I attend with people from rationalist-adjacent forums got into a long argument about Dennett's multiple-drafts model and whether it actually escapes the Cartesian theater. I was asked to referee afterward and couldn't do it cleanly, so I'd like a careful adjudication. Here is the exchange as I remember it.

Priya: Dennett's whole case is that there is no Cartesian theater — no single place and time where contents are assembled for a privileged observer. But every version of the multiple-drafts model still owes us an account of why some contents are conscious and others aren't. Whatever criterion it offers — a draft's winning out over its competitors, becoming globally available, having enough downstream influence — picks out one draft as privileged. That's a theater. It hasn't been demolished; it's been re-labeled with a less suspicious name.

Marcus: No. The theater isn't just the fact that some contents are privileged. It's a specific commitment to a single site, a single moment, and a single audience. Multiple drafts denies all three. Contents can be famous in different brain systems at different times, and the report I give is itself just one more draft, not a broadcast from a central office. If you allow 'theater' to mean anything that distinguishes conscious from preconscious processing, then every control system with priorities has a theater, and the term has lost the content Dennett was attacking. That's not an objection; it's a definitional slide.

Priya: But then what's left of the view? If 'conscious' means nothing more than 'a draft with enough influence,' you've given a theory of access — of which contents get to steer memory, report, and action — and you've said nothing about why the lights are on for the subject in those cases. And if the view is compatible with any architecture — winner-take-all broadcast, global workspace, serial inner narrative included — then it makes no predictions. A model that can absorb every possible result isn't a model.

Marcus: It's not compatible with any architecture. It commits us to the absence of a canonical temporal threshold for consciousness, to the possibility that the apparent order of an experience can be revised after the fact, and to the claim that subjective sequencing and neural sequencing can diverge. It also predicts that the search for a single moment at which consciousness occurs will fail, and that findings about threshold timing won't line up. A global-workspace account with a single unified broadcast commits to exactly the sort of canonical moment the drafts model denies. Those are empirically distinguishable postures. As for the lights being on: the view says that in revision cases there is no extra fact about what the subject really experienced, over and above the draft that wins out in the telling. That's not an omission; it's the substantive claim.

Priya: That's my point, restated. If you admit the view implies there is no fact of the matter about the real content of an experience in those cases — that the remembered sequence can be fabricated while there is no actual experience of which the report is a misrepresentation — then this is no longer a friendly correction to the ordinary picture of a single stream of experience. It's anti-realism about experience, with the hard problem dissolved rather than answered. A defender can hold that, but they should stop advertising it as the moderate, philosophy-free option.

Neither of them was obviously wrong to me, and each conceded small points without giving ground on the main one. Please adjudicate this move by move. For each of the five moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then say who is right about which specific point, who is more right overall, and what both parties are missing that a better account of the dispute would include.

Please keep the entire response to at most 1,200 words.
