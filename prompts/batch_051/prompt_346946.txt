Rerun Frank Jackson's Mary thought experiment, but change one methodological assumption. In the standard version, Mary has spent her whole life in a black-and-white room, knows every physical fact about color vision, and then sees red for the first time; the intuition is that she thereby learns a new, non-physical fact. Now suppose the whole scenario is studied from a heterophenomenological standpoint: the experimenter may treat Mary's verbal and non-verbal behavior as data, and may take her utterances as sincere expressions of what she believes or takes herself to experience, but may not treat those utterances as authoritative or incorrigible reports about the existence or nature of inner phenomenal states. The method's deliverable is a heterophenomenological world — Mary's world as she reports it — plus a physical/functional explanation of why those reports get produced.

The pivotal moment is this: after leaving the room, Mary looks at a ripe tomato and says, in effect, that she has learnt what it is like to see red. Now run the knowledge argument under this protocol. Does it still go through, and if so, where does its force now lie? In particular: which premise, if any, becomes unavailable once first-person authority is dropped? Can the datum that Mary gains something be restated as a change in her abilities, dispositions, or subsequent reports, and does that restatement preserve or deflate the anti-physicalist conclusion? And does heterophenomenology merely ignore the explanatory target the knowledge argument is pointing at, or does it show that the target was never properly specified in the first place?
