For a unit on punishment in my philosophy class, I tried to build an argument about statutes of limitations from premises that each seemed true on their own, but the conclusion they seem to force is one I cannot accept. Help me find where it goes wrong.

(1) What a wrongdoer deserves is fixed by the wrongdoing itself: the act they performed, their understanding of it, and their capacities at the time.
(2) The passage of time after the act changes none of those facts. A crime committed in 1990 is the same historical event in 2025 that it was in 1991; "old" and "recent" are facts about the calendar, not about the act or the wrongdoer's state of mind at the time.
(3) So the mere passage of time never changes how much punishment a person deserves. If D deserved ten years on the day after the crime, then D deserves exactly the same ten years forty years later.
(4) This cannot be dismissed by pointing to faded evidence or faulty memory. Imagine an admitted crime of a kind subject to a limitation period, caught on clear video, where the offender never repented, apologized, or made amends, and is still alive and competent. The only thing that has changed is the date.
(5) A statute of limitations would still bar prosecution after the deadline. Yet by (3), the person protected by the limit is just as deserving as they were the day after the crime.
(6) An institution that knowingly lets two equally deserving wrongdoers be treated differently — one punished, one immune — has abandoned desert as the basis of punishment. And punishment is nothing other than the practice of giving wrongdoers what they deserve.
(7) Therefore either every statute of limitations is unjust and should be abolished, or desert itself must fade with time.
(8) The first option is hard to accept: limitation periods exist in nearly every legal system, and having them for some wrongs seems obviously sensible. The second option seems even stranger: it makes a person's desert depend on how long the system took to get around to them, so the state could extinguish guilt by being slow, or create different deserts for two identical wrongdoers just by filing charges at different times.
(9) So the argument must go wrong somewhere. But each step seemed forced by the one before it, and I cannot locate the exit.

Where does this argument go wrong? If you had to keep (1) and (2), where is the least costly place to resist, and what does rejecting that step cost? After you diagnose it, explain whether the corrected argument still leaves any legitimate moral work for the passage of time to do.
