Question: In On Liberty, chapter 5, Mill writes:

"The maxims are, first, that the individual is not accountable to society for his actions, in so far as these concern the interests of no person but himself. Advice, instruction, persuasion, and avoidance by other people if thought necessary by them for their own good, are the only measures by which society can justifiably express its dislike or disapprobation of his conduct."

Suppose a government changes the default for organ donation from opt-in to opt-out, while leaving it easy to choose either option. Would Mill have to count this as one of the permissible measures he names? Explain.

Student answer: A nudge like opt-out isn't coercive because nobody is forced to donate, and you can still opt out at any time. Since Mill allows "advice, instruction, persuasion," and a default is basically just advice built into the choice situation, he would likely permit it. He can't object on grounds of interference with self-regarding conduct, because refusing to donate isn't self-regarding: someone else needs the organ. In fact, once we see that, the state could legitimately go further and mandate organ donation, since it would save lives. Even if you don't go that far, a transparent default should be fine—Mill's only requirement is that persuasion be open, not hidden. As long as the government announces the default, the nudge is consistent with the passage.
