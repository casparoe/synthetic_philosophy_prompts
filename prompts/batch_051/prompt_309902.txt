A friend and I just spent a long evening arguing over Hobbes's treatment, in Leviathan chapter 14, of covenants made under fear. He maintains that when Hobbes says a promise made under threat of death is binding so long as no civil law forbids performing it, Hobbes is implicitly admitting that the original social covenant is itself extorted by fear and therefore cannot ground genuine obligation. I maintain that the passage cuts the other way: by showing that fear does not void a covenant, Hobbes is explaining why the transition out of the state of nature can be morally binding even though it is motivated by terror of violent death. Write a dialogue between two characters, one defending each of these readings, in which they work through that passage's implications for whether the social contract's origin in fear is a problem for political obligation. Let them pursue the argument into the harder questions: whether there is a relevant difference between a covenant with an enemy for one's life and the covenant that institutes a sovereign, and whether Hobbes's insistence that fear and liberty are consistent helps or hurts each reading. Neither character should be a strawman, and they should genuinely respond to one another's actual moves rather than talk past each other.
