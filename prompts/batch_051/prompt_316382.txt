I spent my career in physical chemistry, and one thing my old colleages always insisted on was that a real cause has to do something: push, transfer energy, change a gradient, leave a trace. That background is probably why I've been stuck on the old argument that omissions can't be genuine causes. As I've picked it up from reading around, the argument runs like this: if you don't water a plant and it dies, the death was caused by desiccation—by the lack of water—not by some "omission" that was never there to do anything. There's no event, no energy exchange, no mechanism connecting a non-doing to the outcome, so talk of causing-by-omitting is shorthand at best, or maybe a category mistake.

But here's what's been bothering me. In the last few decades, causal modeling—structural equations, interventionist accounts, the sort of machinery used in epidemiology and machine learning—has made it routine to treat "no treatment" or "did not intervene" as a level of a variable, and to say that failing to treat can be a cause when the right counterfactual holds: had the variable been set to "treated," the outcome would have differed. My retired-scientist reaction is that this is very clever mathematics for summarizing a system, not evidence that non-events have suddenly become real causes out there in the world.

So here's the question. Does the old argument that omissions cannot be real causes survive this development? I want to know exactly which premise the causal-modeling crowd would have to reject or quietly replace for their "omission causes" to be literal causes, and whether that premise can be restated in a way that keeps the old argument alive—or whether my former colleagues were confusing the ontology of pushes-and-pulls with a broader notion of cause. Did the success of causal modeling kill the argument, repair it, or just change what "cause" has to mean for the argument to matter?
