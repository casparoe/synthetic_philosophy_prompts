One of the other grad students in my probability-and-modality seminar keeps pushing a version of this argument, and I'm at the point where I can't tell if its deep or just has a buried equivocation. He says it shows that there cannot be a fair lottery with countably many tickets, but I suspect the real issue is how "possible" and "nonzero probability" are being hooked together. I've cleaned up his argument:

(1) In a fair lottery, every ticket can win: for each ticket i, it is possible that ticket i wins.
(2) If it is possible that ticket i wins, then the probability that ticket i wins is greater than zero.
(3) So in a fair lottery, each ticket has positive probability of winning.
(4) Now take a lottery with one ticket for each natural number. Since it is fair, every ticket has the same probability of winning; by (3) this common probability is some epsilon > 0.
(5) The events "ticket i wins" are mutually exclusive. By countable additivity — the probability of a disjoint union is the sum of the probabilities — the probability that some ticket or other wins is epsilon + epsilon + ..., summed over all naturals, which is infinite.
(6) But probabilities cannot be above 1 (or infinite). Contradiction.
(7) So there is no fair lottery with countably many tickets.

Is the argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails, and what would the corrected step say? I keep wanting to reject (2), but in finite lotteries it seems completely right to say that possible outcomes have nonzero probability, so I can't articulate why the move breaks in the infinite case. And if we do reject (2), are we stuck saying that a possible outcome can have probability literally zero, or is there a better way to repair things that keeps the countable lottery alive?
