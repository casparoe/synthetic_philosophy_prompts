I've been reading about peer disagreement—conciliationism (finding out an epistemic peer disagrees with you means you should move toward them) vs. steadfastness (you can stick to your own view if you think your reasoning was good)—and instead of arguing in the abstract I'm trying to write an actual conflict rule for my research group. Here's the draft. Whenever two coauthors on a paper disagree about the main conclusion and there stated confidence levels (on 0–100) are more than 20 points apart, neither can submit until each has moved their stated confidence at least a third of the way toward the other's number, and the abstract has to report the midpoint of their final numbers. Escape hatch: either coauthor can call a lab meeting, and if the rest of the lab agrees that the other person is not actually an epistemic peer on this topic (different training, bad track record, didn't do the analysis themselves), the surviving coauthor can keep their original number without moving. Treat this as a mechanism, not a moral question. Walk through what it does to how people state confidences, to who gets asked to be a coauthor in the first place, to who gets certified as a "peer" over time, and to what conclusions the lab ends up publishing. Where does it get gamed first, and can the loopholes be fixed without making everyone a full conciliationist or turning peerhood into a popularity contest?
