I'm the science editor at a general-interest magazine, and I keep hitting the same problem. We run a lot of AI coverage, and freelance copy comes back saying things like "the model understands the rule," "the model knows the sentence is ironic," "the model reasoned its way there," "the model hallucinated a citation." Our style guide is silent on this, so every week I'm making ad-hoc calls under deadline, and whichever call I make, a writer is annoyed at me. I'd like a reusable procedure the whole desk can use before copy gets to me — I sign off in the end, but I don't want to be the only thing keeping it consistent.

The stakes are real. If we over-anthropomorphize, readers think we bought the hype; if we over-correct and write "the system generated tokens consistent with," they think we're clueless about what these things can actually do.

So: build me a checklist-or-flowchart-in-words for deciding when a sentence about an LLM may use mental-state vocabulary (understand, know, reason, believe, mean, hallucinate) and when it has to be rewritten in mechanical terms (predicts, generates, matches patterns, and so on). Ground it in the strongest versions of both sides of the stochastic-parrots-versus-understanding debate, not the slogan versions. In particular, don't smuggle in one side's conclusion: if a step of the procedure depends on a premise about what "understanding" requires — say, that understanding needs grounding, or that it's just a matter of competent behavior — name it, and tell me how the checklist changes if the desk rejects that premise.

The writers won't read the philosophy; they need something they can run a sentence through in a couple of minutes. Include three or four test sentences covering the kinds of cases that actually show up, and a worked example of each. Include at least one case where the procedure makes us rewrite a sentence I'd personally be happy to keep, and one where it lets through a sentence that gives me the creeps. And mark clearly the points where the procedure can't settle the question and an editor has to make a call — mark them as judgment calls rather than pretending the flowchart decides.

One last thing, and be honest here: I'm assuming there's a usable distinction in this area and that the debate supports a procedure at all. If that's wrong — if a neutral checklist would just be a dishonest way of hiding a contested premise, or if "understanding" isn't one thing but a bunch of seperate capacities — say so plainly and give me the closest defensible thing instead of a tidy procedure.
