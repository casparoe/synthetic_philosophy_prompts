I'm taking a philosophy of cognitive science seminar this term and we've been working through Dennett's three stances — the physical stance, the design stance, and the intentional stance — plus the real patterns idea that the patterns picked out by the higher stances can be just as real as the physical ones. I did an ML elective last year, and last night the two things collided and I can't tell if I've invented something or just relabled 'be pragmatic.' I call it the ensemble stance. The idea is that for messy systems — a glitching phone, a dog that might be sick or might just be acting neurotic, a start-up where the product keeps breaking, a language model mid-hallucination — each of the three stances captures part of the behavior but none captures all of it, and flipping between stances case by case loses information. So instead you run all three in parallel and treat their pattern of agreement and dissagreement as the real pattern you're tracking. You predict by taking a weighted average of what the physical, design, and intentional readings forecast, and you update the weights by how well each stance has done on that system recently. My stronger claim is that the disagreement itself has structure: in a failing phone, the intentional and design readings (I can't help it, I say my phone is 'trying' to update) systematically diverge a little before the physical stance notices anything, so the ensemble gives you an early-warning signal no individual stance can. So it isn't just a tiebreaker — it's a new predictive object. I want an honest assessment. Is this genuinely a fourth stance, or does it collapse back into the intentional stance because I'm treating the committee of stances as a little agent with beliefs and confidence weights? Does something like this already exist in the literature under another name? And give me the hardest case you can think of — a system where averaging across stances would be actively worse than committing to the single best one.
