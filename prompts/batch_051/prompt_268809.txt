I've been reconstructing an argument attributed to the Buddhist philosopher Dharmakīrti for the conclusion that everything real is momentary (kṣaṇika), where "momentary" means existing for exactly one instant. The argument turns on the idea that reality is just whatever is causally efficacious (arthakriyā). I have suppressed the historical apparatus and present the argument as I understand it; please evaluate the argument as stated, not its fidelity to Dharmakīrti.

(1) Whatever exists is capable of producing an effect.
(2) Any effect is produced at a definite time: there is some moment before which it does not exist and at which it comes to be.
(3) An entity that endures unchanged has exactly the same intrinsic nature at every moment at which it exists.
(4) If an entity has exactly the same intrinsic nature at two moments, then its causal capacity is exactly the same at those two moments.
(5) If a thing's causal capacity is exactly the same at an earlier moment t1 and a later moment t2, then any effect it produces at t2 it also produces at t1.
(6) But in the cases where we posit an enduring cause, the effect is produced at the later moment and not at the earlier one.
(7) Therefore no enduring entity can be the cause of an effect that appears at a definite time.
(8) Since (1) and (2) require every real thing to be a cause of some effect that appears at a definite time, and (7) rules out enduring entities as such causes, no real thing endures; everything real is momentary.

I find (1)–(4) independently plausible, and the conclusion seems to follow if (5) is granted, but (5) is where I suspect a trick. Is the argument valid as stated?
