I am a novelist planning a book about a person whose mind is gradually moved into a machine, and I want to settle the metaphysics before I commit to how the story handles it. I have been reading around in the idea, associated with Hofstadter, that a self is not a thing but a self-referential pattern: a loop in a system of symbols that can point at itself and treat that pointer as an "I." The idea attracts me, but I keep finding that my gut reactions to concrete cases do not line up neatly with it.

Here are my verdicts on the cases I care about. If a person's neurons are replaced one at a time over several years by functionally identical artificial units, and the person remains awake throughout and reports an unbroken sense of being the same person, I am fairly confident the same person survives. If a machine instead scans a person completely, destroys them in the process, and prints an exact physical replica elsewhere, my gut says the person who stepped into the machine died, and the replica is a new person, even though he remembers stepping in and feels continuous with the original. If the scan does not destroy the original, then plainly two people result: the original is the same person as before, and the copy is a distinct person who merely shares the original's past. If a computer runs a detailed self-model of a person, with memories, dispositions, and a self-referential symbol that refers to itself as "I," I am inclined to say there is a genuine self in that system, though I am unsure whether it is a continuation of the modeled person or a new self altogether. And if I write a character who becomes aware that he is a character and begins to refer to his own referring, I cannot shake the sense that the character has a real kind of self, a pattern that matters, even though he does not have a body at all.

These verdicts pull against each other. The gradual replacement and the destructive scan end in the same physical state and differ only in tempo and continuity, yet I want to say one preserves the person and the other does not. The teleporter verdict also sits badly with my sympathy for the pattern view, since the replica is as good a self-referential pattern as the original was. I am prepared to give something up, but I do not know which thing it should be.

Please give me the principle you would defend. State it precisely, say what it predicts about a case I keep vacillating on, namely a scan-and-replace procedure sped up to the edge of being instantaneous while the person is anesthetized, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts your principle rejects and why. Do not lay out the options for me to choose among; take a definite position and defend it.
