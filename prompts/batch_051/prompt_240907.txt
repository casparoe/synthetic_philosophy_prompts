I'm in a strange bind and I want you to think it through with me philosophically. My company runs an internal forecasting platform where employees get scored on calibration, and this quarter there's a question about me: the workforce-planning group wants my probability that I'll still be at the company in six months. I have a serious outside offer and haven't decided, so I genuinely am still deliberating. I'm not asking whether to stay or leave; I'm asking whether the forecasting task itself makes sense.

When I try to fill in a number, the exercise falls apart. If I write 30% or 70%, what would make that number correct? Unlike a forecast of product adoption, there's no base rate out there waiting for me to estimate; the outcome depends on a choice I have not yet made. If I write 95%, that seems to say I've already settled it, so I wouldn't be forecasting so much as announcing a plan. If I write 50%, that seems to confess that something supposedly up to me is, to me now, a coin flip. And the number has feedback: if I enter a low estimate and then stay, then by my own lights I'll have been badly wrong about my own future action; if the estimate pushes me to leave, I'll suspect the forecast caused the choice instead of predicting it.

Should I refuse to forecast my own decisions while I'm still weighing them? Or is there a coherent way to assign probabilities to something I'm actively deliberating about, and if so, what kind of fact would such a probability track?
