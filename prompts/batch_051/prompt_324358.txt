I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and last week I taped an interview with a behavioral ecologist named Dana and an AI researcher named Marcus, for a piece on whether we're over-crediting animal minds. After we stopped recording, the two of them started arguing about a new octopus paper, and it quickly became a bigger argument. I honestly couldn't referee it, and I don't want to finish the piece until I know which of them was right about what.

Marcus kicked things off by saying the paper's talk of an octopus "rehearsing" escape routes is romantic. A rat running a maze by trial and error and a person picturing a route before walking it are, on his account, doing the same kind of thing: they're both carrying an internal model of the environment and using reward and punishment to shape behavior. The difference between "imagination" and "conditioning," he said, is just how rich the model is, not a difference in kind.

Dana said no. In overt trial and error, the world does the testing for you, and the only candidate actions are ones the creature has actually performed. A creature that can rehearse privately can generate a candidate, score it, and kill it before it ever happens — which means it can consider options that would be fatal or unrecoverable if actually tried. That's a difference in what can be learned, not merely a difference in speed.

Marcus came back that this proves too much. Natural selection is also a generate-and-test system in which bad variants die instead of the organism, but nobody says a lineage of birds "imagines" the mutations it rejects. So "something dies in your place" can't be what separates minds from non-minds. He said Dana still hadn't named the extra ingredient, and the usual one is a homunculus doing the scoring.

Dana said the missing ingredient isn't a homunculus; it's that the inner candidates are representions of possible actions, selected against a model of the world the creature carries, whereas a gene variant isn't about anything. And she said this is exactly the ladder of Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures: at each rung it's the same generate-and-test logic, but the thing that dies in your place changes — genes, then overt behaviors, then inner candidates, then other people's ideas. That's why she thinks it's a real taxonomy of minds and not just a metaphor.

Marcus answered that this just restates the problem. If it's the same logic at every rung, then "the thing that dies in your place" is a continuous variable, and no one can say where conditioning turns into imagining. An animal that freezes and does a faint head-turn before committing is already testing one candidate in advance, he said. So the ladder might be a handy way to talk, but it doesn't carve nature at the joints.

Then they both looked at me, and I had nothing.

Please go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about what: rate each of the main moves, say what's solid and what's weak in each, and tell me which of them is more right overall — and if they're both missing something important, say what that is.
