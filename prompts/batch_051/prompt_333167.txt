I'm a PhD student in epistemology, and I've been going in circles on the Meno problem. The setup, roughly: a true belief about how to get to Larissa gets you to Larissa just as well as knowledge does, so why do we all want to say knowledge is the better thing to have? Most of the literature treats this as a question about what we mean by "valuable" or about the nature of knowledge, and my supervisor keeps telling me it's not the kind of thing you could test. I'm not convinced it's that closed to evidence.

Here's what I'd like you to do. Take the claim "knowledge is more valuable than mere true belief, over and above their equal practical success" as if it were a testable hypothesis. Design the strongest test you can think of: what would you measure, and what pattern of choices, judgements, or other behavior would count as evidence that agents value something about knowledge beyond the true belief's just being right? For instance, might people pay more to keep a belief that would survive misleading counterevidence, or prefer a belief they can articulate grounds for when challenged? And what result would count against the claim? Then I want you to be honest about the residue: what part of the claim, if any, could no test settle because it's doing evaluative or definitional work rather than describing something we could observe?

I'm half expecing the answer to be that the best test reveals we value "stability" or "non-luckiness," and that the philosophical question then just moves to whether those are what we meant by knowledge all along. But I'd like to see it laid out carefully.
