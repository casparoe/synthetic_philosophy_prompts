For the past several months I have been one of three trustees of a small mutual-aid fund, and for the first time the utility monster has become a practical problem rather than a seminar example: I have to defend, to the other trustees and to myself, how much extra weight to give to one recipient whose needs can absorb almost any amount of help, at the cost of many ordinary needs. I have tentatively settled on a prioritarian answer — a given gain in well-being counts for more when it goes to someone who is worse off — and I am writing a paper that asks whether this answer actually escapes Nozick's separateness-of-persons objection or merely relocates the monster into the choice of how much priority to give. The case I am commissioning from you is meant to force that question into the open.

Act as a professional moral philosopher who works on distributive ethics and formal theories of aggregation. Think carefully and do the construction in stages. Begin by brainstorming at least three candidate structures for the case and explicitly reject the ones that violate one of the constraints below; then present the case that survives.

Construct a two-individual allocation problem in which a prioritarian is driven to be more deferential to a utility monster than a classical utilitarian — someone who simply maximizes the unweighted sum of well-being — is. By that I mean: the classical utilitarian allocation feeds the monster up to some point and then stops once the next unit of resource would do more total good in the other person's hands; the prioritarian allocation, using a standard concave priority-weighting of well-being, continues to feed the monster meaningfully beyond that point and thereby leaves the other person worse off than the classical allocation would have left them. The monster must be an ordinary human being, not an alien, a sadist, or a stipulated being with infinite or ex nihilo utility: describe its condition realistically, give it an unusually high but finite and diminishing capacity to convert resources into well-being, and make clear why it still qualifies as a utility monster in the relevant sense. Use explicit small numbers for each person's starting level of well-being and for the marginal well-being each unit of resource produces. Keep the scenario to one ordinary setting — a fund, a clinic, or a household budget — with no exotic physics, no perfect information, and no one making an arithmetic error.

After presenting the scenario, run the check yourself. State the allocation each theory selects and show the calculation that produces it. Then identify the single structural feature of the case that produces the reversal — the low baseline, the specific shape of the monster's marginal-utility curve, the concavity of the priority weighting, or whatever it is — and say whether removing that one feature, with everything else held fixed, collapses the case back into the standard and expected result in which prioritarianism simply helps the worse-off person a little more. Finally, explain how a committed prioritarian would best resist the case — by capping the priority weight, by adding a sufficiency threshold, by rejecting the numbers, or by some other amendment — and say what that defense costs: which other intuitive recommendation the amended view can no longer make.

The point of the case is to test the view rather than to illustrate it, so the reversal must fall out of each theory's decision rule applied to the same two people and the same resources; a scenario that simply stipulates the verdicts fails.
