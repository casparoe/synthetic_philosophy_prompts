I'm a retired geochemist and I've recently started reading philosophy, mostly philosophy of causation. I also got asked to join the safety-review panel at a university field station, partly because I've written up a lot of incident reports in my own lab days. We meet after an incident, maybe once a month, and have to produce a short causal narrative for the record and for insurance. The part we argue about every single time is omissions: when does 'the postdoc didn't check the reaction' or 'nobody checked the weather' get written down as a seperate cause rather than just part of the background? If you include one omission you seem on the hook for including the omission of everyone who could have prevented the thing but didn't, which is absurd, and if you include none you miss what actually mattered. I've been reading around causation by omission and it feels like there's something usable there — about difference-making, normal conditions, what people were expected to do — but I can't turn it into something my panel colleagues would accept. Can you write me a decision procedure for the panel: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where we stop with a no, and which steps are genuine judgment calls where a person has to decide rather than something the checklist can settle. Constraints: we have about two hours, nobody on the panel has philosophy training, and the chair has to sign off on the final wording. Then run the procedure on two cases: (i) a lab fire where a postdoc was told to monitor a reaction and didn't; (ii) a field injury where nobody checked the weather forecast before a boat trip. I'd also like to see at least one case where the procedure forces us to say something we won't like, plus an honest note about where the procedure will misfire or what it can't settle. And please don't give me a reading list — I want the reasoning, not references. At the end, say how confident you are in the whole procedure and which step you are least sure about.
