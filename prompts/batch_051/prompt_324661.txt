we're doing the protestant ethic in my world history class — the idea that calvinist values about work, saving, and treating your earthly job as a calling helped make early capitalism take off in parts of europe. i made the mistake of bringing it up at dinner and my cousin (an econ major) and my aunt (sociology person) got into it, and now they both want me to say who was right. i have no clue. here is roughly how it went:

my cousin said it's basically unfalsifiable. you can't directly observe values, so if you infer someone's values from how they behaved, and then use those values to explain the same behavior, that's circular.

my aunt said you don't have to infer values only from behavior. historians use sermons, diaries, letters, and church rules to reconstruct what people said they valued before the economy shifted. that's evidence independent of the economic outcome. plus explaining actions by values is normal: we explain someone's studying by their ambition, and we can get evidence for the ambition from what they say and what they give up, not just from the studying.

my cousin said those texts are still behavior, and a calvinist merchant writing anxious stuff about salvation might be rationalizing what he's already doing for business reasons. and even if the values were real, the thesis doesn't say how much they mattered. saying "culture was one cause among many" is too vague to be a real explanation.

my aunt said causal explanations don't need exact weights. we say smoking causes lung cancer without being able to assign a precise percentage to any individual case. and weber's whole point was never that values were sufficient; it was a pushback on the idea that material and economic factors are the only causes. the question is whether values can be a cause at all.

my cousin said but if it's just "values can be a cause somewhere," that's trivial. everyone believes that. the actual thesis is supposed to explain a huge historical outcome, so the test should be comparative: other places had similar calvinist values and didn't industrialize first, so the cultural variable isn't doing the predictive work.

my aunt said that comparative point is fair, but it cuts both ways. one place can have the values but lack other necessary conditions like markets or property rights. absence of capitalism doesn't prove values were inert, just that they aren't sufficient. a weaker version — given the right institutions, those values made saving and reinvesting feel morally urgent — still survives and still argues against pure economic determinism.
