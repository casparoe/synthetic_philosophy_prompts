I am currently taking a seminar on medieval moral psychology, and we have been reconstructing Peter Abelard's account of sin. The tutor has been keeping a running list of verdicts on cases, guided by the idea that sin lies in consent rather than in the outward act or in the impulse and suggestion that precede consent. So far the tutor has ruled on four cases:

1. A monk is troubled by an unbidden sexual image and feels no pleasure in it; the tutor ruled he has not sinned.

2. Another monk is visited by the same kind of image and feels pleasure before he can collect himself, but as soon as he reflects he turns away and gives no assent; the tutor ruled he has not sinned, though the passing pleasure is a weakness or liability.

3. A merchant consents in his heart to commit adultery, but is prevented before taking any outward step; the tutor ruled he has sinned as fully, in the relevant sense, as if he had carried the deed through.

4. A clerk's hand is physically forced by another person against a document, producing a fraudulent signature he inwardly refuses; the tutor ruled he has not sinned, because the motion of his limbs was not his own and he gave no consent to the fraud.

We have now been given this fifth case before the tutor rules on it. A student, fully in control of his thoughts from the beginning, deliberately conjures up a vivid fantasy of an affair with a married classmate. He is resolved that nothing will happen in the outside world, and he would refuse the opportunity if it actually arose; but he does it because he knows the fantasy will give him pleasure, and he lets himself dwell in that pleasure at length, telling himself throughout that, since he has consented to no outward act, he has not sinned.

The assignment is to argue how the tutor should rule if he wants to be consistent with his own four earlier decisions. Should the student be judged to have sinned in the relevant sense, or merely to have indulged a weakness of the kind covered by the first two rulings? Which earlier ruling is the closest analogue, and why? If the four rulings genuinely leave this case open, set out the strongest principle that would support a verdict of sin and the strongest principle that would support a verdict of no sin, making clear which rule the tutor would be adopting under each. Then give your best recommendation, with reasons, and describe a further case whose resolution would force the two principles apart.
