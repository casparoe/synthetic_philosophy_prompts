I am trying to sort out what I actually think about the old Daoist trouble that some things are spoiled by the very effort to bring them about — the harder one aims at wu-wei, the further one lands from it. Rather than start from the texts, I want to start from my own verdicts on cases and see what principle, if any, they add up to.

Here are the cases as I honestly judge them.

1. Sleep. When I lie down telling myself to fall asleep and check every few minutes whether I am drowsy yet, I stay awake. On nights when I give up on sleeping and simply listen to the rain, sleep arrives quickly. Verdict: the direct attempt is self-defeating; the non-attempt succeeds.

2. Intrusive thought. During a long stretch of writing, telling myself not to think about an awkward email brings the email to mind every few minutes. If I simply notice the thought and let it be there without fighting it, it stops intruding. Verdict: trying to suppress backfires; refraining from the fight works.

3. Spontaneity. Before a party, commanding myself to be loose, quick, and funny makes me stiff and self-conscious. On evenings when I am too tired to care what impression I am making, people later tell me I was funny. Verdict: trying to be spontaneous fails precisely through the trying.

4. Skill. As a child, I improved at the piano fastest when a teacher isolated a two-bar passage and made me drill it slowly and deliberately, with the specific intention of correcting one error. Playing the piece straight through and letting it be natural felt better but changed nothing. Verdict: effortful, goal-directed trying works and beats non-trying.

5. Memory. In an exam, straining to remember a name I know blocks it; it surfaces five minutes after I stop. But deliberately using a mnemonic while studying helps me recall later. Verdict: effort applied at the moment of recall backfires; effort applied earlier, in studying, pays off.

6. Patience. Forcing myself in the moment to be patient — counting to ten because I promised I would — usually makes me angrier or produces a brittle, performative calm. Yet months of a nightly practice in which I deliberately hold my attention on irritating sounds have made me genuinely more patient. Verdict: direct trying in the moment backfires; deliberate indirect practice works.

I cannot see, just from these, what does the work. Is it that some targets can be caused directly and others only arise as by-products? Is it about attention and self-monitoring? Is it about the difference between states and skills? Or is my verdict in one of the cases simply wrong?

What I would like from you is this: give me the single principle — or the smallest set of principles — that best captures these six verdicts, stated precisely, with the feature of each case that drives the verdict. Tell me which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and would require me to overturn. Then apply the principle to the case I keep going back and forth on: deliberately setting aside ten minutes each morning to practice not trying — sitting with no agenda, no goal. Is that the skill case, where deliberate practice works, or the sleep case, where the attempt manufactures the very thing it is chasing? Or does your principle sort it under some third category? Say what it predicts and why.

End with a single line beginning "Summary:" that gives the one-sentence version of the principle you settle on — or, if you conclude no single principle will do, the one-sentence version of the dilemma that remains.
