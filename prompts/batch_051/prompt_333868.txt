I've been trying to get a handle on the absurdity doctrine — the idea that a court can sometimes refuse to apply a statute even when the words plainly cover the situation — by running a made-up ordinance through my own gut calls and seeing what pattern falls out. The ordinance: no person shall make, or cause to be made, any noise louder than 70 decibels within 100 feet of a hospital. My calls, just on instinct: a couple having a screaming fight on the sidewalk outside the maternity ward, cite them, easy; an ambulance coming in with the siren on, obviously don't cite, no court in its right mind would; a construction crew running a jackhammer during visiting hours, cite them, even thought they're just doing a job; a baby crying at full volume in its stroller by the entrance, I don't think you cite the mother, but 'cause to be made' seems to cover it and I can't say why not; a teenager standing just inside the 100-foot line screaming at his friends for fun, cite, even though he's probably not louder than the baby; the hospital itself testing a backup generator with a loud engine echoing off the wall, I'd be stunned if that got cited, but I can't tell you how it's different from the jackhammer; a cop's siren left on by mistake at the curb, I honestly keep flip-flopping; a street preacher with an amp at a bus stop across the street, cite, the good cause doesn't matter to me. Is there a principle that gets all of these, or nearly all, and which of my calls would a clean principle make me give up? And what does the principle say about the case I really can't settle: a parent who deliberately lets her toddler shriek because a nurse keeps ignoring them? I also can't decide whether the principle should be about what the legislature actually intended, what it would have intended if it had thought of the case, or just a backstop against applying laws where applying them is crazy.
