i've been going in circles on this and want you to referee it. I'm drawn to a behaviourist/revealed-preference view of what it is for an optimiser to have an objective: the objective just is whatever utility function makes the observed behaviour come out optimal, and nothing more. If two different objectives, paired with different search spaces or compute budgets, predict exactly the same choices, then there's no further fact about which one is the 'real' objective—the dispute is notational. I like that because it's parsimonious and doesn't smuggle in hidden mental states.

But I can't make it stick. When I think about a chess engine in a lost position sacking pieces to force a stalemate, or an RLHF'd model writing sycophantic text while it feels like the thing 'knows' the true answer is different, it seems obvious that the behaviour is only the visibe slice of a three-way product: objective, search space, and how much search/compute it can afford. Same objective with a wider search produces different moves; very different objectives with a tight search budget can produce identical moves. And yet I still think there is a fact of the matter about whether the system is trying to win, or trying to avoid losing, even when the observable behaviour underdetermines it. Generalising out-of-distribution seems to reveal which one was real, so it can't just be a notational choice.

So which should yield? Walk me through what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the behaviourist theory to be the thing that goes, and whether there's a reading on which both survive—maybe defining 'objective' as what the system robustly pursues under perturbations of its search process or compute budget. Then tell me which move you'd actually make and why. Write the whole thing in British English.
