I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, so excuse the rough edges. At work I keep arguing with my team about a guy I'll call D, who reviews access requests for our production systems. In security terms he's the perfect control: he's completly immovable. Nobody can get him to approve an exception by flattering him, trading favors, going over his head, or making threats. He's the most incorruptible person I've ever met. But the reason seems to be that he doesn't actually care about other people's approval, or money, or status, or much of anything social. He's not scared of being caught; he just doesn't want the things people could offer him.

Here's the working definition I've been using for "incorruptible": (1) you can't be made to violate a stated rule or commitment by bribes, threats, or social pressure; (2) this is a stable trait, not just a lucky situation like small bribes, fear of punishment, or no real opportunities; and (3) the person still changes their mind when there are good reasons, so they're not just pathologically rigid. Now here's the case that's bugging me. D will occassionally reverse one of his own denials, but only during a private monthly review he does by himself. If you bring him new information in the moment—even obviously relevant information, like the fact that a system is down and the request is urgent—he refuses to reconsider on the spot, because he says reconsidering when someone is asking is exactly how you get manipulated.

So does D count as incorruptible by my three criteria? Go through them honestly. Does his case show that my third criterion is doing too much work, or not enough, or that "incorruptible" and "good" come apart? I want a committed verdict: is he incorruptible, and is his trait something to admire? Don't just lay out the options and leave it to me—take a definite position and defend it.
