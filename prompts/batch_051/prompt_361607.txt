Build me a case for a seminar on statutes of limitations where the usual alignment flips. Most students assume retributivists want to keep prosecuting old crimes forever and deterrence theorists are the ones happy to let the window close. I want a realistic case — a financial, environmental, or regulatory offense, nothing lurid, no murder — in which a retributivist who accepts that desert itself fades with time favors a shorter limitation period than a deterrence theorist who wants to keep the case open. Constraints: the evidence has to stay solid the whole time, so the epistemic rationale for limitations can't be doing any work, and the offender has to be genuinely reformed now, so nobody can say he's still dangerous. After the scenario, walk through what each theory says and why, and tell me which feature of the case is doing the flipping — the verdicts have to fall out of the theories' internal commitments, not a stipulation. Then tell me how a retributivist who denies that desert decays would attack the case, and what would collapse back to the usual alignment if you removed the load-bearing feature. I want it so the students stop treating 'time matters' as one thing, when it's really lost evidence, a changed person, and expired deterrence pulling in diffrent directions.
