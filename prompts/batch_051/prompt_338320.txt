Two people in a reading group I was listening to argued about this passage from Thomas Aquinas’s Summa Theologiae, II-II, q. 64, a. 2:

“Now every part is directed to the whole, as imperfect to perfect, wherefore every part is naturally for the sake of the whole. For this reason we observe that if the health of the whole body demands the excision of a member, through its being decayed or infectious to the other members, it will be both praiseworthy and advantageous to have it cut away. Now every individual person is compared to the whole community, as part to whole. Therefore if a man be dangerous and infectious to the community, on account of some sin, it is praiseworthy and advantageous that he be killed in order to safeguard the common good, since ‘a little leaven corrupteth the whole lump’ (1 Corinthians 5:6).”

Their argument was about the standard trolley switch case.

Elena: In the trolley case, the person on the side track is innocent. Aquinas’s stated justification for killing is that the man is “dangerous and infectious to the community, on account of some sin.” So this passage cannot justify throwing the switch.

Marcus: The dangerousness condition is just the example he uses. The stated principle is “every part is naturally for the sake of the whole.” If the whole community’s preservation requires the loss of one part, then losing one part to save five parts is exactly what the principle directs. He says every individual person is compared to the whole community as part to whole, not merely guilty persons.

Elena: You are ignoring that the body analogy is specifically about removing a decayed or infectious member. That is a protective justification: remove the thing that is harming the body. The person on the side track is not harmful or infectious. You are treating him as available material rather than as a person who has to be made dangerous before he can be excised.

Marcus: But the principle is stated independently of the example. The diseased member is one case of part-for-whole sacrifice, not a criterion. If I can kill a guilty person for the common good, then a fortiori I can kill an innocent one only if the common good is even clearer and the killing is not punishment but mere consequence. I’m not endorsing pushing someone in front of the train, because there the death is the means; in the switch case death is a side effect.

Elena: That is the doctrine of double effect doing all the work, not this passage. And the cost is that footbridge and loop cases become hard to distinguish. You are importing a limit that does not appear in the text. The only limit in the text is that the person be dangerous and infectious.

Marcus: But your reading also imports a limit: that innocent persons cannot be sacrificed. So both of us are adding something.
