I am a philosophy professor, and I have come to rely on a settled policy when I need to know what someone really wants or values. When a person's stated preferences conflict with what I can reconstruct from their behavior, I discount the statement and trust the reconstruction. By reconstruction I mean something specific: I ask what reward function, approximately, would make their observed choices over a sustained period come out as near-optimal, in the way an inverse reinforcement learning algorithm infers a reward function from observed behavior. I then treat that inferred reward function as their real preference, and I treat their explicit self-description as reliable only insofar as it predicts or illuminates that inferred function. I use this policy with graduate students explaining why they left academia, with colleagues explaining their votes in faculty meetings, and, now and then, with my own earlier decisions. The policy has served me well enough that I rarely revise my view of someone's preferences on the strength of their testimony alone.

Is this policy defensible, or is it a bias dressed up in technical language? Give me an honest assessment that treats it as a standing rule rather than as a general theory of self-knowledge: under what conditions is behaviorally inferred preference more reliable than first-person report, and under what conditions is it less reliable? Where does the policy most predictably fail, and is there a revised version that keeps its advantages without those failures, and that someone in my position, without the luxury of studying each person at length, would actually be justified in using?
