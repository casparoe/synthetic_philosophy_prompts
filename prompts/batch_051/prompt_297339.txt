Consider the following position, which I will call tranquilism: the only intrinsic good in a human life is the absence of craving and the absence of suffering. Everything else ordinarily valued — pleasure, knowledge, love, achievement, moral virtue — is either of no intrinsic value or of value only insofar as it promotes or constitutes tranquility, understood strictly as the absence of craving and suffering. A defender of tranquilism is committed to evaluating lives, and deliberating about them, by that single criterion.

The following five objections to tranquilism are already on the table, and I take them to cover the familiar moves:

1. The insentient-limit objection. If only the absence of craving and suffering matters, then a stone, a corpse, or a permanently unconscious body would be as well off as any living person can be — or better. That is a reductio; it suggests the view leaves out consciousness, or positive value, or a subject for whom things can go well.

2. The epistemic objection. Craving and suffering are not mere noise. Grief, fear, frustration, and longing carry information about what one values and about how one's situation stands relative to those values. A life systematically stripped of these states would be deprived of that information and, by the view's own single standard, would be likelier to go worse rather than better.

3. The constitutive objection. Many of the goods whose loss we would not accept — deep attachment, trust, ambition, aesthetic absorption — are constitutively dependent on liabilities to longing, disappointment, and sorrow. One cannot have them while insulating oneself entirely from their negative correlates, so the pursuit of total tranquility would quietly sacrifice goods with at least as strong a claim on us as tranquility itself.

4. The self-defeat objection. Reaching and maintaining a state without craving takes a persistent directed desire — that is, a craving — for that very state and for the practices that produce it. A state of complete success would have no internal engine: no dissatisfaction to prompt planning, avoiding future harm, or continuing the discipline. The ideal therefore undermines the conditions of its own pursuit and maintenance.

5. The authenticity objection. Some suffering is the fitting response to a genuine loss or to something one values; grief is not merely a bad feeling attached to a loss but the appropriate acknowledgment of a relationship. To eliminate it, whether by training or pharmacology, is to falsify one's own evaluative commitments, not simply to make a life go better.

Give me one more objection to tranquilism that is not on this list and is not a rewording, special case, or combination of any of the above. State the objection clearly, identify which commitment of tranquilism it targets, and say what it adds that the five listed objections do not. Then assess it honestly: would a careful defender of tranquilism have a good reply to it?
