For a philosophy of psychiatry seminar I am writing a background document, and I have realized that debates about whether to add, split, merge, or retire a diagnostic category usually proceed by pointing at one favored case: a category that spread once it entered official manuals, a study in which reliability improved while validity did not, a patient community that began to describe itself in the language of its diagnosis. Before I read any more theories of classification, I want the full specification in place. Assume the goal is an account of psychiatric classification adequate for real use: it should guide decisions about revising official diagnostic manuals, support both population-level and mechanistic research, and not systematically mislead clinicians, patients, or institutions about what a diagnosis says and does.

Write the desiderata that any such account must satisfy. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp. The list should at least address: Ian Hacking's looping effect, on which the classified are self-interpreting and their responses to the label can alter the phenomenon the label picks out; the distinction between having a condition and how severe or impairing it is; the observation that genetic, neurobiological, and cognitive findings routinely crosscut current diagnostic boundaries; cultural and historical variation in symptom expression and prevalence; the tension between criteria that are reliable but thin and criteria that are valid but judgment-dependent; and the practical demand that a diagnosis earn its keep by guiding treatment, prognosis, or access to support. Flag any pairs of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. Then identify the single requirement that, in your judgment, does the most damage to the main positions on offer — realist accounts of psychiatric kinds, pragmatist accounts, and constructivist accounts — and say why, briefly. The list and its justification are what I most need; do not spend more than a short paragraph on the scorecard.
