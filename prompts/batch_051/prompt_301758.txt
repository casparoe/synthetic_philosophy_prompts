People are comfortable forecasting from base rates in modest matters, and we treat that comfort as rational. A restaurateur who ignores the failure rate of new restaurants because she cares more than other owners is deceiving herself; an investor who ignores the long-run record of active fund managers because he is exceptionally diligent is doing the same. Yet propose the same outside view—forecasting a case by placing it in a reference class of similar cases and letting the class's base rate discipline the prediction—when the case is one's own polity, and the same people resist. Earlier great powers, many of them convinced that their institutions, geography, or values made them exceptions, nonetheless declined; but suggest that the country I happen to belong to be forecast like a member of the class of past great powers, and even historically literate people will say the base rate does not apply here. We tend to call this humility when applied to a stranger's restaurant but reckless or disloyal when applied to our own country; a citizen of a rival power returning the favor looks to us like parochialism. Is anything more than parochialism justifying the asymmetry? Consider an American and a Chinese observer, each willing to apply the outside view to the other's polity but refusing it for their own. They cannot both be correct. What, if anything, could make one of them correct? Work through what a consistent outside view would say about at least one contemporary power, and then state whether the asymmetry between forecasting one's own country and forecasting other people's projects should be kept, reversed, or abandoned.
