I was arguing with a flatmate about whether direct democracy actually works, and he wheeled out Condorcet's jury theorem. In the version he gave: there are n voters choosing between two options, each has probability p > 1/2 of picking the right one, and the votes are independent. Then the majority is more likely to be right than any single voter, and as n grows the probability that the majority is right tends to 1. Fine. But I said the independence assumption is doing nearly all the heavy lifting: in an actual referendum, everyone is watching the same three news channels and reading the same dodgy posts, so people's errors are correlated. He told me to make that precise and then see whether the theorem survives.

So here is the version I want to work through. Suppose each voter left to themsleves has a probability 0.6 of getting a referendum question right. But before polling day, with probability 0.4, a widely watched TV segment airs; the segment is right with probability 0.6. If it airs, every voter simply follows it, so their votes are perfectly correlated. If it doesn't air, each voter votes independently exactly as in the classic setup. (Notice a voter's ex ante chance of being right is still 0.6.) Work through what Condorcet's theorem says here. Does the chance of a correct majority still tend to 1 as n grows? Does majority rule still beat every single voter? What is the asymptotic probability that the majority is right, and what does your number suggest about how much the independence assumption matters?
