I am a high school student in a philosophy-and-logic reading group that meets on Thursday afternoons, and I would like to close our final session, on quantified modal logic, with a poem. Last week we asked whether everything that exists exists necessarily. The discussion kept circling around two formulas that our advisor wrote on the whiteboard. The Converse Barcan formula, □∀xφ(x) → ∀x□φ(x), runs the conditional in one direction and is usually taken to rule out contingent existence: anything that actually exists exists in every possible world, so the answer to our question would have to be yes. The Barcan formula, ∀x□φ(x) → □∀xφ(x), runs in the opposite direction and is usually taken to rule out merely possible individuals: anything that could exist in some other possible world already exists in the actual one. I was struck by how the two formulas are almost perfect mirror images of each other, a universal quantifier and a necessity operator trading places across the arrow, and how that tiny syntactic difference flips the entire picture of which individuals there could have been.

Could you write a poem that makes this mirroring do the structural work? I would like a reverse poem (not a letter-by-letter palindrome, but the kind whose lines are written once and make a coherent claim read top to bottom and a different, converse claim read bottom to top) of roughly ten to fourteen lines. Read top to bottom, the poem should articulate the Barcan-formula picture: nothing is merely possible, and no other world's inventory outruns the actual world's. Read bottom to top, the same lines should articulate the Converse-Barcan-formula picture: nothing actual is contingent, and nothing that exists here slips out of existence at another world. The point where the forward reading turns back on itself should act as the arrow of the conditional, the place where antecedent and consequent trade roles, and it should leave the reader with the recognition that accepting both formulas leaves the inventory of what exists fixed across all the worlds. You may make the smallest changes of punctuation or minor wording needed for the reversed reading to remain grammatical and natural, but the reversal should carry the conceptual flip rather than merely decorate it. The poem need not contain formal symbols, but someone who knows the Barcan formulas should recognize exactly which commitment belongs to which direction, and someone meeting the issue for the first time should come away with the right distinction.
