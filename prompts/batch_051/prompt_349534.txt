In the Second Meditation of Descartes's Meditations on First Philosophy, Descartes makes an observation I will paraphrase. Looking down from a window, he says, he would ordinarily say that he sees men crossing the square, but what he strictly sees are only hats and coats, which could be covering automata; that they are men is a judgment made by the mind rather than something given by sight. I want to test a skeptical argument this passage suggests, which I will call the NPC argument. I half-suspect one of the steps is doing more work than it should.

(1) Sensory perception presents me only with outer surfaces, clothing, movement, and sound; it never itself presents me with a mind.

(2) My belief that a human-looking body is inhabited by a mind is therefore a judgment that goes beyond what sensory perception presents.

(3) If a judgment goes beyond what sensory perception presents, then it can be justified only by an inference from the observed features to an unobserved mind.

(4) Any inference from observed features to an unobserved mind must rely on some general principle connecting bodily appearance and behavior with mental life.

(5) The only plausible principle is something like: bodies that look and behave this way are accompanied by minds like mine. But this principle cannot be justified from my own case alone, because a single observed correlation between my body and my mind cannot establish a generalization to all similar bodies. And any appeal to other people as further confirming cases already assumes that those bodies have minds, which is exactly what the inference is supposed to show.

(6) Therefore my belief that other people are minds rather than mindless automata dressed in hats and coats is not justified.

Which step, if any, fails? If more than one fails, identify the first point at which the argument goes wrong, and say whether a repaired version of the argument could still support the conclusion.
