I recently had a long argument with a friend about whether the market is best understood as an epistemic institution, and I came away with a set of positions that I would like checked for consistency. I am holding: (1) Hayek is right in The Use of Knowledge in Society when he says that the knowledge of circumstances "never exists in concentrated or integrated form but solely as the dispersed bits of incomplete and frequently contradictory knowledge which all the separate individuals possess," and that the price system is what enables agents to act on this dispersed knowledge. (2) The no-trade theorem gets something right, understood informally: when two rational agents have common priors and know they differ only in information, it is irrational for them to trade purely on that informational difference, because the counterparty's willingness to trade is itself information. (3) Real-world markets are epistemically impressive: prices seem to reflect aggregated local knowledge better than any central procedure. (4) Speculative trade can be rational when traders use different models or have different priors, even if it would not be rational under common priors and a common model. I am no longer sure these all hang together. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and how severe is the deepest tension? What is the least costly revision that would make the set consistent?
