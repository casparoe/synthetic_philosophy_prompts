Here's my current gloss. Prototype theory says a concept is a stored summary, roughly a central tendency over a set of weighted features, and typicality is distance to that summary. Exemplar theory says there is no summary stored; you classify new items by comparing them to remembered instances. Theory theory says a concept gets its content from its place in a naive causal-explanatory theory of the domain, so it's more like a small structured model than a list of features or a cloud of instances. My translation: prototype theory is a parametric model where the fitted centroid is the sufficient statistic and the rest of the exemplars are thrown away; exemplar theory is a nonparametric model where the training instances are the model and categorization is nearest-neighbor-ish similarity; theory theory is a generative model with latent causal variables and a prior over mechanisms. But I suspect that translation quietly turns their fight into a bias–variance tradeoff, and I don't trust that.

For the residue report, the specific spots I want checked are: (1) whether a prototype is stored as such or reconstructed on the fly; (2) graded typicality, family resemblance, and the difference between high family resemblance and high cue validity; (3) why exemplar theory was supposed to handle non-linearly separable categories that prototype theory can't, and whether that's just "nonparametric wins when the boundary is weird"; (4) conceptual combination, like "pet fish" taking on properties from neither constituent, and whether a generative-model reading predicts that or just accommodates it after the fact; (5) the developmental story that kids shift from something exemplar- or prototype-like to something more theory-like, plus adult revision of naive theories; (6) the fact that people can learn a new concept from one or two examples rather than a few hundred training cases.
