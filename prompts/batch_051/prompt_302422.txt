So here's roughly where I've landed after a long argument with a friend about whether Mill's harm principle and his actual views on India and colonial rule can be squared. (1) For competent adults, the harm principle is right: the only justification for coercing someone is preventing harm to others; making someone better off against their will never justifies it. (2) I also think Mill had a point that a society can be, as he thought, not yet ready for representative government, and that a period of non-consensual improving rule can be legitimate when the alternative is civil war or worse oppression, so long as the ruler is actually trying to develop the population rather than extract from it. (3) I think the British suppression of suttee was justified, because in many cases the woman had no real choice and the practice was enormously destructive. (4) More generally, I think a colonial power can permissibly prohibit harmful traditional practices in a subject population without waiting for the population's consent, when the harm is serious and the prohibition is enforced for real rather than as a pretext. (5) But I also think liberty is a universal value that doesn't track race, culture, or level of civilization, and any theory that treats whole populations as unready for liberty is very often an ideological cover for domination. (6) And I think paternalism toward adults is wrong even when adults choose badly, because coercing them for their own good fails to respect them as agents. I honestly cannot tell wether this is a consistent package or whether I am quietly helping myself to something the rest of it should rule out. Could you audit the set: which commitments actually pull against each other, which pairs are only apparently in tension, and if you had to restore coherence with the least damage to the overall position, where would you cut and what would that cost?
