I have been drafting an argument for a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of causation, and it now seems to prove too much. I would like a diagnosis, not a summary.

The argument runs as follows:

(1) If causal relations are real, then either they are fundamental constituents of the world or they reduce without remainder to something non-causal that is fundamental.
(2) Causal relations are not fundamental. This is Russell's familiar charge, brought up to date: the fundamental laws of physics are stated as differential equations relating states, and they contain no primitive notion of one event producing another.
(3) Nor are causal relations reducible. Each main form of reduction fails in a way that looks structural. Regularity theories conflate correlation with causation; counterfactual theories mishandle preemption and overdetermination unless causal facts are already built into the device that ranks worlds; probabilistic theories cannot separate genuine causation from common causes; and interventionist theories characterize causes through interventions, which are themselves causal.
(4) Therefore causal relations are neither fundamental nor reducible.
(5) Any notion that is neither fundamental nor reducible, yet is offered as a real feature of the world rather than as a heuristic, should be eliminated.
(6) Therefore causal relations should be eliminated: talk of causes is at best a useful façon de parler, and the world contains no causation.

The trouble is that this conclusion proves too much. Run the same standard on belief, valid inference, or evidence — none of which appears in the fundamental equations, and each of which faces unresolved reduction problems of its own — and those too would have to be eliminated. The argument also seems to rely on the very thing it banishes, since it presents its premises as supporting the conclusion and as considerations that ought to move a rational agent.

Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? I would like a committed diagnosis rather than a menu of options: identify the step or inference you reject, explain why it looked plausible, and state what rejecting it costs the eliminativist about causation. For what it is worth, my own instinct is to keep premise (5) and resist the conclusion, but I cannot make that instinct precise.

Please give your answer as exactly five numbered items, each no more than two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
