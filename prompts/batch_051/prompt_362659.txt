You are a historical sociologist who works on long-run social change and is known for making comparative concepts precise rather than telling single-case stories. I am putting together a seminar on the concept of "collapse," and I have hit a wall: whenever we discuss a case — the end of the Bronze Age palatial world, the Classic Maya lowland cities, Norse Greenland, the eclipse of the Western Roman Empire while the eastern empire carried on — the argument immediately turns on what collapse even is. Some scholars say the Maya collapsed while Maya people clearly did not disappear; others say Easter Island was never an ecocidal collapse but a story of adaptation and resilience; still others say "collapse" is just a value-laden word for transformation. I do not want to adjudicate individual cases before I have the standard of adjudication.

I want the specification: a list of requirements that a theory (or account, or definition — your call how to frame it) of societal collapse must meet to count as adequate for classifying and comparing cases, not merely retelling one narrative. For each requirement, give the motivation — the case, distinction, or result that forces it. Make sure the list covers at least the following: the Late Bronze Age transition, where the palatial economy ends in some places yet many settlements and material traditions continue; the Classic Maya, where urban centers empty out over generations while Maya populations and practices persist; the Norse abandonment of Greenland, where a whole society ends in one place but its parent culture continues elsewhere; and the Western Roman Empire set against its eastern continuation. In addition, run your criteria through this vignette I constructed to trip people up: a regional polity shows a sharp drop in population, an end to monumental building and elite record-keeping, and a collapse of long-distance trade within two generations — but villages remain continuously occupied, local pottery evolves without a break, and there is no sign of conquest or famine. Does that count as collapse, and if your criteria cannot answer, fix them until they can.

Then do three things. First, mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp (suddenness, single-cause explanations, the insistence that collapse must be involuntary or catastrophic, and so on). Second, identify the single requirement that excludes the most popular candidate views — single-cause environmental accounts, complexity-cost accounts, and the revisionist line that collapse is nothing but transformation — and explain why it does the most work. Third, check whether any subset of your requirements is jointly unsatisfiable given how heterogeneous the canonical cases are; if so, say which subset and what that tells us about the concept itself. Begin by brainstorming the candidate criteria before you settle on the final list, and reason through the vignette before fixing the wording of any requirement.
