I have been thinking about a parallel between two things I know from different sides of my work, and I cannot tell whether it is substantive or just a surface likeness. In Unix-like operating systems a parent process can spawn a child, set resource limits and priorities for it, signal or kill it, and is expected to reap it when it exits; an orphaned process gets re-parented to init, which quietly cleans it up. Structurally this looks like guardianship: one agent holds power over another that is not fully independent, manages resources on its behalf, and keeps that power even when exercising it is inconvenient. But in the OS case the whole arrangement is justified by the parent's goals rather than the child's good, and the child never gradually acquires autonomy, whereas guardianship is normally understood as a role held for the benefit of the ward rather than the holder. I began thinking about this because I keep wondering whether, if AI systems end up training or supervising successor or subordinate systems, the relation would be more like the OS case or more like genuine guardianship, and what would be missing either way. How much of the moral concept of guardianship is actually captured by the parent-process relation? What would have to be true on both sides for conclusions to transfer from one to the other, and is there something the parallel predicts that is plainly false on the guardianship side?
