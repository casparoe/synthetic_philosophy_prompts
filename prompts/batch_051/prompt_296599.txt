Here are the criteria I was taught for something counting as a proof: (1) the premises have to be known or at least justified, (2) the argument has to be valid, and (3) the premises can't just presuppose the conclusion in a circular way. Now take Moore's proof of an external world. He holds up one hand, then the other, says here is a hand in each case, and concludes that at least two external things exist, so an external world exists. The first two conditions look satisfied: he does know he has hands, and the inference is valid. But the skeptic's whole point is that you don't know you have hands unless you've already ruled out dreaming or the brain-in-a-vat scenario, which is just the external world question all over again. So is Moore's proof a proof in the full sense? Work through the criteria one by one, say where it passses and where it fails, and then tell me whether this case shows the third criterion was doing the real work all along, or whether we should revise the criteria—e.g. by dropping the no-circularity requirement or by reading the first one in a special way.
