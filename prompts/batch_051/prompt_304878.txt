I have spent the last few weeks trying to decide whether to make a costly commitment I will not want to honor later, and the advice I keep receiving is that the rational thing is to make myself into the sort of person who honors it. That led me to Parfit's hitchhiker, and the argument I built from it has ended somewhere I cannot accept. I want to know where it goes wrong — or what I have to give up to stop it.

The familiar case: I am stranded in a desert, and a driver will rescue me only if I promise to pay him once I am safely home. We both know that, once home, paying is a pure loss to me, and that a rational maximizer of my interests would break the promise. So the driver will not help unless I already have a standing disposition to keep such promises even when keeping one costs me and gains me nothing; only such a disposition makes my future payment credible. Yet a standing disposition to act against my interests when the cost is real is exactly the kind of disposition we would normally call irrational. This is the chain I arrive at:

(1) In the hitchhiker case, the option that best serves my interests overall is to be rescued.
(2) Whether I am rescued depends on the driver's being able to rely on my later payment, and what he can rely on is not my present intention but the agent I actually am by the time I get home.
(3) So if I am rational now, I should take whatever steps now would make my future self the kind of agent who pays — most plausibly by cultivating a disposition to keep promises even when keeping them no longer serves me.
(4) If this is right, then rationality can require me to make myself irrational, at least in a localized way.
(5) But the same logic applies to belief. Consider a person who would be happier, calmer, and more effective if she genuinely believed something the evidence does not support: that a risky undertaking will succeed, that her spouse is faithful, or that she will recover from an illness. The benefit exists only if the belief is really held; a self-deception the person can see through is just pretense and does none of the practical work. By parity with (3), she should take steps to make herself the kind of believer who believes what is useful rather than what the evidence supports, whenever doing so would make her life go better.
(6) But that cannot be right. An account of rationality cannot reasonably tell a person to engineer flatly false beliefs in herself merely because they are useful. That is not a surprising consequence to be managed; it is a reductio.
(7) Therefore either (4) is false after all, or there is a principled difference between cultivating promise-keeping dispositions and cultivating useful false beliefs.

I am inclined to keep the hitchhiker lesson in (1) through (4) and reject the extension in (5), but I cannot state the principle that separates the two cases without it looking ad hoc. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it commit me to?
