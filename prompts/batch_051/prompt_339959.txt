I'm a PhD student writing on Cicero's old-age stuff, and I'm stuck on the logic of one of Cato's moves in De Senectute. One of the complaints he answers is that old age is deprived of nearly all pleasures, and his reply feels like it slides between two senses of "pleasure." I put it in premise form like this:

(1) Sensual pleasure (voluptas) is bad for us — it corrupts reason and virtue, and nothing is more dangerous to the mind.
(2) Old age weakens or removes precisely those sensual pleasures.
(3) So being deprived of sensual pleasure isn't a mark against old age; if anything it's a point in its favor.
(4) But old age isn't without pleasure: conversation, study, farming, and the memory of a good life supply a calmer, more stable kind of delight.
(5) Therefore the complaint that old age takes away life's pleasures is false; it only looks true if we tacitly restrict "pleasure" to sensual pleasure.

I cant figure out whether this is a valid argument that draws a legitimate distinction, or whether "pleasure" just shifts between (3) and (4) — something bad that's lost vs. something good that's retained — and the conclusion leans on the shift. If a step fails, tell me exactly which one and whether any reading of it saves the argument. If it's valid, say what load each step is actually bearing.
