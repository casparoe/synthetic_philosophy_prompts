I work on the metaphysics and ethics of death, and I have been teaching the Epicurean argument in its standard two-part form. The first part is the no-subject premise: nothing can be bad for a person except in virtue of something they experience, and since death is the end of experience there is no subject left for death to harm. The second part is the symmetry argument: the time before one's birth and the time after one's death are both periods in which one does not exist, and since the former is not a misfortune, the latter is not either. The standard deprivation reply rejects the first premise, holding that death is bad for the person who dies not because of anything suffered but because it deprives them of life they would otherwise have lived; it also rejects the symmetry by saying that, while one could have lived longer, one could not have been born earlier without being a different person, so prenatal nonexistence deprives one of nothing.

Here is the variation I want to work through. Suppose a person undergoes a procedure that annihilates them completely for one year — no body, no experiences, no continuity of any kind — and then reliably reconstitutes them with their psychology and identity intact, so that they live out the rest of the life they would have had. Stipulate that the person after the gap is the same person as before it, that the procedure is known in advance to be safe, and that the only thing lost is the year itself.

How should the two sides analyze the missing year? The no-subject premise seems to say the gap cannot be bad for the person, since during the gap there is no subject and nothing is experienced. The deprivation account seems to say the year is bad, since the person would otherwise have lived it. If the deprivation account is right about the gap, does that show the no-subject premise is doing the real work in the Epicurean argument, with the symmetry claim as a supplement the gap case does not engage? If the Epicurean argument is right about the gap, does it overgenerate by forcing the conclusion that a lost year of life is not a loss? Work through what each standard position should say about the temporary-annihilation case, and say which position the case puts more pressure on and why.
