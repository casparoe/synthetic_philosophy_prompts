Last night a friend and I argued for several hours about whether a country should try to rise in the World Happiness Report, and he left me holding an argument that seems to prove more than either of us believes. He convinced me that each step is plausible, yet the reasoning appears to show that governments should stop pursuing health, education, and prosperity as well, which cannot be right. Here is the argument in the form he finally wrote out:

(1) The rankings rest chiefly on self-reported life evaluation, above all the Cantril-ladder item that asks people to rate their own lives on a 0-to-10 scale stretching from the worst possible life they can imagine to the best possible life they can imagine.
(2) A score produced that way can move for reasons that have little to do with how good people's lives actually feel or go: who is sampled, how the question is translated, where it sits in the survey, what counts as the best and worst imaginable life in a respondent's frame of reference, and what respondents take to be an acceptable answer.
(3) If a state officially commits itself to climbing the ranking, it makes the score a target.
(4) Once a score is made a target, incentives gather around the number itself: ministries are assessed by it, statisticians are directed to improve it, and governments acquire reasons to encourage favorable reports rather than to change the conditions the reports are supposed to describe.
(5) So a state that resolves to top the rankings will tend to improve its score rather than improve its citizens' lives; the measure will drift away from the thing it measures exactly when it is pursued most intently.
(6) A measure that systematically detaches from the underlying good when it is adopted as a target is not a sound basis for public policy.
(7) Therefore no country should make topping the happiness rankings an aim.
(8) But the same reasoning applies, step for step, to GDP per capita, average life expectancy, literacy, vaccination coverage, and every other quantity a government might track: each has been gamed somewhere, and each becomes gameable once it is named as the goal.
(9) Yet we would not conclude that governments should stop aiming at economic security, longevity, or literate, healthy populations just because the associated numbers can be gamed.
(10) So at least one of the premises must go.

I have an instinct that self-reported happiness must be special, that survey responses are softer than death records or school enrollments. But my friend answered that mortality statistics have been miscoded to meet targets, GDP counts expenditures that do not improve life, and literacy figures have been inflated by governments anxious about rankings; the difference is one of degree, not of kind, and a matter-of-degree difference cannot do the work step (8) needs it to do. That reply left me without my preferred exit.

I want very much to keep (9), and I would give up (7) before I gave up (9), but I cannot see how to reject (7) without rejecting (6), and (6) struck me as the tightest premise in the chain. I am no longer sure whether the argument is valid with one costly implausible premise, or whether something slid by between steps. Which step should be rejected, and what exactly does rejecting it cost?
