I am an undergraduate currently taking a course in the philosophy of perception, and I have been working through the debate over disjunctivism and the argument from illusion. I find myself pulled in two directions, and I want help thinking the conflict through rather than a quick verdict.

On reflection, I am drawn to the disjunctivist position: a veridical perception and a subjectively indistinguishable hallucination are not two instances of a common kind of mental state. When I see the table, my experience is directly and constitutively a relation to the table; when I hallucinate a table, I am in a different kind of state altogether, one with no shared experiential core. I like the view's systematic virtues. It keeps perception in direct contact with the world, it respects the transparency of experience, and it blocks the familiar path to scepticism. If the good case and the bad case shared a common factor, then what I am immediately aware of would plausibly be that factor rather than the table itself, and the veil of perception would fall back into place. Disjunctivism strikes me as the cleanest way to avoid that outcome.

And yet I cannot shake the intuition that there must be a common factor after all. If a hallucination is indistinguishable from a veridical perception, if it seems exactly the same from the inside, how can the two fail to be the same kind of mental state? There is something it is like for me in both cases, and that something seems identical. This is the core of the argument from illusion or hallucination: because the two cases are indiscriminable, the state I am in must be the same in both, and that common element, the experience itself, is what I am directly aware of. I do not want to accept sense-data or the veil of perception, but the intuition behind this argument feels firmer than the disjunctivist's claim that the two cases merely share the property of being indiscriminable while differing in their underlying natures. Disjunctivism sometimes seems to be resolving the problem by stipulation: the hallucination merely seems to be of the same kind as the perception.

Please present the strongest version of each side: the best case for the disjunctivist denial of a common kind, and the best case for the common-factor intuition that drives the argument from illusion. Then give me a negotiated verdict. Should the intuition be debunked, for instance as an over-inference from the fact that I cannot tell the two states apart to the conclusion that they are identical in nature, and if so, what exactly does the debunking work? Should disjunctivism be amended or abandoned instead, and if so, what would an amendment need to preserve in order still to count as disjunctivism? Or is there a reading of "same kind of mental state" on which the two views are compatible, and how would I distinguish that from a merely verbal compromise? I want to know what would justify each of these moves, not just which side you favour.
