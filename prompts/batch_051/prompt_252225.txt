I served on a jury last year, and it started me thinking about how we choose the people who make decisions for the rest of us. I found myself trusting the jury more because its members were chosen by lot; I would rather be judged by that randomly selected group than by professional jurors, however experienced. But when I imagine a legislature chosen the same way, I recoil — the idea seems unstable, and it seems to me that drafting and amending laws is a skill that would get lost. Meanwhile I am inclined to trust a citizens' assembly convened for one year to produce a climate plan more than the elected committee that would otherwise do it, even though the assembly members lack experience. I support term limits for mayors, governors, and presidents, but I oppose them for judges, because I want an experienced judge and see no point in forcing a good one to retire.

Where I really start to tangle myself up is on career politicians. Part of me thinks incumbents who never leave are a problem, and that term limits for legislators would be a healthy way to rotate them out. But another part of me thinks that drafting a budget or negotiating a trade agreement is genuinely hard, and that someone who has done it for twenty years is probably better at it than a first-timer. I would like to know whether there is a single principle, or a small set of principles, behind these verdicts. If there is not, which of my verdicts would I have to give up, and what would the resulting view say about the cases I keep going back and forth on: term limits for city council members, a randomly selected body that sits permanently rather than for a single task, and an allotted citizens' chamber that can block legislation but not write it?
