In my doctoral work on comparative electoral institutions, I keep returning to a puzzle about what compulsory-voting systems presuppose about the content of a vote. Take the Australian arrangement. Citizens who fail to participate in a federal election are liable to a fine, yet the ballot is secret: nothing in the process could later reveal how any given citizen voted. A citizen who arrives, has their name marked off, and drops an empty or deliberately spoiled paper into the box has, in effect, completed their obligation — the state is in no position to say that they failed to vote, and the electoral authority separately counts and publishes these papers as an informal-ballot category. The same institution therefore appears to treat casting a ballot as a strict civic duty, while treating the content of the ballot as something the state may neither require nor inspect, and treating an empty ballot as a completed act. What theory of what a vote is does this bundle of practices presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as possible, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely tolerates or fits with, and say whether the three features presuppose one consistent view or are underdetermined between rival views. Then test the bundle against three familiar pictures of voting — as an expression of the voter's judgment, as a duty to deliberate and thereby affect the outcome, and as an instrument for choosing the least bad among the likely winners. Which, if any, of these pictures rationalizes all three features of the practice? If none does, which feature should give way first, and what would the revised institution look like under the surviving picture?
