Me and a friend got into a long argument last night and I can't stop picking at it. I'd been saying that using one of the chat-style language models as a check on your own reasoning is basically worthless, maybe worse than worthless, since it mostly reflects whatever you prompt into it and is tuned to be agreeable. He said the peer-disagreement literature doesn't care what the peer is made of: if something that seems about as reliable as you on the topic disagrees with you, that's higher-order evidence you've made a mistake, and on the equal weight view you should drop your confidence substantially. I get the standard move — you can't just dismiss the other guy's answer by rehearsing the very reasoning he's rejecting, that's question-begging — but it seems to me the whole argument assumed the peer is an independant agent who actually made up their mind. A language model isn't independent in that sense. Its outputs are a statistical mash of human text, and if I've fine-tuned it or fed it a long prompt, its "disagreement" could just be my own patterns echoed back with a confident tone. So my question is whether the equal weight / conciliationist argument survives that change. Does the argument still go through when the apparent peer is a model whose outputs are downstream of both aggregate human text and my own input? Which premise exactly is doing the work, does it fail for a non-independent peer, and can it be repaired into something weaker but still true? If the argument is just dead in this case, I want to know what the remaining principle should be — what exactly am I prohibited from doing, and why, when the only disagreement I can find is with a system that learned from me?
