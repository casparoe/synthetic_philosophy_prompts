Next month I am presenting a paper at a workshop on testimony and disagreement. The paper defends a reconstruction of the Mīmāṃsā position that the Veda's authorlessness (apauruṣeya), combined with the doctrine of the intrinsic validity of cognitions (svataḥ prāmāṇya), yields a coherent and underappreciated epistemology of testimony: default acceptance is rational because the usual grounds for suspicion — an identifiable author's interests, competence, or sincerity — are absent, and doubt must be earned by a specific defeater rather than required in advance. The audience consists of analytic epistemologists working on testimony and disagreement, roughly half reductionists and half anti-reductionists, plus two specialists in classical Indian philosophy who are not Mīmāṃsā partisans: one works on Nyāya, the other on Buddhist epistemology. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by a combination of how likely they are and how much damage they would do, phrased the way these people would put them; for each, give a two-minute reply, say what I should concede and what I should hold, and note where the reply is weakest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and tell me which is the greater liability with this audience — the authorless-source claim or the intrinsic-validity claim.
