A judge's bench book includes the following instruction, intended to prevent juries from deferring to a majority or to a forceful individual: "You must decide solely on the evidence presented in court. You must not abandon your own assessment of that evidence merely because another juror disagrees with you." A juror in a fraud trial has followed the evidence closely and is strongly convinced that the defendant is guilty. Mid-deliberations, the juror privately receives a letter from their own consultant psychiatrist, written before the trial but opened only now, stating that a medication the juror is taking is likely to have temporarily reduced their ability to weigh probabilistic evidence, and that the juror's current confidence in complex inferences should be treated with materially less trust than usual. The letter is not court evidence and is not shared with the other jurors.

Does the instruction, read as a principle rather than only as a rule about peer pressure, require the juror to retain their first-order assessment? Or does treating the letter as higher-order evidence about one's own reliability fall outside the instruction's intended reach? How should the juror square the instruction with the claim that one may rationally lower confidence in a first-order judgement without committing epistemic akrasia? Please answer in British English.
