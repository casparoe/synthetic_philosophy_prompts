I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy, and I've been running a semester-long adversarial collaboration between two faculty members in my department. One is a committed act-utilitarian who treats maximizing total well-being as the criterion of rightness; the other holds a broadly Rawlsian–Nozickian separateness-of-persons view, on which there is a deep moral difference between trade-offs within one life and trade-offs across different lives. They have worked past the familiar organ-harvesting and life-for-life cases, and they are now stuck on the following dispute. The utilitarian concedes that explicitly summing expected utilities is often a poor decision procedure, but insists this is a pragmatic, epistemic point that leaves aggregation intact as a criterion of rightness. The critic replies that separateness constrains the criterion itself: an interpersonal trade-off is not fully justified just because it produces a greater total good, since no single subject enjoys that net gain, and each separate person is owed a justification that does not treat her as a mere container of value to be dissolved into an aggregate. They have agreed to co-author a short joint statement recording what the collaboration has actually established.

You are a professional philosopher specializing in normative ethics and the structure of moral theories. Please think carefully about which formulations each side could honestly sign, and begin by brainstorming the possible readings of the separateness objection before settling on the strongest one. Then draft the document they should have produced. It should contain: (1) a numbered agreed-claims section, in wording both parties can accept, covering what they share about persons, well-being, aggregation, and the role of decision procedures; (2) a disagreements table with columns for the neutral statement of each residual disagreement, the utilitarian's preferred formulation, the critic's preferred formulation, the crux that would most efficiently settle that disagreement, a concrete case or argument both accept as bearing on the crux, and each side's advance prediction about it. In particular, separate disagreements that are genuinely about the metaphysics of persons from disagreements that are really about decision procedures, and identify any disagreement that appears to be partly verbal, reformulating the substantive residue. Aim for formulations that are neither vacuous nor slanted.
