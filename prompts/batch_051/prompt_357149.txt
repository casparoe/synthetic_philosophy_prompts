A game theorist I know has been pressing the following argument on me, and although it seems to prove too much, I cannot locate the step where it first goes wrong. It concerns iterated deletion of strictly dominated strategies when mixed strategies are allowed as dominators.

Let G be a finite normal-form game. Say that a pure strategy c of player i is strictly dominated when there is some strategy of i that is still available in the current reduced game — pure or mixed — that gives i a strictly higher payoff than c against every pure profile of the opponents' remaining strategies. Iterated deletion removes strictly dominated strategies stage by stage until none remain.

(1) Suppose c is strictly dominated by a mixture λa + (1−λ)b of two pure strategies a and b, with 0 < λ < 1.

(2) Fix any pure profile of the other players. A weighted average of two numbers cannot exceed a third number unless at least one of the two numbers exceeds it. So at every profile, either a gives a strictly higher payoff than c, or b gives a strictly higher payoff than c.

(3) Since this holds at every profile, one of the two strategies must give a strictly higher payoff than c at every profile. There are only two strategies, and each profile must be covered by one of them; a set of profiles cannot be covered by two strategies working piecemeal without one strategy covering the whole set.

(4) Therefore c is strictly dominated by a pure strategy.

(5) Repeating the same reduction, any pure strategy that is strictly dominated by a mixed strategy is strictly dominated by a pure strategy.

(6) Hence iterated deletion of strictly dominated strategies has the same outcome whether or not mixed strategies are admitted as dominators, and the machinery of mixed-strategy dominance is redundant.

I am convinced that (6) cannot be right, and I want to hold on to the standard view that dominance by mixed strategies is a genuine strengthening of dominance by pure strategies. But I cannot see where the argument from (1) to (5) fails. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, what would accepting the argument commit us to about the relationship between strict dominance and never being a best response, and where would that commitment go wrong?
