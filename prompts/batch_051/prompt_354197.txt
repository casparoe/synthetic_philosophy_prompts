In a seminar on the ethics of memory, we read Avishai Margalit’s *The Ethics of Memory* and David Rieff’s *Against Remembrance*. Their positions pull in opposite directions. For Margalit, the obligation to remember the victims of great wrongs is not a civic nicety but something rooted in the thick relations that make us members of a shared ethical community; to forget is, in his view, to wrong the dead a second time. Rieff, by contrast, suspects that collective remembrance often functions as a kind of moral vanity: it lets a polity congratulate itself on its sensitivity while keeping old wounds open, and he argues that forgetting is sometimes the only thing that makes peace possible.

I want to test these two claims against Spain’s transition from Franco. In the late 1970s, Spain returned to democracy under an informal pact of forgetting: the 1977 Amnesty Law shielded both Franco-era officials and anti-Franco activists from criminal liability, no truth commission was created, and public life largely avoided a systematic reckoning with the Civil War and the dictatorship. The trade was that forgetting bought stability. Beginning in the 2000s, however, Spain partially reversed that settlement: the 2007 Historical Memory Law formally condemned the Franco regime, ordered the removal of Francoist symbols, and recognized victims, and the years since have seen mass-grave exhumations and growing demands to annul Franco-era judicial sentences and prosecute surviving perpetrators.

Analyze the Spanish case through Margalit and Rieff’s disagreement. Specifically: was the pact of forgetting a prudent accommodation, a failure of duty, or both? Does the later turn toward exhumation and legal memory vindicate Margalit’s duty to remember, or does the enduring controversy vindicate Rieff’s worry that memory can be a weapon rather than a cure? Be disciplined about the mapping: identify who bears the duty, what exactly is to be remembered, and where each theorist’s framework is silent or forced. In particular, say whether the case shows that a polity can defer the duty to remember without repudiating it, and whether forgetting and remembering are better understood as a sequence than as opposites.
