I cover science and ideas, and one impasse I keep returning to is Newcomb's problem. For roughly half a century, one camp (roughly, causal decision theorists) has said the rational act is to take both boxes: the predictor has already acted, and two-boxing yields more than one-boxing whatever the prediction was. The other camp (roughly, evidential decision theorists) says the rational act is to take only the opaque box, because choosing one box is evidence that the predictor predicted one-boxing, and that yields a far higher expected payoff. Both sides accept the same payoffs, the same predictor reliability, and the same formal arguments; each accuses the other of mishandling the distinction between what an act causes and what it indicates. My working hypothesis is that the debate is stalled because it is not really about which act is rational within one shared theory, but about what a theory of rational choice is supposed to be responsive to: the causal consequences of an act or its evidential significance. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not a survey of decision theory. Is it substantive, verbal, two different projects under one word, or an ill-posed question? And what, concretely, would count as progress — a formal result, a distinction, a redesigned problem, or an admission that the camps are answering different questions?
