I've been moderating a small subreddit and got into a debate with another mod about whether the karma system is actually broken. The way we run it, a single score is supposed to represent how much a user has contributed, and it determines posting rate limits, comment sorting, and partly how seriously the person gets taken. But the score doesn't distinguish between a comment that is accurate and one that is just funny or well-timed; someone with a novelty account who makes thousands of people laugh once can rack up more karma then a person who quietly helps a handful of users solve real problems. Stack Exchange has the same shape: reputation points are earned by getting upvotes from other users (some of whom know less about the topic than the person they're voting on), and those same points later buy moderation powers like editing posts and voting to close questions.

So I'm trying to figure out what a system like this actually presupposes. Does it presuppose that the aggregated judgments of a crowd are a reliable signal of something like truth, usefulness, or trustworthiness? Or is it committed to a different picture of value, where whatever the community happens to reward just is the good, and reputation is only a name for popularity? Could a pure popularity contest and an actual competence-tracking system end up with exactly the same mechanics, since both would use the same kind of vote-counting machinery? And what would have to change if we wanted the score to track reliability rather than approval?
