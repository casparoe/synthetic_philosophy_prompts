I'm a software engineer with no formal background in law or philosophy, and I recently came across the absurdity doctrine in statutory interpretation: courts sometimes refuse to apply the plain text of a statute when doing so would produce a result that seems absurd. I want to analyze it as a mechanism, not as a principle. Here is a precise version for you to work with.

Suppose a court system adopts the following rule for cases that turn on a statute. The court must first apply the most literal reading of the text, the way an interpreter executes the literal semantics of a program. It may depart from that literal reading only when all four conditions hold:

1. The whole panel agrees that the literal result is absurd.
2. The panel can state, citing only the statute's own text and structure, a purpose of the provision that the literal result defeats.
3. The alternative reading the panel adopts is one the statutory words themselves can bear.
4. The panel publishes the absurd result, the defeated purpose, and the alternative reading in the decision.

Higher courts may not review whether the exception was correctly invoked.

Walk through how this rule would actually behave. What does it incentivize for the legislature that drafts the statutes? Once drafters know the exception exists and is unreviewable, how does their drafting style change? What do litigants do with it? What do judges do with the discretion the unreviewability creates? Where is the system likely to be gamed first, and what second-order effects follow? Which of these failure modes could be fixed by tightening one of the four conditions, and which are inherent to any rule that lets an interpreter override plain text? Finally, address whether the rule undermines itself: if drafters begin writing text while expecting courts to repair absurd outcomes, does the literal reading stop being a meaningful baseline, and what replaces it?
