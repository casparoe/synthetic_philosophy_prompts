I've been trying to pin down what I think about unilateral action: one member of a group going ahead when the rest of the group has decided, or would have decided, against it, in cases where the action would be very hard to undo and its main effects fall on people who had no say. My verdicts on particular cases are stable, but I can't state the principle that generates them.

One case is a virology lab of twenty people that spends a month weighing whether to post a detailed diagnostic protocol that could also be repurposed into a biological weapon. Nineteen members conclude it should not be posted. The twentieth, who sincerely believes the risk is exaggerated and the public-health benefit is being neglected, posts it that night. I judge this wrong outright, not merely a violation of laboratory rules. Another case is an ecological restoration cooperative of nine members considering whether to release a carefully chosen weevil to suppress an invasive vine. Eight refuse. The ninth, who studied insects and is confident the weevil cannot harm native species, releases it on common land anyway. I judge this wrong too, but I am less certain than in the first case, and her expertise is the main thing giving me pause. A third case is a safety engineer at a fertilizer plant who learns that management has suppressed a report showing that a tank could explode. After formal complaints fail, he leaks the report to a local newspaper against an explicit order. I judge this right, not merely excused. A fourth case is a country that, after an international working group fails to agree on whether a small stratospheric-aerosol experiment is safe, begins the experiment anyway because its own climate panel judges it worthwhile and likely reversible. I judge this wrong, and I would hold that even granting that the panel is the most competent one involved.

The closest I have come to a principle is this: acting alone is justified when it exposes a risk that already exists and is being hidden or neglected, but not when it creates a new risk that others did not consent to. That handles the engineer and the lab dissenter, but I doubt it does justice to the weevil case, and I suspect it merely relabels the distinction I needed to explain. It also says nothing about a further observation that worries me: when I imagine the virology case scaled down to a two-person lab, with the dissenter's evidence, intention, and the expected consequences held fixed, the action feels less wrong. Is there a defensible principle that captures all or most of these verdicts, and would it treat the number of people who could have acted as a genuinely relevant factor or as an artifact to discard? Please state the strongest candidate principle precisely, say where it draws the line, give its verdicts on these four cases and on some further case between the engineer and the lab dissenter, and tell me which of my verdicts it says I should give up.
