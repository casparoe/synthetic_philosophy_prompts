You are drafting a scene in a novel in which two philosophers, neither of them a fool or a strawman, argue late into the evening about what counts as evidence in philosophy. Write the dialogue. One holds that considered judgments about particular cases are the ultimate evidence in moral philosophy, and that if a chain of apparently acceptable pairwise comparisons culminates in a comparison we find repellent, a structural principle such as the transitivity of betterness must be surrendered, whatever the loss of simplicity. The other holds that principles such as transitivity are among our firmest commitments, that their simplicity is itself evidence of their correctness, and that recalcitrant judgments about long chains of cases are best explained away as effects of framing, vagueness, or flagging attention. Have them press a concrete example: a sorites-like spectrum of marginal improvements leading from an outcome plainly better than another to one plainly worse. Each should answer the other's strongest point rather than merely restating her own position, and the scene should leave the reader genuinely uncertain which character has the better methodological case.
