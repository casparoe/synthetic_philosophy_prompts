Working physicists say one thing about the electromagnetic potentials and do another, and I want to understand exactly what the doing commits them to. They say the scalar and vector potentials are a redundant description: two potentials related by a gauge transformation represent the same physical situation, and only the electric and magnetic fields, or more generally gauge-invariant quantities such as Wilson loops, are physically real. Yet when they actually compute, they begin by fixing a gauge. In atomic physics they impose the Coulomb gauge (∇ · A = 0); in relativistic problems they impose the gauge in which ∂_μ A^μ = 0; in other contexts they impose yet other conditions. They then solve the equations for the gauge-fixed potential and manipulate it as if it had a definite value at each point, even though the whole point of the redundancy was supposed to be that it does not. Different gauges make different features of a problem manifest, and a calculation that is simple in one gauge can be intractable or uninformative in another. The same physicist who calls the potential a mere mathematical convenience will, when teaching the Aharonov–Bohm effect, describe the vector potential as acting on the electron in a region where the fields are zero.

What theory of what is physically real does this practice presuppose? I am not looking for a survey of interpretations of gauge theory; I want a reconstruction of the implicit metaphysics that the routine practice of gauge fixing makes sense under. Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it leaves a reasonable physicist free to disagree. Does the practice presuppose that only gauge-invariant quantities exist? That the gauge field exists but carries redundant degrees of freedom we are free to eliminate? Or something else — and is the practice internally consistent when it treats a choice of description as if it were part of the physics?
