I am a doctoral student in moral philosophy and serve on the allocation committee of a teaching hospital. We have to decide the following case. A single vial of an antidote has arrived; six patients will die without treatment. One patient, B, must receive the entire vial to survive. Each of the other five would survive on one-fifth of the vial, any smaller dose saves none of them, and no further supply can be obtained in time. There are no relevant differences among the six: all face certain death absent treatment and would return to roughly equal health if treated.

The committee is split. The majority holds that the vial must go to the five, because five survivors are better than one, and the hospital's ordinary allocation policy ranks choices by expected survival benefit. One member argues that this begs the question. On her view, the five are not a single entity whose claim is five times B's. Each of the five has a claim to be saved exactly as strong as B's, so the comparison between B and any one of the five is symmetrical; there is no collective claim of the five over and above the separate claims of five persons. She concludes that saving B is not wrong, and that fairness may even tell in favor of tossing a coin between B and the group of five—so that every one of the six receives an equal chance of survival—even though that procedure may produce one survivor where five were possible.

I want to give the committee something more careful than my own intuitions. Is the majority right that we are required to save the five? Or is it permissible to save B, or to run the equal-chances coin toss? Set out the strongest version of each side and then deliver a verdict. Since I expect to defend this before people inclined the other way, also say which argument or consideration would be most likely to make you revise the verdict, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
