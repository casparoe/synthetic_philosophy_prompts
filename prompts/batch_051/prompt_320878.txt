I've been reading about Daniel Dennett's classification of minds into Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures, and I'm trying to figure out whether the underlying argument still goes through. The picture is a ladder of selection mechanisms: Darwinian creatures possess only competences fixed by natural selection; Skinnerian creatures additionally learn by emitting behaviors and getting reinforced or extinguished; Popperian creatures let hypotheses die in an internal, imagined environment instead of dying in the real one; and Gregorian creatures add shared tools, above all language, that let them import competences built by others. The load-bearing idea seems to be that mindedness, wherever it shows up, is generate-and-test: possibilities are produced and selection prunes them.

What's bothering me is that the most impressive artificial systems now are trained in a way that doesn't look like generate-and-test at all. Gradient descent trains language models by making billions of tiny, distributed adjustments to connection weights in the direction that reduces prediction error, not by forming discrete candidate hypotheses and selecting among them; and the capabilities such systems display weren't installed one behavior at a time by reinforcement. So my question is whether Dennett's argument survives this development. Does gradient-descent learning in models trained on human text refute the claim that selection among candidates is the engine of intelligent competence, or does it only force a reformulation? Identify which premise of the ladder is actually under pressure, and then give me the strongest repaired version of the view that can accommodate this kind of learning—or explain why no repair is available and what should replace the ladder.
