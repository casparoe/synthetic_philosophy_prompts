Over the past few years I've been marrinating in the rationalist/libertarian-ish end of the internet, where exit-as-a-check-on-Leviathan and charter cities as governance experiments are basically unstated background assumptions, and I've internalized the Hirschman move that exit and voice aren't rivals — exit is what makes voice credible. Now I have to defend a version of that to a room that is not going to share the priors. I'm giving a paper at a political theory colloquium whose organizer works on deliberative democracy; most of the audience are democratic theorists and public-law scholars, plus two law-and-development people who like special economic zones for growth but think seasteading is unserious, and at least one person in the republican-freedom camp who will hear the word "exit" and think rich people fleeing the demos. My thesis is narrower than the full ancap thing: I claim a genuinely democratic state should treat a credible exit option as a structural precondition of meaningful voice, and therefore should actively tolerate, maybe even facilitate, charter cities and similar opt-out governance experiments, instead of treating them as a threat to self-government. The argument is roughly: (1) without somewhere to go, voice degenerates into cheap talk, because the cost of ignoring it approaches zero; (2) the modern territorial state is a near-monopoly on law, so the exit margin is mostly reserved for the rich; (3) charter cities and SEZs are currently the cheapest way to create a real exit margin at scale; (4) so even from inside democratic theory, consent-by-staying only means something if leaving is a live option. My friends from the blogs all nod, which is exactly my problem: I've never had to defend this against people who think legitimacy comes from collective self-rule and not from choice.
