I cover science and ideas for a living and I'm working on a piece about whether large language models are actually changing how a society decides what counts as true. I keep coming back to Foucault — specifically the 1970s lectures and interviews where he talks about power, knowledge, and discipline. His line, roughly, was that every society runs on a regime of truth: the institutions, gatekeepers, and procedures that separate the true from the false — courts, peer review, the psychiatric exam, the university. You dont get to be the person whose statement counts as knowledge unless you're positioned in those systems.

Here's my problem. A model like ChatGPT doesn't pass through any of those gates. It's trained on a huge corpus, tuned by humans clicking preference labels, and then it produces statistically fluent sentences that millions of people treat as authoritative. So I want to know how Foucault would have handled it if he'd lived to see this.

Would he say an LLM is the archive turned into an automatic speaker — the whole system of possible statements finally able to talk back, which would be a pretty literal version of what he meant by discourse? Or would he say its not a regime of truth at all, because there's no institution vouching for the statement and no one occupying the position of the one who speaks and is believed? And would he sort it under discipline, biopower, or something he'd have to invent? Give me your best reconstruction of his actual argument, not a vague "Foucault would be suspicious." Pick a reading and defend it with the concepts.
