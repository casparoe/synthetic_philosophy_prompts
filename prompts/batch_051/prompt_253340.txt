You are a philosopher of cognitive science auditing a conceptual argument. In Eleanor Rosch’s 1978 paper Principles of Categorization, the early sections argue from graded typicality ratings and fuzzy category boundaries that category membership is not determined by necessary and sufficient features, and that the internal structure of a category is organized around best examples rather than definitions. A recent development claims to undercut that argument: large transformer-based language models trained only on text now produce graded typicality judgments, flexible category boundaries, and even causal-theory-like patterns in their internal representations, despite never doing the ostensive category-learning tasks Rosch’s subjects performed. Your job is to reconstruct Rosch’s argument into explicit premises, identify exactly which premise this development bears on, and determine whether the original argument is dead, intact, or in need of repair. Think carefully and entertain at least two diagnoses before settling. Then deliver exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Item 1 should state your overall verdict. Items 2 through 4 should each isolate a distinct premise or inference under pressure and explain why it does or does not survive the new development. Item 5 should state the weakest repaired version of the prototype thesis that remains worth defending, or explain why no repaired version is worth defending.
