You are a philosopher of language with a research focus on generics and a side interest in cognitive psychology, and you are unusually disciplined about distinguishing a conclusion from the premises that support it. Take the following question slowly: first reconstruct the argument premise by premise, then identify exactly where the new evidence bites, and only then deliver your assessment.

The classic argument runs like this. Speakers happily accept generics whose actual prevalence is tiny: 'ducks lay eggs' is true even though most ducks never lay eggs; 'mosquitoes carry malaria' is true even though only a minuscule fraction of mosquitoes carry it; 'sharks attack bathers' is true even though almost no sharks ever do. According to the influential striking-property account associated with Sarah-Jane Leslie, this is because the proportion required for a generic to be true collapses when the predicated property is dangerous or striking: a harmful property needs only a very few exemplars to support the corresponding generic. That observation was then used as a load-bearing premise in the argument that generics are not quantifiers over members of the kind—that they are not equivalent to 'most', 'typically', or 'almost all'—but instead express a more primitive default generalization with a content-sensitive threshold.

Now suppose a new, well-powered wave of vignette studies shows that danger does not by itself lower the acceptance threshold. People fail to accept the generic when a kind has a harmful property that is causally incidental to the kind's life (example: a beetle species whose members become toxic only when they happen to eat a rare plant, with the toxin playing no ecological or reproductive role). At the same time, people do accept low-prevalence generics for harmless properties that are stably built into the kind's life history (example: a fish species in which only the hatchlings, during a fixed developmental stage, grow an extra fin ray). The driver of low-prevalence acceptance appears to be not strikingness but the property's stability and causal integration into what the kind is like.

Does the classic argument that generics are not quantificational survive this development? Say which premise is under pressure, state whether the anti-quantificational conclusion still follows under the best available repair, and make explicit what the repaired view must say about 'mosquitoes carry malaria' and 'sharks attack bathers' if it is to remain adequate.
