I am a senior at a public high school and serve on the student–faculty Academic Integrity Council, which hears two or three plagiarism and cheating cases each month and recommends sanctions to the dean, from a warning and a resubmission up to a recommendation of suspension. Many of the students who come before us say, in one form or another, that they had no real alternative: overlapping deadlines, pressure from parents, fear of failing a class, or being coerced by another student. Our faculty advisor has told us that the panel currently works from the principle that a student is responsible for an infraction only if they could have done otherwise, and that if there was no real alternative we should reduce or drop the sanction. I have since learned that this is close to something philosophers call the principle of alternative possibilities, and that it was thrown into doubt by Frankfurt cases, in which a hidden fail-safe guarantees that a person will act a certain way but never actually fires because the person acts on their own. It now seems to me that our procedure may be built on a principle that is too simple, but I am not sure what should replace it or how far the revision should go.

Could you write a decision procedure our council can actually use, step by step, to decide whether a student is responsible for an academic integrity violation when the student claims there was no alternative? It needs to be usable by a group of sixteen-to-eighteen-year-olds with no legal training in about thirty minutes per case. For each step, say what a sufficient answer looks like, where the procedure stops with a finding of no responsibility or a recommendation of leniency, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. It should keep whatever is right in the alternative-possibilities idea without letting the claim that a student had no choice work as an automatic excuse.

Then run the procedure on these three cases, including one where the procedure gives an answer the council will not like, and end by saying what the procedure cannot decide. First, a junior copied a lab report from a friend after a week of illness and argues that she could not do otherwise because her teachers refused extensions and she was going to fail. Second, a senior wrote an essay for a classmate who was blackmailing him with a humiliating photo; he says he had no real choice. Third, a Frankfurt-style case adapted to our setting: a student bought an essay online and submitted it, but the school's course software had been set, unbeknownst to him, to auto-submit an old plagiarized draft from his account at 11:59 p.m. if he had not submitted anything himself; he submitted the purchased essay at 11:50 p.m., so the auto-submission never fired. It seems he could not have avoided submitting a plagiarized essay that evening, but he also acted on his own.
