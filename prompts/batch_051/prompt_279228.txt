I have taught the debate over Hume's account of causation for many years, and I have watched it fail to move. The scholarly literature has long been split between two readings. According to one, Hume is a regularity theorist: all we ever observe is constant conjunction between events, and the idea of necessary connection is explained away as a projection of the mind's habit, with nothing corresponding to it in the objects. According to the other, the so-called New Hume reading, Hume believes there are real necessities or causal powers in nature, but holds that we can form no adequate idea of them; his skepticism is about our knowledge and our idea of causation, not about the existence of real connections themselves. Both camps appeal to the same texts — his two definitions of cause in the Treatise and the first Enquiry, and the passages in which he allows that there may be connections in nature of which we have no adequate idea. The regularity reading takes the definitions to tell us what causation is; the New Hume reading takes them to define only our idea of causation, leaving open what causation itself might be. Both camps accuse the other of anachronism, or of loading a systematic gap with an isolated remark. After decades of this, the two sides seem to be making the same moves under new names, with no resolution. I want a diagnosis of the impasse, not a verdict on Hume. Is the disagreement substantive or at bottom verbal, turning on different senses of 'real' or 'power'? Are the two camps answering different questions — one about what Hume literally asserted, the other about what his principles commit him to, or one about ontology and the other about semantics or psychology? Is it a methodological dispute about how to weigh systematic argument against scattered passages? Lay out what each side actually maintains, identify where the true fault line lies, and say what, if anything, would count as progress given that diagnosis.
