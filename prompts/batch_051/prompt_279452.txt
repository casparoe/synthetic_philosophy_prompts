I'm a journalist who covers tech and intellectual property, and this has been bugging me for a while. The legal category of orphan works — books, films, photos whose copyright holder cant be located, so no one can clear the rights and the thing sits in legal limbo — feels structurally identical to abandoned software, ie old programs that are still under copyright but whose developer has disappeared, gone out of business, or just stops responding. In both cases the asset is still protected, nobody is around to authorize its use, and the result is the same kind of freeze: libraries can't digitize the orphaned novel, archivists and hobbyists can't legally patch or redistribute the old program. My hunch is that this is one underlying problem, "the owner is gone," and that the legal tools countries have built for orphan works (diligent-search requirements, licensing schemes, exceptions for cultural institutions) could be ported over to abandonware almost as-is. But maybe I'm pattern-matching. Software has dependencies and multiple contributors, licenses that attach to code in weird ways, and an old game or app is arguably a tool you'd want to modify rather than a cultural artifact you'd want to preserve intact. So: is the orphan works / abandoned software parallel deep or superficial? If its deep, what exactly transfers from the orphan works toolkit to abandoned software, and where does the analogy snap? Also please keep the answer in American English.
