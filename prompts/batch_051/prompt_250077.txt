I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the design of political institutions — sixteen students, one 75-minute session per week. I want a single-session activity on futarchy, run before the students have read anything about it, that makes them feel both the force of the idea and its most serious difficulty. The force is that a well-run prediction market can pool dispersed private information and produce a judgment about consequences that no single participant holds. The difficulty is that the market can only ever answer a question that someone has already chosen to put to it — what counts as the outcome worth predicting, over what horizon, and for whom — and that upstream choice is itself a political choice, not something the market can price for you. My students tend to arrive either dismissive (markets have no place in politics) or uncritical (markets are just information, so why not use them), and I want both camps to have their positions complicated by what happens in the room rather than by anything I say up front.

Please design the activity as a game with explicit rules, roles, and a payoff scheme that works without real money (points, tokens, or a small real stake such as deciding how a modest amount of extra credit or a snack budget is allocated). Give me a timeline for the 75 minutes, including setup, the play itself, and the debrief. The structure should be arranged so that participants first experience successful information aggregation — for instance, a market on a verifiable, value-neutral quantity about which the group's information is genuinely scattered — and then run into the wall when the same mechanism is pointed at a decision they care about, where the selection of the predicted variable and the use of the resulting price are doing the real work. The facilitator should not name futarchy or deliver a lecture until the debrief.

In the debrief, map specific moments in the game onto the parts of futarchy: the conditional prediction, the aggregation of dispersed information, the selection of the welfare metric, and the difference between trading on a belief and expressing a preference. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — for example, a couple of students cornering the market while everyone else checks out, prices that never move because no one sees a reason to trade, or participants treating the game as a referendum — and how I should handle each failure. Finally, be frank about what the classroom cannot reproduce: real-money liquidity, the discipline of being wrong over years, and stakes that bind outside the room — and say what the facilitator will have to supply explicitly for the lesson to transfer to actual governance.
