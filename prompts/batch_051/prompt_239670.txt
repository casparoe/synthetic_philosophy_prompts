I cover physics and philosophy for a general-interest magazine, and I keep returning to a dispute that has not really moved in decades: what, if anything, Kurt Gödel's rotating-universe solution to Einstein's field equations tells us about time.

Gödel produced an exact solution to the field equations in which matter rotates and which contains closed timelike curves, so that a path through spacetime can loop back and meet its own past. Unlike the expanding universes cosmologists normally study, it has no global cosmic time — no objective slicing of the whole universe into successive instants. Gödel himself took this to be philosophically momentous. If objective passage were a real feature of the physical world, the argument ran, a sound theory of that world ought not to permit a coherent physical situation without it; here was such a situation. He concluded that the intuitive flow of time is not something the physics itself supports, and he took that as support for a broadly idealist, anti-realist attitude toward time.

The counter-positions are familiar too. Many physicists treat the solution as an artifact: the actual universe shows no sign of the global rotation the solution requires, and a mathematically admissible geometry is not thereby a physically realizable one. On this view the rotating universe tells us something about the internal consistency of general relativity, but nothing about whether time passes. Others draw the opposite lesson: the solution shows that the theory does not and cannot select a temporal structure, so "time really passes" is not a physical claim at all but a metaphysical addition. A middle camp concludes that the theory permits worlds with and without passage-like time, so physics alone leaves tensed and tenseless pictures of time underdetermined.

Seventy-plus years on, the exchange has settled into a pattern: each side accuses the others of mistaking a mathematical possibility for a fact, or of smuggling in a metaphysics under neutral-sounding language, and there is no convergence on what would settle it.

My working hypothesis is that the real disagreement is less about physics than about what an exact solution is allowed to do in a philosophical argument — what weight a merely possible world gets when the question is about actual time — and that the word "time" is quietly doing several jobs at once: coordinate, global succession, moving present. But I am not confident. It might instead be a hidden physical question, such as whether the model is physically realizable or whether cosmic rotation could be measured and ruled in or out; it might be a difference about standards of evidence; or it might be a difference in bedrock intuitions about realism.

What I want is a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of the Gödel-metric literature. Which kind of disagreement is this: verbal, factual, about standards, about which cases count as data, or about starting intuitions? And what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a theorem, an observation, a distinction, a change of question, or an honest verdict that none should be expected? If the debate as I have described it is really several debates stitched together, or if my framing leans on a mistaken reading of what Gödel actually showed, tell me so plainly rather than answering on those terms.
