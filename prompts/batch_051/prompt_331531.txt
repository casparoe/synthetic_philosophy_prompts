I've come around to the view that a writing system, especially an alphabet, is best understood as a theory of the phonology of its language rather than a neutral transcription of speech. Like any theory, it has to pick out what it treats as real (phonemes, or slightly more abstract units that stay fixed across related words) and idealize away everything below that level; on that picture the one-letter-per-phoneme ideal is just one modeling choice among others, not the telos of a script. It also means the usual hand-wringing about English spelling being unphonemic mostly misses the point, and that orthographic depth should be read as something like how much structure a system is willing to expose, not as a deviation score from an ideal.

What I'd like from you is a map of where this position is exposed: what would count against it, and how hard. Not the trivial stuff; I don't need another list of ghastly English spellings. I mean the serious cases: scripts borrowed into languages with very different phonological systems, spelling reforms that genuinely improve reading outcomes, evidence that deep orthographies impose real processing costs, the existence of healthy non-alphabetic scripts that encode syllables or whole morphemes rather than segments, and the way writing systems fossilze and get kept in place by institutions long after the phonology has moved on. For each consideration, tell me whether it's fatal, merely awkward, or something the view absorbs by redescribing it. I'd also like a straight answer to a reflexive worry: has the view been softened enough that no fact about actual writing systems could get at it, and if so, is that a problem for a view of this kind or not? I'll be most moved by cases where the theory idea fails on its own terms — a script that makes a principled idealization but the wrong one for its language — rather than by cases of mere inconsistency.
