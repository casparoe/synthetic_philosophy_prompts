Some philosophers defend a version of the inefficacy objection: if a person's act makes no perceptible difference to a large-scale harm, then the fact that the act belongs to a harmful collective pattern gives that person no reason to abstain. Consider someone who takes one short flight, buys one factory-farmed chicken, or casts one vote for a candidate whose platform would loosen environmental protections. In each case the individual act is surrounded by millions of similar acts, and its marginal causal contribution, measured or expected, looks negligible. Another view resists this: contribution to a collective harm need not be the same as making a marginal causal difference. An act might be a genuine contribution if it is a proper part of a set of acts that jointly ensure the harm, even when its own marginal effect is nil.

Your task is to ask whether the second view is anything more than a redescription, and to bring evidence to bear on it. Choose one of the three domains — climate, meat, or voting — and design the strongest feasible empirical investigation of the claim that an individually inefficacious act can nevertheless be a genuine contribution to a collective harm. Specify the observable quantities, data, or interventions involved rather than remaining at the level of philosophical paraphrase. Do not collapse the question into a bare calculation of expected disutility; the disputed issue is whether such a calculation exhausts what "contribution" means.

Then set out:

(1) the result that would most favour the inefficacy objection — the view that marginal causal difference is what matters — and why it would favour that reading;
(2) the result that would most favour the contribution-as-part-of-a-collective-set view, and why it would favour that reading;
(3) what a positive result in either direction would and would not settle, including which part of the dispute is conceptual or normative and therefore untouched by any observation, and whether that residue reflects a defect in the test or an instructive limit on operationalizing the notion of contribution.
