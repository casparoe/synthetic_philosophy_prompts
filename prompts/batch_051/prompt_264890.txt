I got into an argument with my brother over breakfast about how you're supposed to confirm a claim like "all ravens are black." My rule has always been: only ravens count. If I see a black raven, that's a small point for the claim; if I see a raven that isn't black, the claim is dead; and if I see a green apple or a white shoe, it tells me nothing about ravens at all. My brother says that's inconsistent. He says "all ravens are black" is equivalent to "all non-black things are non-ravens," so a white shoe—being a non-black non-raven—should support "all ravens are black" just as much as a black raven does. I think that's obviously wrong: how can a shoe tell you about birds? But I can't find the exact mistake in his reasoning.

I also don't want to give up my rule, because if white shoes count, then every piece of junk I notice becomes evidence for every true "all X are Y" claim where the vocabulary happens to line up, which feels like it cheapens evidence into meaninglessness. But my brother says I'm just being stubborn, and that if the shoe confirms it even a tiny bit, I should definately become a tiny bit more confident. So is my rule defensible? Is there a fixed-up version of it that keeps the sensible part—green apples don't move me—while avoiding the shoe thing? And if the shoe really does confirm "all ravens are black" a little, should I actually update my belief, or is that a sign something in the rule is broken?
