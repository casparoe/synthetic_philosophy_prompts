There is a position in the philosophy of mind that I find almost irresistible when I do metaphysics and almost incoherent when I introspect. Epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness holds that conscious experience is real but causally inert: the felt quality of a toothache, the taste of tonic water, the reddishness of a red afterimage — these exist, yet nothing in the physical world happens because of them. The case for the view is systematic. Given the causal closure of the physical domain, every physical event has a sufficient physical cause; if a felt quality were an additional, distinct cause of my wincing or my saying that it hurts, it would either overdetermine the physical outcome or violate closure. The exclusion-style argument seems to leave no causal work for the experience itself to do. The view thereby honors both the reality of consciousness and the unbroken causal sufficiency of physics, and it does so without the conceptual strain of claiming that the experience is exhausted by its functional role.

And yet. When I stub my toe and judge, immediately and with utter conviction, that I am in pain, it does not merely seem that the pain accompanies the judgment; it seems that the pain itself is what makes me judge it — that my access to my own experience is precisely a matter of the experience reaching into my cognitive life. If epiphenomenalism is true, that appearance is systematically false. The judgment is produced entirely by neural processes that would produce the same judgment even if the felt quality were absent or different. The flavor, on this picture, never gets to leave the tip of the tongue causally; the report about the flavor is caused by machinery that, ex hypothesi, runs without the flavor. This is the paradox of phenomenal judgment, and it seems to me worse than a mere counterintuitive consequence. It threatens to cut the ground from under the one source of evidence I have for believing in consciousness at all: if the pain does not cause my belief that I am in pain, then the correlation between my experiences and my judgments about them is unexplained by any causal or selective story, and I am left with no account of how introspection could be knowledge rather than luck.

I do not want a referee's verdict. I want to know what it would cost to resolve this in each direction, with both sides given their strongest form. What would it take, specifically, for the intuition to be the thing that goes? What kind of error theory about the phenomenology of introspection would a committed epiphenomenalist owe me — for example, an explanation of why judging feels causally responsive even when it is not — and can such an explanation be more than a promissory note? What would it take for the theory to be amended or abandoned instead — for example, identifying phenomenal properties with physical or functional properties so that they inherit causal powers, or rejecting the closure premise outright — and which of the view's systematic virtues would be lost thereby? And is there a genuine reading on which both the theory and the intuition survive, perhaps by distinguishing the phenomenal property from its physical substrate in a way that lets the substrate cause the judgment without letting the property as such do so, or by distinguishing two senses of access to experience? If such a reconciliation is merely verbal, how would I tell? What I am asking for is not an account of which side wins, but of which side should give ground, under what pressure, and at what cost.
