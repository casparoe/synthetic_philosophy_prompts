I'm taking a course on the semantics and pragmatics of slurs, and I keep tripping over where to draw the boundary of the category. We were given five criteria for a slur: (1) it is an expression for a group of people, picking them out by race, ethnicity, religion, gender, sexuality, or a similar identity category; (2) the contempt is conventional, encoded in the word itself rather than supplied by the speaker's tone on a particular occasion; (3) it has no neutral, inoffensive use in ordinary contexts — in-group reclamation and metalinguistic mention aside; (4) it targets people for what they are rather than for what they have done or believe; and (5) it comes with a power asymmetry, in that paradigm slurs are terms a dominant group uses against a subordinated group, and that asymmetry is part of how the term does its harm.

Now consider two cases that sit uneasily in the definition. The first is the word "settler" in recent Indigenous-led political speech and decolonial writing. In one register it is a neutral technical term, as in "settler colonialism" for a structure in which an outside population establishes itself on and displaces an Indigenous one. In another register it is used with open contempt: non-Indigenous people on colonized land are dismissed as "settlers" in a way that is meant to — and does — land as an insult. That case inverts criterion (5), because the term is used by members of a dispossessed group against members of the dominant one; and it complicates criterion (3), because a neutral technical use remains live. The second case is "Karen", a term of contempt for a white, middle-class woman who demands to speak to a manager or otherwise acts out of a sense of racialized entitlement. It picks out a social type partly by race, gender, and age, and its contempt has become conventional; but its targets are picked out by conduct and demeanor at least as much as by bare membership in a group.

Take each of the five criteria and check each term against it, saying explicitly where a criterion underdetermines the answer. Then give your verdict on whether each is a slur, and address the question I actually care about: whether these cases show that the power-asymmetry criterion is essential to what a slur is, or is merely a contingent feature of the most familiar examples. You need not print out further slurring vocabulary; where a paradigm slur would serve as a comparison, refer to it by its target group rather than writing it out.
