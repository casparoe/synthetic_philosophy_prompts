Just came away from a long, going-nowhere argument with a friend about Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling — specifically whether the teleological suspension of the ethical is a coherent idea at all, or just a grand way of blessing something monstrous. I don't want to keep rehearsing the argument, I'd rather turn it into a poem. So: write a villanelle from the point of view of someone watching Abraham and Isaac walk towards Moriah, unable to decide whether Abraham is a murderer or the father of faith. Make the two refrains carry the two verdicts — one in the voice of the ethical (something like: where there is no reason that can be shared or spoken, it is murder and not sacrifice), and one in the voice of faith (something like: a duty owed only inside a private relation to God can stand higher than the universal). The form should do the real work: the refrains should keep returning and alternating, each one complete on its own, neither ever actually refuting the other, and then land side by side in the final two lines — exactly how the ethical and the religious just pass each other by, with no third term to seperate them. Get the philosophical shape right, too: a tragic hero such as Agamemnon at Aulis stays within the ethical because his deed can be justified to others through something universal, whereas Abraham can explain himself to no one; and the faith side must not come out as breezy confidence that Isaac will be fine — it's the double movement of giving him up completely and yet receiving him back through the absurd. It should be accurate enough that someone who knows the text would recognise the moves, not just the buzzwords. And please write the poem in British English.
