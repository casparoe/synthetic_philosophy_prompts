I am a novelist, and I would be grateful for help thinking through a professional decision that has started to feel less like a career choice and more like a problem of moral arithmetic.

For the past decade I have written literary fiction that is well reviewed but does not reliably support me. Four years ago, a close friend — I shall call her N. — was offered a substantial advance to write a commercial crime series under a pseudonym. She accepted, saying she would write the series quickly, set the money aside, and return to her own work. Since then, two other friends have done the same, and a third has begun publishing a genre series under his own name. N. has now signed for a second series. At a dinner last month, all four said some version of the same thing: that if they did not take the work, someone else would, and that the market would not notice their abstention. Each said they would rather be writing their own novels. One said she now finds it hard to remember why her own novels ever mattered to her.

I have now been offered the same kind of contract: an excellent advance for a four-book series under a pseudonym, on the condition that I do not disclose the arrangement and that I meet a demanding schedule. The money would buy me, among other things, three years of freedom to write a novel I have been planning for a decade. I have no dependants, and I could instead continue to piece together a living from advances, grants, and teaching.

What troubles me is not whether the choice is prudent. It is the impression that all of us are being moved by the same current, and that our individual decisions feed that current. If I refuse, I do not slow it. If I accept, I become one more piece of evidence that serious writers can be bought, and the next offer becomes a little harder for the next writer to refuse. Yet it seems perverse to turn down an opportunity that would be good for me, and for at least one book I care about, on the strength of a causal effect I cannot specify.

Can you help me say, first, whether this discomfort is intellectually defensible rather than merely a mood, and, second, whether there is any principle for deciding what a person in my position ought to do?
