I keep running into this in philosophy of math and I can't figure out which side to trust, so I want someone to walk me through what each option actually costs. On the one hand, the set-theoretic story about numbers is compelling to me: numbers are sets, everything reduces to a single ontology, arithmetic drops out of the axioms, and we get a definite answer to every number question — including whether 2 is an element of 4. On von Neumann's encoding, 4 is {0, 1, 2, 3}, so "2 ∈ 4" is just true. That's exaclty the kind of tidy systematization I want from foundations. On the other hand, I have a very strong intuition that "is 2 an element of 4?" is not the sort of question that ought to have an answer at all. Numbers aren't containers. It feels like a category mistake, like asking whether Tuesday is taller than a color. I don't want to be told which side wins. I want the actual trade.

The usual Benacerraf move pushes toward structuralism: Zermelo's encoding gives the opposite answer, so membership questions are only well-formed relative to an arbitrary implementation, and there's no fact of the matter about whether 2 is an element of 4 "really." But I don't see why that's a resolution rather than a surrender of the claim that there are determinate numbers at all. So tell me: what would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes — would I just have to accept that "2 ∈ 4" is a weird-looking truth about an object I have no direct intuitions about? And what would it take for the reduction to go — does structuralism leave my original question (what are numbers, really?) unanswered, or does it say it was a bad question and that's itself an answer? Is there a reading on which both survive, where the set-theoretic reduction is a model of arithmetic rather than a metaphysics of numbers, and the intuition only bans the metaphysics? And if I go that way, what precisely did I give up that the set-theorist still gets to keep?
