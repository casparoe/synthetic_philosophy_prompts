Three months ago I was appointed to the scientific advisory panel of our regional water authority, which is updating its long-term flood and water-supply planning. In that role I have had to take a position on how far the spread of climate model projections should drive infrastructure spending, and I have ended up with a view I want pressure-tested before I defend it in front of the board.

My position is this: the spread of a multi-model climate ensemble — the differences among the projections of structurally distinct models — is not itself a probability distribution over future climate states, and it should not be treated as one in expected-utility or cost-benefit calculations. Agreement across models built by largely independent groups is a form of evidence for robustness, but it cannot be upgraded, without strong additional assumptions, into a likelihood or a confidence interval. For decision purposes, ensemble spread is better treated as a range of physically plausible scenarios than as a probability density over outcomes.

I want to know what would have to be true for this position to be false — not the generic criticisms, but the specific observations, formal results, and decision-theoretic outcomes that should make me abandon or substantially revise it. Please also tell me which of these a careful defender of my position would themselves concede counts against it, and which they would dismiss as only apparent.

Structure the response in the following sections, in this order, with the indicated rough lengths:

1. Restatement and sharpening (~150 words). Restate my position with whatever precision it needs, distinguishing it from nearby views — the claim that ensembles are useless, the claim that model output should never be used quantitatively, and the claim that ensemble spread is literally meaningless.

2. The case against (~400 words). Be specific. What empirical patterns in hindcasting, calibration, or out-of-sample performance would count against my position? What formal results — for instance about Bayesian model averaging, exchangeability, or the calibration of perturbed-physics ensembles — state conditions under which an ensemble's spread is usable as a probability distribution? What decision-theoretic considerations could show that refusing to treat spread probabilistically is more costly than the alternative?

3. Ranked defeaters (~350 words). Order the considerations from the previous section from decisive to merely awkward. For each, say why it has the weight you assign it and what it would or would not establish about robustness, independence, and calibration.

4. What my position's defenders should concede (~250 words). Which counter-evidence would an honest defender admit as counting against the view, and where is the line between "this complicates the view" and "this refutes it"?

5. Insulation and verdict (~200 words). Assess whether the position, as usually defended, has been qualified into unfalsifiability. If it has, is that a problem for a view that is ultimately about how decision-makers should represent uncertainty rather than about predicting the climate? End with a bottom line: should I keep the view, revise it, or treat it as an open question?

For what it is worth, the evidence that would move me most is a demonstrated track record. If an ensemble whose spread was interpreted as a predictive distribution were repeatedly well calibrated on held-out periods and on the subsequent observational record — including high-impact tail events where the models disagreed with one another — that would do more to reverse me than any formal argument alone.
