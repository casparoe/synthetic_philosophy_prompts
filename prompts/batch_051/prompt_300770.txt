Take Nozick's experience machine, but strip the simulation. Instead of a convincing virtual life, it just directly induces apatheia: a stable, unshakeable absence of emotional disturbance. Once wired up you sit in a tank, and from then on nothing rattles you — not boredom, not pain, not the news that people you love are grieving outside, not the thought that you're doing nothing real. The machine also adjusts your desires so you dont mind being in the tank, don't want to be unplugged, and don't experience the feeling that you're missing out. Assume the effect is permanent, cheap, and reliable.

Nozick's original argument was that we wouldn't plug in because we want to actually do things, not merely have experiences of doing them, and want to be in contact with a deeper reality. In the apatheia version that reply looks unstable: the machine removes precisely the desires the reply appeals to. A Stoic who takes freedom from disturbance as the goal is in an especially awkward spot — why not take the cheapest, most reliable route to the target? Is there a principled difference between an apatheia reached through years of judgment and training and the same state induced directly? If you say the induced kind doesn't count because it bypasses your rational assent, or because it's inauthentic, say what that amounts to and why it matters if the state feels identical from the inside. Work through whether the standard anti-experience-machine argument still gets a grip in this variant, or whether the variant shows that Stoic apatheia has to be defined by something other than the resulting inner calm.
