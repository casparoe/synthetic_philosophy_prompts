Recently I sat on a jury in a negligent-driving case: the driver had been distracted, the basic conduct was not seriously in dispute, and a pedestrian died. The jury did not split over the facts but over whether the death itself should make the punishment substantially harsher than it would have been for the identical act of negligence that happened not to hit anyone. That division has stayed with me because it mirrors a longstanding philosophical standoff over moral luck. One position says an outcome the agent did not control is irrelevant to what the agent deserves, so attempted and completed versions of the same wrong should be judged alike; another says the actual outcome cannot be screened off from our blame, so the driver who killed and the driver who nearly killed are simply not in the same moral position. Both sides seem reasonable, share the facts, and yet neither moves.

I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a survey or a verdict. Is it a hidden factual disagreement, for instance about what punishment or blame actually does socially? Is it a difference in what the two sides mean by blame or desert, or about whether the question is fundamentally moral rather than institutional? Or is it a clash of unargued bedrock intuitions about luck and control? My own working hypothesis is that the two sides mean different things by blame: one is grading the agent's quality of will, while the other is responding to what actually happened in the world; but I am unsure whether that is a real diagnosis or just a relabeling of the impasse. Given your diagnosis, say what would actually count as progress. Finally, be explicit about what would change your mind: which consideration would most likely lead you to a different diagnosis, or where your own diagnosis is least secure.
