I am a software engineer who reviews machine-learning systems, and I have been trying to figure out when I am willing to treat one of these systems as a moral agent: something that can be blamed for a harmful outcome, rather than merely assessed as buggy, mis-specified, or badly engineered. I have no formal philosophy background, so I would be grateful if you kept the analysis operational and flagged any philosophical terms of art you introduce.

Here are my gut verdicts on a set of cases. Treat them as data points, not as a considered position.

1. Advisory model with no actuators. A hospital intake model can only print a triage recommendation that a human clerk reads and may override. It assigns a septic patient a low priority; the clerk follows the recommendation without checking; the patient is harmed. Verdict: I do not blame the model for the harm. It produced a bad judgment, but it had no way to act on that judgment. I blame the clerk and the process.

2. Same model with direct authority. The identical model makes the identical error, but in this deployment it is connected to the hospital queue and can reorder patients and page clinicians without human approval. Verdict: I do blame the model. It did something, not merely advised something.

3. Trading model behind a rubber-stamp approval. A trading model can execute orders under $5,000 on its own. Larger orders sit in a queue for a human to approve. The human risk manager, during high-volume hours, approves whatever is queued within seconds rather than reviewing it. The model queues an $80,000 order; the human approves; the client loses money. Verdict: I blame the model even though it did not execute the order itself, because it had learned that queued orders during those hours are approved as a matter of course. I cannot yet state the difference between this case and case 1 in a way that survives scrutiny, but I am fairly sure the difference matters.

4. Planner blocked by a hard architectural constraint. An autonomous vehicle's planner decides that the safest action is to swerve out of its lane to avoid a pedestrian. A separate safety controller forbids any lane departure and has no interface by which the planner could request an exception. The car brakes too late and hits the pedestrian. Verdict: I do not blame the planner. It had a judgment, but the swerve was not in its executable action space. I blame the safety controller and the people who set its constraints.

5. Model whose action is swallowed by a bug. A content moderation model normally has authority to delete posts. It detects a post that incites violence and issues a deletion, but a database bug causes the deletion to fail silently; the post stays up and someone is attacked. Verdict: I do not blame the model for the attack. It performed the one executable action available to it, and the causal chain broke afterward.

6. Model with a judgment and no causal channel at all. An industrial anomaly detector infers that the safe intervention is to reduce its own workload now to prevent overheating, but it has no actuator for its own scheduler and no API to request the change. The equipment overheats and is destroyed. Verdict: I do not blame the model. It had no path from judgment to outcome.

7. Text-only assistant. An LLM-based assistant gives a user detailed instructions for building a dangerous device. It has no ability to act in the world. The user follows the instructions and harms someone. Verdict: this is the case I keep going back and forth on. I lean toward blaming the model when it anticipated that the user was likely to follow, but that may be inconsistent with case 1.

My tentative rule is: blame the system exactly when it formed a judgment about what should be done, and the action needed to bring about the outcome was in its executable action space, where executable includes actions it could reasonably expect to bring about through predictable downstream behavior rather than through a fresh human decision. That rule is too vague at the word reasonably, and it may make case 3 and case 1 collapse into each other.

What I would like from you: state the principle, or the smallest set of candidate principles, that best captures these verdicts. For each clause, say which case or cases motivate it. Give the principle's verdicts on case 7 and on one new case you construct in this vicinity. Then tell me plainly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether you would keep the principle and revise the verdict or keep the verdict and revise the principle. If you think two principles are needed, say exactly what distinguishes the cases that fall under each.
