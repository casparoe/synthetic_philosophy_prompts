A friend and I spent the evening arguing about whether historians can escape the standards of their own time, and we kept colliding with an asymmetry in the discipline's practice. The same historians who denounce as presentism the habit of calling a sixteenth-century inquisitor intolerant — because it imports a later standard — will, in the next chapter, describe the growth of religious toleration as moral progress, or call modern medicine an advance over the medicine it replaced. Both verdicts grade an earlier age by a later standard: the first condemns an individual, the second commends a practice and flatters the path that led to the writer's own present. I want the strongest principle that could generate both judgments at once, permitting narratives of progress while forbidding anachronistic condemnation. If no such principle will do the work, I want an honest verdict on which half of the pair should be given up. And if historians truly cannot escape the standards of their own time, then the ban on presentism is itself one of those standards — so I also want to know what, if anything, remains of the distinction between presentism and good history once that is conceded.
