I keep circling back to the idea that elections are basically an aristocratic filter: whenever you ask a mass electorate to choose, they reliably pick the rich, the famous, the credentialed, the smooth talkers, so you end up with an oligarchy wearing a democratic costume. The people who push this line usually reach for sortition — filling offices by lot, like juries — as the fix. I want to stress-test that theory against classical Athens, since Athens actually ran something like a hybrid system for a long time: the Council of 500 and the courts were picked by lot, ordinary citizens rotated through magistracies, but the generals were elected and could be re-elected, and the assembly was open to every citizen. I don't need a history recap, I know the basics.

What I'd like is the two-way analysis. Working from the "election is aristocratic selection" view, what would you predict about how power in Athens got distributed between ordinary citizens and the wealthy or famous, and how well do those predicitons hold up against what actually happened — the elected generals like Pericles running things for years, the demagogues, ostracism, and the fact that a striking number of prominent Athenian politicians still came from old money? Then run it the other direction: where does Athens look like a clean confirmation of the theory, where does the theory start to look too crude, and does the Athenian case reveal that the theory is missing something (say, the gap between how you get selected and how much influence you actually have)? I'd rather you say where the theory is silent than force it to speak.
