In three weeks I am giving a paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on the foundations of rational choice under uncertainty, and I want to be ready for the question period. The thesis I am defending is that expected-value reasoning gives systematically wrong guidance for a large and practically important class of decisions, and that the right criterion for a single agent who lives through one realized history is the time-average growth rate of the relevant stock, not the ensemble average over parallel possible histories. The core example in the paper is a gamble offered every period that, with equal probability, multiplies a player's capital by 1.5 or by 0.6. The expected multiplier is 1.05, so the ensemble-average calculation says the gamble is favorable; but the geometric mean is the square root of 1.5 times 0.6, roughly 0.95, so over many periods a typical single trajectory decays toward zero. The paper argues that many meaningful choices—portfolio allocation, career bets, insurance, public health measures under resource constraints—are non-ergodic in just this way: the average across many copies of the world diverges from the average along one unspooling life.

My argument runs roughly as follows. Expected-value maximization draws its normative force from a law-of-large-numbers picture in which a decision is repeated across many independent trials, or the process is ergodic, so that time and ensemble averages coincide. When a decision is repeated within a single trajectory and the dynamics are multiplicative, the two averages can come apart, and maximizing the ensemble average can systematically select policies that degrade the path actually realized. I therefore propose that, in such settings, rationality requires maximizing the time-average growth rate of the quantity at stake, and that apparent risk-aversion should be redescribed as growth-rate sensitivity rather than as curvature of an arbitrary utility function. I try to preempt the obvious objection that this is just expected utility with logarithmic utility by arguing that the growth-rate criterion parts company with any single utility function in finite horizons, when there are absorbing boundaries, and in nonstationary settings.

The audience is roughly thirty people: half are mathematically trained decision theorists who work with Savage- and von Neumann–Morgenstern-style expected utility and care above all about axioms and dynamic consistency; a third are behavioral economists who will say prospect theory already handles deviations from expected-value maximization; and the rest are physicists and complexity scientists who know ergodicity well but doubt that a purely mathematical fact can settle a normative question. Please prepare me for that room. This is a preparation sheet for me, not a polished defense of the paper. I want the objections in order of likelihood and damage, phrased the way each group would actually raise them, with a note on which have real force, what I should concede up front, which reply has to land if the paper is to survive, and the one objection I am hoping nobody asks, including an honest account of where my reply to it is weakest. I have about twenty minutes for questions, so rank accordingly.
