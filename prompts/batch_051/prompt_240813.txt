I am a novelist, and a structural feature of my craft has begun to seem to me like an argument about the world rather than merely a convention of narrative. In first-person fiction, the narrator's interiority is given directly to the reader; every other character's inner life is assembled from observed behavior, speech, and the narrator's imputations of motive, and much of the time the narration simply leaves unsettled how much interiority those secondary characters actually have. It has come to trouble me that my ordinary experience appears to work the same way. I have direct access to exactly one center of experience — my own — while other people present themselves to me only through their behavior and the mental states I attribute to them. Their consciousness never enters my experience as mine does.

This has led me to suspect that the problem of other minds and solipsism are, at bottom, the same problem, and that the first-person point of view gives the solipsistic reading a kind of default status. If I can never directly verify another person's inner experience, why isn't the better conclusion that the other minds I encounter are not discovered but effectively narrated — useful fictions rather than facts? I am not using the NPC line as a joke; I mean it literally, at least as a serious possibility. I would like someone to show me exactly where the move from the inaccessibility of other minds to their nonexistence or fictiveness goes wrong, if it does, and what distinguishes solipsism proper from the fact that each of us lives inside one first-person point of view and must infer everything else.
