I keep coming back to Dennett's essay The Self as a Center of Narrative Gravity, where he compares the self to the center of gravity of an object: it isn't a thing inside the object, it's an abstraction we use to make sense of how the whole behaves, and likewise the self is the center of gravity of a person's ongoing story — a useful fiction we posit, not an inner witness doing the narrating. I'd like you to write a dialogue between two people who disagree about how far this analogy can go.

One character defends a Dennett-style view: the self is real in the way a center of gravity is real, which is to say not a substance or a knowing core but an explanatory posit, and the temptation to look for a thing behind the narrative is the mistake. The other character defends the view that there's a first-person, pre-narrative minimal self — the sheer fact that experience is given to someone — and that any story we tell about ourselves presupposes this and cannot replace it. This person thinks Dennett's view risks explaining away the very thing that makes narrative possible.

Make both characters sharp and generous; neither should be a strawman. The Dennettian should have real answers when the opponent says a center of gravity doesn't do anything, while a self supposedly does things like regret, promise, and reinterpret. The opponent should have real answers when the Dennettian says introspection cannot find the minimal self because introspection is already a kind of storytelling. Have them push on the point where the analogy actually breaks or holds: is the self a thing we invent, or a thing we discover? Can a useful fiction deliberate? At the end, let the dialogue show one view earning its keep more, or leave it genuinely unsettled if both have remaining costs — but don't let them talk past each other.
