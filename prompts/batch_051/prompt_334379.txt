I'm in a grad seminar on population ethics, and I keep going in circles on Parfit's mere addition paradox. Every time I think I've found the step to reject, I realize rejecting it pulls something else down with it. Here's the reconstruction I keep coming back to.

(1) Let A be ten billion people, all with lives of very high quality. Now imagine A+: the same ten billion, plus another ten billion whose lives are worth living but only barely above the level where life stops being worth living. Adding this second group doesn't seem to make the world worse: the original people lose nothing, and the new people's lives are still, on balance, worth living. So A+ is not worse than A.

(2) Now imagine B. Instead of A+'s two-tier split, B has twenty billion people, all living at exactly the average welfare level of A+. Same total welfare, same average welfare, but equalized. B seems better than A+, because the inequality in A+ is itself a bad thing.

(3) If A+ is not worse than A, and B is better than A+, then B is not worse than A. And now we can repeat the same two moves on B: add another group of people with barely-worth-living lives, equalize at the new average, and get C; then D; and so on.

(4) After enough rounds we reach Z: an astronomically large population whose members all have lives that are only barely worth living. By the chain of 'not worse than' steps, Z is not worse than A. But Z is exactly the kind of world the repugnant conclusion points to, and it seems obviously much worse than A.

I want to resist the conclusion, but I can't figure out which premise to give up. I'd most like to keep the mere-addition intuition in (1), but I worry that it alone already starts the slide. Lay out my options: which step should I reject, why does it look so plausible, and what does rejecting it cost? And if the answer is that 'not worse than' isn't transitive, explain why that's more than just relabeling the problem.
