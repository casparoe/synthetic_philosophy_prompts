Take the chapters of Mary Shelley's Frankenstein in which the creature tells his own story—his first confused sensations after being animated, his slow acquisition of language while watching a family from a hiding-place, and his reading of Paradise Lost—and read them through the lens of the meta-problem of consciousness. By the meta-problem I mean the problem of explaining why physical creatures like us are so strongly convinced that there is a hard problem of consciousness in the first place, and why we find it almost impossible to believe that our experiences are simply physical processes in the brain. One prominent answer, illusionism, says that this conviction is itself an introspective illusion: phenomenal consciousness as we ordinarily conceive of it does not exist, and the sense that there is something it is like to be us is a misrepresentation produced by the mind. With that lens in hand, I want a critical reading of the novel, not an allegory. Does Shelley's decision to give the creature a first-person voice, nested inside Victor's and Walton's narratives, manufacture in the reader exactly the intuition the illusionist is trying to explain away? Does the creature's self-report of his sensations make it harder or easier to accept that a constructed physical being could produce such a report with no inner light behind it? Where does the meta-problem illuminate the text—for example, in Victor's horror at what he has made, in his refusal to build a mate, or in the reader's own response to the creature's tale—and where does the text resist the illusionist reading? I am especially interested in whether the novel's moral force depends on the creature's suffering being real in the sense illusionists deny, and whether an honest reading must conclude that Shelley and the illusionist are not doing the same thing. Take both the novel and the philosophy seriously, and be willing to say where the reading breaks down rather than forcing them into agreement.
