I'm an undergrad in a criminal law and philosophy course, and my professor keeps trotting out a line right when we get to proof beyond a reasonable doubt: "It is better that ten guilty persons escape than that one innocent suffer." She says it like it's a argument-stopper. Last class she wheeled it out again when we were doing the naked statistical evidence cases — the blue bus one (a company runs something like 80% of the buses on a route, a pedestrian gets hit and can't identify the bus, and that's basically the plaintiff's whole case) and the gatecrasher one (a big crowd at a rodeo, and we know a majority of the audience sneaked in without paying, but the organizer can't identify any individual gatecrasher, so he sues a spectator more or less at random). She said the law's reluctance to let those probabilities settle liability is "the same idea" as the slogan: we'd rather let a guilty or liable person go than punish an innocent one.

But I don't actually know what the slogan is claiming. Taken literally it gives a ratio, but then a statistical case with a high enough probability ought to clear the bar, so the ratio reading can't be what makes blue bus/gatecrasher evidence feel wrong. If it's not a ratio, maybe it's a claim about which type of error is worse, or about the kinds of evidence that should count as proof at all, or just psychological comfort. And it's odd to use a line about guilt and innocence in a civil case where the standard is just preponderance of the evidence and nobody is being called guilty.

Can you act as an expert in philosophy of law and the theory of evidence, and think this through carefully? Before committing, brainstorm all the different precise claims the slogan could be expressing, from the trivial to the radical. Then give me a menu of readings: for each, say whether it's true, whether it does real argumentative work, and whether it actually supports (a) the beyond-reasonable-doubt standard and (b) the refusal to impose liability on naked statistical evidence. Also tell me which readings survive being ported over to civil cases and which ones only make sense on the criminal side. I'd rather you not settle for one interpretation — I want the options layed out and an honest verdict on where each one breaks or holds.
