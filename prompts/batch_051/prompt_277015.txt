A climate writer I follow keeps making the same move against solar geoengineering: stratospheric aerosol injection is like giving a patient with a bad infection a strong fever reducer. It brings the fever down (that's the global temperature), but it does nothing about the infection itself (the carbon dioxide), and it makes both the patient and the doctors less likely to stick with the antibiotics, which are the ugly, slow work of decarbonization. He says the analogy shows why the moral hazard is the real problem, and why even researching the technique is dangerous: once a cheap symptomatic fix is on offer, the political will to actually cure the patient dissolves.

I want you to audit that analogy for me, properly. Lay out the full correspondence table first: what plays the role of the fever, the infection, the fever reducer, the antibiotics, the patient, the doctors, and the recovery they're betting on. Then walk through the places it breaks. I'm thinking, for example, about termination shock, regional precipitation changes, and the fact that a patient is a single body under a doctor's care whereas nobody is in charge of the planet. But I'd want your list, not just mine. Then the important part: tell me which disanalogies actually matter for the moral-hazard conclusion he's trying to use the analogy to prove, and which are just decoration. Does the conclusion survive the audit? If not, is there a better analogy that could carry the same point without smuggling in the parts that mislead? Answer in American English.
