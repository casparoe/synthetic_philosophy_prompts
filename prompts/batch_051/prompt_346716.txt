I'm a graduate student putting together the plan for a one-semester philosophy-of-language term paper (about 6,000 words), and I need the proposal document to submit to my instructor. My half-formed idea is this. The received answer to the question of what an alphabetic script encodes is that it encodes the phonemes of the spoken language, and that a good alphabet approaches the phonemic ideal of one grapheme per phoneme; by that standard, English and French count as deep, defective, or irregular, while Finnish or Spanish count as shallow and well-behaved. But I suspect this grading gets the explanatory order backwards. We identify phonemes largely through alphabetic practice and through the segmentation abilities of literate speakers, and then we turn around and grade alphabets against the very standard we extracted from them. That looks circular. So maybe an alphabet is better understood as asserting a theory of its language—a hypothesis about which units of the speech stream are linguistically real—and "phonemic" names one such theory rather than a neutral yardstick. On that reading, calling English spelling irregular would be like faulting a language for failing to match a description of another language: English orthography tracks morphology and lexical identity, not just segments, and might simply be committed to different units.

Turn this into a proposal that: states the question precisely and in a determinate form; explains why the standard encoding view and the usual historical-compromise story leave the question open; describes the approach I would take across the term and why it is better than the obvious alternatives (an empirical psycholinguistic study of phonemic awareness, or a purely historical account of how scripts developed); specifies what a useful conclusion would look like and what would count as the project failing; and gives a realistic scope for one semester. I want the proposal to say which sense of "encoding" the project would ultimately need to defend, and to flag the two or three moves most likely to blow the project up.
