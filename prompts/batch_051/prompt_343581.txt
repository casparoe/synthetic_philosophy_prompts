In my philosophy elective this term, we read a short piece on whether what you believe can alter what you literally see, and I have been turning it over ever since. I have ended up with a private distinction I keep using but cannot find stated anywhere. I call it lodged penetration versus leveraged penetration. In lodged penetration, the higher-level state changes the experience itself: an ambiguous smudge on a scan really takes on the shape of a tumor at first glance, and a stick on the trail really looks like a snake before I startle. In leveraged penetration, the experience stays exactly the same, but the belief changes what the perceiver does with the experience — where they move their eyes, how long they linger on one region, which features they sample, how they interpret and report it a second later, and how they remember it afterward — so that the final judgment ends up looking penetrated even though the seeing itself was untouched.

The claim I have become convinced of is that almost every famous example of cognitive penetration is leveraged rather than lodged. If that is right, then someone who says these effects undermine the possibility of theory-neutral observation is attacking a straw man, because the experience itself is never contaminated. But someone who dismisses the whole phenomenon as an artifact of biased reporting is also wrong, because leveraged penetration is a genuine way that belief shapes what we end up seeing in the practical sense. I would like an honest assessment. Is this distinction already standard under different vocabulary, and where does it blur? Is my claim about the famous cases true, or just a generalization from a few examples I got attached to? What are the three or four strongest objections to the distinction and to my claim, and is any of them fatal rather than merely uncomfortable? Please end by saying how confident you are in your main claims, and identify the point you are least sure of.
