I have spent the last few months drafting a speculative novel in which the third-person narrator sometimes states evaluative verdicts about the events described, and my most trusted first reader—a person with unusually consistent instincts about which passages she can enter into—has been working through these scenes with me. This has recently become pressing because my editor wants a climactic scene whose effect depends on readers accepting an aesthetic verdict that I suspect may be met with the same resistance as some of the moral verdicts she has refused.

Her record so far is this. She had no difficulty with a passage stating that in this world ordinary fire is cold to the touch and water is as thick as syrup; she said she simply accepted it as a stipulation about the world. She was likewise untroubled by a passage stating that the people of one region regrow a severed limb in a week. She was disturbed but still able to enter a passage describing, without any narrator comment, a famine in which parents hand their children to a caravan in exchange for bread. But she balked at two passages in which the narrator passed a moral judgment. In one, the narrator said of a politician who framed an innocent rival and destroyed her career that he had done the right thing. In the other, the narrator said of a firefighter who entered a burning building to save a child that his act was wrong. On both, she said she could not make those statements true-in-the-fiction, even for the sake of the story.

The scene I now need to write is not moral, or at least not directly so. It describes a violinist's performance in painstakingly non-evaluative terms: the pitch is unreliable, the rhythm falters, the phrasing is mechanical, and the piece consists of two bars repeated with tiny variations. The narrator then asserts, in the same flat voice used for the moral verdicts, that this was the most beautiful and profound music ever heard.

Would my reader balk at this aesthetic verdict, or would she accept it as she accepted cold fire? Her record suggests she is running some rule that separates descriptions from evaluations, but its scope is unclear to me: is her resistance limited to moral evaluations, or does it extend to any evaluative claim asserted on top of non-evaluative facts? I am less interested in the bare prediction than in the structure behind it. Please identify the rule or rules that best fit her record, identify any other rules that fit equally well, say what each predicts about this new scene, give your best prediction with the reason you prefer that rule, and specify a further passage I could draft that would most cleanly settle the matter.
