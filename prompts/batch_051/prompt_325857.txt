In my animal cognition study group the other week someone cited Morgan's canon to settle an argument, and I left realizing I cannot actually say what the canon asserts. The argument was about a dog who lowers his head, avoids eye contact, and slinks away after knocking over the bin. One person said that is something like guilt, or at least awareness of having violated a rule. The skeptic said no: the behavior can be explained by a much lower-level story — the dog has learned that mess-plus-owner-arriving predicts scolding and slinking softens it — and Morgan's canon says that when a lower-level explaination is available, we are not allowed to reach for the higher-level one. The first person shot back that the canon is either trivial or false when read that way, and anyway it does not rule out the dog also feeling something. We spent the rest of the session trying to pin down a version everyone could accept, and failed.

What are the genuinely different precise principles Morgan's canon could be used to state — the whole range from weak methodological parsimony to something much stronger? For each reading I'd like: the exact formulation, whether it is plausible or defensible (or simply trivial, or too strong to be true), and whether it would actually license the skeptic's move in the dog case. Also, what do higher and lower even mean here, and why does the word fair matter when people paraphrase the canon as requiring a lower explanation to be a fair one? Which reading is actually doing the work when people cite the canon in practice, and does the guilt attribution survive the strongest defensible reading? I'm looking for a menu to bring back to the group, not a single interpretation.
