I spent the better part of four decades working as a research scientist before retiring, and only in the last few years have I begun reading philosophy in a serious way. The position that has most unsettled the working realism I absorbed in the laboratory is the pessimistic meta-induction. I want to examine it as carefully as I would have examined a proposed mechanism in my own field, and I ask you to help me do that.

The position I have been drawn toward is this: the empirical success of a mature scientific theory does not give us good reason to think the theory’s central unobservable posits refer, or that its laws are approximately true, because the history of science contains too many theories that were successful by the same standards and whose central posits we now regard as nonexistent or deeply misleading. Phlogiston, caloric, the electromagnetic ether, and humoral medicine come to mind. If past success is such a poor guide to the fate of a theory’s core ontology, the success of current theories is likewise no reason to expect that their central posits will survive. Thus the rational attitude toward present-day science should be deflationary: its theories are at best predictively adequate instruments, not descriptions of a mind-independent reality.

I want to know what would have to be true for this argument to be wrong. Please proceed as a scientist would in assessing a proposed failure mode, not as an advocate for either side.

Lay out the points at which the argument could break: the premise that past theories were genuinely successful, the premise that their central posits were rejected rather than retained in some refined form, the premise that current theories are relevantly similar to past ones, and the inference from repeated local failures to likely global failure. For each of these, say what the world would have to be like for the premise to fail, and which escape routes are live rather than merely logically possible. Distinguish evidence that would merely embarrass the argument from evidence that would decisively undercut it. In particular, tell me what a defender of the pessimistic meta-induction would themselves have to concede counts against it, not just what a scientific realist would say.

I am especially interested in two possibilities. First, that the argument may be strongest against a naive realism about theoretical entities and weakest against a more cautious realism that treats theories as approximately true only in their structural or relational claims. Second, that the argument may simply show that science revises its ontology, without showing that revision is arbitrary or that mature ontologies are not converging. I would find it most persuasive if a serious competitor to the argument identified a structural feature that distinguishes the successful past theories from the successful present ones, rather than merely pointing to present predictive precision.

I am not asking for a reading list or an account of who has defended which position. I want the argument itself mapped as carefully as a proposed mechanism would have been by my younger colleagues: what it predicts, where it is exposed, and what an observation at the right place would do to it. Please say how confident you are in each of your major claims, and single out the point you are least sure of.
