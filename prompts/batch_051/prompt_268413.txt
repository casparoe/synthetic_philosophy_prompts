The policy is this: when a witness or a noisy measurement supports a claim, I never condition directly on that claim, because I am rarely justified in treating the report as certainly true. Instead, I move my credence one-third of the distance toward where it would go if the evidence were fully reliable, and I leave the rest of my old credence in place to reflect the risk that the evidence is misleading. My friend says this is an arbitrary compromise: either I should do Jeffrey conditionalization with genuine probabilities about the reliability of the evidence, or I am just guessing, and a fixed one-third shift loses information about what made the evidence uncertain. I replied that the full apparatus needs numbers I almost never have, and the one-third rule keeps me from both underreacting and overreacting. Is this defensible as a shortcut, or is it a bias dressed up as a rule? Under what assumptions about the structure of the evidence would a fixed-fraction shift be a reasonable approximation to a proper update, and where does it break down?
