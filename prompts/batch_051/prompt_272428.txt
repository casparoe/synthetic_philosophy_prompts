I've been trying to sort out how much control a story actually has over my imagination, and my gut verdicts keep pulling apart. Maybe you can find the principle behind them.

Take a story that says the moon is made of cheese. I go along instantly; its just a fact of that storyworld. A story that says gravity is repulsive and people walk around on the undersides of ceilings? Fine, I picture it and get on with the plot. But when a story says that in its world two plus two equals five, and the plot actually runs on that, I find I can't grant it: I read it as the characters being bad at arithmetic, or the narrator speaking loosely, or the words meaning something else.

Then there's the moral stuff. A story says a man killed his newborn daughter and that he did the right thing, because she was a girl. I can't make myself take that on board even as true within the story; I read it as the killer's warped view, or an unreliable narrator, or the author being edgy. Same with a story that says a society's chattel slavery is just, full stop, with no further facts given. And yet, a story describes people who, whenever they're killed, simply wake up the next morning in a fresh body with all their memories and no injury, and says killing one of them is no more wrong than waking someone from a nap: that I go along with without much trouble. Presumably because the made-up descriptive facts do the work, and given my own real values, killing those people just isn't very wrong.

Then there are cases that ought to land with one group or the other on any simple principle, but don't. A story says a painting everyone in the story finds ugly is in fact beautiful: I accept it fine. A story says the meeting everyone dreaded was fun: I accept that too, a little less smoothly. Those are evaluative-ish judgments, and I took them from the narrator on no evidence, but I'd never do that for the infanticide being right. What's different about beauty and fun? And why did arithmetic of all things resist like a moral judgment?

My rough theory is this: I let fiction stipulate descriptive facts, however wild, but I only let it stipulate evaluative conclusions when those conclusions follow from the stipulated descriptive facts plus my actual values. That handles cheese moons, ceiling walking, infanticide, slavery, and the reincarnation people. It does not obviously handle the arithmetic case, nor beauty and fun. And I'm genuinely unsure whether my resistance in the moral cases is an inability or just stubbornness.

Is there a clean principle that gets all of these, or at least tells me wich ones I should drop? And what would that principle say about a story that stipulates that a brutal hazing ritual is honorable, or that a clichéd pop song is musically worthless?
