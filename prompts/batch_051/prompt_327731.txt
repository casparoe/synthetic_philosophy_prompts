I am an undergraduate in a course on collective responsibility, and we have been working through Karl Jaspers' The Question of German Guilt, the framework he worked out in the immediate aftermath of the Second World War. I would like to use that framework to examine a later episode, so that the real question is whether Jaspers' categories can survive the move from the generation that lived under National Socialism to the generations for whom it is inherited history.

Jaspers distinguishes four kinds of guilt. Criminal guilt attaches to individuals for acts that violate positive law, and it is adjudicated by courts. Political guilt attaches to citizens as citizens for the deeds of their state, apart from what any individual citizen did, because they belong to and sustain a political community whose regime acts in their name; in Jaspers' treatment it is tied to liability and to the power of the victor. Moral guilt attaches to the particular acts a person commits and is judged before that person's own conscience. Metaphysical guilt attaches to a person who failed to risk life or comfort to oppose crimes committed in their presence, or who survived while others were murdered; Jaspers says it is judged before God alone and that it grounds a solidarity with the victims. He insists that these four kinds must not be run together, because conflating them produces both false accusation and false exculpation.

The episode I want analyzed through this schema is the West German historians' dispute, the Historikerstreit, of the mid-1980s. In rough outline, one side argued that the Nazi past had to be historicized rather than treated as a unique and permanently isolating burden: the crimes of the Nazi regime should be set in comparison with other large-scale crimes, especially those of Stalin's Soviet Union, and the Federal Republic needed a more affirmative national history if it was to function as a healthy democracy. Habermas and others replied that these moves relativized the Holocaust, redirected attention to German suffering, and promoted a form of national identity that sought to loosen the Federal Republic's continuing tie to the crimes of the Nazi state.

Please analyze this dispute through Jaspers' fourfold account of guilt. I am less interested in the historical minutiae than in the conceptual mapping. Be explicit about which kind or kinds of guilt each side appears to treat as the live question; what the framework says, on its most defensible reading, about whether a person born after 1945 can bear any of the four kinds, and in particular whether political guilt, which attaches to the state rather than to individual conduct, can be carried by later citizens; which features of the dispute the framework explains and where it is silent, truncates, or misdescribes; and finally what the episode itself shows about the theory, whether it confirms it, exposes an anomaly it cannot handle, or reveals that the theory was never designed for a case like this. The underlying question is whether a nation, as distinct from its individual members, can be guilty, and whether such guilt can cross generations.
