A statute in a multilingual state contains the following provision, Section 9(1) of the Language Rights Act:

'A person belonging to a linguistic minority shall have the right to use his or her minority language in oral and written communications with public authorities in any administrative district in which that language has been traditionally spoken and remains in common use.'

The Veskans are an officially recognised national minority of the state. Their language was spoken in the northern districts for several centuries, but its last fluent native speakers died in the 1960s, and for roughly two decades afterwards it had no everyday speakers. In the 1980s, descendants and linguists began a revival built on a nineteenth-century grammar, parish registers, court records, family letters, and early audio recordings of the last speakers. Today several thousand adults in two northern districts use Veskan in daily life — in shops, at community gatherings, at municipal ceremonies, and in a primary school that teaches through the medium of Veskan for part of each day — but every one of them learned it as an adolescent or adult; none acquired it from parents, and the first children educated partly in Veskan have not yet grown into adult users. The revival society has also trained a large language model on the digitised Veskan archive to draft teaching materials and to help standardise technical vocabulary.

A resident of one of these districts, a stonemason of non-Veskan descent who has lived there for twenty years and learned Veskan as an adult, submits a building-permit application in Veskan and asks to receive the decision in Veskan. The district authority refuses to process it, arguing: (a) Veskan is not 'in common use' within the meaning of Section 9(1), because it has no native speakers; (b) the language is no longer 'traditionally spoken', because the chain of transmission was broken and the present-day form is an archive-based, partly model-assisted reconstruction rather than the living language the statute protects; and (c) the applicant is not 'a person belonging to a linguistic minority', because that membership cannot be acquired by learning a language as an adult.

Does Section 9(1) require the authority to accept the application and to communicate with him in Veskan? Work through what the wording, the evident purpose of the provision, what its drafters would most plausibly have said if asked directly, and what a reasonable reader would understand the provision to cover. Say which of these approaches should control in this case and why. In particular, address whether 'remains in common use' can be satisfied by a speech community composed mainly of adult learners, and whether belonging to a linguistic minority can be acquired through sustained participation in its language rather than by descent or upbringing.
