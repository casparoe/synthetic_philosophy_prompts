Two friends of mine got into it at a dinner party over Rawls's discussion of envy in A Theory of Justice, and I ended up the designated referee, which I'm not qualified for. I'll give you the back-and-forth as best I remember.

Mara said Rawls has a pretty clear view: envy is not a moral feeling. To explain resentment you need to invoke some idea of a fair claim being violated, but to explain envy you don't need any moral principle at all, just the desire to bring someone down who has more than you. So envy is a vice, not a signal. Devin said Mara was ignoring that Rawls also allows for what he calls excusable envy, particularly when the inequalities are unjust or when the better-off rub their superior position in people's faces. Under those conditions, envy is a symptom of something wrong with the society, not a character flaw.

Mara responded that excusable doesn't mean veridical. Something can be excusable because it's psychologically understandable without being evidence of injustice. A fever is understandable under infection, but the fever isn't itself a reason. Devin said that's a false analogy. Rawls distinguishes envy from resentment partly by what the agent is willing to do: the envious person wants the other to lose even if it costs them too, just to close the gap, whereas the resentful person wants their fair share restored. But in a society with massive inequalities, people may not be able to articulate exactly what makes their situation unfair, so their envy might be inchoate resentment. Calling it envy is a way of dismissing it.

Mara said that move just collapses the distinction. If any negative reaction to inequality can be re-described as resentment, then nothing counts as envy and we lose the ability to criticize someone who wants to destroy another's good fortune simply because they can't stand the comparison. Devin replied that we don't lose the distinction, we just ask a counterfactual: would the negative reaction survive if the inequality were just? If the only thing the person minds is the gap itself, that's envy. But since the actual inequalities are not just, the default should be suspicion that widespread envy is really resentment wearing a different name. Mara said that default is too strong. Rawls himself thinks a well-ordered society will have less envy but not none, so even in a just society there would be some genuine envy. So envy can't be defined away as false consciousness. Devin answered that this actually supports his point: if a just society would have less envy, then the amount of envy we observe is partly a function of how far we are from justice. That makes envy a signal, even if a noisy one.

At that point they turned to me. I honestly couldn't refferee it. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the moves above from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justifying each rating, and then say who is right about what and what both parties are missing. I'm also curious whether Rawls's distinction between envy and resentment can actually bear the weight Devin puts on it.
