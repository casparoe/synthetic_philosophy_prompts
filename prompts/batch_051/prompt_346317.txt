I am taking an undergraduate course on memory and political philosophy, and I have been reading Avishai Margalit's The Ethics of Memory. I keep finding myself confused by a distinction he draws in the opening chapters. As I understand it, he says that ethics governs our thick relations—to parents, friends, fellow citizens, people with whom we share a common memory and a dense web of shared history—whereas morality governs our thin relations to humanity at large. He then announces that his subject will be an ethics of memory, not a morality of memory, because remembering is something we owe, if we owe it at all, as members of communities of shared memory rather than as bare human beings.

What puzzles me is this. Suppose a government in a faraway country orchestrates a massacre of a minority group, and afterwards tries to erase all public record of it: demolishing monuments, altering textbooks, burying the dead in unmarked graves. I have no thick relation to the victims, and neither do most people alive. If Margalit is right that the duty to remember is an ethical duty rooted in thick relations, then it would seem that outsiders have no obligation to remember, and the demand that the international community preserve the memory of the massacre is merely a piece of moral exhortation with no real binding force. But that conclusion seems to contradict the way human rights organisations and international courts speak daily about the duty to preserve the memory of atrocities, and it seems to sit oddly with Margalit's own extended discussion of the Holocaust, which he treats as an event that everyone has reason to remember, not just the communities of victims and perpetrators.

Is this a real contradiction in Margalit's account? Or have I misunderstood what he means by saying that memory belongs to ethics rather than morality? I would like to know how his distinction actually applies to this case, and what part of my reconstruction is doing the misleading work.
