I manage a 40-person operations team at a logistics company, and every quarter I have to sign off on pay adjustments and promotion slots. The same problem keeps coming up: someone puts in a complain that, if you squint, is basically envy — "he got a bigger bonus than me and I do more overtime" or "she got promoted after two years, I've been here eight." My first instinct is to treat envy as a personal issue, something to coach away. But I was reading about Rawls's idea that some envy is excusable, because its not just someone wanting what another person has; it can be a signal that the worse-off can't hold onto their self-respect when big inequalities are stuck in their face and don't look justified to them.

I want a reusable procedure for deciding, when an employee complaint has a strong envy component, whether I should treat it as evidence of a real unfairness I ought to fix, or as a morale and coaching issue I ought to manage at the individual level. Give me the actual procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as enough to move to the next question or stop, where the judgement can't be proceduralized, and where I should hand the decision up instead of making it myself. Constraints: I'm not a philosopher, I have maybe 45 minutes per case, and whatever rule I use gets applied across the team, so I need consistency.

Then run the procedure on two cases. (1) A junior dispatcher is angry that new hires got a signing bonus she never got, even though she makes slightly more than the new hires now. She can point to no difference in output, and the new hires are mostly younger and male. (2) A long-tenured forklift driver wants to know why the new automation technician starts at more than he makes after 11 years. There is a real skill difference and a market-rate study says technician pay is just market, but the driver has been denied training three times and has no path to qualify for the technician role.

Finally, tell me one case where the procedure would force me to treat envy as excusable even though my instinct as a manager would be to shut it down, and one thing the procedure cannot tell me.
