I'm in a philosophy course on free will and moral responsibility, and we've been working through Dennett's Elbow Room: the idea that the free will worth wanting is not an ability to have done otherwise in the exact same circumstances, but a family of compatibilist capacities — rational self-control, responsiveness to reasons, the capacity to deliberate and then let that deliberation issue in action. I'd like to test that framework against a real case rather than another thought experiment.

Take the Leopold and Loeb trial. The facts that matter for my purposes are these: in 1924, Nathan Leopold and Richard Loeb, two University of Chicago students, kidnapped and murdered fourteen-year-old Bobby Franks. On Clarence Darrow's advice they pleaded guilty, and at the sentencing hearing Darrow argued for life imprisonment rather than execution, presenting his clients as products of forces over which they had no control — heredity, upbringing, and circumstance — and attacking the death penalty as retribution rather than transformation. I'm not asking for additional case history.

I'd like a disciplined analysis in both directions. First, map Darrow's argument onto the varieties-of-free-will framework. Which variety of free will is Darrow claiming his clients lacked, and is that a variety the compatibilist account recognizes? Which of the compatibilist capacities — planning, weighing means and ends, acting on settled intentions, susceptibility to reasons — did Leopold and Loeb evidently have? Does Darrow's conclusion about blameworthiness follow from his premises, or does he slide between the absence of one variety of freedom and a claim about desert that requires a further premise? Second, run the mapping the other way: what does the case show about the theory? Does it confirm that the compatibilist capacities are all a court needs to justify blame and punishment, or does it expose a gap — for example, that Darrow's objection to retribution survives the compatibilist analysis, because the capacities may all be present and yet desert still seems to require something the framework cannot supply? I want to know where the theory explains the episode well, where it must go quiet, and whether you take the case as a point for or against the worth-wanting strategy.

Throughout, please keep separate what is settled among philosophers working on free will from what is genuinely contested, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus.
