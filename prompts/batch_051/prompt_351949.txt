I'm drafting a short piece on whether ancient source criticism can say anything about a modern puzzle, and I'd like to test a particular passage before I lean on it. In Book 6 of his *History of the Peloponnesian War*, Thucydides pauses his account of Harmodius and Aristogeiton to correct a popular Athenian mistake. He writes:

"Indeed, the daring action of Aristogiton and Harmodius was undertaken in consequence of a love affair, which I shall relate at some length, to show that the Athenians are not more accurate than the rest of the world in their accounts of their own tyrants and of the facts of their own history."

I want to apply this to a contemporary case. Four years ago, in a small city, a council meeting ended in a physical altercation between two council members. Many residents were present, and the incident was recorded on video from three different angles. Yet the community's public memory of what happened has fragmented along political lines: one group remembers the shove as unprovoked, another remembers the first council member swinging first, and a third group insists the whole thing was staged. The same footage is cited by each side as confirming its own version, and repeated viewing has not produced convergence.

My question is how much work the Thucydides passage can actually do here. Does he diagnose a failing of memory, of partisanship, of credulity toward tradition, or something else? Would fixed video evidence, which he could not have imagined, undermine his principle, leave it intact, or change the kind of error he would predict? And if I wanted to turn his claim into a general rule about why communities misremember their own past, what would the rule have to be, and what would a modern source critic be entitled to conclude from it about my council meeting case? I care about which reading of the passage has the clearest textual warrant, and whether the rule that fits the text is one that would also do useful work today.
