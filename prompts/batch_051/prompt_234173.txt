I'm taking a seminar that's partly about machine translation and philosophy of language, and I keep having the same argument with a friend about whether modern translation systems actually understand the text they translate or are just doing something shallower. My friend keeps reaching for Hofstadter's complaint that statistical translation is shallow, and for Searle's Chinese Room, but I'd rather first get clear on what understanding amounts to, since people use the word in wildly different ways. So give me a working definition of understanding a stretch of natural language — not a dictionary gloss, but the kind of definition you could apply to an actual system and get a verdict out of.

Here are the cases the definition has to handle. It should clearly count a native speaker reading a normal sentence in their own language as understanding, and clearly not count a tourist finding a sentence in a bilingual phrasebook and pointing at it. Then it must give me a definite yes or no on each of these: a modern neural machine translation system translating a news article between two unrelated languages; the person inside Searle's Chinese Room; the Chinese Room taken as a whole; a dog that trots over and fetches the red ball when someone says 'fetch the red ball'; and a large language model that, before translating a pun, can explain why the pun is funny in the source language. If your definition forces a weird answer on one of these, defend it rather than softening it. And if you think understanding comes in degrees, say what exactly is graded and how that interacts with the yes-or-no question.

Lead with your one-sentence definition, then run that definition through the cases, and tell me where (if anywhere) you had to revise it. Keep the whole thing under 350 words.
