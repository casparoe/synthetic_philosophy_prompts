I teach a fourth-year undergraduate epistemology seminar, and I am setting the essay section of the three-hour final examination. We have just spent two weeks on the easy knowledge problem and the bootstrapping problem, and I keep finding that students can recite the standard red-table colour-perception case and the standard gas-gauge case from memory while still being unable to explain what separates the two argumentative structures — or whether they are really the same problem wearing two costumes. I would like an exam question that makes that separation the target, to be answered in roughly 60–75 minutes, together with a model answer of about 750 words and a marking scheme out of 25.

To ensure the question cannot be answered by reproducing the familiar cases, please build it around these two scenarios, reproduced almost verbatim in the question. First: a cartographer is calibrating a newly installed laser rangefinder. She has no independent way of measuring the baseline, but she takes a reading of 247.3 metres and reasons that since the instrument reads 247.3, the baseline is 247.3, so the instrument has read accurately on this occasion; after doing the same at several other unvisited sites, she concludes the instrument is generally reliable. Second: having used the rangefinder normally, she considers the worry that its atmospheric-correction module is systematically inflating every reading by ten metres. She reasons that she knows a certain bridge is 180 metres away because the instrument says so; and if the bridge is 180 metres away, then it is not actually 170 metres away with every reading inflated by ten metres; so she knows the correction module is not erroneous in that way.

The exam question should require students to (i) reconstruct both pieces of reasoning in explicit premise–conclusion form, (ii) state which standard argumentative structure each instantiates and defend that classification, (iii) identify what, if anything, is epistemically defective in each and which background commitments about knowledge make the defect visible, and (iv) argue for a view about whether the two structures are genuinely distinct or whether one reduces to the other. The model answer must not cite or name any external literature; it should earn its claims from the structure of the cases themselves. The marking scheme should separate an A answer from a B answer by naming the precise moves required — for example, recognizing that closure under known entailment is the engine of the second case while iterated self-certification is the engine of the first, and taking a definite stand on whether both are ultimately complaints about reaching conclusions concerning one's own reliability without an independent check — and it should list the specific mistakes that lose marks, such as classifying the second case as bootstrapping merely because both cases involve a single instrument.
