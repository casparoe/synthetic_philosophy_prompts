I keep noticing a configuration in moral life that I do not have a name for, and I would like to know whether the literature has one, or whether the thing is still anonymous.

Here are two instances. First, someone who has wronged a friend sets out to apologize. He knows the apology will be accepted only if the remorse behind it is real. But the harder he tries to produce the right remorse — rehearsing what he ought to feel, telling himself that he owes it, deliberately summoning it so that the apology will go through — the more manufactured the feeling seems, to him and to the friend alike. The friend ends up saying, in effect, that she does not want his remorse because he owes it; she wants it because it is there. Yet if it is not there, she feels wronged all over again.

Second, one partner asks another whether the respect between them is real or just going through the motions. The other answers, sincerely, that it is real, and then sets about showing respect more visibly, to set the first partner at ease. The first partner is not reassured; the respect now seems monitored, deliberately produced and offered as evidence, and for that reason it no longer reads as respect. The request for the attitude appears to have driven the attitude itself out of reach.

The common pattern concerns a class of attitudes — respect, remorse, gratitude, perhaps some forms of love and recognition — that we treat as both owed and spontaneous. On one side, their absence is treated as a distinct failing, not something an action or an outward apology can repair. On the other side, they seem genuine only when they arise in a way that is not prompted by the fact that they are owed. An attempt to satisfy an attitudinal debt seems to undermine the very thing owed, leaving both parties stuck: the one who demands the attitude cannot receive it, and the one who tries to give it cannot produce it.

Does this configuration have an established name, and where is it best treated? I know it is near the idea that what we owe one another is not exhausted by what we do, and near the old thought that certain feelings cannot be commanded. But I suspect I am pointing at something more specific: the tension between an attitude's being owed and its having to arrive unforced, and the way that tension traps both the person who lacks the attitude and the person who wants it. If no name exists, I would welcome a working name with a definition and boundary cases. In either case a bare label will not help me; I want the carving — is there a genuine dilemma here, does one of the two claims give way, and exactly which attitudes belong to the class?
