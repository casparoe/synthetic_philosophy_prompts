A colleague led me through an argument about judgment aggregation, and it seems to prove that majority rule is rationally incoherent; that conclusion is too strong for me to accept, but I cannot find the step that overreaches. The setup is a three-member appointments committee. Its hiring rule is conjunctive: appoint the candidate if and only if she is both a strong researcher and an effective teacher. The members vote in good faith as follows:

- Member A judges that the candidate is a strong researcher and an effective teacher, and therefore votes to appoint.
- Member B judges that the candidate is a strong researcher but not an effective teacher, and therefore votes against appointment.
- Member C judges that the candidate is not a strong researcher but is an effective teacher, and therefore votes against appointment.

The argument then runs:

(1) Under majority rule, a committee's collective judgment on any question is simply the judgment that a majority of its members affirm on that question.
(2) In the vote above, a majority judges that the candidate is a strong researcher, a majority judges that she is an effective teacher, and a majority judges that she should not be appointed.
(3) So the committee collectively judges all three propositions: strong researcher, effective teacher, and no appointment — even though the conjunction of the first two, by the committee's own hiring rule, entails appointment.
(4) A procedure that can issue jointly inconsistent collective judgments is not a rational procedure for forming a group view.
(5) The same split can arise in any committee whenever the members' judgments are not perfectly correlated, so majority rule is not a rational procedure for collective decision-making in general.
(6) Majority rule is the only procedure that treats each member's judgment as counting equally.
(7) Therefore no body governed by majority rule — a hiring committee, a jury, a parliament voting on an ordinary motion — can be both democratic and rational; its democratic procedure commits it to possible incoherence.

I find the vote record in (2) undeniable and (6) very difficult to surrender, yet (7) cannot be right as stated. I want a committed diagnosis rather than a menu of possibilities. Name the step you reject or the equivocation you locate, explain why that step looked innocent, and spell out what a committee that gives it up must accept instead — including any costs to equality among members or to the coherence of the group's view.
