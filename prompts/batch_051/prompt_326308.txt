My friend Sana has spent the last year calling herself a tranquilist and keeping a little file of verdicts about whole lives, and she treats the file like precedent but won't state the rule in one sentence. So far she has said:

- Between a life of constant moderate pain with extraordinary pleasures and achievements and a life with no pain, no pleasures, no achievements, and no strong desires—just a long quiet calm—she says the second life is better, no contest.
- Adding mild enjoyments to an already calm, painless life doesn't make the life any better; the enjoyments add nothing.
- A life of anxious, burning ambition plus lots of pleasure is worse than a life with no desire and no pain, even though the ambitious one achieves things.
- A life that alternates acute anguish with ecstatic joy is worse than a life with neither.

A new case just came up in our reading group: a life with no pain and no anxiety, but with a permanent low-grade wanting—never unpleasant, and the person finds it energizing—that is never fully satisfied. Would Sana say this life is worse than an otherwise identical life without the wanting, equal to it, or something else? I'm after a prediction of her verdict, the closest analog in her file, the two or three rules that fit her verdicts so far, what each rule says about the new case, and the further case I should ask her about to pin down which rule she's actually using. Give me exactly five numbered points, one covering each of those things, each a sentence or two, and no introduction or conclusion.
