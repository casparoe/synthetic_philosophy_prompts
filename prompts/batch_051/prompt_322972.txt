The argument under examination is meant to show that the traditional doctrine of Satan's fall is internally incoherent. The doctrine holds that Satan and the fallen angels were created wholly good, possessed free will, and yet freely committed a first sin of rebellion. The argument runs as follows.

(1) Every sin committed by a rational creature is to be explained by one of three conditions in the agent: ignorance of the good, passion overpowering the intellect's judgment, or the creature's own evil will.
(2) An evil will is a settled disposition of the will toward what is bad; such a disposition must itself be explained either by earlier bad acts of the same creature or by a flaw in the creature's original constitution.
(3) In the case of a creature's very first sin, a settled disposition toward what is bad cannot be explained by earlier bad acts, since there are no earlier bad acts; in the case of the angels, it likewise cannot be explained by a flaw in original constitution, since God created the angels wholly good.
(4) Therefore, in an angel before its first sin, no evil will is available to explain a sinful act.
(5) Nor are ignorance or passion available to explain a sinful act in such an angel: before the fall, an angel knows God and the created order with perfect clarity, and as a purely intellectual creature, an angel has no bodily appetites, fears, or other passions that could overpower its judgment.
(6) Therefore, an angel before its fall cannot commit a first sin.
(7) But the doctrine of Satan's fall attributes exactly such a first sin to Satan and the fallen angels.
(8) Therefore, the doctrine is incoherent.
