I recently received a windfall and placed part of it with a small systematic trading partnership that sizes its positions by a fractional-Kelly rule; before I have to explain and defend the strategy to my spouse, I am trying to determine whether the geometric rationality story behind it is actually a sound norm for real-world bet sizing. Here are the objections I have collected so far. (1) Maximizing expected log wealth is simply one preference, and the long-run growth argument shows what a log-utility agent would choose, not what any rational agent must choose. (2) Kelly assumes exact probabilities and odds; when the edge is only estimated, full-Kelly stakes become unreliable, and fractional corrections are adopted more out of caution than from a formal rule. (3) The growth-rate result is asymptotic, and over any finite horizon a full-Kelly bettor can pass through drawdowns that a reasonable person would decline to accept. (4) The criterion presupposes repeated, independent, stationary opportunities and a bankroll with no competing uses, whereas actual opportunities are correlated and nonstationary, and wealth is also needed for consumption and obligations. (5) Once utility is not logarithmic or the environment is not stationary, the optimal bet is generally horizon-dependent and non-myopic, so the fixed-fraction Kelly recipe loses its clean normative basis. I suspect this list is close to exhaustive. I want one more objection to treating Kelly betting and geometric rationality as the right norm here, one that is genuinely different from all five and not merely a restatement, special case, or combination of them, and then an honest verdict on whether that new objection is any good. Please answer in American English.
