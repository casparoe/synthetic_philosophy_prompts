In my philosophy of religion reading group, two members spent an evening on the stone paradox: whether God can create a stone too heavy for him to lift, which seems to leave a limit on divine power whichever way one answers. We had been reading a passage from Thomas Aquinas, and one of them had printed it out. Here is the passage, from the respondeo of the article titled "Whether God can make the past not to have been?":

"As was said above (Q[7], A[2]), there does not fall under the scope of God's omnipotence anything that implies a contradiction. Now that the past should not have been implies a contradiction. For as it implies a contradiction to say that Socrates is sitting, and is not sitting, so does it to say that he sat, and did not sit. But to say that he did sit is to say that it happened in the past. To say that he did not sit, is to say that it did not happen. Whence, that the past should not have been, does not come under the scope of divine power."

— Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologica, First Part, Question 25, Article 4, respondeo (English Dominican translation, as posted at ccel.org).

Their argument was not about what Aquinas meant as a historical matter; it was about whether this passage settles the stone paradox. I reproduce their exchange from my notes.

Mara: "Read the first sentence. Aquinas is not saying God fails at something; he is saying there is no possible task there at all, because anything implying a contradiction falls outside the scope of omnipotence. 'A stone too heavy for an omnipotent being to lift' is like that: it attributes a lapse of power to a being whose power is limit-free. So the dilemma collapses before it begins, exactly as it would for 'a past that did not happen.'"

David: "The past case is doing the work, and it is not parallel. 'Socrates sat and did not sit' is self-contradictory before any theology enters. But 'a stone an omnipotent being cannot lift' is self-contradictory only under a prior definition of 'omnipotent' as 'able to lift any stone whatever.' That definition is the target of the paradox, so citing it to break the paradox begs the question."

Mara: "Argument always runs on definitions; the test is whether they have independent support. This one does. In the passage, the same criterion rules one way in the past case — God cannot make the past not to have been — and the other way for raising the dead, which is impossible only relative to natural power, not absolutely. Applied to the stone, it yields a definite result and a stated reason: the description ascribes failure to a being with no intrinsic limit of power."

David: "But notice where the contradiction is located in each case. In the past case it sits in the structure of the description itself — the same event both occurred and did not occur. In the stone case it appears only after you import a substantive account of what omnipotence must be like. That makes 'possible' look engineered to track the doctrine: every apparent counterexample gets reclassified as an impossible object, and the attribution of omnipotence can never be tested against anything."

Mara: "It would be engineered if the criterion had no independent content, but it has: it excludes changing the past and includes raising the dead, on grounds independent of this debate. For the stone, it gives the same independent ground — a contradictory description does not specify a task that even unlimited power could be set to perform. If you insist the phrase 'a stone God cannot lift' is coherent as it stands, you owe an argument that unlimited power must extend to contradictory feats. The paradox supplies none."

David: "The asymmetry persists. With the past, the sentence contains its own tense-logical contradiction. With the stone, whether the sentence is contradictory depends on a contested prior account of omnipotent power — and the interesting version of the paradox is precisely the point that no being can realize every ability across every situation. So the criterion is not a neutral test; it is one of the things the paradox calls into question."

At that point they were speaking past each other, and I genuinely could not tell who owed the other an answer at which step.
