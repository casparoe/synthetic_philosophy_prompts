A long thread in my EA-adjacent reading group turned into a two-person fight that I've been asked to referee. Marcus opened with the inefficacy objection about meat and flying: (1) one fewer burger or one fewer plane seat changes no producer's production, the cow is already dead, and personal emissions are a rounding error against total output, so there is no individual moral reason to abstain. Dana replied that (2) this bad expected-value reasoning — restaurants order beef in discrete crates, so a given purchase has a small but real chance of being the swing purchase that triggers an extra crate, and a tiny probability times a very large harm isn't negligible; separately, if everyone treated each choice as a one-off puzzle, the practice could never be dismantled. Marcus answered that (3) this proves too much: the same expected-value math condemns every discretionary purchase, because the money could've gone to malaria bednets with a detectable effect, so either we're all obligated to live like ascetics or expected-value reasoning isn't the test; and "if everyone did it" is universalization, not a causal claim about what his particular burger does. Dana said (4) the relevant counterfactual isn't an imaginary world of universal compliance but the actual supply chain, where being one of the people whose cumulated demand shifts a supplier's standing order is exactly the sort of microscopic chance the expected-value framework is built for; and where there is no sharp threshold, buying meat is still participation in an harmful practice that signals endorsement and normalizes it. Marcus said (5) the signal story needs evidence that one anonymous meal moves norms at all, and if the aim is systemic change, the same effort pushed into carbon-price or animal-welfare lobbying has higher expected value than private abstention. Dana answered that (6) this is a false dilemma: you can abstain and advocate, and expected-value reasoning says to do both, not to pick whichever has the bigger number.
