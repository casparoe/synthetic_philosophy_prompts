I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been gnawing on something while reporting on institutional design—why some well-meaning reform collapses while a boring rulebook keeps grinding along. There's an old Legalist idea, the Han Feizi kind, that you should never build a state on the hope of virtuous people. Virtue is rare, it wilts under temptation, and it doesn't scale. Instead you set up clear laws, rewards and punishments, so ordinary selfish people produce good outcomes automatically, the way a well-designed machine runs on mediocre parts. I'm genuinely attracted to that theory—it's simple, it's predictive, it doesn't rely on saints, and it gives you a tidy decision procedure. But I also have a firm intuition that a society run purely on incentives and penalties is missing something, and that no system of rules can hold unless someone, somewhere, does the right thing for reasons that aren't incentives. I wouldn't want a judge to rule only because his clearance-rate bonus depends on it, and I doubt a legal code that tried to specify every case would survive without judges who actually care about getting it right.

So here's the clash. Present both positions at their strongest—the rule-by-incentives view and the intuition that virtue is irreplaceable—and then tell me what should yield. Should I debunk the intuition as nostalgia or a byproduct of how we're socialized? Should the theory be amended so incentives and virtue do different jobs? Or are they compatible in a way I'm missing? I want you to say what would justify each move, not just pick a side.
