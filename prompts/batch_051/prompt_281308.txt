I am a PhD student in philosophy, writing on the value of death, and I have spent the past term watching two members of our reading group talk past one another. One is a committed Epicurean: on her view, death is never bad for the person who dies, because at the time one is dead there is no subject to be harmed and no subject who can be said to be deprived. The other is a committed deprivationist: on his view, death can be a great bad because it removes goods the person would otherwise have had, and the fact that one is not around to undergo the loss is no objection. Their exchanges have been genuinely frustrating: the Epicurean keeps pressing the missing-subject and timing problems, the deprivationist keeps pressing the prenatal/posthumous asymmetry and the way we regret lives cut short, and neither will accept the wording of the other's central claim.

I would like you to draft the document their adversarial collaboration should have produced, a joint statement both could sign. Begin with a numbered 'Agreed claims' section covering what both accept about death, nonexistence, and well-being: for example, what it means for something to be a prudential bad, what distinguishes a state's being intrinsically bad from a person's being made worse off, and which claims about the phenomenology of dying are irrelevant to the dispute. Then give a disagreements table. Each row should state one residual disagreement in two forms, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording; name the crux, the sub-question whose resolution would move at least one side; and specify a concrete case or piece of reasoning both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Cases might include: a person who dies painlessly in her sleep before completing a project she would have finished; a young adult whose injury loses her decades of life; the comparison between posthumous nonexistence and the prenatal nonexistence before one's conception; and an unconscious patient whose final year is lost without any awareness of it. Where a dispute is partly verbal, for instance about the word 'harm' or 'bad for', say so explicitly rather than forcing a substantive disagreement.

Throughout, please distinguish what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people working on this topic, and avoid presenting either the deprivationist or the Epicurean position as the consensus. I would prefer the statement in British English.
