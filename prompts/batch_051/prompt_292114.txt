In Bayesian confirmation theory, evidence that is already known cannot confirm a hypothesis in the standard conditionalization framework: once a proposition has probability 1, conditioning on it leaves every probability unchanged, which is exactly the problem of old evidence. Suppose a major journal adopts the following rule to handle this. A paper may present an observation as evidence for a hypothesis only if either the observation was first reported after the hypothesis was publicly stated, or the authors filed a timestamped "ignorance declaration" with the journal before inspecting the observation, describing the observation and the likelihoods they would assign to its possible outcomes under the hypothesis and its rivals. A standing committee then reconstructs the probability the relevant community would have assigned to the observation before the hypothesis was on the table, and discounts the evidential credit accordingly; observations already in the standard textbooks at that time receive zero credit. Analyze this mechanism as something that will actually be operated by ambitious, strategically minded scientists. What order of hypothesis-building and data-inspection does it reward? What behaviors around pre-registration, timing, and the description of prior ignorance would it produce? Where would it be gamed first, and which equilibrium outcomes would make its evidential scores less meaningful than the informal judgments it replaced? Finally, say whether the failure modes can be patched by changing the rule or are just the problem of old evidence reappearing inside the institution.
