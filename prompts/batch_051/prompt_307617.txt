I keep going back and forth on wether death is bad for the person who dies. The Epicurean idea that death can't be bad for you because you're not around when it happens sounds right in the abstract, but my gut reactions to concrete cases don't fit it at all. Can you tell me what principle I'm actually tracking, and which of my verdicts it forces me to give up?

Here are the cases and how I react to them:

1. My great-aunt died at 92 in her sleep, no pain, after a full life. I don't think anything bad happened to her.
2. A six-year-old dies instantly in a car crash, no pain. This feels like one of the worst things that can happen to a person.
3. A man in a permanent vegetative state, never to regain consciousness, has his ventilator removed. I don't think removing it harms him, but I do think the real harm already happened when he lost consciousness for good.
4. A miscarriage at six weeks, and a stillbirth at eight months. I can't get worked up about the first; the second feels like a real loss. Though honestly I can't tell if the second one is about the baby or about the parents.
5. After a woman dies, an old rival publishes a book of lies that trashes her reputation. I think this wrongs the living people who loved her, and it damages her reputation, but I cant bring myself to say it harms her. She's not there.
6. A prisoner has been exonerated but the paperwork is too slow, and he's executed painlessly at 40. He would have had decades of decent life. That execution seems like a huge harm to him.
7. A person with advanced dementia who doesn't recognize anyone dies painlessly at 70. I feel like something is lost, but much less than in the six-year-old's case, and maybe less than in the prisoner's case.

I can see these mostly pointing toward the idea that death is bad because it deprives you of a life you'd otherwise have had. But then I don't know what to do with case 5: the woman there doesn't experience the loss of her reputation any more than the six-year-old experiences losing his life, so if I'm squeamish about one, I should probably be squeamish about the other—or I need a principle that treats them differently. Cases 3 and 7 also sit awkwardly with the simple deprivation story.

So: is there one principle, or a small set, that captures these verdicts? State it precisely, say what it predicts about a case I haven't listed, and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't accommodate. The case I keep going back and forth on is this: a person dies painlessly at 40 without ever knowing, and their next 40 years would have been mostly misery with just a few brief lucid stretches. Is their death bad for them, and how bad compared to the six-year-old?
