Here's a clause from my firm's old exchange membership manual (drafted in the early 70s, long before anyone was parsing the tape with a model):

"No member shall purchase or sell a security while in possession of material information concerning the issuer which is not generally available to the investing public."

Now the case. A quant on our desk built a model that reads the public consolidated tape and flags when a stock's order flow looks like a single well-informed buyer is slowly working a large position, cutting it into small orders. Last month the model fired on a small-cap biotech; nobody on the desk had a tip, nobody knew anything about the company's drug trials, and every order the model noticed was printed on the public tape. The desk bought. The stock doubled two weeks later when the company announced the trial results, which the mystery buyer must have known or guessed. A typical retail investor staring at the same tape would not have seen the pattern; it took a model and a lot of training data to pick it out.

Did the desk trade while in possession of "material information ... not generally available to the investing public"? I know the practical compliance answer might be "ask a lawyer," but I'm asking the interpretive question. Give me each of the readings: the words of the clause, the purpose of the clause, what the people who wrote it in the 70s would have said if asked, and what a reasonable reader would take "generally available" to mean. If those readings diverge, which one should win here and why?

And the deeper thing bugging me: if the tape is public, in what sense is our inference not generally available? Is availability not the same as visibility, because only some people have the key to decode the raw tape? I also cant shake the no-trade idea that the very fact someone wants to trade is itself information you should update on. Does that cut for or against the desk here?

Oh, and write it in American English please.
