I am a novelist who co-writes a long-running shared-world fiction project with eight other writers. We govern the project with a written constitution: a one-page rulebook that sets out how the group makes decisions about the fictional world, including who may add to or alter canonical material. One clause states that any rule in the rulebook may be amended by a two-thirds vote of the current members, and that this includes the rule that specifies how rules are amended. We are now split. One faction wants to use that clause to amend the amendment rule itself, so that future changes to the constitution require unanimity rather than a two-thirds vote. Another faction says the manoeuvre is invalid: a procedure cannot use a rule to replace the very rule that confers its authority, because once the old amendment rule is struck, the new rule is left standing on a foundation that no longer exists. A third faction says we are not in a legal problem but in a loop of self-reference, like a sentence that denies its own truth. As a novelist I keep noticing the narrative version: a story can revise its own conventions from inside, but when the convention being revised is the one that permits revision, the story seems to catch hold of itself. I can feel that something is off, but I cannot say whether the vote would be valid, void, or simply a coherent move in a self-amending system, nor what would stop the entire constitution from being reduced to a single momentary majority. Who is right? May an amendment clause be used to amend itself? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning. Do not restate my question and do not begin with a preamble. Please write your answer in British English.
