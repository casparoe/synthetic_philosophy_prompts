I'm a retired volcanologist, recently got properly into philosophy, mostly through the philosophy of risk and decision-making, and I run a small blog for other science types who've caught the philosophy bug. I want a post that's a "which decision rule under ignorance do you actually hold?" quiz, built around cases from natural hazards, conservation and a bit of local government, not the usual abstract gambles. Could you write it?

You're an expert in decision theory and the philosophy of risk, and a decent science communicator. Take your time and think carefully. Start by brainstorming half a dozen candidate questions as scratchwork, then refine them into the final quiz. Write in British English.

The quiz should sort readers into the following positions (you can give them friendlier names for the post, but include the technical labels at least once in the key):
- maximin / Rawlsian (concentrate on the worst-case outcome)
- minimax regret / Savage
- subjective expected utility (including the Harsanyi-style impartial version)
- principle of insufficient reason / Laplace
- optimism-pessimism / Hurwicz
- a dominance-first / status-quo position, if your cases support it.

I'd like 8 to 10 questions. Each one should be a concrete case with three or four plain options — e.g. 'build the full defences now', 'build the cheap version and accept some risk', 'hold off and gather more data' — never options that say 'the maximin answer is...'. The idea is that people get sorted by what they choose, not what they call themselves.

Use at least these cases, since I know the science: (1) a town downstream of a volcano deciding whether to spend scarce money on lahar defences when eruption probability can't be estimated, (2) a fishery setting a quota under shifting ocean temperatures, and (3) whether to translocate a species into a restored habitat when climate trajectories are unknown. Invent more in the same spirit — invasive-species eradication, mine tailings, coastal retreat, that kind of thing. Include one or two distributive questions too, like a council behind a veil of ignorance splitting a fixed flood-defence budget across three districts with different vulnerabilities, so the Rawls/Harsanyi divide shows up. Just don't use the philosophers' names in the questions.

Build the instrument from the logical space rather than a list of names: first work out which questions and answer combinations your cases actually permit, then locate the positions in that space. If some combinations are empty, say briefly why — is the position incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored? Give a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions, and explain how to handle mixed patterns. And flag any pair of positions your instrument can't seperate; don't gloss over it. For instance, a Rawlsian and a cautious expected-utility person with a very worst-case prior may answer most questions the same — if your quiz can't tell them apart, say so.

No citations, reading lists, or bibliographies — the post should be self-contained. Keep jargon out of the questions themselves.
