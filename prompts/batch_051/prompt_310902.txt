I work in trust and safety for a large online platform, and I have no academic training in philosophy. My working vocabulary is practical: deplatforming, suspension, shadowbanning and downranking, strike systems, community guidelines, terms of service, reporter reliability, brigading, ban evasion, appeals, reputation scores. I have been trying to think from first principles about what my team actually does when it punishes someone socially, and I keep coming back to a passage in John Stuart Mill's On Liberty, Chapter IV, "Of the Limits to the Authority of Society over the Individual":

"Nor is this all that society may do. The acts of an individual may be hurtful to others, or wanting in due consideration for their welfare, without going the length of violating any of their constituted rights. The offender may then be justly punished by opinion, though not by law. As soon as any part of a person’s conduct affects prejudicially the interests of others, society has jurisdiction over it, and the question whether the general welfare will or will not be promoted by interfering with it, becomes open to discussion. But there is no room for entertaining any such question when a person’s conduct affects the interests of no persons besides himself, or needs not affect them unless they like (all the persons concerned being of full age, and the ordinary amount of understanding). In all such cases there should be perfect freedom, legal and social, to do the action and stand the consequences."

I want to use this passage to pressure-test both Mill and the vocabulary I work in. Please restate Mill's position in this passage in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can, term by term. Treat "law" as roughly platform enforcement—binding penalties imposed from above—and "opinion" as roughly community judgment, or correct that mapping wherever it fails. Then give me the residue report: which parts translate cleanly, which you had to distort to fit, and which have no expression in moderation vocabulary at all.

The thing I most want to learn is where the translation leaks. Mill describes social sanction as something society does diffusely, as a body of opinion among people who continue to live alongside the offender. My team's sanctions are centralized, automated, global, and often permanent, and they can follow a user across platforms; they are closer to banishment than to a cold shoulder from neighbours. Is that a difference my vocabulary can capture, or does Mill's legal/social distinction simply have no slot for it? Likewise, his test—whether the conduct "affects prejudicially the interests of others"—looks clean in a village and nearly unusable as a moderation rule when harms are statistical, diffuse, and amplified algorithmically. I want an honest verdict at the end: is the residue a defect of my vocabulary, a defect of Mill's framework once it is exported to platforms, or a sign that his distinction between legal and social sanction was less crisp than it looked even in his own setting?
