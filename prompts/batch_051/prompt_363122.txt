I teach an undergraduate unit on the rhetoric of thought experiments, and after assigning Daniel Dennett's *Intuition Pumps and Other Tools for Thinking* I've become bothered by a pattern: the same scenario can produce sharply different verdicts depending on how much sensory detail and narrative colour it carries, even when the stipulated moral facts are unchanged. Dennett describes intuition pumps as tools that can be used honestly or cunningly, and warns that some pump intuitions in ways that outrun the evidence. I want a case built to order to test this.

Construct a pair of thought experiments — call them the Vivid Case and the Schematic Case — that are identical in all morally relevant stipulated facts (same agent, same action, same consequences, same options, same background duties) but differ only in the degree of narrative concrete detail: one is written in affect-rich, textured prose, the other in deliberately dry, legalistic language. The pair should be designed so that a reader who endorses the agent's action in the Schematic Case nonetheless feels that the action is impermissible in the Vivid Case, and the reader should be able to see, on reflection, that no morally relevant feature changed. The action should be one a normal person might face — no trolleys, no ticking bombs, no exotic science. Keep each vignette under one paragraph.

After presenting the pair, do the following with the commitments Dennett invites. (1) State precisely which intuition is pumped in the Vivid Case and which in the Schematic Case, and give the reasoning an ordinary reader would give for each, not just the verdict. (2) Identify the specific rhetorical feature that is doing the work — is it detailed sensory imagery, emotional identification with a character, narrative order, or something else? (3) Test whether the intuition survives if that feature is removed, and say whether a defender of the Vivid Case's verdict could reasonably argue that the detail is conveying morally relevant information rather than merely pumping an intuition. (4) Finish with what Dennett's distinction between using an intuition pump honestly and using one cunningly would say about which case, if either, is a legitimate tool for moral inquiry.

I want a committed answer: choose one specific action and one specific pair of vignettes, defend the claim that the morally relevant facts are held fixed, and take a definite position on whether the Vivid Case's detail is mere rhetoric or genuine moral information. Do not list multiple options or leave the choice open.
