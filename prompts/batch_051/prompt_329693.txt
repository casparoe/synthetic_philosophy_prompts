Benjamin Franklin's *Autobiography* contains two famous moments about moral self-improvement that seem to pull in opposite directions. In an earlier passage, he describes what he calls a bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection. He draws up a list of thirteen virtues, devises a little book with a grid for daily self-examination, and commits himself to working through them one at a time so that the virtues reinforce one another. He presents this as a serious, systematic discipline: the goal is to make virtuous conduct habitual enough that no watchful effort is needed for the rest of one's life.

Later, however, he reports that he never reached the perfection he had aimed at, but that the attempt nevertheless made him a better and happier man than he would otherwise have been. He then offers a further reflection, through the story of a man who asked a smith to grind his axe until the whole surface was bright. While turning the grindstone, the man came to prefer the axe with its speckled surface intact. Franklin says the story stayed with him because it seemed to authorize allowing a few faults in himself: a benevolent man, he suggests, should leave some faults in place so as not to make his friends uncomfortable by comparison.

These two moments appear to conflict. The first treats moral perfection as an achievable project worth pursuing with rigor; the second seems to treat a degree of imperfection as positively desirable, even something to be preserved. Are they genuinely inconsistent? Spell out the most plausible readings on which Franklin could hold both without contradiction, including the possibility that he simply changed his mind or that one of the passages is not meant as moral doctrine in the same way the other is. Then give your own verdict on which reconciliation is most honest to the text and which ones would be special pleading. At the end, tell me how confident you are in your overall reading and single out the point you are least sure of.
