Last night a friend and I spent several hours arguing about whether thought experiments in philosophy can ever function as evidence, or whether they merely illustrate positions one already holds and dress that endorsement up as a discovery. Neither of us could persuade the other, and I came away convinced that the familiar dispute between evidence, illustration, and intuition pump is missing a distinction in the middle. I have been trying to give that distinction a precise form, and I want an honest assessment of it.

I have been calling the notion evidential yield. By the evidential yield of a thought experiment I mean the quality and quantity of the collateral revisions a reasoner must make elsewhere in her web of belief in order to accept the case's intended verdict, over and above that verdict itself. The core claim is this: a thought experiment plays an evidential role only to the extent that its yield is nontrivial and independently checkable. If accepting the case revises nothing but the single target judgment, the case is functioning as an illustration or an intuition pump, whatever its rhetorical power.

To fix ideas, consider an example. Gettier-type cases have high yield on this criterion. To accept that a justified true belief can fall short of knowledge, one must abandon the sufficiency of the traditional three-part analysis, and that abandonment has further consequences: it forces one to ask what a fourth condition would have to do, and it alters how one assesses a range of neighboring cases involving luck, defeaters, and testimony. The yield is checkable because those collateral revisions make predictions one can test against other cases. By contrast, the standard trolley pair often strikes me as having low yield. Many people accept that it is permissible to turn the trolley while retaining the conviction that some difference between the two cases must exist elsewhere, and nothing else in their moral outlook moves; they acquire a new classification and an unresolved asymmetry. If the pair is instead coupled with a principle such as the doctrine of double effect, I am inclined to say the principle is doing the evidential work and the case is doing the illustrating.

This view has consequences I find attractive. It explains why some thought experiments seem to settle something while others merely redraw a boundary, and it gives a name to the sense that a case can do real work beyond confirming the person who constructed it. But I am aware the notion may be confused or unoriginal.

I would like a candid assessment. Is evidential yield coherent, or am I smuggling in a prior theory of evidence under another name? Does something like this already exist in the literature? It seems close to talk of robustness, reflective equilibrium, or explanatory power, but I cannot tell whether it coincides with any of them. Most importantly, where does the criterion break? I already feel two pressure points: a thought experiment could have high yield and still mislead, since a vivid bad case might force wide but mistaken revisions; and a zero-yield case might still be the clearest way to make a fine conceptual distinction visible. Give me the three strongest objections to the criterion, and tell me which of them, if any, is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable.
