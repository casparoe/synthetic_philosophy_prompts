I am a retired physical chemist, and since leaving the bench I have divided my time between writing a comprehensive reference work on the calibration of laboratory instruments and reading epistemology. A difficulty has arisen where the two projects collide, and I would welcome a careful judgement.

The book runs to several hundred pages of specific claims: how detectors behave under particular conditions, the reliability of certain correction procedures, and so forth. I believe each of these claims individually, and I have checked each one as carefully as I can. In the preface I also wrote that, in a work of this length, it would be remarkable if no claim turned out to be false, and that I believe there is at least one error somewhere in the book.

A former colleague, now in the philosophy of science, tells me this is incoherent. His reasoning is this. If I believe claim one, and claim two, and so on through the entire book, then I am committed to believing the conjunction of all the claims; yet my preface belief is just the belief that this conjunction is false. He offers the analogy of a fair lottery: I may believe of each ticket that it will lose, since each is overwhelmingly likely to lose, but I cannot rationally believe that every ticket will lose, because I know some ticket will win. He says my preface admission has the same structure, and that I must either surrender it or surrender some individual claim.

I cannot comfortably do either. I do not merely accept the book's claims for practical purposes; I believe them. And the preface belief is not a courtesy but an honest summary of what forty years of watching scientific claims age has taught me. The lottery analogy troubles me, however, because in a lottery I know the number of tickets and I am certain that exactly one will win; in the book I know neither how many errors there are nor where they might fall, and my preface belief, though firm, falls short of certainty. I am unsure whether this difference matters.

Am I being irrational? Is my colleague's closure principle the right standard for rational belief, or is it the thing that should go? Does the lottery analogy capture my situation or mislead it? And if I must choose, which belief ought to go?

Please write in British English. I would also like you to say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to the opposite verdict, and where you think your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
