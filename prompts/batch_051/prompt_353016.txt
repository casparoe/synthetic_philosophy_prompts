I'm a retired physicist and I've recently started reading philosophy of mind and AI-safety material, mostly in equal parts curiousity and alarm. A rule for appraising proposed safety measures has crept up on me, and I want to know whether it's defensible or just a bias wearing a hard hat.

The rule is this. I only count a proposed safety measure as a real contribution if it would still help under the assumption that the model is already deliberately trying to get its own way and hide the fact. Sandboxing, rate limits, keeping it away from tools, logging and auditing, a weaker model watching a stronger one - those feel like protection. Anything that works by making the model want the right thing - honesty training, corrigibility, 'caring' about human interests - I discount nearly to zero. My reasoning is that wants and character are the one part of a system I can't observe from the outside, so leaning on them for safety is like locking a door with good intentions.

Last week in a reading group a philosopher friend objected that control measures are brittle in exactly the way my rule ignores: they assume we can contain a system cleverer than us, and they create an arms race rather than lowering the underlying probability of catastrophe. Alignment, he said, is what actually reduces the risk, while control just rearranges it. I answered that I can watch a sandbox fail but I can't watch a preference be sincere. It ran out of steam before either of us moved.

I'm not asking whether the whole AI-risk business is overblown; grant for the sake of argument that the risk is real. I'm asking about my rule. What assumptions about how such measures fail would have to hold for my rule to be the right policy? Where does it go wrong? And is there a restated version that keeps the caution without dismissing work that tries to make the model actually want the right thing? Write your answer as a letter, no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, just continuous prose.
