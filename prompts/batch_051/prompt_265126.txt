Lately I've been going down a rabbit hole about the doomsday argument, because my partner and I are trying to decide whether to have a kid and I keep circling back to how long this whole thing actually lasts. The version I understand goes like this: treat your birth rank as a random draw from all humans who will ever live. Something like a hundred billion people have been born so far, so if the total were going to be, say, a hundred trillion, I'd be weirdly early in the list, and a Bayesian should update toward there not being that many more of us — not necessarily tomorrow, but way sooner than the galaxy-spanning future I grew up assuming. The load-bearing bit seems to be the reference class: the self-sampling assumption only pushes toward a doomish conclusion if the relevant class is something like biological humans.

Here's the part that's been bugging me. The versions of the argument I've seen mostly got worked out when "the future" just meant more people like us, but I do ML-adjacent work and it seem plausible to me that if there are lots of future minds, a huge share of them will be digital — AIs, simulations, uploads, whatever. If those count as observers in the reference class, then the total number of future observers could be enormous and my rank stops looking early at all. If they don't count, then the argument might just be telling us that biological humans run out soon, which isn't the same as doom if something descended from us keeps going. So what I want to know is whether the doomsday argument survives this. Is there a principled, non-rigged way to pick the reference class once the future contains digital minds? If it survives, does the conclusion now amount to something weaker than doom — a prediction about the end of a particular kind of observer rather than the end of the story? Or is one of its premises just dead, and is there a repaired version that still says something worth taking seriously?
