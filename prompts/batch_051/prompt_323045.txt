I'm taking a moral philosophy module this term and I keep noticing a pattern I don't have a name for, and its starting to bug me. In some cases we seem completely unwilling to let a big cost to one person be outweighed by lots of small benefits to other people. Nobody thinks a healthy patient should be cut up to save five people who need organs, even though five saved lives would be a huge overall benefit. The one person's complaint is treated as if it simply can't be outweighed by numbers. But in other cases we do exactly that without blinking. The council replaces a parking lane with a cycle lane: the shopkeeper on that street loses her delivery access and complains, but we think it's fine because hundreds of cyclists each save a few minutes a day. Or a road is closed for a marathon, and the few residents who can't get their cars out are told, look, thousands of people benefit. The minutes all got added up and the small group lost.

A few of us argued about this after the seminar and nobody could say whether it's one phenomenon or two. Is there an established concept for the first kind of case, where someone's complaint is treated as un-outweighable by numbers, and is there a name for whatever separates that kind of case from the second? Or is the distinction just something people pretend is principled becuase the organ example feels awful and the traffic example doesn't? I'm not after a label for its own sake; I want to know whether there's actually a stable line here, where it sits, and whether the two kinds of case can really be held apart.
