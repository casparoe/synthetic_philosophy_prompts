You are a professional philosopher whose specialty is metaphilosophy, the study of what counts as evidence in philosophy and how philosophical arguments should proceed. Please think carefully before answering, beginning by brainstorming the different ways the position below could fail, and then present your answer in an organized form.

I have come to hold the following methodological view: when an intuitive judgment about a concrete case conflicts with an intuitive judgment about a general principle, the case judgment should normally be treated as the stronger evidence. On this view, philosophical progress works roughly by using particular case judgments as data and general principles as theories fitted to that data. By a case I mean the familiar kind of thought experiment, such as trolley cases, Gettier-style scenarios, or transplant cases; by a principle I mean general claims such as "the right act maximizes expected utility" or "knowledge is justified true belief plus an anti-luck condition." A principle earns its keep on this view by covering a large and varied range of case judgments in a simple and unified way, and when a principle conflicts with a case, we revise the principle rather than discard the case judgment, even when that principle would otherwise be simpler or more illuminating.

I want you to map the exposure of this view. What would have to be true for it to be wrong? In particular, tell me which observations, experimental findings, formal results, or arguments would count decisively against it, which would be merely awkward or force only a minor qualification, and which apparent defeaters a committed defender would themselves concede as counting against the view rather than deflecting. I am especially interested in four pressure points my friends keep raising: the intransitivity of case-by-case judgments across a series of small changes, which seems to show that treating each case verdict as authoritative can yield an inconsistent body of data; the possibility that simplicity and other theoretical virtues are themselves a source of legitimate evidence not reducible to case coverage; the experimental-philosophy challenge that many case judgments are unstable, culturally variable, or generated by irrelevant features of the presentation; and the concern that the distinction between a case intuition and a general theoretical commitment is too soft to bear the weight I am placing on it. For each pressure point, say whether it is fatal, seriously damaging, or merely something the view can absorb, and describe the most honest qualified version of my view that a careful defender would retreat to under that pressure.

Finally, I want an honest assessment of whether the view, in its most defensible form, still risks anything at all, or whether it has been qualified into unfalsifiability. And I want your judgment about whether, for a view about philosophical method rather than about the world, insulation of that kind is a real defect or simply a sign that the view is describing the ground rules of a practice.
