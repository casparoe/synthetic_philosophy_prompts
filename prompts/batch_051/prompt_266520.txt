I work as a software engineer and I have no formal background in philosophy, so I'd appreciate a point-by-point ruling on an argument two of my coworkers got into at lunch. One of them had just read a popular book on moral foundations research; the other was skeptical. I was the only bystander and didn't take a side. Here is the exchange as faithfully as I can reconstruct it.

1. Maya opened with what she took to be the book's main finding: across many countries, people on the political left lean heavily on two clusters of moral intuition — harm and fairness — while people on the right draw on those plus three more — loyalty, authority, and purity. Her claim was that if your deepest moral convictions are just a function of which psychological channels your politics happens to activate, then those convictions cannot be trusted as evidence about what is actually right. If someone on the other side has equally strong intuitions pointing in the opposite direction, then the intuition isn't doing independent work.

2. Dmitri replied that this proves too much. People disagree about factual questions all the time, and the existence of disagreement doesn't show there is no fact of the matter. If the finding is a reason to discount anyone's convictions, it is equally a reason to discount hers. Unless she has an independent argument for why only her two foundations count, she is just announcing that the other side's intuitions are noise.

3. Maya answered that the asymmetry is in the content of the foundations, not in the psychology. Harm and fairness are at least capable of being applied consistently to strangers and extended outward, while loyalty, authority, and purity inherently protect the local group, the hierarchy, and the in-group. So the psychology isn't symmetric after all: it explains conservative judgments as the output of foundations that are partial and exclusionary by their very structure.

4. Dmitri said "partial and exclusionary" is doing all the work, because it assumes impartiality toward strangers is the standard of moral correctness. Someone who values loyalty will simply deny that partiality to one's own is a defect; they may think it is a requirement. And her test — "can it be generalized to strangers?" — looks like one of her own two foundations dressed up as a neutral criterion.

5. Maya granted that this is a fair objection to her, but said he has the mirror problem. If the research shows that conservative-specific foundations track hierarchy and in-group boundaries, then the fact that millions of people have those intuitions is still just a fact about group psychology, not a justification of them. A claim that partiality to one's own is a moral requirement needs its own argument, and he hasn't supplied one anywhere in the exchange.

6. Dmitri closed by saying that on that reasoning neither side gets to use its own psychology as justification, so maybe the honest conclusion is that moral foundations research is purely descriptive and has no bearing on who is right. But he added that many people who report the research still walk away saying it shows neither side is more moral — which would itself be a normative conclusion smuggled past the is-ought line.

Please go through the exchange move by move and tell me who is right about what, where each of them overreaches or begs the question, and what both of them are missing. In particular, decide whether the purely descriptive conclusion is correct, or whether the findings could play some more modest role — for example, in telling each side when it should lower its confidence — without settling which side is actually right.
