I've been turning over the Buridan's ass puzzle — the one where a donkey exactly halfway between two identical bales of hay starves because it has no more reason to go left than right — and I think everyone keeps making the same mistake about what a tie is. Here's my theory, which I've been calling "fiat preference." When I'm genuinely indifferent between two options (same can of soup on the shelf, literally no difference to me), I don't stay indifferent until some external tie-breaker arrives. The act of picking is itself what breaks the tie. And the weird part: after I pick, I do have a preference — the one I picked is the one I now want, at least a little. That preference wasn't in me before and it isn't based on any new information about the cans; it's something the pick creates, the way a declaration creates a debt or a promise. Indifference is not a stable state that action has to wait out; it's raw material the will just cuts. So Buridan's ass doesn't need a reason to go left. It needs to notice that its will is the thing that forges reasons, not the thing that waits for them.

I keep feeling like this is either a real insight or a pile of confabulation, and I can't tell which. The obvious objection is that what I'm calling a fiat preference is just post hoc rationalizing: I grabbed a can at random, and then my brain invents the sense that I now "wanted" that one so the whole thing feels intentional. Maybe introspection cant tell those apart, which would make the whole theory unfalsifiable from the inside. But I also want to know whether this is just the old "picking vs choosing" distinction under a worse name, and whether saying the fiat preference is real (not just a story I tell myself) is actually doing any work or just flattering the agent.

So: honest read on fiat preference — is it coherent, is it anything someone hasn't already said, and where exactly does it break? I'd also appreciate it if you said how confident you are in what you're telling me, and point at the one thing you're least sure of.
