You are a philosopher of aesthetics with a specialty in the philosophy of music and emotion. I want you to think slowly and not commit to the first principle that occurs to you. Begin by brainstorming at least three candidate explanations for the asymmetry below, then test each against a range of cases before defending your final view.

I am a retired physical chemist, recently deep in the aesthetics literature, and I have been puzzled by a feature of our ordinary critical language about music. We freely say of an instrumental work that it is sad, cheerful, joyful, angry, anxious, agitated, tender, serene, playful, or menacing. But we do not, in ordinary practice, say that an instrumental work is jealous, envious, ashamed, embarrassed, guilty, grateful, or indignant. The latter descriptions sound like category mistakes when applied to the music itself rather than to a person depicted in a song. The absence is not a general ban on negative affect—anger, anxiety, and menace pass without objection—nor on complexity—melancholy, wistfulness, and serenity pass too.

So the question is: does this asymmetry track a real difference in what pure instrumental music can express, or is it an accident of critical vocabulary? I want the strongest principle that draws the boundary, and an honest verdict on whether the boundary is defensible.

Naive candidates will need handling. For instance, one might say music can express only objectless moods and not object-directed emotions; but hope and longing take objects and are widely and naturally heard in purely instrumental music, so that principle either has to explain away those cases or give up. One might say music can express only emotions with a distinctive vocal or bodily contour available for sound to mimic; another might say music can express only states with a clear valence-plus-arousal signature and a characteristic temporal course, leaving out the self-evaluative emotions. Each of these has trouble with cross-cutting cases.

Please structure your answer as follows:

1. Distinguish, as carefully as you can, the expressive use ('the music is sad') from the merely evocative use ('the music is saddening' or 'disgusting'). Tell me which of the two is at issue in the asymmetry.

2. State the strongest principle you can find that separates the expressible list from the inexpressible list. Make it precise enough to be tested.

3. Test it on the difficult cases: hope, pride, nostalgia, homesickness, disgust, tenderness, and any others you think cut against it. Say explicitly where the principle succeeds and where it fails.

4. Consider whether the inexpressibility of jealousy or shame is a fact about musical resources or a fact about Anglophone critical convention. Could a purely instrumental piece, without title or text, ever be heard as jealous rather than merely agitated and angry? If so, under what conditions would that hearing be well-founded rather than fanciful?

5. Conclude with a verdict: should we keep treating this asymmetry as a real boundary in our talk about music, or should we regard it as revisable convention—and if the latter, what would a principled revision look like?

Do not hide the cases that embarrass your view. I care less about a label for a theory than about the exact shape of the line and why it falls where it does.
