So my brother-in-law hit me with this over dinner and Ive been chewing on it for a week. It looks like it proves universal salvation from the principle that punishment should fit the crime, but something about it smells like an argument that proves too much and I cant find the bad step. Here's the chain as he gave it:

(1) A punishment is just only if it is proportionate to the offense.
(2) Proportionate means the severity of the punishment has to be comparable to the gravity of the offense: a finite offense can't call for an infinite punishment, and an infinite offense can't be discharged by a finite one.
(3) Any offense a human commits has finite gravity. It's a finite act by a finite will with finite knowledge and finite capacities, so its moral weight—whatever its exact size—is finite.
(4) Eternal conscious torment is an infinite punishment: it never ends, and each additional moment adds more pain, so total inflicted harm grows without bound.
(5) From (2), (3), and (4), eternal conscious torment is never proportionate to any human offense.
(6) God is perfectly just and wouldn't impose a disproportionate punishment.
(7) So God imposes eternal conscious torment on no one, and universal salvation is true.

Here's why I can't sleep. If you instead measure the gravity of an offense partly by the standing of the one wronged, then every sin wrongs God, who has infinite standing, so every sin—even a small one—has infinite gravity. Then by (2) every sin deserves infinite punishment, which makes a white lie and a genocide equal at the level of desert, and it makes any finite penalty look like loose change against an infinite debt. And don't tell me to go annihilationist to escape it; permanent snuffing-out deprives you of an endless future, so it looks just as infinite in outcome, unless I'm willing to bite the bullet that not existing isn't a loss. So the same proportionality principle seems to push me either into universalism or into a flat picture where no sin can be worse than another. I can't tell whether this is a proof, a reductio of proportionality, or a reductio of perfect justice. Which premise do I actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If you had to keep one of (1), (3), or (6), which do you keep and why?
