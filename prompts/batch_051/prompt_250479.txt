I cover the governance of emerging technology for a general-audience magazine, and I have been trying to think through a dispute that sits at the junction of statutory interpretation, old legislation, and the rule of law. A mid-sized city I'll call Kirkfield is attempting to block driverless delivery vans by reviving an ordinance that has not been enforced in living memory. The operative clause, enacted in 1912 and never formally repealed, reads: "No vehicle propelled by mechanical power shall be used upon any public way within this City unless at all times attended by a person of full age and sound mind, competent to direct, govern, and stop the same."

The vans carry no one on board. Each is supervised remotely by a licensed operator in a control room who monitors as many as twelve vehicles at once, sees and hears each van's surroundings through cameras and microphones, can change its route, and can take over steering and braking within a few seconds. The company argues that this is attendance in every sense the ordinance cared about: a competent person is continuously in a position to direct, govern, and stop the vehicle, and the clause existed to protect the public from runaway machines. The city replies that "attended" meant bodily present, that the 1912 drafters could not have conceived of remote attendance, and that the text requires a person with the vehicle. The backdrop matters: the ordinance was omitted from the consolidated traffic code in 1968, officers are trained not to cite it, and the city attorney has for decades treated it as superseded — yet it was never repealed.

I want you to work through what the clause requires here, using the words, the evident purpose, what the drafters would most likely have said if asked, and what a reasonable reader of the ordinance today would take it to mean. Then I want the harder question: should the city's century-long non-enforcement have any legal significance? Some of my sources call this desuetude — the idea that a long-unenforced statute loses its force — and see it as a check on opportunistic revival; others say an unrepealed statute remains valid law until the legislature repeals it, and non-enforcement is just prosecutorial discretion. Where you can, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested position as the consensus. I care less about the practical outcome than about which approach to interpretation, and which underlying view of the rule of law, should win and why.
