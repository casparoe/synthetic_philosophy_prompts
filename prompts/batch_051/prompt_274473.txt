I've been reading up on the "new mechanist" account of explanation in biology — the Machamer, Darden, and Craver version — and my study group keeps arguing about an edge case. As I understand it, a mechanistic explanation has to do four things: identify entities and activities, where entities are the things and activities are the doings; show productive continuity from setup or start conditions to termination conditions, so each activity hands off to the next through the entities with no gaps; respect the spatial and temporal organization of those entities and activities; and bottom out at whatever size or detail level the relevant field treats as unproblematic. Things like the sliding-filament explanation of muscle contraction or the action potential clearly count.

The case that bugs us came up in a developmental biology lecture. Our professor described how chick embryos get evenly spaced feather buds: two morphogens, an activator and an inhibitor, diffuse through the tissue and react with each other, the uniform state is unstable, and random fluctuations get amplified into a regular pattern of peaks. The explanation doesn't trace a sequence of molecular steps that builds the pattern; it just says the pattern emerges from the global dynamics of the reaction-diffusion equations. Half my group says it is obviously mechanistic (there are entities — the morphogens — and activities — diffusing, activating, inhibiting — and there is a clear setup-to-pattern endpoint). The other half says it cannot be mechanistic because there is no productive continuity: the pattern is not handed from one stage to the next, it is a property of the whole instability.

So is this a mechanistic explanation in the Machamer-Darden-Craver sense? Go through the criteria one by one, say where they underdetermine the verdict, and then say whether the critieria themselves need adjusting to handle this kind of case. Give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
