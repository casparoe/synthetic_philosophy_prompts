Spent years reading LessWrong and the surrounding rationalist blogs, and there's a move I keep seeing that never quite sat right with me. Someone will be in the middle of an argument about a weird decision-theoretic case, and somone else will reply "shut up and multiply" as though that settles it. The instance that finally made me want to pin this down was a thread about the Pasadena game. In case you haven't met it: you flip a fair coin until it first lands heads, and if that happens on flip n you are paid ((-1)^(n-1) × 2^n / n) dollars. The expected-value calculation you'd write down is 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which adds up to ln 2 in that order; but since the series is conditionally convergent, rearranging the terms makes it converge to any number you like, or blow up. So "just multiply" doesn't hand you a single answer — what you get depends on how you sum the possibilities. The commenter brushed this off: shut up and multiply, the confusion is about infinite series, not decision theory.

What I want is a disambiguation of the slogan, not a paper on the Pasadena game. What are the distinct precise claims "shut up and multiply" could be making? My rough starter list: (a) when expected value is well-defined and you've agreed on probabilities and utilities, do the arithmetic rather than going off vibes; (b) every gamble has a well-defined expected value; (c) maximizing expected value just is practical rationality, full stop; (d) there's always a privileged or "natural" order for summing the states, so conditional convergence isn't a real problem; (e) cases where EV is undefined are irrelevant because they can't occur in the real world. Take whatever's useful from that list and expand it. For each reading, say whether it's a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or false — and say which reading the commenter would actually need for "shut up and multiply" to dissolve the Pasadena worry instead of sidestepping it.
