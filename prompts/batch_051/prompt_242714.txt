I sit on the research-integrity committee of a physics department, and we are deadlocked over a case. Section 4(c) of the university's code on the conduct of research reads: "No observation shall be presented as evidence in support of a hypothesis unless the hypothesis was formulated before the observation was made." The clause was added after a misconduct case in which a researcher ran several hundred analyses on one dataset and reported only the associations that happened to survive, and there is no dispute that its intended target was post hoc data dredging of that kind.

The case before us is this. A doctoral candidate has submitted a thesis in which he proposes a modified theory of gravity and offers, as central supporting evidence, the anomalous rotation curves of galaxies — observations that have been reported and reproduced for decades and were thoroughly familiar to him before he began constructing the theory. One committee member says the clause plainly bars this: the observations precede the hypothesis, and the clause contains no exception for long-standing anomalies. Another replies that this cannot be the right reading, because read literally the clause would have made it misconduct for Einstein to present the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion as evidence for general relativity, which everyone treats as a paradigm case of confirmation. The drafters, he insists, meant to stop researchers from manufacturing hypotheses to fit data they had just gathered, not to forbid a theorist from explaining an old published result. The candidate argues that a rule disallowing the explanation of long-standing anomalies would disallow much of theoretical science.

Please give me an argued resolution of how Section 4(c) applies to this case. Work through: (i) what the words alone require, including the ambiguities about who must make the observation and whether the observation must be new to the researcher or new to science; (ii) what the purpose of the clause, and the likely answer of its drafters if asked, would yield; (iii) which approach should govern here and why; and (iv) what the dispute reveals about the difference between genuinely novel evidence and evidence that was merely not used in constructing the hypothesis. On that last point, engage with the Bayesian problem of old evidence: if an observation is already known with certainty, its probability is already one, and the standard measures of confirmation seem to say that such an observation cannot confirm any hypothesis — yet physicists have always treated Mercury's precession as strong support for Einstein's theory. Does the clause's novelty requirement track something real about when evidence confirms, or is it a crude institutional proxy for a subtler notion of novelty? If it is crude, say how the clause should be amended so that it still catches data dredging without banning the legitimate use of old evidence.
