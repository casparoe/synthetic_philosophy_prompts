So my friend and I spent an hour going around on Locke's memory criterion and Reid's brave officer, and we ended up stuck on a line I hadn't read carefully before. In An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, Book II, Chapter XXVII, section 3, Locke writes:

'In the state of living creatures, their identity depends not on a mass of the same particles, but on something else.'

My friend says this undermines the usual way people state Locke, because he's not saying personal identity just is memory; he's saying there's a 'something else' that keeps an organism the same across matter change. I tried to lay out the disagreement as an argument:

(1) For Locke, a living creature remains the same over time in virtue of some non-material something else, not its particles.
(2) If a person is a kind of living creature, then a person's identity should also be secured by that same something else.
(3) But Locke later explains this something else for persons as consciousness, and Reid's brave officer is supposed to show that consciousness/memory isn't transitive.
(4) So either the 'something else' for living creatures and consciousness for persons can't both be Locke's view, or Reid's brave officer attacks a version of Locke that wasn't really his.

Is this argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what's the strongest reading of the 'something else' passage on which the memory criterion still survives Reid's case?
