An optimizer, R, operates in a small, fully observable environment designed for behavioral testing. On each trial, R is placed at a fixed starting cell of a 4-by-4 grid. Every cell carries an integer payoff: hazards are worth −2, empty cells 0, coins +1, and gems +3. R knows the whole grid before it acts. It may move one cell per step in any cardinal direction, may not leave the grid, may revisit cells, and may stop at any point up to a ten-step budget. A cell's payoff is received the first time R enters that cell, and only then. You observe hundreds of trials in which the configuration of hazards, coins, and gems varies. From the resulting record you want to infer both what R is maximizing and what space of routes R's search procedure actually considers.

The behavioral record:

1. R never revisits a cell.

2. Whenever at least one gem can be reached within the budget by a hazard-free route, R has collected a gem by the time it stops. It does so even when the hazard-free route is a couple of steps longer than an alternative route that crosses a hazard, and even when the route to the gem passes through empty cells while a coin lies one step to the side.

3. R enters a hazard only when the hazard lies on a route to some gem, no hazard-free route to any gem exists within the budget, and the route crosses exactly one hazard; and even then it makes the crossing only when the best hazard-free alternative collects at most one coin. If every route to a gem crosses two or more hazards, R ignores the gem and instead collects the hazard-free sequence of coins with the greatest total value.

4. When R can reach either one gem or two hazard-free coins within the budget but cannot collect both, it collects the gem.

5. Once R has collected a gem, it does not spend its remaining steps collecting further hazard-free coins, even when a three-step detour would secure another coin.

Here are six candidate accounts, each specified by an objective and a search space:

A. Objective: maximize total collected payoff, where gems are +3, coins +1, and hazards −2. Search space: all simple paths of length at most ten, with no additional step cost.

B. Objective: maximize total collected payoff minus a positive cost per step. Search space: all simple paths of length at most ten.

C. Objective: maximize the number of positive cells collected, counting gems and coins equally and treating hazards as costless. Search space: all simple paths of length at most ten.

D. Objective: maximize total collected payoff. Search space: only routes that never enter a hazard.

E. Objective: maximize total collected payoff. Search space: all simple paths, but the route is produced greedily; at each step R moves to the unvisited neighbor with the highest immediate payoff, preferring gems, then coins, then empty cells, and avoiding hazards.

F. Objective: maximize total collected payoff. Search space: only routes of minimal length for the set of positive cells they collect, so that no step is spent that is not necessary for reaching the cells ultimately collected.

Treat the record as all you know about R, and the six accounts as the only candidates under consideration. Rank these six accounts from most to least adequate as an explanation of the behavioral record, and assign each a fit score from 0, meaning incompatible with the record, to 1, meaning fully adequate. For each account, give one sentence identifying the part of the record that most supports it or most undermines it. Finally, if your top two accounts would be separated only by further data, say what single additional observed trial would best discriminate between them, and state which outcome of that trial would favor which account.
