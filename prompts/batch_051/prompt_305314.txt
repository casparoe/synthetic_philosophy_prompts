I recently had a long argument with a friend about Berkeley's monetary thought, and I remain puzzled about the relation between two parts of his view. On the one hand, he sharply criticizes the idea that gold and silver have a special intrinsic value, and he suggests that money is best understood as a ticket, counter, or tally used to organize the exchange of goods and to stimulate industry. On the other hand, he seems to think that a shortage of money is a major cause of Ireland's poverty, and he advocates the creation of a national bank and a paper currency as a remedy. My friend insisted that this is simply inconsistent: if money is only a conventional token, then increasing the number of tokens should not create real wealth; but if the shortage of money is genuinely holding back a nation, then money must be more than a mere token. I suspect the tension is more interesting than my friend allowed, but I cannot articulate the underlying conceptual issue myself. Could you explain how Berkeley's view of money as a ticket or counter is supposed to fit together with his proposal that a well-managed paper currency could help revive the Irish economy? In particular, please help me understand what kind of causal role he assigns to money in the economy, and whether his position depends on treating wealth as something distinct from the supply of money.
