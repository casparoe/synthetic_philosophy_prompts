I am a novelist, not a philosopher, and I have been asked to give a craft lecture on where my ideas come from. Rather than tell the usual anecdotes, I want to present, as plainly as I can, the working creed about creativity that I actually rely on. I have written it out below. Since the lecture will be given to people who enjoy poking at loose joints, I would like an audit of the creed before I submit to the room. I can feel that parts of it pull against one another, but I am too close to the material to distinguish real tensions from apparent ones, and I do not know which of my commitments would have to go. I would like you to do that sorting for me.

1. I accept, in broad outline, the knob-twiddling picture one associates with Douglas Hofstadter: a creative act is not the making of something from nothing but the discovery and turning of a parameter — a knob — that was already implicit in an existing structure, such as a genre, a plot shape, a sentence pattern, or a character type. What gets called originality is mostly the distance a knob is moved from its customary setting, not the creation of an altogether new knob. I do not claim to be a faithful exponent of Hofstadter; this is my own working version of the idea.

2. I believe variation on a theme is the central creative act, not one exercise among many. A theme is a bundle of constraints, and invention lives in what varies against them. Constraint is therefore not the enemy of creativity but its precondition; a writer with no fixed elements has nothing to push against and produces mush.

3. I also believe the distinction between a genuinely new work and a merely mechanical variation is real and worth defending. A novel that only turns the knobs a genre already advertises — change the setting, change the names, keep everything else — is exactly what is called formulaic. A novel that turns a knob nobody had noticed, a feature everyone else had treated as a wall rather than a dial, can be world-opening.

4. I believe the value of a variation lies in surprise under constraint, with the surprise visible only in retrospect. The best variation is one a competent reader could not have predicted from the theme, and yet, once made, it seems to have been latent in the theme all along. Pure randomness — for instance, a machine that twiddles knobs at random and discards the ungrammatical results — can supply novelty, but I deny that it supplies creativity, because it produces no retrospective sense of inevitability.

5. I further hold that a deliberate refusal to vary can itself be a creative act. A literal translation, a faithful transcription, or the decision to repeat a passage without alteration can be inventive when the refusal is chosen in full knowledge of the variations one has declined.

6. Two verdicts from my own practice matter to me, and I would be reluctant to surrender either. First, a writer who spends a lifetime producing hundreds of variations on one short story — same plot, same cast, same opening sentence — can be doing something more creative than a competent writer who turns out twenty unrelated novels, because the variations map, and thereby reveal, the space of possibility the theme contains. Second, most genre fiction that keeps a plot and swaps only its surface details is mechanical and uncreative, even though it too is, in the letter, variation on a theme.

7. Finally, my method is as follows. I begin by building a theme and then consciously turn one constraint at a time — the ages, the order of scenes, the tense, the degree of disclosure. But my best work has occurred when I noticed that something I had classified as a fixed wall, something I would have sworn was not a variable at all, was in fact a knob. That suggests the decisive act may be the noticing of a new knob rather than the turning of an old one.

What I want is not a lecture on creativity but an audit of this particular set. Please go through the commitments in pairs where you think they genuinely conflict, rank those conflicts by severity, and say in each case what would have to be given up and at what cost. Where two commitments only appear to conflict, say why the appearance is misleading. Then, taking the set as a whole, tell me which single revision would leave the position in the best shape, and what I would lose by making it.
