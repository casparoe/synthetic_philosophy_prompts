A character's ruin will be the convergence of four pressures—a debt he has concealed, a friend he once humiliated, a rival's forged letter, a pride that refuses help—each of which the story makes sufficient on its own; remove any one, and the ruin would still arrive. The effect is tragic weight: no single thread is load-bearing, yet the outcome does not read as arbitrary. On the other side, I gather that historians and philosophers of history use the same term for events whose candidate causes are each supposed sufficient, so that no single cause was necessary and counterfactual reasoning of the form "absent this factor, the event would not have occurred" is frustrated. The outbreak of a war or a financial panic may be overdetermined in this sense, leaving historians with the difficulty of saying what it means for any one factor to be a cause at all.

The apparent structural identity is this: a fixed outcome, a set of factors each sufficient, none individually necessary, the outcome robust under the deletion of any one factor.
