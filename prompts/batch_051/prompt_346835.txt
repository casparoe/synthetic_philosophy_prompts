Alfred North Whitehead's philosophy of education is organized around a contrast between what he calls inert ideas and ideas that are genuinely connected to experience, and he argues that the primary vice of education is the handling of ideas in an inert manner. He also distinguishes three stages in the rhythm of education, conventionally labelled romance, precision, and generalization. Please explain the conceptual structure of this theory. Specifically, I would like a careful account of what makes an idea inert in Whitehead's sense, why he takes inert ideas to be not merely useless but actively harmful, and how the movement from romance through precision to generalization is supposed to keep ideas from becoming inert. Focus on the underlying argument rather than historical or biographical context. I am especially interested in an apparent tension: the precision stage seems to involve discipline, exactness, and drill, which on the surface look like the very conditions that produce inert ideas. How does Whitehead think precision can be carried out without undoing his own educational aim?
