Imagine a city is designing a "civic contribution ledger"—a karma-style reputation system that will allocate access to a municipal time bank, a tool library, and priority in the emergency housing queue. People accrue points when others record that they helped, and points can be spent on priority access. The design is still open. Write a decision tree that classifies the main philosophical positions on what the ledger's score fundamentally is, and which design choices follow from each position.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of party labels. Each internal node should be a single concrete design question that a committee member could answer without already knowing which position it routes toward. Organize the questions so that, jointly, they encode at least two axes: whether the score is backward-looking (a record of what happened) or forward-looking (a lever for shaping future behavior), and whether the score is moralized (praise/blame, desert) or non-moralized (prediction, bookkeeping, or access control). Use cases like the following, improving them as you see fit:

- Should a single early betrayal become invisible after a decade of reliable conduct, or remain discoverable permanently?
- If someone records a favor, should the resulting score be visible to everyone, only to potential partners at the moment of a transaction, or only to the recipient?
- Should unused contributions work as a spendable private balance, or as a public summary of standing that cannot be spent?
- Should submitting an evaluation itself cost a small amount of the rater's own score, so that evaluations are costly signals rather than cheap talk?

The leaves should be the live positions in this debate. Include at least these positions: a desert-tracking moral ledger; a predictive trust score; a forward-looking incentive instrument; a settlement currency or mutual-credit ledger; a centralized social-credit governance score; and a minimalist or abolitionist position that resists any persistent quantified score. For each leaf, give one sentence on what the score primarily is, and one sentence on the position's most awkward commitment. If two leaves turn out to be design-equivalent under reasonable assumptions, flag that rather than manufacturing a distinction. Also mark and briefly diagnose the unoccupied cells in the logical space you build, and in particular explain why a decentralized, strongly moralized, and purely forward-looking score is so rarely occupied: is that combination incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored?

Keep the tree to roughly 8–11 leaves and at most 9 internal questions; the value is in the diagnostic questions, not exhaustiveness. End with a one-sentence summary of the taxonomy's main axis, as the last line, in the form \answer{...}.
