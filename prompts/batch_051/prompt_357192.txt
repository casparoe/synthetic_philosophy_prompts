I'm teaching an upper-level decision theory course and I want an essay prompt that gets students past the usual St. Petersburg reflex. Here's the game. A fair coin is tossed until it lands tails; if the first tails is on the nth toss, the player receives (-1)^{n-1} * 2^n / n dollars. Each outcome has probability 1/2^n, so if you write the expectation in the natural order you get 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which equals ln 2. But that's misleading: the positive and negative parts each diverge, so the expectation isn't actually defined — the series is only conditionally convergent, and rearranging its terms can make it sum to any real number, or diverge. Write an essay on whether decision theory should have anything to say to an agent who is offered this game, and if so what it should say. Cover at least two candidate responses (e.g., refusing to price the game, imposing bounded utilities, fixing a canonical summation order as a 'weak' expected value, or allowing rational preference to be incomplete) and defend a specific verdict. Don't end on 'it depends'; if your verdict is that the question is ill-posed, show why that's a substantive position rather than a dodge. Seperate what's settled mathematics (the undefined integral, conditional convergence, the rearrangement theorem) from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested fix as the consensus. Hard cap: 900 words. Lead with your bottom line.
