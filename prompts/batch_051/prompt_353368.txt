Im trying to get clear about what a semantics of generics actually has to deliver before I read any more papers, because every account I see nails two examples and quietly ignores everything else. Write me the desiderata for an adequate theory of generics. It has to handle the classics — why "ducks lay eggs" is true when most ducks never lay one, why "mosquitoes carry malaria" is true when almost none do, why "dogs are mammals" is true of the kind even if some individual dog is an oddball — and the cases each side reaches for when it wants to win: "sharks attack bathers," "peacocks have colorful tails," the striking-property stuff. It also has to respect some distinctions it can't collapse: generic vs universal, generic vs statistical frequency claim, kind-level vs individual-level predication, and descriptive vs normative generics like "boys don't cry" or "a real man provides" — if your theory only handles the descriptive ones, say what has to be added. And it has to live with the developmental fact that kids accept generics on very thin evidence, rather than making that impossible. Flag any pair of desiderata you think are jointly unsatisfiable, since thats the part I most want. Then commit: which single requirement knocks out the most going theories, and which family of accounts — majority-based, normality-based, dispositional, whatever — does best against your full list, not just the two examples its inventor picked.
