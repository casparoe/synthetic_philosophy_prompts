In my early Chinese philosophy course, we have been discussing the Zhuangzi's treatment of wu-wei, and I keep getting stuck on the swimmer story in the chapter called "Da Sheng" or "The Full Understanding of Life." As I understand the passage, Zhuangzi describes a man who is seen moving through a dangerous gorge where the water rushes violently. Confucius asks about his method, and the swimmer answers that he has none: he was born in these hills and grew up in the water, and he simply follows the water's own swirls and hollows, letting the current carry him, without deciding in advance where to go.

I cannot tell what philosophical work this story is doing. On the one hand, the text seems to present the swimmer as an example of wu-wei. On the other hand, wu-wei is supposed to resist being made into a method, because trying not to try reintroduces effort under another name. Yet the swimmer's reply looks like a positive explanation: he is not applying a skill but simply following conditions. Is the point that wu-wei is not something one does but something that happens once one's training or nature is right? If so, why is the story offered as a kind of teaching? Or is the swimmer still doing something intentional—trusting the water, refraining from interference—so the paradox is left standing rather than resolved? I want to know whether this passage is meant to dissolve the trying-not-to-try problem or to illustrate a hidden form of intentional skill that still needs analysis.
