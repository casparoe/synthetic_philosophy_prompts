I keep thinking about the Challenger launch decision as a test case for something I've been trying to get clear on. The night before the launch, the booster contractors' engineers said the O-rings might fail in the cold and recommended not launching, and then the managers overrode them and pushed it through; everyone knows the rest. Skip the engineering recap. What I actually want is for you to analyze that decision through the debate over whether optimism and hope are epistemic vices or moral virtues. It looks like a textbook case of people treating hope as a form of evidence: the managers wanted it to work, they framed the engineers' caution as a lack of confidence rather than a technical disagreement, and they seemed to think a good outcome was more likely because a can-do attitude made it feel inevitable. But here's the thing I cant settle: if you call that a vice, you might have to condemn basically all optimistic risk-taking, and spaceflight is exactly the kind of enterprise where you sometimes need hope to justify launching at all. So I want a careful mapping. What precisely did the managers' optimism do to their handling of the evidence, is the failure better described as epistemic, moral, or both, and does the case show hope is only a virtue in some domains, or that we need to draw a hard line between hoping for an outcome and predicting it? Also, some philosophers hold that beliefs aren't chosen, so it's unfair to blame someone for them. Does that let the managers off the hook, or does the case reveal that view is too coarse — maybe the culpability lived in their actions, or in how they responded to uncertainty, not in the belief itself.
