I just spent the evening arguing with a friend about Plato's views on drunkenness, and I came away with a draft essay that I suspect overreaches in one direction and misses something obvious in another. I'd like your comments on it, especially on whether my reading of the passage is accurate and whether the thesis is interesting enough to develop.

The passage I'm working from is Plato, Laws, Book 1, in R.G. Bury's translation:

"And for the purpose, first, of providing a cheap and comparatively harmless test of these conditions, and, secondly, of affording practice in them, what more suitable pleasure can we mention than wine, with its playful testing—provided that it is employed at all carefully? For consider: in the case of a man whose disposition is morose and savage (whence spring numberless iniquities), is it not more dangerous to test him by entering into money transactions with him, at one's own personal risk, than by associating with him with the help of Dionysus and his festive insight?"

Here are the notes for the essay:

- Working title: "Drunk as a Rehearsal: Wine, Testing, and Self-Mastery in Plato's Laws."

- The standard reading, the one my friend defended, treats wine in the Laws as a diagnostic: by getting citizens drunk under supervision, the city reveals what they are like before trusting them with money, power, or sex. The passage supports this: testing a morose man through money is dangerous, but testing him through Dionysus is comparatively safe.

- But the word "practice" in the passage is doing more work than the diagnostic reading allows. If drinking is practice, not just testing, then the symposium is not only a device for revealing character but for building it. The idea would be that by undergoing a controlled loss of self-command in the presence of sober older drinkers, a person learns what the onset of anger, lust, and overconfidence feels like from the inside, and thereby becomes better at recognizing and resisting those states in real life.

- The puzzle my friend kept pressing: how can voluntarily entering a condition that disables self-control train you for self-control? If the drinker's judgment is impaired, what is being practiced? My provisional answer is that Plato is not thinking of self-mastery as a single power that is either on or off, but as something partly environmental and partly anticipatory: the practice may be in learning to submit to a supervised impairment, to observe one's own desires emerging without acting on them fully, and to know the descent well enough to notice its early stages next time.

- But then I worry: if the lawgiver is standing there preventing real consequences, is the drinker practicing self-control at all, or just practicing being a passenger? A modern reader might say this is like learning to swim while a lifeguard holds you up. Plato might reply that the relevant skill is not last-moment resistance but early recognition, and that the supervised symposium builds exactly that. I don't yet know if that reply is in the text or just in my head.

- I also need to deal with the fact that, in the Laws, drinking is heavily age-gated and not prescribed for the very young. That may complicate the practice reading: if the young are the ones who most need training in self-mastery, why restrict the exercise to those already formed? I haven't resolved this.
