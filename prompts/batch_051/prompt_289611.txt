I'm an economics PhD student and I keep running into a pattern in my toy models that I don't have a word for, and it's making me suspicious of a slogan my supervisor likes to repeat. The slogan is that if you feed enough shared evidence to people who start from different beliefs, their starting positions eventually wash out and the data takes over. Fine. But in small models I keep seeing the opposite: two agents with the same likelihood function and very different priors both condition on the exact same observation, and afterward their posterior means are further apart than they were before. No bias, no cherry-picking, no difference in what each one attends to — just priors doing all the work. A labmate told me I was just redescribing confirmation bias, but in the model neither agent is being unreasonable in the update itself, so that explanation feels off.

What I want to know is whether this short-run divergence under shared evidence has a name of its own, and how it can coexist with the convergence claim, if that claim is actually a theorem rather than just something people say loosely. I care less about the label than the carving: what has to be true of the priors, or of the sequence of observations, for the long-run convergence to actually kick in, and is the divergence I keep seeing the typical case or just a weird exception? Also, does the convergence result require giving positive probability to the truth, and is that where the whole thing turns?
