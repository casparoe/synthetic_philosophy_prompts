I sit on the selection committee of a selective public STEM high school, and next month we vote on how much weight our admissions process should give to a standardized cognitive-ability test that has long served as an early screen. Two members want to drop the test altogether and rely instead on portfolios, teacher recommendations, and a structured interview; they argue that the test captures only a narrow slice of a child's capacities, and that it mainly rewards children who have been coached, who work quickly, or whose families could pay for practice materials. Two others want to keep the test: whatever its flaws, they say, it is the one piece of the file that means roughly the same thing from school to school, and the reason we have identified strong students in the past is that a single, general capacity genuinely shows up across very different kinds of problems. The remaining members, me included, are undecided.

I have no formal background in this area, but I have been reading on my own about whether intelligence is best understood as one underlying ability or as several loosely connected abilities, and about how hard it is to separate what a child can do from what a child has already been taught. Beneath the practical argument I keep returning to a more basic question: if we cannot say clearly what intelligence is, can we defend any particular way of measuring it? Please draft the decision memo I would circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action — keep the test, drop it, or change its role, for example by lowering its weight or using it only as a flag for further review. It should give reasons the full committee can act on, engage the strongest case for keeping the test honestly rather than dismissing it as defense of the status quo, and name the unstated assumption in this debate that is doing the most work.
