I write novels. Over years of making fiction, I have ended up with two convictions that sit uneasily together, and I want them adjudicated rather than merely decided.

On the one hand, I find Douglas Hofstadter's knob-twiddling account of creativity theoretically compelling. On that view, the crux of creation is variation on a theme: what we call an original work is a previously unoccupied point in a high-dimensional space of inherited possibilities, and making something new is a matter of turning parameters — voice, structure, point of view, pacing, subject, syntax, register — until a fresh configuration appears. Stated this strongly, the theory unifies a great deal. It explains why influence is inescapable without reducing influence to copying. It explains multiple independent discovery, since many explorers working through the same inherited space will converge on the same promising neighborhoods. It explains a writer's recognizable fingerprint as a set of habitual knob-settings. And it dissolves the fog around genius into something learnable: mastery of a parameter space plus taste for which configurations are worth stopping at. In my own practice I can often reconstruct the finite chain of small adjustments that produced a passage I had believed to be wholly new.

On the other hand, I cannot shake a contrary intuition about my own most consequential creative moments. They do not feel like finding an unused point in the available space; they feel like breaking the panel on which the knobs are mounted. There seems to me a real difference between a surprising combination of familiar elements and a move that changes the axes along which later work will be organized — a difference between novelty within a frame and novelty of the frame. When a scene turns somewhere I did not plan and yet lands with an air of inevitability, my first-person experience is not one of continuous adjustment but of discontinuity, of something arriving from outside the frame I had been working inside. The intuition is not that influence is false. It is that no amount of parameter adjustment, however fine, can amount to the addition of a new dimension.

I am not asking for exegesis of Hofstadter's actual essays; treat the theory as I have characterized it.

What I want is not a verdict but an account of the conditions of surrender. What would it take for the intuition to be the thing I abandon — that is, for me to accept that frame-breaking novelty is an artifact of attention, of failing to perceive the continuum of intermediate steps? What would it take for the theory to be the thing I abandon or sharply limit — that is, for knob-twiddling to be an adequate account of ordinary craft but not of the exceptional cases? And is there a reading of "space" or "variation" on which both convictions survive, perhaps one that permits a knob-turning process to modify the machine that does the turning — and if so, does that move rescue the theory or empty it? Please argue for the conditions under which each surrender or reconciliation would be justified, rather than simply choosing a side.
