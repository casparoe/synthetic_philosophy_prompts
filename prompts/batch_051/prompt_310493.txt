I'm a philosopher of science, and for the past year my reading group has been arguing about natural kinds: whether the classifications in mature sciences earn their authority because they track mind-independent joints in nature, or because they're just stably useful ways of sorting things for our purposes. The two most dug-in members — a joint-carving realist and a promiscuous pluralist — have finally agreed to stop talking past each other and do a proper adverserial collaboration, and I've offered to draft the document they'd sign. I don't want a verdict on who's right; I want a constructive text both could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact.

So draft me that joint statement. Open with a numbered list of claims both sides can sign, including claims about why scientific classification succeeds, how to recognise a good taxonomic scheme, and what the periodic table and the species problem each do and don't show. Then a disagreements table: for each point of residual disagreement, give the realist's wording of her own view and the pluralist's wording of his, each phrased so its own side would sign it, plus what would have to change for each of them to shift. Nail down the cruxes. My hunch is that one crux is whether cross-cutting but equally projectable classifications (biological taxonomy's many species concepts, or rival psychiatric classifications) are the norm or a marginal embarrassment, and another is whether joint-carvingness does any independent explanatory work beyond predictive success. For each crux, specify a concrete case or test both sides agree bears on it, and write down what each side predicts in advance. Where you think a disagreement is partly verbal, say so and give the deverbalised formulation both would accept. Please write it in British English (spelling and conventions), and keep the whole thing signable — no side should feel their view has been strawmanned.
