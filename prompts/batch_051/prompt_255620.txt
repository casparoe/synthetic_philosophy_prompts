I run a small workshop on the conceptual foundations of AI alignment for about sixteen graduate students and postdocs in computer science, economics, and philosophy, and we meet for two hours. I'm looking for a session activity in which participants discover, before any lecture, how much is hidden behind the apparently simple task of inferring an optimizer's objective and search space from its behavior. My rough picture is a two-team game: Team A secretly commits, on a written form, to an objective function over a small finite option space and to a restricted feasible set within that space, then makes a sequence of choices; Team B observes those choices and tries to reconstruct both the objective and the feasible set. I'd like the option space to be concrete and easily computed by hand — perhaps options as vectors with two numeric attributes (for example, three-day meal plans characterized by cost and calorie total) plus a binary label (for example, vegetarian or not) — so that teams can encode objectives as small weighted sums and feasibility as a threshold or labeling rule. Team B should also receive a small budget of probe queries: Team B names a hypothetical option that was not among the observed choices, and Team A must say whether it was in the feasible set and would have been chosen under the stated objective; each query costs Team B one point from its score. After Team B submits its inference and the scoring key is applied, the teams swap roles with a new hidden specification, and then one round should work differently: the facilitator hands both teams a printed sequence of choices and challenges them to produce two very different objective-plus-search-space pairs that both render every observed choice optimal, so that the underdetermination becomes vivid rather than merely asserted.

The deliverable should be a complete, runnable activity for this group and time budget, not a lecture or discussion guide. Please specify: the exact option space and how it is displayed; the form Team A fills in; the number of observation rounds and probe queries and what may be asked in a probe; the scoring rule for how closely Team B's inferred objective and feasible set match Team A's hidden specification, including a penalty for spurious complexity; and an hour-by-hour schedule for the two-hour session. Then write a debrief that maps concrete moments in the game to the ideas participants should walk away seeing: that a finite sequence of choices can be rationalized by many different objective-plus-search-space pairs, that inference improves only once the search space or its constraints are pinned down, that what the optimizer can even consider is as constitutive of its behavior as what it wants, and that no finite set of probes fully closes the gap between what behavior reveals and what the objective actually is. Please also say what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run — for example, teams smuggling in assumptions about simplicity or about what a reasonable objective would be, or designing objectives that are too easy to read off from the first two choices — and how a facilitator should handle those failures without giving away the lesson. Finally, be explicit about which feature of real optimizer inference this tabletop game cannot reproduce on its own, such as the fact that a real optimizer's choices are produced by a search process over the space under computational limits rather than by an omniscient ordering over a tiny grid, and say how the facilitator should supply that point in the final ten minutes.
