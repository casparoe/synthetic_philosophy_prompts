I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, mostly evolutionary biology for a general audience, and over the years I've noticed a tic in my own writing and in almost everyone else's. We reach for the gene's-eye view automatically. A story isn't considered finished until we've said what the genes are "doing" or "wanting"—the gene for this, the gene that gets itself passed on. I don't object to that as shorthand. The move that bothers me is stronger, and I've taken to calling it the gene's-eye veto: the unstated rule that an evolutionary explanation doesn't count as a real explanation until it's been restated at the level of genes, and that any explanation framed in terms of the good of the group, the organism, or the species is suspect—maybe not false, but second-class—unless and until somebody translates it into gene-level terms. I've had editors push me to do exactly that, and now I do it to myself. When I push back, collegues say they're just following Dawkins and Williams, who showed the gene is the unit of selection and group-level stories are usually lazy. But there's a gap between "usually lazy" and "guilty until proven innocent," and I'm not sure the gene's-eye view actually licenses the veto.

Here's what I want. Is this already a thing—is it just Dennett's greedy reductionism with a biological accent, or is it its own phenomenon? Does anyone on the critical side (Gould, the multi-level selection people, the niche construction folks) actually argue against this specific methodological rule, or are they arguing against something else? Most importantly, is the veto defensible? Give me the strongest case for it, the three strongest objections to it, and whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable. And where does it break: what real evolutionary phenomena refuse to be restated at the gene level without distortion, and is that a boundary or a refutation? Keep the whole thing under 500 words.
