I am trying to get clear about the line between manipulation and legitimate persuasion, and I keep finding that my reactions to particular cases do not add up to any simple rule. I want you to help me find the principle—or a small set of principles—that best captures my verdicts, and to tell me honestly which of my verdicts, if any, I should abandon.

Here are my reactions to a range of cases. In each case, assume that the person doing the influencing says nothing false unless the case says otherwise.

1. Rebalancing a portfolio. An adviser explains to her client, in plain language and with the downsides stated, why she thinks the client's stock allocation is too risky, and makes clear that the decision is the client's to make. The client is slightly anxious about money, and the adviser knows that this anxiety makes the client more likely to accept the recommendation. Verdict: clearly legitimate persuasion.

2. The last car. A car salesperson tells a buyer that another customer is on the way to see the same car and that the buyer must decide today or lose it. None of this is true. Verdict: clearly manipulation, and dishonest.

3. The rented truck. A friend asks me for help moving. When I hesitate, he says: "You said you'd help, and I've already rented the truck. If you don't come, I can't do this alone." Circumstances have made this true, and he genuinely would be stranded without me. The appeal makes me feel guilty, and he knows it will. Verdict: legitimate, though it does put pressure on me.

4. The friendship test. A different friend asks for a loan and says: "If you were really my friend, you'd do this for me." He would not, if pressed, actually treat a refusal as ending the friendship; he says it because it works. Verdict: manipulative, though it contains no falsehood.

5. Two frames. A surgeon tells one patient that an operation has a 90% survival rate. To another patient in the same situation, she says that it carries a 10% risk of death, and she chooses the second wording because she knows that patient tends to underestimate risk. Verdict: I am uneasy, but I cannot call it outright manipulation; it feels more like steering than deciding.

6. Two rooms left. A hotel website states, accurately, that only two rooms are left at the listed price. I feel a spike of urgency and book. Later, I confirm that the statement was true. Verdict: legitimate, because it gave me information I actually needed, even though it triggered urgency.

7. The endless sale. A shopping app shows a countdown banner: "Ends in three hours." The countdown does end, but an identical sale begins the next morning, and the company deliberately sequences the sales to keep permanent, low-grade time pressure on shoppers. Verdict: manipulative, even though each specific countdown is arguably true.

8. The boost. A teacher tells a hesitant student, "I wouldn't be setting you this task if I didn't think you could do it." The teacher genuinely believes the student can do it; she also says it partly because saying it tends to make students try harder. Verdict: legitimate.

9. The coach's flattery. A career coach tells a client who is weighing a safe job against starting a business: "Someone with your drive will feel dead inside in a corporate job." The coach thinks this may be true, but his main reason for saying it is that it keeps the client paying for sessions about the startup plan. Verdict: manipulative.

10. The secure partner. During an argument, one partner says to the other: "A secure person wouldn't react the way you're reacting right now." Saying this reliably makes the other person doubt their own response and back down. The speaker does, at least in the moment, believe the sentence. Verdict: manipulative.

I have a tentative principle that feels close: an influence is legitimate if it engages the other person's own judgment by providing accurate considerations relevant to their ends, and manipulative if it moves the person through pressures that bypass or distort their judgment—guilt, insecurity, urgency, flattery, social comparison, self-doubt—especially when the influencer's purposes or methods are not made explicit. But I can already see the tensions. Cases 3 and 4 both work through guilt, yet I excuse one and condemn the other. Cases 5, 6, and 7 all influence through framing and urgency with true content, yet I treat them differently. The teacher and the coach both appeal to the other person's self-image, and I split those two as well.

Please give me the best reconstruction. State the principle, or small set of principles, that captures most of my verdicts with the least strain, and say exactly where each clause earns its keep. Test it against cases I have not yet considered—for instance, a poker player's bluff, a high first offer in a salary negotiation, a truthful but overwhelming disclosure document that buries a key fact, and a public health campaign that uses graphic images of diseased lungs rather than an argument. Then tell me plainly: which of my verdicts must be dropped or revised if the principle is to be simple, and which could be saved only by adding epicycles? If you think the very idea of a sharp line between manipulation and legitimate persuasion is confused, or that my tentative principle is the wrong sort of thing to be looking for, say that directly rather than humoring me.
