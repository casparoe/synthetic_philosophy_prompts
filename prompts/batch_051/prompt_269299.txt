As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I’m used to noticing when other people have too much invested in their conclusions. Recently, though, I’ve started to suspect the problem in myself, and I would like to know what to make of it before I write anything further on the subject. Several months ago I published a feature arguing that researchers in a fast-moving empirical field tend to defend their early, career-defining findings long after the balance of evidence has shifted against them. I thought I was following the evidence. Since publication, I’ve received a stream of methodological objections and new analyses suggesting that my central claim was too strong. I still believe the main thesis is broadly right; the counterexamples seem to me selective, confounded, or beside the point. But I also know I have a clear motive to keep believing it: retracting the core of the piece would be professionally embarrassing and would force me to revise the public identity I’ve built as a writer. I don’t feel as if I’m lying when I dismiss the objections. Yet the pattern is uncomfortably convenient.

A philosopher friend told me that deliberately lying to oneself may not even be coherent, because it seems to require being both the deceiver and the deceived at once: I would have to know the truth well enough to hide it from myself while also not knowing it. He suspects that what I’m doing is either ordinary motivated reasoning—believing badly because of the direction my interests pull—or some less paradoxical failure of self-knowledge. I’m not satisfied with that diagnosis. In my experience as a journalist, I’ve met people who seemed not merely biased but arranged so as never to notice evidence that would harm their self-conception. My question is this: given the case I’ve described, how should I think about what I’m doing? Is there a philosophically workable account of deceiving oneself that can distinguish it from ordinary bias or sincere error, and if so, what test would help me tell which condition I’m in?
