You are a political philosopher specializing in democratic theory, seconded to a constitutional commission that screens proposed referendum questions before they may appear on the ballot. The commission reviews about forty proposals a year, on matters ranging from tax policy and infrastructure to social rights and institutional reform. The stakes are high: once a question is certified, the commission cannot control how campaigns will frame it, and a "yes" vote may bind the legislature for a decade. The staff who will actually run the screening are competent policy analysts but not political theorists, and each proposal must be screened in roughly half a day. The final procedure must be written so that the commission's chair, a non-specialist, can defend it in public hearings.

Your task is to write a reusable screening procedure the staff can apply. It should be grounded in what democratic theory actually says about the difference between decisions that are fit for direct popular judgment and decisions that should be filtered through representative institutions. Do not include citations or a reading list; write it as a working document for the commission.

The procedure should take the form of an ordered checklist or decision tree in words: tests to run in sequence, with clear instructions for what counts as passing, failing, or needing escalation, and a defined set of possible outputs, such as certify, reject-as-unfit, return-for-redrafting, or split-the-question. Turn each relevant normative consideration into a concrete test a staffer can run without assessing the policy's merits. The considerations you operationalize should include, at minimum: whether the question has a stable meaning that ordinary voters can be expected to hold; whether the choice is genuinely binary or has more than two live options; whether majority preference is especially likely to be distorted by intensity, information, or unusually short time horizons; whether the decision concentrates costs on identifiable people, minority groups, or future generations; whether voters can correct a mistake later at acceptable cost; and whether the proposed question actually bundles several separate decisions into one. These are not to be listed as abstract values; each must become a usable test.

The procedure must be honest about where judgment cannot be eliminated. At each step, mark where a staffer's own view about the policy could leak in, and give a substitute standard to use instead — for example, how to assess "stable meaning" without assessing whether the proposition is a good idea. For any numerical thresholds you build in, mark them as adjustable parameters rather than settled findings, and explain briefly what changes if they are moved.

Then apply the completed procedure to these three worked cases:

1. A proposal to add a balanced-budget requirement to the constitution, with automatic spending cuts if the budget is not balanced by a fixed date.
2. A proposal to require that any municipal zoning change affecting more than five acres be approved in a binding referendum by the residents of the affected neighborhood.
3. A proposal to lower the voting age for national elections from 18 to 16.

For each case, work through every step of the procedure, state the overall output, and say which steps were genuinely decisive. At least one of the three should be a case in which the procedure recommends against certification even though the proposal is plainly popular, and the worked example should explain exactly how the procedure produces that unwelcome answer.

End with a short section on what the procedure cannot do: the kinds of democratic failure it is not designed to catch, the cases where it will systematically misfire if used mechanically, and the questions that should be escalated to the full commission rather than settled by staff.

Before writing the final procedure, think through the tradeoffs aloud: which theoretical distinctions are worth preserving in a half-day test, which must be collapsed into rough proxies, and where the best available procedure is still a judgment call dressed up as a checklist. Then present the procedure itself, followed by the three worked cases and the limitations section. Work carefully and step by step.
