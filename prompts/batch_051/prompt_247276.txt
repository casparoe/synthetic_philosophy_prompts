Been going down a social choice rabbit hole lately and I keep getting stuck on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, specifically the impartial-observer version of it. The standard setup: an observer behind a veil of ignorance has an equal probability 1/n of ending up as each person in society, has von Neumann–Morgenstern preferences over lotteries on social states, and so their expected utility is just the average of everyone's utilities. Add the Pareto condition and you get utilitarianism (or at least a weighted sum, depending on how you present it). Fine.

But I want to vary one thing. Suppose the observer is not actually impartial about identity. Instead of 1/n each, they have fixed, known probabilities p_i of ending up as person i, with the p_i not all equal—maybe they think it's more likely they'll wake up as someone similar to themselves, or they've got some weird prior about which lives are more 'theirs.' Run the Harsanyi argument with that change. Does the theorem still go through, and what exactly falls out? I can see itll be sum p_i u_i, but I want to know whether the Pareto condition alone forces that weighted-sum form, or whether you have to hand the p_i to the observer as an extra assumption. And here's the part I actually find philosophically interesting: is the result still utilitarianism? If the weights are arbitrary, has the impartial-observer defense of utilitarianism just collapsed into 'you get out whatever weights you put in,' or is there some formal feature that still makes equal weights the privileged case? Also, if two observers disagree about the p_i, do they just get different social welfare functions and there's nothing more to say, or does that disagreement point to a deeper problem with the whole veil-of-ignorance move?
