Last week in my networking class we went over framing, headers, and payloads, and the whole time I had the weird feeling I'd seen this layering before. Then I remembered the passage in Gödel, Escher, Bach where Hofstadter distinguishes the frame message, the outer message, and the inner message. The way I understand him: the frame message is just the brute announcement, carried by the physical object or signal, that there is a message here to be decoded at all. The outer message is the implicit structural information about how to decode it—what language it's in, that it's a poem, where the symbol boundaries are. The inner message is the content you get after decoding. Map that onto networking and framing/delimiter bits are the frame message, protocol headers are the outer message, and the payload is the inner message.

At first I thought this was just the generic fact that communication has levels, but the more I poked at it the more it felt like a real structural analogy, especially because Hofstadter's point is that meaning doesn't live in any one level but in the relationships between them—which sounds a lot like the way a packet only means anything because the frame, headers, and payload line up correctly, and parsing ambiguity (header smuggling, a mis-set Content-Type) makes the whole thing mean two different things depending on which decoder you ask. Is this parallel deep or superficial? I want to know where the two pictures genuinly match, where they come apart, and whether anything nontrivial transfers either way. In particular, does the fact that network headers are explicit, standardized, and spoofable sharpen or break Hofstadter's idea of an outer message as something more implicit and convention-based?
