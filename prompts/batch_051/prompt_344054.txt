I am taking a philosophy of mind course on pain and affect this term, and we have been discussing pain asymbolia. I have assembled the following argument from the case, and I half-believe it but worry that it is moving too fast. Could you go through it step by step and tell me whether it is valid, and if not, exactly which step fails and how the step should be restated?

(1) Pain is standardly divided into two dissociable components: a sensory-discriminative component, covering intensity, location, and quality such as sharp or burning, and an affective-motivational component, covering unpleasantness, distress, and the urge to withdraw from or stop the stimulus.
(2) In pain asymbolia, the sensory-discriminative component remains while the affective-motivational component is absent: the person can identify the stimulus as painful, locate it on the body, and describe it as sharp or dull, yet shows no signs of suffering, no avoidance, and no inclination to describe the experience as something they mind.
(3) If the badness of a pain were intrinsic to the pain sensation itself, a feature the sensation carries on its own independently of any further state such as aversion or distress, then anyone who had that sensation would, merely in having it, be in a state that is bad for them.
(4) But the asymbolic person has the sensation and is not in a state that is bad for them in the relevant sense: they are not suffering, and they do not mind the stimulus; the usual motivational objectionableness of pain is missing.
(5) Therefore, the badness of pain is not intrinsic to the pain sensation. It instead belongs to the affective-motivational component, which can come apart from the sensation.

I think the evidence for (2) is fairly robust, and (3) seems to me close to a statement of what intrinsic badness would have to mean. The move I distrust is from the first part of (5) to the second: even if badness is not intrinsic to the sensation, it might belong to the whole package, or to a relation between the sensation and something else, rather than to the affective component alone. Am I right to distrust that move, or does the argument fail earlier, or does it succeed as it stands? If you think a specific premise should be rejected, please say which one and how you would repair it. And please also tell me which consideration would most likely change your verdict about where the argument fails, or where your diagnosis is least secure.
