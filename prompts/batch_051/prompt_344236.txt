I've been trying to figure out whether loss aversion and the endowment effect are actual mistakes or just normal preferences, and I thought I'd write down my gut reactions to a bunch of cases to see if some principle catches all of them.

- The mug. If I don't own the mug, I'd pay maybe $25 for it at most. If you hand it to me and offer to buy it back ten minutes later, I'd hold out for more like $40. I don't think this is obviously dumb — the mug in front of me is a particular object now, and selling it means losing it — though I admit ten minutes is not much time to get attached to anything.
- The tanked stock. I bought shares at $80, they're now at $55, and I can't bring myself to sell because I keep thinking I'd be "locking in" the loss. This feels like a mistake, full stop. The $80 shouldn't matter anymore; the only question is whether I'd buy the stock today at $55.
- The concert ticket. I paid $120 for a ticket a month ago, it's now snowing and I'm exhausted, and I go anyway because staying home means wasting the $120. If the ticket had been free I'd stay home. I don't love that I do this, but I'm not sure it's wrong either — something about keeping faith with my past self?
- The family house. A developer offered my uncle 40% over appraised value for the house he's lived in for thirty-five years and he refused. I think that's a completely legitimate preference, not an error. The house is woven into his life in a way the money can't be.
- The minivan. My brother always insisted he'd never drive a minivan. Then he had twins, rented one for a week, and now won't give it up. I don't know whether that's the endowment effect or just him learning he actually likes minivans. It feels fine either way.
- The 401(k). My employer's default is a 3% contribution into a fund I never would've picked, and I've left it untouched for two years. That feels like pure inertia and I'm mildly annoyed at myself about it. But then I think: maybe my not caring enough to spend an hour changing it is itself a real preference, and the annoyance is the mistake.

I can't tell if their is a stable line between the cases where I want to say "that's a genuine preference" and the cases where I want to say "that's a bias or a failure of reasoning." Sometimes I think the line is about framing: a bias is when I'd reverse the preference just by changing how the choice is described, while a real preference survives the reframing. But the mug case fails that test, since I only care because the mug happens to be mine. Sometimes I think the line is instead: real preferences are about the particular thing you actually have, and biases are about numbers in your head like purchase prices. But then again, the stock case is all numbers and the house case is all particular things, so maybe that works.

What principle, if any, captures my verdicts? Which of my verdicts does it force me to give up? And what does the principle say about a case I keep going back and forth on: someone who won't sell a lottery ticket to a friend for $50, even though they'd never pay $50 for that ticket if someone else owned it?
