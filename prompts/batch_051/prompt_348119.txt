I'm taking a political theory course this term on whether authoritarian governments can ever be legitimate, and I've been turning over an argument that started as a defense of what our instructor called "performance legitimacy" — the view that states like China (or Singapore on a smaller scale) can earn their legitimacy by delivering order, growth, and stability rather than by elections. I actually got pretty sympathetic to the view, until I realized it might commit me to a conclusion I find repulsive. I keep trying to find the exit and failing, so I'd like someone to locate it or price the alternatives.

The argument runs like this:

(1) A state's claim to authority is justified by what it does: providing security, keeping civil peace, and creating the conditions for people to live decently. That's the whole point of having a state.

(2) So a state that reliably delivers those goods is legitimate, and its subjects are under a genuine obligation to obey it and not try to undermine it — regardless of how the state is chosen.

(3) By this test, a competent one-party regime that has (let's just grant for the sake of argument) lifted a large share of its population out of poverty and kept basic order is legitimate. Its subjects' lack of a vote doesn't matter, because its decision procedure isn't part of the test.

(4) But the same reasoning reaches an occupying power. Suppose a foreign army invades, drives out a corrupt government, and then runs the territory much better: less violent crime, more honest administration, steady growth. The population has no more say over the occupier than over the one-party state in (3), and the occupier passes the only test in (2).

(5) Yet successful post-conquest administration plainly does not make an occupation legitimate. A foreign army ruling you competently is still ruling you by force, not by right, and you owe it no obedience.

(6) So (1)–(2) proves too much: it seems to hand legitimacy to any effective ruler, and that's exactly the conclusion that makes me recoil. But if I block step (4), it looks like I have to give something up in (1) or (2), which would also undercut (3) — the conclusion I wanted to keep. Which step is doing the real work, and what do I pay for rejecting it? If I want to keep (3) but reject (5), what extra condition do I have to add to (2), and am I willing to accept everything that condition commits me to?

When you answer, could you keep separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among political theorists, and not present a contested view as if it were the consensus?
