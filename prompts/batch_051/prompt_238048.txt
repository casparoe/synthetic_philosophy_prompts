I am trying to reconstruct the principle behind a friend's notebook. She works on practical rationality, and for years she has written brief verdicts about what she calls rational irrationality: situations in which a person arranges something in advance for good reason, and then, when the time for performance comes, the original reasons have evaporated and only the fact of the promise, instruction, or announced policy remains. The entries I remember run like this. A version of Parfit's desert hitchhiker: a motorist will drive a stranded man to safety only if the man promises to pay him afterwards, and the price is roughly what a taxi would have charged; she wrote, pay once home. Ulysses and the Sirens: the crew should keep him lashed to the mast even as he begs to be untied. Exam week: a student gives her flatmate the Wi-Fi password and says not to return it until Friday; when the student begs on Thursday, the flatmate should refuse. A mugger returns a man's keys only after extracting a promise of a later cash drop; she wrote, do not pay, because a promise extracted by a wrongful threat is not binding. A sick neighbour: you promised to fetch her prescription, and when the afternoon comes you are exhausted and it is raining, but you should still go. A shopkeeper puts a sign in the window saying he will always prosecute shoplifters; she wrote, prosecute the next one, even though the prosecution is costly and cannot undo that particular theft.

These verdicts feel as if they could issue from more than one principle, and that is what I am trying to work out. The newest page in the notebook has no verdict. A hillwalker breaks his ankle on a remote fell as the weather turns; a passing outdoor instructor reaches him but will not call mountain rescue unless the walker promises to pay him four thousand pounds once he is off the hill, a price several times the going rate and far beyond the instructor's costs. The walker promises, is winched to safety, and now wonders whether he should send the money.

I would like you to do three things. First, state the two or three most plausible principles that would make all her earlier verdicts hang together: I can make out at least a reliance view, on which a commitment binds because the other party relied on it and it was not extracted coercively, and a disposition view, on which a commitment binds because the disposition to honour commitments of that kind is worth having in advance even when the later performance is a dead loss; tell me if you think there is a better candidate. Second, say what each principle predicts she would write about the fell case, attending to how that case is like and unlike both the fair-price hitchhiker and the mugger, and give your best prediction of her actual verdict, with your reasons. Third, name a further case whose outcome would cleanly separate whichever principles you take to be live, and say which way you think it cuts.
