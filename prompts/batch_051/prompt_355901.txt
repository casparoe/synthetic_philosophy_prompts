Construct a letter-string analogy problem in the Copycat microdomain — one source pair of lowercase strings and one target string — engineered so that three specified models give three different answers, with the divergence coming entirely from conceptual slippage rather than from any ambiguity in how the task is posed. The first model is a literal rule-induction model: it extracts an explicit transformation rule from the source pair (for example, replace the rightmost letter by its alphabetic successor), stores it as a rigid schema, and applies that schema to the target, halting or taking the nearest available letter without reinterpreting anything when the schema cannot be applied. The second is a static similarity model: it compares the target with the two strings in the source pair by surface overlap, retrieves the more similar one, and transfers the changed material wholesale, with no recombination of roles under pressure. The third is a slippage-capable analogical model: it represents roles such as rightmost, successor, and group-of-identical-letters as fluid bonds that can slip to neighboring roles (rightmost to leftmost, successor to predecessor, a particular letter to the group it instantiates) when the target's structure makes the straightforward mapping fail, and it settles by a parallel, stochastic search rather than by executing a fixed rule. Design the case so that the literal model and the similarity model each produce a different completion that is defensible as a reasonable but rigid answer, while the slippage model produces a third completion that a human judge would prefer. Constraints: every string is two to four lowercase letters; the source transformation must support at least two distinct rules that agree on the source pair but diverge on the target; the target must itself supply the pressure for slippage through its own structure (an alphabet boundary, a symmetry, a letter grouping), not through any added instruction; and no model may arrive at its answer through a tie-breaking convention silently built into the problem. The case is for isolating what conceptual slippage contributes to analogical competence, so it is cheating if a verdict is stipulated rather than generated by a model's architecture, or if the models diverge only because they were given different information. After you present the problem, walk through what each of the three models does and why, state exactly where the slippage occurs and which role slips to what under which pressure, identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, say what happens if that feature is removed, and explain how a defender of the literal rule-induction model would attack the case. Respond in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write a letter.
