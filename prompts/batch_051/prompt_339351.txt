I'm a philosophy professor, and I sit on the board that manages our county's 1,200-acre working commons—residents graze sheep, cut firewood, keep hives, and every season we get a few applications from people who want to fence off a piece and call it theirs because they've "improved" it (a pond, an orchard, a shed), and once we grant an enclosure it's effectively permanent. I keep thinking Locke's labor theory of acquisition and the proviso ought to give us a decision procedure, not just a vibe. Write me one: a checklist a non-philosopher commissioner can run in under an hour before the full board votes, grounded in what Locke actually says, that tells us when mixing one's labor with an unowned resource is enough to ground a property claim and when it isn't. Build in the hard cases—does a cash payment into a stewardship fund satisfy the proviso, does leaving a seperate but equally good resource elsewhere count, does it matter whether the baseline is before the labor or after the improvement, do people not born yet have standing. Work up two or three realistic applications, and make sure one comes out as a denial that the applicant and half my colleagues will hate; also say plainly where the procedure gives out and the board has to just decide. And commit: don't give me a survey of readings of the proviso—pick the interpretation you'll stand behind and build the procedure around it.
