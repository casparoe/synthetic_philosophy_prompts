I work in philosophy of mind, and at a recent workshop a colleague was defending constitutive micropsychism: the view that a person's unified consciousness is literally composed of the consciousness of the microphysical constituents of the body, with nothing added over and above the many micro-subjects and their relations. When someone raised the combination problem, he reached for an acoustic analogy. Individual tones, he said, combine into a chord. Each tone remains present in the chord, yet the chord is not a further thing added to the tones; it just is the tones standing in certain temporal and harmonic relations. Likewise, he argued, a person's unified consciousness just is many micro-subjects standing in the right relations, and the demand for some extra ingredient that binds them into one subject is as confused as demanding an extra ingredient beyond the notes to get a chord. I would like an audit of how far this analogy can carry that conclusion. Give the correspondence structure explicitly: what maps onto the tones, the chord, the relations among the notes, the listener, and the unity of the sound. Then identify the places where the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended use from the ones that merely point out incidental differences. In particular, say what the acoustic case quietly imports about a perspective or field in which the notes are unified, and whether that feature transfers to the experiential case. Finally, give a verdict on whether the deflationary response to the combination problem survives the disanalogies, and, if you think a better analogy is available, say what it is. Please also indicate what would most likely change your verdict: which consideration, if it turned out otherwise, would flip your assessment, and where your own reasoning about the analogy is least certain.
