Write a Shakespearean sonnet in the voice of Johannes Kepler, addressed to a patron who has commissioned a horoscope. Use the first two quatrains for the public face of the work: Kepler the imperial mathematician, contemptuous of the popular astrology of zodiac signs and fatalistic forecasts, insisting that his own horoscopes are legitimate because planetary aspects act on the earth and on human temperament through the same geometrical order that governs his astronomy. The volta should arrive in the third quatrain, at line 9, and turn to what he does not put in the letter: that the practice lives on the vanity of clients who want flattery and forecasts, that casting these figures is part of how an imperial mathematician is paid, and that the serious study of the heavens has to be fed by a trade he half-despises. The closing couplet should make the word harmony do double work — the mathematical harmony of the third law, that the square of a planet's orbital period is proportional to the cube of its mean distance from the sun, and the social harmony of a court astrologer telling a patron what the patron hopes to hear. The poem should stand as a poem for a reader who knows none of this history, but it should also be accurate enough that a historian of early modern astronomy and astrology would recognize Kepler's actual compromise, and would not mistake him for either a true believer in the vulgar art or a cynical fraud.
