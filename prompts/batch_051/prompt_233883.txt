As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I am preparing a feature on how civic bodies learn—or fail to learn—from public consultation, and I need a controlled thought experiment to anchor the piece. I want you to build the case from John Dewey's experimentalist account of experience and inquiry, using the following passage from John Dewey, *Democracy and Education* (1916), Chapter XI, "Experience and Thinking," as the test:

"Mere activity does not constitute experience. It is dispersive, centrifugal, dissipating. Experience as trying involves change, but change is meaningless transition unless it is consciously connected with the return wave of consequences which flow from it. When an activity is continued into the undergoing of consequences, when the change made by action is reflected back into a change made in us, the mere flux is loaded with significance. We learn something."

Here are the specifications. Design a realistic, ordinary civic case—no science fiction, no exotic psychology—in which two evaluators looking at the same public consultation process reach opposite verdicts. The first evaluator applies Dewey's criterion from the passage: an activity becomes experience only if an action is continued into the undergoing of its consequences and the resulting change is consciously connected back to the action. The second evaluator applies a minimal information-transmission standard: the process counts as successful if the relevant material was presented clearly and participants can afterwards recall or recognize its main points. Engineer the scenario so that Dewey's evaluator denies that the participants underwent experience in the specified sense, while the information-transmission evaluator counts the process as successful. After presenting the scenario, do the check: identify the activity, the undergoing, whether and where the backward-and-forward connection failed or held, and exactly why each criterion gives the verdict it does. Then name the structural feature of the case that does the decisive work, and say what would happen if that feature were removed—would the disagreement collapse, or does it persist because the two evaluators are really tracking different values? End by giving the strongest response available to a defender of the information-transmission standard who wants to argue that Dewey's criterion is simply too demanding for democratic practice.
