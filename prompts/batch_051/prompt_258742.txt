I'm a philosophy doctoral student, and in a fortnight I have to teach a single seventy-five-minute session on Cox's theorem to final-year undergraduates taking a philosophy of science option. They have done a unit on propositional logic and have been shown the Kolmogorov axioms for probability, but they have not been asked before why degrees of belief in propositions ought to obey those axioms. The course convenor has warned me that the class will treat probability as straightforwardly about frequencies and will suspect that any proof that belief degrees must be probabilities is either circular or merely stipulative.

I don't want you to explain Cox's theorem to me. I want a plan for how I should explain it in the room, whiteboard only and no assigned reading. Please give me the sequence in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced, and what depends on what: where plausibility as a degree of belief must be set up before the desiderata can mean anything; where the structural conditions on combining plausibilities for negation and for conjunction must be distinguished from the substantive assumption that negation behaves additively; and where the "it's just a convention" objection should land in the arc. Choose a single running example for me to build the session around: it should let plausibilities be compared and combined in a disciplined way, but it must not import known frequencies, symmetry arguments over outcomes, or betting odds, since each of those quietly presupposes the very thing the theorem is supposed to earn. Say exactly what the example is for and what would disqualify it.

Then give me the misconceptions to expect—my guesses are that the theorem is circular because it already assumes the sum rule, that plausibility is subjective so no consistency condition can bite, and that the theorem tells you what your prior probabilities ought to be—and for each, the precise point in the sequence where I should head it off, along with the two or three sentences of stage-setting, not a digression, that would defuse it. Finish with one question I can set in the last five minutes that would tell me whether a student has understood the conclusion's modal force—that the probability rules are forced by the desiderata rather than merely consistent with them—and say what a satisfactory answer would include. Also tell me what I should explicitly leave out, why, and give me a sentence for declining it that won't make the students feel something important was skipped.
