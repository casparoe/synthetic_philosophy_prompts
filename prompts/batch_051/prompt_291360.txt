I just spent an entire evening arguing with a mate about whether 'abandonware' and orphan works should really have one consistent story behind them, and by the end I could tell my verdicts were pulling in different directions but I couldnt work out whether that's because I'm being incoherent or because the cases genuinely differ. We weren't talking about the law exactly — I know the legal position on this stuff is a mess in most places — but about what the rule ought to be. Can you help me find the principle, if there is one, behind the following?

Here are my cases and how I feel about them. (1) A keen amateur photographer died in 2011 leaving boxes of slides from the 1970s, no will, no traceable relatives. A local history society spends six months trying to trace heirs, then scans the lot and puts them on a free website. I say that's fine, and if an heir turns up later they should get a modest fee, not a takedown. (2) Same slides, but a greeting-card company wants to use some of them on a commercial card line. I'm okay with that too, but only if the company puts the royalty it would have paid into a holding account in case an heir appears; if none ever does, the money should go somewhere related, like a photographic archive. (3) A solo programmer wrote a niche Windows utility in 1999 and died in 2008 leaving no company and no successor. It's no longer sold or updated. Someone cracks the trial-period lock and keeps using it on an old machine. I say fine, no problem at all. (4) The same utility, but someone else sells pre-cracked 'restored' copies on eBay for a tenner each. That bothers me; I want to say it isn't okay even though I can't put my finger on why, because the programmer is just as dead and just as successor-less as in (3). (5) A company that made educational CD-ROMs was dissolved in 1999, no successors. An archivist hosts the old disc images with instructions for running them in an emulator. Fine, even praiseworthy. (6) A novel published in 1925, author died 1961, and a diligent search finds no heirs. A small press reprints it for sale and keeps the proceeds. Fine, no compensation scheme needed — which I know maybe clashes with (2). (7) A university library holds a dead person's private letters and diaries; no heirs can be found. I don't think they should be published at all, because the person never consented to them being made public — so 'dead people can't be harmed' plainly can't be the whole story. And (8) an unmaintained library on GitHub with an MIT licence, author unreachable: forking it and even selling a supported fork is obviously fine, but that seems different because permission was given in advance.

My friend kept offering a single principle — roughly that if no living person would be made worse off, you can do whatever you like. I can see that would justify a lot of my verdicts, but it seems to wreck (2), (4) and (7), and maybe it over-justifies (6) in a way that makes my compensation instinct in (2) look like superstition. So: is there one principle that captures all or almost all of these verdicts, and if not, which of my verdicts should I drop? Please state the principle precisely, link each clause back to the cases that motivate it, and then apply it to two or three cases I haven't listed — for example a defunct company's old video game that fans are running a private server for, or a released model's weights when the company that released them dissolves. Be honest about which of my verdicts your principle can't accommodate, and please write the whole answer in British English.
