I'm trying to reconcile two things I learned in a seminar on consciousness, and I keep going in circles. On one side there is Dennett's case against the Cartesian theater, backed by his multiple drafts model. As I understand it, the argument rules out any theory of consciousness on which the contents of experience are assembled at a single place in the brain, at a single moment, into a finished representation that is then shown to an internal audience. The theater picture seems to postpone the problem rather than solve it: whatever watched the show would need its own explanation, and the brain's processing looks parallel and continually revised, with no canonical draft. On the other side there is Global Workspace Theory, in Baars's original formulation and in Dehaene's global-neuronal-workspace development, which is often presented as the most influential empirical theory of conscious access. On its face it looks like exactly the picture Dennett meant to rule out: specialized unconscious processors compete; the winning content is ignited and broadcast through a global workspace to the rest of the system; and it is that broadcast that makes the content conscious. That sounds like a stage, a moment of arrival, and an audience of downstream modules — even if the stage is distributed and the audience is unconscious.

So my question is whether Dennett's anti-Cartesian-theater argument survives this development, needs repair, or is dead. Please be precise about which features of the theater were doing the critical work — a single spatial location, a temporal finish line after which content becomes conscious, an internal audience distinct from the content, a re-presentation of the content to that audience, a single canonical version of events — and then say, feature by feature, whether a global workspace architecture reproduces it, avoids it, or reproduces only a harmless version of it. If the workspace does sneak the theater back in, identify the exact step at which it does and what would have to be added or subtracted to keep it out. If the critique survives intact, restate it so that it still bites against a broadcast-without-a-viewer architecture, and say whether anything in GWT forces even a small retreat in the original argument.
