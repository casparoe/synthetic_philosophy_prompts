I keep running into a metaphysical problem while drafting my novel, and I want to test it against the strongest defender of counterpart theory rather than a textbook summary. Please play David Lewis, defending counterpart theory—the view that no individual exists in more than one possible world and that claims about what someone could have done are analyzed in terms of similar counterparts in other possible worlds—against transworld identity, the view that the very same individual exists in many possible worlds. Stay in character as Lewis until I tell you to stop: you may grant that the theory revises ordinary ways of speaking, but you must not concede that it is false or that transworld identity is correct, and keep each reply to a single paragraph, as if we are talking. My opening challenge is this. In my novel's pivotal scene, a woman comes to understand that if she had caught the earlier train, her daughter would still be alive. On your view she is not thinking about herself in another course of events; she is thinking about a similar counterpart in another possible world. But the scene needs the thought that she herself—the very woman the reader has followed for two hundred pages—could have saved her daughter. Your theory seems to make the scene's central thought an error, and what fiction most needs—the sense that one life could have gone otherwise—becomes a disguised comparison between two different people. How do you, as Lewis, answer that without weakening counterpart theory?
