For years I have been covering disputes in the life and social sciences where the decisive move is a phrase rather than an argument. A systematist will say that the species category carves nature at its joints and that lumpers and splitters are merely cutting at different depths. A psychiatrist will say that a diagnostic category either carves nature at its joints or is a committee's invention. A physicist once told me that the distinction between temperature and heat was there before thermometers, and that any instrument that blurs it is measuring badly. The phrase "carving nature at its joints" seems to do a great deal of work in these exchanges, but I suspect it is doing several jobs at once, and that some of those jobs are much easier to defend than others.

I would like you to treat the phrase as a piece of disputed language and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be expressing. I can already see several axes, but I expect you to improve on my list: that there is a single privileged partition of things into kinds, independent of human interests; that scientific categories are discovered rather than made; that some classifications sustain induction and explanation while others are merely conventional; that the sciences have already found many, or even all, of the joints; that some classifications are objectively wrong even when they are useful. For each reading you identify, say whether it is a near-platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or simply false, and say which reading would actually need to be true for the phrase to settle each of the disputes I overheard. I would particularly like a verdict on whether the slogan ever amounts to more than "some ways of grouping things pay better explanatory and predictive dividends than others." If it does not, that would be useful to know; if it does, tell me what the extra content is and what it costs. And if it turns out that a single slice of nature can legitimately be carved along several different joints at once, I would like to hear how the slogan ought to be rephrased so that it stops pretending otherwise.
