I have been trying to understand why mathematicians choose their definitions the way they do, and it keeps looking, from the outside, philosophically strange. I want to describe the practice as I understand it and ask what picture of concepts, meaning, and discovery it quietly commits someone to.

Consider continuity. The first workable account says a function is continuous when small changes in the input produce small changes in the output, which depends on having a notion of distance between points. The standard modern definition, as I understand it, says a function between two spaces is continuous when the preimage of every open set is open. Distance does not appear in it, and it applies to spaces where talk of small changes has no literal sense. Mathematicians seem to prefer the second definition not because it matches the original idea more closely, but because it moves the original idea to the level of generality at which important theorems hold in their widest form.

The same pattern appears with groups. A group is not defined as the symmetries of some object, even though that is where the idea came from. It is defined by a short list of axioms about a set and an operation, and that definition admits the one-element group and other structures no one would have reached by collecting symmetries. These boundary cases seem to be treated as evidence that the definition is on the right track rather than as a defect. Definitions are routinely arranged so that degenerate or trivial cases count: the empty set is a subset, the zero vector space is a vector space, the one-point space is connected. When a definition behaves badly under a natural operation—forming products, taking subobjects, passing to limits—mathematicians usually adjust the definition rather than accept that the operation works only sometimes.

I have also noticed that mathematicians speak of definitions as right or wrong in a way that is not simply about convenience. A definition may be convenient to compute with and still be judged not the right notion, because it sits at the wrong level of generality, because it draws a line through something that later turns out to be a single phenomenon, or because it gets the boundary cases wrong. A definition may also be accepted even when most of the clean examples it covers look quite unlike the original examples, provided it fits well with neighboring definitions and survives standard constructions.

What I want to know is what view of concepts a person is committed to if they take this practice at face value. What kind of thing would a general notion have to be for extending a definition beyond its original examples to count as revealing the right notion rather than changing the subject? Does the practice presuppose that mathematical concepts are something out there to be tracked by definitions, or only that some stipulations pay off better than others? Does it rule out the view that a concept simply is whatever the motivating examples had in common? Are its commitments internally consistent—can a boundary case be both included by convention and required by the thing itself? And how much does the practice actually settle? I would like to know where several different accounts of what a concept is could all fit the same definitional choices, so that the practice by itself proves less than it seems. Finally, if someone held the opposite view, that a concept is fixed by the examples that introduced it and a definition extending past them simply changes the subject, what would have to change about the practice to make it consistent with that view—and would the result still look like mathematics?
