My philosophy of psychiatry study group burned a whole evening on this and went around in circles. The two-factor story about delusions says you need two things: an anomalous experience, plus some failure to properly evaluate or reject the hypothesis that the experience suggests. So far so good. The trouble started when we asked whether the state the patient ends up in is even a belief. One of us laid out the following chain, and every step seemed right individually until we got to the end.

(1) To believe that P is, in part, to be disposed to use P as a premise in practical reasoning when P is relevant. (2) A person with Capgras delusion says their spouse has been replaced by an identical impostor, but they often keep living with the "impostor," don't call the police, don't look for their real spouse, and so on—the old double-bookkeeping pattern. (3) So by (1), the patient's state is not a belief, because the action-guiding disposition is missing. (4) But the same test would strip away a lot of ordinary belief. A man convinced his wife is cheating on him may never check her phone; a smoker convinced cigarettes will kill him keeps smoking; someone with a fear of heights may be convinced the balcony is safe but still won't step out. In each case the person fails to use the content in the relevant practical reasoning. (5) Therefore either (1) is false—belief isn't constitutively action-guiding after all—or those ordinary cases aren't really beliefs either.

I suspect the argument pulls a move somewhere, but I can't name it without just relabeling things. If I had to choose, I'd most like to keep (1), because it feels like the only thing tethering "belief" to something more than what a person will say. But I also don't want to just insist the Capgras patient acts on the delusion in some other context when the double-bookkeeping evidence seems to show they largely dont. Which step should we reject, and what does rejecting it cost for the broader question of whether delusions are beliefs?
