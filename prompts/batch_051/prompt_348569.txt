I've been in too many internet arguments about predestination, and I keep seeing the same maneuver, and I'd like to know whether it has a name. Someone starts with the full-strength claim: every event without exception is foreordained, and nothing happens outside God's will. Then you bring up some actual moral evil, and they immediately soften it: God doesn't will sin, he only permits it, and permission isn't the same as willing. Then if you ask whether the specific act of permitting was itself foreordained (which on their earlier claim it must have been), they either flip back to the strong claim or play the mystery card. A few comments later they're back to 'nothing happens outside his will,' and the will/permit distiction has quietly been dropped. I've seen the same shape in debates over whether infallible foreknowledge leaves real alternatives open: the point that foreknowledge doesn't cause the choice shows up only when the alternative would otherwise collapse, and vanishes again afterward. I know this is somewhere near the old 'two wills of God' idea and near compatibilism, but those are stable positions; what I'm pointing at is the intermittent use of a distinction, trotted out under pressure and shelved when the pressure is gone. Does this move have an established name, and how is it different from a real will/permission distinction or plain compatibilism? If it has no name, what would you call it, and where's the boundary between a legitimate retreat to a distinction and whatever this is?
