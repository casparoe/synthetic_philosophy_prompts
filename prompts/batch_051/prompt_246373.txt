I'm giving the toast at my doctoral advisor's retirement dinner, and I want it to do him justice in a very specific way. He spent forty years working in constructive mathematics: the view that a proof of existence is only a proof if it actually hands you the thing, and that the law of excluded middle is not, by itself, an argument. Half the room will be logicians and former students who will notice immediately if the toast smuggles in classical moves; the other half will be his family, who loved him without ever quite learning what he did. Please write me a five-minute toast that obeys his scruples as a matter of structure rather than decoration: no disjunctions you cannot resolve to one side, no existence claims for which you cannot exhibit the witness, no conclusions reached by assuming the opposite and deriving a contradiction. It should still be a toast and not a lecture, and it should let the non-logicians feel what constructivism costs and gives without ever announcing that they are being taught.

As a seed, here is the line I keep coming back to: in his mathematics, saying that there exists a student who will carry on the work is not finished until you can point to a student and show what they have done. You may use me as that witness, but only if the speech actually exhibits something I did — a problem solved, a habit learned, a proof built, a student taught — rather than merely asserting that someone must exist to carry the work forward. Where an ordinary toast would reach for a vague classical assurance, find a way to say something more modest and more definite instead; I want the logicians to see that the constraint was honored down to the last inference, and the family to come away understanding why he found that constraint worth forty years.
