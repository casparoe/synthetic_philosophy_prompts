I'm giving the short commencement address at my old physics department this spring, and I keep scrapping drafts over what the speech is allowed to say. I'm an alum who stayed involved but isn't an Einstein scholar; the room will be half new physics graduates, half their families (some fairly religious), and a few faculty who will catch any sloppy statement about Einstein immediately. The chair asked me to make Einstein the spine of the speech, specifically his views on religion and free will, and I'd rather not just reach for the famous dice quip. Please write it, about eight minutes. The philosophy has to do actual work in the speech: it should rule out the usual commencement move about choosing your own path and then freely choosing your future, because Einstein didn't think the will is free in that way, but it also shouldn't turn into a lecture on Spinoza aimed at people who came for graduation. As far as I understand the position, its a Spinozist reverence for the order of nature, no personal God who intervenes, and a determinism about the will that still leaves moral responsibility in place as something practical. Can the speech use that to offer real consolation and encouragement rather than just decorating a generic address? I also don't want it to pick a fight with the religious families, so it has to land for both without saying anything Einstein would have rejected.
