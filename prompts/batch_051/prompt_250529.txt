For a long time I have treated the standard reading of Libet's experiments as one of the strongest empirical challenges to free will. As I understand it, the argument runs roughly like this:

(1) In Libet's self-initiated movement task, the readiness potential (RP) begins around 550 ms before the movement.
(2) The participant reports the conscious intention to move (W) at around 200 ms before the movement.
(3) If the neural process that initiates the movement is already underway roughly 350 ms before the conscious intention, then the conscious intention does not initiate the movement.
(4) Free will, or at least conscious volition, requires that the conscious intention initiate the movement.
Therefore, conscious will arrives late and merely witnesses a decision the brain has already made.

But I have also been persuaded by the later accumulator-model reinterpretation of the readiness potential, associated with Schurger and colleagues. On this picture, in a self-initiated task the motor system is integrating noisy neural activity toward a movement threshold, and the movement occurs when the accumulated activity crosses that threshold. The classical RP is an average of trials locked to movement onset. Conditional on a threshold crossing having occurred, the average path will rise before the crossing even if the underlying process is largely stochastic, because trials that happened to be trending downward do not cross at that time and so do not get aligned. The RP is therefore not evidence that a movement-specific decision was made 550 ms early; it is a selection artifact of averaging threshold-crossing fluctuations.

What I want to know is whether the Libet argument survives this development, needs repair, or is dead. In particular:

- Which premise in the original argument carries the weight under this reinterpretation, and how much of the argument is left standing once the RP is no longer read as an early decision?
- Does the timing gap between the brain's commitment to move and the subject's reported decision survive once the neural commitment point is the threshold crossing rather than the start of the RP?
- Is there a repaired version that still says something important, such as that conscious intention is a late stage in a stochastic decision process rather than its initiator, or does the argument shrink to a claim about arbitrary decisions about when to move now, with no bearing on the choices people actually mean by free will?
- What is the strongest defensible residual claim, and is it strong enough to justify the standard one-line debunk that the brain decides before you do?

I want a gears-level account of which premise fails, which premise survives, and what the most defensible repaired version of the argument would have to look like, rather than a dismissal of Libet by name or a recitation of the standard objections.
