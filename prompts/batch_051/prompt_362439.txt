Take the familiar form of the inefficacy objection: in a large, diffuse collective harm such as industrial chicken production, a single household's decision to buy or not buy factory-farmed meat makes no causal difference to the number of animals raised and killed, because any one purchase is only an imperceptible perturbation in aggregate demand. This is usually treated as a conceptual point about causation and thresholds rather than as an empirical hypothesis.

I want you to treat it as at least partly testable. First, state the precise causal claim you would test—the unit of analysis, the intervention or comparison, and the outcome measure. Then design the best feasible test, using only realistic records or observations from a retail supply chain, of whether one household's purchases ever make a discrete difference to the number of birds produced or killed. Specify what pattern of results would support the strict causal form of the inefficacy claim and what pattern would refute it.

Second, say what no test of this kind could settle even in principle. Address in particular whether a very small probability of being the marginal purchase that triggers an entire additional batch order counts as making a difference, and whether a consumer's responsibility should be assessed by expected difference-making or by actual contribution. Build the test from reasoning about supply chains alone; do not cite or invoke named studies.
