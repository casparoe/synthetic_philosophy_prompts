Consider Ian Hacking's looping-effects argument about psychiatric classification. In its original form, it runs roughly as follows. Psychiatric categories are not indifferent kinds of the sort one might find in the natural sciences, because the people classified under a psychiatric category are aware of the classification and react to it. A person given a diagnosis may reinterpret her history, change her behavior, take the label up as a self-description, seek out others who share it, and trigger different responses from institutions, clinicians, and family. These reactions alter both who falls under the category and what the category comes to mean. The kind therefore loops: it is a moving target, partly constituted by the activity of classifying it, and this is meant to block a straightforward realist or essentialist reading of psychiatric categories.

Now suppose psychiatric classification shifts to a different regime. Diagnosis is no longer primarily a clinician applying a public category to a patient's self-report. Instead, machine-learning systems are trained on passively recorded behavioral streams—sleep, movement, message timing, tone, spending, location, social contact—and they output a continuous severity score or a probabilistic label, say for depression. The original looping argument ran through the classified person's semantic self-understanding: the classification had to be taken up in the person's self-conception in order to loop. In the algorithmic regime, the classification can operate and have consequences without the subject ever grasping the label, the score, or the fact that a diagnostic inference has been made. The score may surface only as an app notification, an automatically adjusted recommendation, a change in insurance terms, or a clinician's subtly altered manner. The system may then be retrained on behavior that has already been modified by the interventions its earlier scores triggered.

Does Hacking's looping-effects argument survive this development, require repair, or collapse? Identify which premise of the original argument the algorithmic regime puts under pressure. Then either show that the argument still goes through unchanged, give the weakest repair that preserves its core insight, or explain why that core insight must be abandoned and what should replace it. In particular, say whether a loop that runs through scores and interventions rather than through a shared public label is still a Hacking-style loop, and whether the interactive/indifferent distinction needs to be redrawn once classification can alter its objects without their comprehending the classification.
