I keep noticing the same move in arguments about risk, and I don't have a word for it. A friend will tell me that for a man of our age the chance of developing heart trouble is only 6%, so I shouldn't worry. When I ask where the number came from, he points to all men of that age. A week later, arguing that our other friend ought to start the medication, he says the risk is actually 28% — and now he means men of that age with this family history and those two lab numbers. Each group is perfectly defensible on its own; the denominator is never just wrong. It's that the choice of group seems to be dialed to fit the conclusion, and the fact that the dial moved is never acknowledged. I've seen the same thing with a risk calculator a doctor used: default settings gave one number, toggling on the patient's exact profile gave another nearly four times higher, and the doctor quoted whichever intermediate grouping matched the treatment she already favored.

I'm not asking about the general problem that a single event belongs to many groups and each group gives a different probability — I gather that has its own name already. What I'm after is the narrower pattern where the background group is treated as a free parameter, turned until it reads the desired probability, while the result gets presented as "the" probability for that person. Is there an established name for that specific move? If not, can you coin one and define it tightly enough that it stays distinct from plain cherry-picking, from the general class-selection problem, and from Simpson's-paradox-type reversals? A bare name won't help me unless it does that carving.
