I've been reading Cicero's dialogue on old age, in which Cato answers the usual complaints that old age withdraws people from an active life, weakens the body, removes nearly all physical pleasure, and brings them close to death. I keep wondering what that outlook would make of the present-day movement to treat biological aging as a curable disease—not just extending lifespan, but so decoupling biological age from chronological age that a ninety-year-old could have the health and capacities of a forty-year-old. Would Cicero see this as a technical extension of his own counsel about growing old well, or as a refusal of the limits that his defense of old age teaches us to accept? Try to give his considered judgment rather than an offhand reaction to a technology he could not have imagined, and say whether the distinction between chronological and biological age strengthens his position or quietly dissolves the subject he thought he was defending.
