I’m drafting a short essay that uses Mill’s discussion of state education in On Liberty to think about how a proposal for education vouchers would actually play out in the school-choice landscape. The working argument is that Mill’s famous worry about state monopoly gets imported, a bit too easily, into a different economic context: his concern was about a centralized state curriculum and “moulding,” not about which institutions receive public money. I’m not convinced the quote does the work people want it to.

Here’s the passage I’m leaning on (from Chapter V of On Liberty):

“A general State education is a mere contrivance for moulding people to be exactly like one another: and as the mould in which it casts them is that which pleases the predominant power in the government, whether this be a monarch, a priesthood, an aristocracy, or the majority of the existing generation; in proportion as it is efficient and successful, it establishes a despotism over the mind, leading by natural tendency to one over the body.”

I’m trying on the view that when people use this to defend voucher systems, they’re making a mistake about the level at which the moulding concern bites. A voucher system that channels public money to private schools still has to decide which schools count, what counts as a school, what counts as satisfying a state-mandated core, and what counts as acceptable oversight. You might not get one central curriculum, but you get an administrative definition of a qualified education, plus an incentive structure where schools shape themselves to fit that definition. That’s still a mould, just a more diffuse and harder-to-inspect one.

But I can also see the opposite reading: the moulding worry is specifically about the state directly running the curriculum and enforcing it through attendance, and if the state has to make eligibility rules anyway, better to let a thousand schools each try to satisfy them than to have one pipeline producing one sort of graduate.

Can you act as a political philosopher who works on education and institutional design, and give me comments on this essay? I mostly want to know where the argument is doing more than the evidence can support, where the voucher-side reading of Mill is strongest, and whether I’m missing a cleaner way to separate the “who teaches” from the “who sets the criteria” questions. Also flag anywhere I’ve been sloppy about the difference between public money and public operation, because I keep sliding between them. First give a few honest reactions to the core argument before you get into the line-by-line stuff.
