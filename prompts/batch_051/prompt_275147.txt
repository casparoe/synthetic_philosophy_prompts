Here is an argument I have been working through, and it now seems to me to prove too much. I cannot locate the step I should reject.

(1) Hedonism is true only if there is a single property that all pleasures share, in virtue of which they are pleasures and in virtue of which they are good.
(2) The only candidate for such a property is a distinctive felt quality: a hedonic tone, a pleasantness present in every pleasurable experience.
(3) Introspection finds no such felt quality. The pleasure of a hot bath, the pleasure of seeing a proof come together, the pleasure of relief when a pain stops, and the malicious pleasure of a deserved misfortune do not feel alike in any respect; their phenomenology seems exhausted by the warmth, the click of understanding, the cessation, the spite.
(4) So pleasures have nothing in common, and the word "pleasure" does not name a real kind of mental state.
(5) If "pleasure" names no real kind, hedonism is not merely false: it makes the good something that does not exist.
(6) The same reasoning applies to every other mental-state term. Pains are just as heterogeneous — a burn, a cramp, the ache of grief. Beliefs are heterogeneous too — an occurrent judgment, a standing conviction, a stored disposition. If heterogeneity of instances shows that a term fails to name a real kind, then "pain," "belief," "memory," and "anger" fail to name real kinds as well.
(7) Therefore there are no pains, beliefs, memories, or emotions; there are only loose families of states we misleadingly cover with single words.

I cannot accept (7). So the reasoning must fail somewhere. Tell me where. I would most like to keep the denial of (7); beyond that, I want to know what each repair costs before I decide. I want a committed diagnosis, not a survey of options: name the step or inference to reject, explain why it looked compulsory, and say what each available repair gives up.
