I'm a retired chemist and I've recently gotten into the natural kinds literature — the question whether the sciences carve nature at its joints. I keep seeing the periodic table held up as the poster child for real joints, so I tried to knock that down with a argument of my own, and I can't tell whether I've actually shown something or just fudged a premise. Here it is:

(1) If a science carves nature at its joints, then for a given set of things there is exactly one correct, interest-independent way of sorting them into kinds.
(2) Take carbon-12 and carbon-14. Sorting them together is correct and interest-independent: both have six protons, so their electron structure and ordinary chemistry are the same. But sorting them apart is also correct and interest-independent: they are different nuclides, one stable and the other radioactive with a half-life of about 5,730 years, and that nuclear difference is there whether anyone happens to care about it or not.
(3) So there are two different correct, interest-independent sortings of the same two things into kinds.
(4) Therefore the uniqueness claim in (1) is false for this case.
(5) So chemistry is not carving nature at its joints after all.

I have a feeling step (1) is where the trouble is — maybe I've smuggled in a uniqueness requirement nobody actually believes. But go through it step by step: is the argument valid? If it's valid, is it sound? If something fails, which numbered step is the first to go, and what's the corrected version of it? Once the correction is made, does the conclusion still follow?
