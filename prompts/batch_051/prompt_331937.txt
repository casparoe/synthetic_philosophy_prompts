I teach a course on the history of violence, and the same argument comes up every single semester and never gets anywhere. One side says violence has declined steeply: per capita war deaths and homicide rates are way down from where they were in, say, the fourteenth century or in pre-state societies. The other side says thats a conjuring trick, because in absolute numbers the twentieth century killed more people through organized violence than any before it — a falling rate and a rising total can happen at the same time, since the population grew — and anyway the first side's definition of violence is rigged to exclude deliberately inflicted famines, police violence, and structural violence. Everyone accepts the numbers each side cites, and still nobody moves an inch. My working hypothesis is that the word "violence" is doing at least three jobs at once: a per capita rate for a narrow category of direct killing, an absolute count for that same category, and a much wider moralized category that doesn't reduce to either. If that's right, is there really no fact of the matter about whether the world has become less violent, just several facts about different quantities? Or is the disagreement something else — a genuine empirical dispute wearing a definitional costume, a values dispute about which quantity should matter, or a clash of starting intuitions neither side can argue for? Diagnose the actual structure of the stalemate, and then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a metric stipulation, dropping the word "violence," a new distinction, or accepting that the question is ill posed. Be specific about what would actually move one of the camps.
