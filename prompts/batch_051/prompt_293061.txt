I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and a couple weeks ago I sat through a long panel on algorithmic management — the way companies set targets for delivery drivers, call-center employees, students, or even AI models. I stuck around afterwards, and two of the panelists kept arguing long after everyone else left. Dana, who studies incentive design, said Goodhart's law isn't just a quirk of badly choosen KPIs; it's structural. The second you attach any proxy to pay, grants, or rankings, people (and models) start moving the proxy without moving the real thing you care about, and the more intuitive the proxy seems, the more blind you are to the gap it opens up. She ran through the usual cases: call-center agents coaching customers on the satisfaction survey, teachers narrowing the curriculum toward the test, hospitals massaging wait-time numbers, and what the ML people call specification gaming. Marco, who used to run operations at a logistics company, pushed back equally hard. He said this treats every measure as if it were equally gameable, which just isn't true; some targets survive for years because they're cross-checked against outside data or because they track the real outcome closely enough. For him, Goodhart's law is a warning about bad governance — high stakes, no audits, no voice for the people measured — not a fact about measurement itself.

I asked them what would change their mind. Dana said Marco's cure actually proves her point: if you can independently audit the underlying quality, then you didn't need the proxy as more than a diagnostic; and if you can't audit it, that's exactly where the gaming hides. Rotating targets just relocates the game — publish a new metric and effort migrates that afternoon, while the hidden quality decays at the margins where auditors aren't looking. Marco answered that this isn't an argument against metrics, it's a point about information asymmetry, and the alternative is worse: going back to gut judgment and manager discretion quietly reinstates bias and makes anyone unaccountable. He added that teaching to the test is only a scandal when the test is a bad sample of the skill; a well-built test is a legitimate target. Dana replied that tests are always narrower than the real skill, and the ones with the most riding on them are the ones that get narrowest.

At that point the conference staff kicked us out of the room, and I honestly couldn't have adjudicated it on the spot.
