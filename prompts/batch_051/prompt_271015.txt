I keep seeing the same move in discussions of free will and divine foreknowledge, and I want it audited rather than simply asserted. Most recently, in a forum debate about whether God could know my choices in advance without causing them, someone reached for Boethius's image from the Consolation: God is like a spectator on a height who sees the whole road at once, while a traveler on the road sees only the stretch immediately ahead; God therefore knows all of history as simultaneously present rather than foreknowing it from inside time. The intended conclusion is that the word "foreknowledge" is misleading: God's knowledge is not temporally before the events, so it no more coerces or causally determines a choice than a bystander watching someone decide does.

That analogy is doing a great deal of argumentative work, and I want to know how much weight it can actually carry. Treat me as someone who can follow metaphysics of time and modality but has not worked through this specific argument before. Please lay out exactly what maps onto what — the spectator, the height, the road, the traveler, the act of seeing, the stretches of road already in view — and then identify the points where the mapping breaks or quietly imports a controversial claim. In particular I want to know: does the picture already assume something like a block universe or a B-theory of time, in which future events are simply there to be seen? Does it import features of ordinary perception — for instance, that seeing is receptive, that it is caused by the thing seen, and that it requires the thing seen to exist — into what an atemporal God's knowledge could be like? Then say what Boethius's conclusion — that God's knowledge is compatible with freedom of the will, because it is more like seeing than like foreordaining — can and cannot get from this analogy once the disanalogies are on the table.
