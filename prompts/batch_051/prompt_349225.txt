I've been re-reading some stuff on the politics of technology and it started to line up, in my head, with a debate in ecological psychology, and I want a sanity check on whether this is a real structural parallel or just me pattern-matching. The politics-of-technology people say artifacts can embody political arrangements. The classic case: a low overpass on a parkway that lets cars through but not buses, so anyone who can only reach the beach by public transit is kept out. There's no sign saying excluded, and you don't need a bigoted intention; the politics is in the poured concrete. The affordance people, meanwhile, describe the same sort of thing in deliberately flat, apolitical language: a physical layout offers possibilities for action relative to an agent's capacities. A bench affords sitting to a human; a low tunnel affords passage to a car but not to a bus. And I keep wanting to say the overpass case just is an affordance description with a political gloss added. The concrete affords passage to cars and blocks passage to buses; add a premise about who has cars, who has buses, and who wanted to go to the beach, and you get an exclusion. Is that all the politics is, or is something doing work in the word 'political' that affordance talk can't see?

So my question is whether 'artifacts have politics' reduces to 'artifacts have affordances' plus a distributional premise. If the structural identity is real, results should transfer. Affordances are agent-relative: the same ledge is a step for an adult and a barrier for a toddler. Does it follow that an artifact is never political full stop, only political relative to a population with certain action capacities? Affordances are also meant to be value-neutral facts about environment-animal fit; so where does the normativity come from, the sense that the overpass is unjust and not merely inconvenient? My worry is the analogy breaks exactly there, because 'politics' seems to require interests, standing, and a history of power, none of which an affordance description names. The pure affordance reading would predict that once you fix the overpass the exclusion is over, but in the real case the harm also lives in the surrounding distribution of cars, buses, beaches, and whose time counts. So: where does the mapping hold and where does it come apart, and wether anything from the tech-politics side should force a psychologist to revise the idea of an affordance?
