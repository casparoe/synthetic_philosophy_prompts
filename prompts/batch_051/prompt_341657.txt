Before I go read Church, Kleene, and the hypercomputation literature, I want the spec first: what does any adequate account of "effective computability" actually have to get right? I keep seeing people produce a formal definition of what a computable number or function is and then, in the same breath, assert that it captures the informal idea, and I want the requirements list thats doing the real work, with the case or consideration behind each requirement.

Here's the passage I want you to work through, from Alan Turing's "On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem" (1936), just after he's set up the tape, the scanned square, and the m-configurations:

"It is my contention that these operations include all those which are used in the computation of a number. The defence of this contention will be easier when the theory of the machines is familiar to the reader. In the next section I therefore proceed with the development of the theory and assume that it is understood what is meant by “machine”, “tape”, “scanned”, etc."

Now the task. Give me the desiderata for any theory of effective computability, with the consideration behind each, and tell me which ones are non-negotiable and which are only the prefernces of one camp (the finite-memory camp, the physically-computable camp, the constructivist camp, whatever). Keep it concrete: which cases it has to classify the right way (addition, primality testing, the decimal expansion of pi; the halting set, the Entscheidungsproblem, definable-but-not-computable numbers), which distinctions it can't collapse (computable in principle vs feasibly computable, algorithmic vs hypercomputational, finite procedure vs unbounded process, enumerable vs countable), and which established results it can't contradict (the undecidability of the halting problem, the enumerability of the computable numbers, the equivalence of the main formalisms). Then do a tension check: flag any pair of desiderata that might not be jointly satisfiable—I have a hunch that "finite means" and "unbounded tape" are already in tension, and that Turing's hedge in the quote is where the epistemic status of the whole thesis gets decided. So end by saying what that hedge commits him to: does reading the quote as a claim about human computers turn the Church–Turing thesis into an empirical hypothesis, a definition, a heuristic, or something else?
