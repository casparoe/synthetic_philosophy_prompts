I retired a few years ago from a career in microbiology — I spent most of it tending cultures and arguing with a flow cytometer — and only recently started reading philosophy of biology. I thought I'd have a settled answer to what counts as alive, but the more I press my own gut verdicts, the less they hang together. So I'm hoping you can do for me what the philosophers call reflective equilibrium, or at least tell me where my case judgments are inconsistent.

My gut says: a flame is not alive, even though it consumes fuel, grows, and can spread (a forest fire "reproduces" in some loose sense); there's no heredity, no boundary, no effort to keep itself the same. A crystal in a supersaturated solution is not alive either, for roughly the same reasons. A mule is obviously alive, even though it can't reproduce — I won't budge on that one. A measles virion sitting on a doorknob is not alive: it's inert, no metabolism, just a bit of packaged chemistry. But the same virion inside a lung cell, redirecting the cell to make more virions, still doesn't seem alive to me, because the host cell is doing the work — though I'm less comfortable there. A Bacillus endospore that's been dry in a drawer for decades is alive; I'd never call it dead, since it'll germinate if I give it water and food. Yet it also has no metabolism while it sits there, which sounds like the same situation as the virion on the doorknob, and I'm saying opposite things. A desiccated tardigrade in its tun, no detectable metabolism, is alive, just paused. A synthetic minimal bacterium of the kind with a stripped-down genome built from scratch is alive: it metabolizes, grows, divides, and I don't care that it's an artifact. A computer simulation of every molecule in a bacterial cell, however detailed, is not alive; it's a model of a cell, not a cell. And a body on a ventilator after brain death is dead as an organism — the person is gone — even though plenty of its cells are still alive.

My old working rule was: alive means it metabolizes, maintains itself, and has the machinery to reproduce with heredity. But the mule breaks the reproduction clause, the endospore and tardigrade break the metabolism clause at least while dormant, the virion has heredity and evolution but no metabolism of its own, and the flame metabolizes in a loose sense but I don't want to count it. So either the principle has to get more disjunctive, or some of my verdicts have to go.

What I'd like from you: state a principle (or a small set of candidates) that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, say which clause earns which case, and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't accommodate — especially whether I can keep both the virion-not-alive and spore-alive verdicts, and whether I can keep the mule alive without giving up reproduction as a requirement. Then run your best candidate on some cases I'm still unsure about: a self-replicating ribozyme in a test tube, a transposable element that copies itself within a genome, a virus-like particle assembled from synthetic DNA and capsid protein, a two-thousand-year-old seed that still germinates, and a nanobot that gathers fuel, repairs itself, and builds copies of itself. If the cleanest principle forces me to abandon one of my original verdicts, tell me which one is cheapest to give up.
