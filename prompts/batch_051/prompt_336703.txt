I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I lead a small platform team. Several times a year we face a recurring, high-stakes decision: whether to keep investing in a feature, service, or internal tool that has already consumed significant engineering time, infrastructure cost, and organizational goodwill, or to stop. In practice these discussions are dominated by the fact that we have already spent a great deal. I understand enough to see that this is the sunk cost fallacy, but I have no way to turn the slogan "don't throw good money after bad" into a procedure my team can actually run.

Please write a decision procedure for deciding whether to continue or kill an engineering project, grounded in whatever the best thinking about sunk costs actually licenses rather than the slogan alone. It must be usable by an engineer or engineering manager in under an hour, with no background in economics or philosophy, and it must be defensible to a director who signs off on the decision. Include the following:

1. The questions to ask, in order, with clear standards for what counts as a sufficient answer to each.
2. The points at which the procedure should return a definite "continue" or "kill" recommendation.
3. The points at which it must hand the decision to a person, because the question is one of values, risk appetite, or organizational judgment rather than fact.
4. An explicit treatment of the cases in which sunk costs might rationally matter after all — for example through reputation, credibility, team morale, learning, or precommitment — and whether those are genuinely sunk-cost effects or forward-looking considerations that ought to enter the calculation on their own terms.

Then run the procedure on three worked examples:

(a) an internal build-versus-buy tool that is nine months late and has already cost more than the commercial license it was meant to replace;
(b) a user-facing feature that has consumed two quarters of effort and shows weak engagement so far, but is strongly championed by an influential executive;
(c) a data migration that is eighty percent complete, where the remaining twenty percent will require reworking the parts already built, making the total remaining cost greater than the cost of discarding the work and starting fresh.

Base each worked example on the procedure rather than on intuition, and ensure that at least one reaches a conclusion I will be reluctant to accept. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do: where someone following it could still rationalize a bad decision, and what residual judgment the person running it must supply.
