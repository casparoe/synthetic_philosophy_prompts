I'm taking a seminar this term on original sin and inherited guilt, and our prof keeps framing the debate around the standard secular objection: the doctrine is supposed to be incoherent or unjust because moral guilt can't be inherited. The argument, roughly, is that you can be answerable for your own acts — maybe for what you negligently allowed — but not for something an ancestor did before you existed; a newborn hasn't done anything, so calling a newborn guilty is either an category mistake or a massive injustice. That always struck me as the strongest move against the doctrine. But I've also been going down a rabbit hole on intergenerational trauma recently — the Dutch Hunger Winter findings, descendants of atrocity survivors showing altered stress responses, some contested epigenetic results — and it's left me unsure. Not because any of that research shows someone is literally blameworthy for their grandmother's suffering, obviously. But it does seem to show that a kind of damage or vulnerability can be biologically transmitted across generations, not just through stories or parenting.

So here's what I'm stuck on: does the old objection survive this? Is the objection's real premise that nothing morally relevant can be inherited, in which case the science starts to make the idea of an inherited corruption of nature look less like a metaphor and more like a description? Or is the premise narrower — that culpability, the thing that makes punishment deserved, can't be inherited — in which case the biology is beside the point and maybe even strengthens the objection by casting the descendants as victims rather than guilty parties? Basically, I want to know whether the argument against inherited guilt survives the empirical development, whether it has to be reformulated, or whether it was always aimed at something the science can't touch. Could you lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating the question?
