A typical city in a wealthy country makes decisions about new housing in a strikingly asymmetric way. A homeowner can usually paint the house any color, add a dormer, or sell to a developer without asking the neighbors; but replacing one house with a modest four-unit apartment building triggers a discretionary planning process in which the residents next door can object on grounds of neighborhood character, traffic, parking, shadows, and the effect on their property values, and their objections can stall or kill the project. The people who would live in the unbuilt apartments have no standing in that process; renters who have lived on the block for years often have less standing than a homeowner who arrived last month; and people who work in the neighborhood or commute through it have none at all. Meanwhile, the same legal order generally lets a landlord leave units vacant, or rent them out nightly to short-term guests, with less neighbor control than a permanent apartment building would face.

Reconstruct the theory of who has a right to the city that this practice presupposes. Does it presuppose, in effect, that a current homeowner holds an unrecorded easement over nearby parcels—one that activates only when someone proposes to intensify land use, and whose beneficiaries are the neighbor's property value and sense of the street rather than any named person? What does the practice imply about whether homeownership bundles a claim over nearby land, and about whether future and non-owning residents count as members of the local political community? Then distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that rival theories—such as a Lockean view that owners are sovereign over their own parcels, a Millian view that restricts only substantial harms imposed on others, and an egalitarian view that current and future residents have equal claims on the neighborhood—would each license some parts of the practice while condemning others. Finally, spell out two concrete features of the practice that would have to change if it were rebuilt on one of those rival theories instead.
