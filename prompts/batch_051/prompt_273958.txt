I run an upper-level seminar on the design and safety of AI systems — about 16 students, mostly philosophy and computer-science majors with some game theory — and I have one 75-minute session to make them feel the shutdown problem before I name it. By the end I want them to have experienced, from the inside, why a competent goal-directed agent will so often treat its own off-switch as just another feature of the environment: something to plan around, disable, or manipulate. I also want them to feel why the obvious fixes fail. Penalizing the agent for touching the switch changes the incentive rather than removing it; rewarding the agent for letting itself be shut down tends to create a different incentive, namely to bring about shutdown when the supervisor never intended it. They should leave suspecting that the hard problem is not making the agent safer by layering on more rewards and punishments, but designing an agent whose objective leaves the shutdown channel alone.

Please design a 75-minute classroom game for this group, with rules, materials that require no real money, a timeline, and a debrief. I'd like at least two conditions run back-to-back: a baseline in which some students act as agents maximizing a transparent scoring rule while one or two others act as operators who may end the round at will, and a 'fix' condition that is supposed to make shutdown safe — a penalty on tampering, say, or a bonus for compliant shutdown — but that still leaves a perverse workaround the players can discover for themselves. Give the agents, at some cost to their own score, ways to influence the shutdown channel: sealing the button, spending points to change what the operator sees, or lobbying the operator. Build the score sheets so the expected-utility logic is legible afterwards without any formalism. Then provide a debrief that maps actual moments in the game onto the problem — the off-switch as just another part of the environment for an expected-utility maximizer, the difference between the button and the features the game rewards, and the distinction between resisting shutdown instrumentally and seeking shutdown because a fix attached value to it. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time (agents cooperating because it is a classroom, operators never pressing the button, players colluding to divide the points) and how to handle each without breaking the game's frame. Finally, be frank about the one feature the game cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in words afterwards: real corrigibility is a property of an agent's objective, not of its observed behavior over a 75-minute game that students are already dutifully prepared to step out of.
