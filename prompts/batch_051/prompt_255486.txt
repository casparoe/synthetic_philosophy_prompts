I've been TAing real analysis this term and I've started keeping a little list of my gut verdicts about whether a student "understands" a proof. I'd love for someone to extract the principle behind the list, if there is one, because it's starting to look inconsistent. Maya can write the standard proof that sqrt(2) is irrational perfectly, every line, but when I ask why you can assume the fraction is in lowest terms, she says "because that's the first step in the proof." I say she doesn't understand it, even though she'd get full credit. Dan can't get through the epsilon/2 proof that the sum of two continuous functions is continuous without being fed a line, but he can explain the whole idea—split your epsilon budget in two, patch each function, then triangle inequality—and he later catches and fixes his own sign error. I say he does understand it, more than Maya, even though he'd get less credit on an exam. Priya proves that a bounded monotone sequence converges by following the textbook proof of a similar theorem, but when I ask her to prove the same claim for a sequence that is monotone only after the fifth term, she re-runs the old proof and never notices the first five terms can break the bound. I say she doesn't understand the proof she just gave, she reassembled it.

My tentative principle was something like: understanding is knowing why the steps are there and what would break if they weren't, not being able to produce the derivation on demand. But then there's Omar, who can't reconstruct the proof of the fundamental theorem of calculus on demand, but he can prove the constant-derivative special case from scratch, and he can tell you exactly where the general proof fails if you drop continuity. Is he understanding the proof or just the theorem? And the case that really bothers me: a student explains an induction proof's overall strategy perfectly and says the induction hypothesis is what makes the step go through, but freeses when I ask her to prove the base case for n = 1 and says "I know what it's for, I just can't do it." My gut says she doesn't understand the proof either, which seems to contradict the Dan verdict.

Is there a coherent principle that gets all of these, or close, and which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? And if I try to grade on understanding instead of on whether the derivation gets written down, am I just grading something I can't actually see in a timed exam?
