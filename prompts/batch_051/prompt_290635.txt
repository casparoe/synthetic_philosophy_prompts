You know the old "memes are like genes" thing—not internet memes, but the original argument that ideas are little replicators: they get copied from one person to another with variation, and the ones that survive are the ones that are good at getting themselves copied, regardless of whether they're true or useful. Under that story, if a person hears an idea and then forgets it without passing it on, the idea is just dead; it lives only by being carried in living heads.

But I just came across the word antimeme for the opposite: an idea that people systematically fail to remember or repeat, not because its too hard or dull, but because something in its content makes minds drop it, like a thought that resists being thought. And here's what I can't settle. The old genes-for-ideas story was made for a world where forgetting meant extinction. Now an idea can sit in a searchable archive, a screenshot, or a language model's weights, and get retrieved and used without any single human ever actually carrying it or repeating it on purpose. So suppose you have an antimeme that survives only because a server remembers it and an algorithm can pull it up on demand. Is it still useful to call it an antimeme? Does the old survival-of-the-copyable story still hold as a story about ideas, or has it quietly become a story about disk space, indexes, and retrieval? I want you to say which premise of the original argument is doing the real work, and whether that premise still holds in that setting.

And please tell me what would change your mind: which changed fact or counterargument would make you flip the other way, and where your reasoning is most likely to go wrong.
