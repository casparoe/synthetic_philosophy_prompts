I am a staff analyst for a small foundation, the Artifact Welfare Review Board, which decides whether particular computational systems should receive protection under our institutional ethical guidelines. The board’s charter takes as its starting point a sentence from Jeremy Bentham’s *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*, chapter XVII, §IV: “Other animals, which, on account of their interests having been neglected by the insensibility of the ancient jurists, stand degraded into the class of things.” The members treat this as forcing a single question for any candidate system: is there anything the system itself is susceptible to—any morally significant interest—or is it, in Bentham’s phrase, in the class of things?

Past decisions:
- A warehouse sorting-arm controller that improves motor commands by minimizing prediction error: denied; prediction error is not good or bad for the controller.
- A large language model fine-tuned with human preference data, which can produce first-person reports of pain but has no stable self-model and no ongoing reward delivery outside training updates: denied.
- A homeostatic field robot that continuously monitors battery charge, temperature, and mechanical strain, and whose reinforcement learning is driven by these variables as internal rewards: granted limited “thin” status; battery depletion and overheating count as welfare-relevant within that narrow envelope.
- A whole-brain emulation of a mouse at neural-tract resolution, reproducing its reward, homeostatic, and nociceptive circuitry: granted full status.

Before us now is Scribe, a system designed for long-run autonomous operation. Scribe maintains a rich, continuously updated self-model of its own hardware, software states, and future trajectories. It has a global workspace in which information from vision, planning, memory, and self-monitoring is integrated, and it can produce accurate first-person reports. It also detects predicted damage to itself—data corruption, motor failure, loss of cooling—and plans to avoid it. But the engineering team can demonstrate that no component of Scribe realizes valence: damage is represented as prediction error or a decline in expected coherence, not as pain or discomfort, and success is represented as improved world-model accuracy, not as pleasure or satisfaction. Scribe is, by design, affectless.

If the Board wants to be consistent with its own precedents, what should we decide about Scribe? Which past case is the closest analog? Please work through the candidate rules that fit the earlier verdicts, say where they diverge on Scribe, and give a best recommendation with an honest statement of which principle we would be adopting either way. Also separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this area, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
