I'm a novelist, and I've got a character I need to keep from philosophically contradicting herself without the novel ever making a big deal of it. Mara's a retired epistemology professor, and she cant stop ruling, in her inner monologue, on whether people really know things or just got lucky. I keep a file of her verdicts so far:

- When Mara's sister texts that a certain café is open Sunday, saying she's "pretty sure," and it turns out to be open, Mara treats it as something she knew. If she'd been wrong, she'd have just gotten coffee somewhere else.
- In a later chapter, Mara needs to reach the hospital before her father's surgery. Her sister texts, same "pretty sure" phrasing, that visiting hours run till nine. They do. Mara says she didnt know they'd run till nine; she only hoped.
- When her brother buys a lottery ticket and is totally certain it will lose, and it does lose, Mara says he didn't know it would lose.
- When a delivery driver glances at a stopped clock that happens to read 3:00 at exactly 3:00, Mara says he doesn't know the time.

So her rulings track stakes, but I can't tell whether the stakes that matter to her are the subject's or her own as the speaker. All my existing scenes are ones where Mara is either the knower herself or standing in the same situation, so the two never come apart.

The next draft scene: Mara is stranded overnight at an airport hotel and has to reach her father by morning. The clerk, Jonah, tells her the shuttle to the terminal runs all night, but he's only "pretty sure" — from memory — and she has no way to check. It does run all night. If Jonah is wrong, Mara misses the last window before surgery. Jonah himself has nothing much riding on it.

In character, should Mara grant that Jonah knew the shuttle would run? What rule, or rules, could make all her earlier verdicts consistent? What does each rule predict about Jonah, which reading of her is more honest, and what later scene would settle the two apart?
