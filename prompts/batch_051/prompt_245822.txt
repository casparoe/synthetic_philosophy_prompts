Here is a decision I am trying to make, and I want a defended allocation of effort rather than a survey of the Great Filter. Suppose a new three-year initiative — a small group, modest budget, no observatory of its own — has to commit to exactly one of the following lines of work, and its only objective is to move our credence about whether the Great Filter lies behind us or ahead of us. The five candidates on the table are:

(1) The origin of life, treated as a probability question: how likely is the transition from prebiotic chemistry to an evolvable, reproducing system, and how much of that probability depends on Earth-specific conditions.

(2) Eukaryogenesis: how contingent was the endosymbiotic origin of the complex cell, and how much of the apparent delay before complex life on Earth it explains.

(3) The transition from complex multicellular life to intelligence: how convergent nervous systems, language, and cumulative culture are once animals exist.

(4) Technological longevity: how likely a technological species is to survive, or remain detectably active, over geological timescales, together with what observation-selection reasoning implies about our own survival prospects.

(5) Interstellar expansion: how costly or slow colonization actually is, and whether the absence of visible artifacts should update us much.

Rank these five, not by how interesting they are in the abstract and not by how probable each step is to be the filter, but by how much a single small initiative could realistically move our credence about where the filter sits. Make your criteria explicit — tractability for a small group, expected information value, neglectedness, and how many other candidate-filter assessments depend on the answer — and say where the trade-offs between them bite. Then tell me which argument, piece of evidence, or reassessment of tractability would most likely change your top pick, and where you think your ranking is least stable.
