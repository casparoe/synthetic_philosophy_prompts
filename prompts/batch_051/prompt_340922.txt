Bertrand’s paradox asks for the probability that a randomly drawn chord of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and the principle of indifference appears to give different answers depending on whether one treats the endpoints, the radius, or the midpoint as uniformly distributed. Consider a new selection procedure: choose a point uniformly from the perimeter of the square that circumscribes the circle, choose a direction uniformly from [0, π), and draw the line through that point in that direction; if the resulting chord is inside the circle, record its length; otherwise discard the trial. Does the principle of indifference now decide a single distribution over chords, or does the role of the square and the discarding stage reintroduce the same ambiguity? Work out what probability this procedure gives for the chord being longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and discuss whether the original paradox lies in the notion of selecting a chord at random or in the principle of indifference itself.
