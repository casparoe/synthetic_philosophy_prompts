I am teaching Boethius's Consolation of Philosophy this term and plan to teach Kurt Vonnegut's Slaughterhouse-Five alongside it, because the novel seems to run a kind of thought-experiment on the doctrine of divine eternity. In the closing sections of the Consolation, Lady Philosophy argues that God does not look forward through time at a not-yet-existing future; all times stand before the divine mind in an eternal present. She insists that this removes the threat to freedom. Whatever necessity attaches to a future event when it is known is merely conditional — the necessity that what is seen to be the case is the case — and not the simple necessity of a thing that is fated or forced. Vonnegut's Tralfamadorians appear to hold a superficially similar metaphysics: all moments exist at once, and human talk of free will is, on their view, an Earthling illusion. But they draw from this a posture of pure resignation, and Billy Pilgrim seems to adopt it, down to his calm foreknowledge of his own death.

I want a careful reading of the novel through Boethius's distinction. Where does the novel genuinely dramatize a Boethian insight — perhaps the consolation of seeing one's life as already whole rather than a strung-out future one must anxiously control — and where does it instead collapse eternity into fatalism or mere acceptance? Does Billy gain the consolation Lady Philosophy promises while quietly surrendering the freedom she insists is preserved, and does the novel notice the difference? Which episodes should I put before my students to sharpen the contrast, and which episodes most resist the Boethian reading? I would like the essay to be faithful in both directions: do not make the novel a tidy allegory, and do not soften Boethius's actual argument to make it fit. End by stating what the novel supplies that the Consolation's arguments alone do not. Finally, tell me what consideration or counter-reading would most likely change your verdict about whether the novel is Boethian or fatalist, and where your interpretation is least secure.
