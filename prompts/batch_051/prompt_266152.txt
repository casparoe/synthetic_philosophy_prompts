I'm teaching an upper-level undergraduate seminar on Bayesian epistemology and rational choice — fourteen students, 90-minute sessions — and I want to spend one meeting on Good's theorem: the result that, for an expected-utility maximizer facing a fixed decision problem, costless information can never have negative expected value, because any plan for using the information can simply be replaced by the plan that ignores it. My students can reproduce the proof, but they don't feel its force; they keep offering cases in which someone is badly served by a true but misleading test result, or in which they would honestly rather not know. I'd like you to design a runnable classroom game — with concrete roles or rules, cheap physical materials I can make beforehand, payoffs in points, a realistic timeline within the 90 minutes, and a debrief — arranged so that the theorem's force shows up in what the students actually do before anyone names it. By the end, I want them to come away not with the proof, but with a working intuition for why the theorem has to be true and for where its apparent counterexamples come from.

The game should include at least three phases: one in which students must commit to a contingent strategy before seeing a signal (so that "ignore the signal" is palpably just one of the strategies available to them), one in which they may observe a fallible signal for free, and one in which they may pay to avoid the signal; the scorekeeping should ideally produce a moment where two students faced with the same signal finish with different payoffs, so the discussion can turn on why ex post regret is not ex ante bad value. In the debrief, please map specific moments of play onto the parts of the theorem — the ignoring strategy, the role of the prior, the difference between the value of information and the value of what the information leads you to do, and the conditions under which the theorem is licensed — anticipate what will predictably go wrong when the game is run (students responding to a random outcome as though they should have beaten it in hindsight, or conflating a signal's accuracy with its value), and be candid about what the activity cannot reproduce: the theorem's full generality, the fact that real information can change preferences or moods rather than merely beliefs, and the strategic, computational, and emotional channels through which information can genuinely hurt in expectation.
