Epistemic status: I've read enough of this literature to be confused, not enough to have a horse in the race. I keep trying to adjudicate the cliodynamics argument — Turchin, secular cycles, structural-demographic theory, all that — and it feels like the debate has been running for twenty years without either side moving an inch. The pro-cliodynamics side says: we have an explicit dynamical model with a handful of variables (population pressure, elite overproduction, state fiscal strain), we fit it to centuries of data from multiple societies, and it reproduces a characteristic boom–stagflation–crisis–collapse oscillation rather than just telling a story about one period. Their favorite exhibit is the 2010 forecast that the US and Western Europe were heading into growing instability over the next decade, which looked a lot better after 2020 than most people expected. Historians and area specialists concede the raw data points but not the inference: the proxies are hand-coded and debatable, the periodization is flexible enough to line any model trough up with some war or riot, and "instability" is so coarsely operationalized that the forecast couldn't clearly fail. So the same regression table gets read as a discovered regularity by one side and as fitting noise by the other, and then everyone goes home.

My working theory is that this isn't really an empirical dispute, or at least not a dispute that more data will settle. I think the two sides have different implicit standards for what counts as explaining a historical event: the cliodynamicists want a portable, law-like mechanism that issues forecasts and generalizes across societies, while the historians want a narrative that respects contingency, period-specific causes, and the evidential texture of the sources. Under that theory, "can history be predicted?" is just the visible tip of a disagreement about explanation, and asking who's right is asking the wrong question. But I'm not sure I've got the diagnosis right, and I keep worrying I'm just laundering my sympathies for the modeling side into a meta-level story. So: what kind of disagreement is this actually? Is there a real empirical or formal question underneath — something a pre-registered forecasting tournament, a measure of the degrees of freedom in the fitting procedure, or a sharper operationalization of "instability" could settle? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress, or should we expect this argument to keep going in circles forever?
