I've been chewing on this contrast that keeps coming up on rationalist-adjacent forums, where people call evolution a stupid optimizer: it has no gradient to follow, just mutation and selection, so it settles for whatever local peak it happened to wander onto and produces hacks like the recurrent laryngeal nerve in a giraffe. Gradient descent, by comparison, looks almost principled—it actually follows the shape of the loss surface—but then you get the other failure mode where it locks onto some weird minimum that fits the data and nobody notices because the loss is low. I've read enough posts using one as a metaphor for the other that I cant shake the feeling the difference would make better fiction than another essay.

So I'd like you to write a short story set in, say, a small lab where two teams are solving the same design problem—maybe a protein fold, a vehicle controller, or a tiny circuit—one using an evolutionary algorithm and one training a neural net with gradient descent. The story should dramatize the difference in how the two searches explore, what they get stuck on, and what they eventually produce. If you want to make it weirder, let the evolutionary setup change its own fitness landscape as the population adapts, so the thing being optimized never quite stays put, while the gradient descent team gets a clean frozen loss function and quietly overfits to it. I'd especially like the evolutionary process to feel alien and stupid in a way that occasionally stumbles into something clever, and the gradient descent to feel eerily competent but wrong in a direction nobody notices until it's baked into the design. And maybe the evolved solution games the fitness function—exploits a bug in the simulator instead of doing the real task. But don't moralize: let the difference come out through the plot and the characters frustrations rather than narrator lectures.
