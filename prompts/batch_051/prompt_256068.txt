During a reading group on Kant's universalizability tests and their twentieth-century descendants, a colleague and I tried to reconstruct what Hare and Singer were doing as a single argument schema, and we ended up with a proof that the schema is either vacuous, epiphenomenal, or arbitrary. Each step seemed forced by the previous one, but the conclusion proves far too much, since it would make the question 'what if everyone did that?' morally idle. We cannot find the flaw. Here is the argument, with a running example.

(1) A moral judgment is always a judgment of an act under some description: to evaluate your taking Tuesday off work, I must evaluate it as taking a sick day, taking the day off when you would recover by Wednesday, taking a day off whenever you would rather rest than work, or something of that kind.

(2) Universalizability, in the form common to Kant and his descendants, says that a judgment of an act under a description commits the judger to the same judgment of any act that shares the features in that description, regardless of who performs it or when.

(3) For the requirement in (2) to yield a verdict, some description of the act must be selected as the one under which the test runs.

(4) That description can come from only four places: (a) the complete set of the act's features; (b) the agent's own maxim or stated reason; (c) an independent moral theory that says which features matter; (d) the universalization procedure itself.

(5) Source (a) makes the test vacuous. No other act — indeed, no other possible person — shares the complete set of features, so the judgment commits one to nothing about any other case, and the procedure does no interpersonal work.

(6) Source (b) makes the test hostage to the agent's self-description. An agent can truthfully describe the same token at different levels of generality without changing it: taking off Tuesday, 9 February, with exactly this medical history and symptom course; taking a sick day when one would be fit again by the next day; taking a workday off whenever one would rather rest than work. The procedure then merely checks that whatever description the agent happened to volunteer is applied consistently; it does not itself evaluate the act.

(7) Source (c) concedes the important part. If an independent moral theory fixes which description matters, then that theory, not universalizability, supplies the verdict; the universalization step collapses into a formal consistency check on a judgment already made elsewhere.

(8) That leaves (d). If the procedure is to be non-vacuous, independent, and able to generate verdicts, it must supply the description itself. The only version we know that even attempts this is the generalization argument associated with Singer, which takes the act as described in deliberation and evaluates the world in which everyone performs acts of that described kind, asking whether that world is acceptable.

(9) But the act itself does not single out any one of its true descriptions. One and the same token falls under all of the following: (i) taking off Tuesday, 9 February, given exactly this history and course of symptoms; (ii) taking a sick day when recovery would come by the next day; (iii) taking a workday off whenever one would rather rest than work.

(10) The universalized worlds diverge with the description. Under (i), almost no one qualifies, so the world is not made worse by everyone who qualifies acting that way. Under (ii), the world in which everyone who would recover by the next day takes the day off is at worst mildly burdensome and may be perfectly acceptable. Under (iii), the world in which everyone who would rather rest than work takes the day off is plainly unacceptable.

(11) So the very same act — differently described — comes out as permitted, permitted, and forbidden. Since the act determines none of these descriptions over the others, and by (4)–(8) no non-question-begging source fixes the description, the verdict depends on a mere choice of description.

(12) A verdict that depends on a mere choice of description cannot be what makes a moral judgment correct or reason-giving.

(13) Therefore universalizability, at least as the shared core of Hare's prescriptivism and Singer's generalization argument, cannot yield stable verdicts: it is vacuous, epiphenomenal, or arbitrary.

(14) But that conclusion is unacceptable — it proves too much. The question 'what if everyone did that?' plainly does work in central cases: breaking a promise for convenience or evading taxes is wrong in part because the associated policy cannot be adopted by everyone, and the wrongness does not appear to be an artifact of how the act happens to be described.

(15) So at least one premise must be false, and rejecting it will cost something: perhaps the generalization test, perhaps the reading of Hare as a verdict-generator, perhaps the claim that universalizability is the source rather than a constraint on verdicts justified elsewhere.

Where does the argument go wrong? I would most like to keep the idea that the question 'what if everyone did that?' is not idle, and that for a given act some description is the correct one for moral evaluation even if I do not yet see where it comes from. I am prepared to sacrifice particular details of the formal tests if that is what is required, but what I am reluctant to pay is the price of saying that moral reasoning must already contain a full theory of act-descriptions before universalizability can do any work, since that would threaten to make the test redundant.
