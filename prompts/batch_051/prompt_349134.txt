Suppose a small country's central bank act contains this clause, unchanged since 1934: "No person other than the Bank shall issue any note, token, or other instrument intended to circulate as money." A licensed payments firm now runs a phone-based money service. A customer deposits 100 units of the national currency with the firm, and the firm credits the customer's account with 100 tokens; each token is redeemable on demand for one unit of the national currency, and the currency backing the tokens is held in a supervised trust account. Customers transfer tokens to one another and to merchants to settle everyday debts. The tokens pass at par with the national currency, and almost no one redeems them because they are accepted as cash in practice. The firm does not lend the deposited currency, so every token in circulation is matched by currency sitting idle in the trust.

The firm's lawyer says the clause is not engaged: a token is only a ledger entry recording a claim on money that already exists, like a digital traveller's cheque, so nothing has been "issued" and no new money has been created. The prosecutor says this is exactly the arrangement the clause was written to stop — private claims circulating as money, whatever the ledger says. Does the firm violate the clause? Argue it from the words of the provision, from the purpose of reserving money issuance to the Bank, from what the 1934 drafters would have said if asked about phone-to-phone ledger entries that pass at par without ever becoming notes, and from what a reasonable merchant and a reasonable customer would say they are holding. Then say which approach should control, and whether — whatever the legal answer is — the firm has created money in any economically meaningful sense.
