People think nothing of keeping a deceased parent's voicemails or watching old videos of them, but an interactive AI simulation of that same parent, built from the same recordings and messages, is widely felt to be ethically suspect, something between impersonation and denial. I am trying to determine whether this asymmetry in ordinary reactions is justified. The obvious differences are interactivity and novelty, but it is not clear that either tracks a moral difference: both practices traffic in representations of the dead, both are used by the bereaved, and in the typical case neither has the dead person's explicit consent. My question is whether there is a principle that draws a real line here, something about being addressed by the simulation, about mistaking a reconstruction for a person, about reducing a life to training data, or about what grief is for, or whether the voicemail is simply a griefbot old enough that it no longer feels like one. Give a charitable reconstruction of the strongest rationale for the asymmetry, test it against hard cases such as a pre-recorded video message addressed to the bereaved, a chatbot that answers only in the dead person's own words, a one-time private simulation, or a commercial subscription service, and say whether the asymmetry should be kept, narrowed, or abandoned.
