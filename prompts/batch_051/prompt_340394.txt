I spent most of my career as a research physicist, and I was trained to judge a definition by the work it does rather than by its pedigree. Since retiring, I have been reading the foundations of topology, and one question about how mathematical definitions are chosen has come to seem important to me.

I understand the open-set definition of a topological space as follows: a topology on a set X is a collection T of subsets of X, called the open sets, such that X and the empty set belong to T, T is closed under arbitrary unions, and T is closed under finite intersections. I have also been told that this definition is the right generalization of the familiar epsilon-delta notion of continuity because it meets three desiderata. First, it reduces to the usual notion in the motivating case: the open balls of a metric space generate a topology, and the topological definition of continuity then agrees with the epsilon-delta one. Second, it preserves the central theorems we want, for example that a composition of continuous functions is continuous and that the continuous image of a compact set is compact. Third, it is general enough to include spaces of genuine interest that are not metrizable, without being so permissive that the term continuous loses its contrast or its content.

Now consider the indiscrete topology on a set X with at least two points: the only open sets are the empty set and X itself. It satisfies the formal definition exactly. But no two points can be separated by open sets; every sequence in X converges to every point of X; and every function from any topological space into X is continuous. One might say that this topology does not give a notion of nearness at all, but rather a limiting case in which the topology refuses to make any distinctions.

My question is whether the case qualifies. Is the indiscrete topology a genuine instance of the concept the open-set definition was introduced to capture, or is it only a formal model that satisfies the axioms while missing the point? Please go through my three desiderata one by one and say whether the case satisfies them or exposes their limits. Then give a clear verdict: does this case show that the standard definition of a topological space should be tightened, for example by adding a separation axiom such as T0, or is the better lesson that a maximally useful definition should admit such degenerate cases as a price worth paying?

In the third column, state for each desideratum whether it does real work in separating the indiscrete topology from a genuine notion of nearness, or whether it is too coarse to settle the case one way or the other.
