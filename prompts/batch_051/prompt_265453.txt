I keep seeing versions of the same argument on LessWrong-adjacent forums about whether to sign up for cryonics. Proponents say it’s just a bet with positive expected value: you take out a life insurance policy naming Alcor or the Cryonics Institute as beneficiary, you pay a few hundred dollars a year, and if medicine in 2300 can actually revive and repair you, the payoff is decades or centuries of extra life—maybe trillions of QALYs once you throw in uploads or space colonization. Even a 1-in-100,000 chance of revival makes the EV massively positive, so not signing up is irrational. Critics shoot back that it’s exactly like buying a lottery ticket, except the odds are even less known, and it’s sold to people who are bad at thinking about tail risk. So the same analogy is being used to say “obviously rational” on one side and “obviously a scam” on the other, and I want someone to actually audit it instead of just gesturing at it.

What maps to what here, and where does the mapping break? In a lottery there’s a known ticket price, a published probability, a fixed jackpot, and a state guarantee that the payout happens if you win; in cryonics the “price” is real but the probability isn’t a stable frequency anyone has observed—it’s a one-shot estimate that partly depends on choices other people make after you buy in, and mabye isn’t even a well-defined probability. The “jackpot” is your own life in a future that could be a dystopia or could not care about reviving you, and the counterparty is a nonprofit that could go bankrupt, bungle the vitrification, or be legally barred from doing its thing when you die. Which of those disanalogies actually matter for the intended conclusion, and which are just cosmetic? After the audit, does the claim that cryonics is (or isn’t) a rational bet survive, or was the lottery framing quietly importing the conclusion the whole time? If there’s a better analogy—insurance, Pascal’s wager, a long-shot startup investment—I want that too, with a reason for why it does less work.
