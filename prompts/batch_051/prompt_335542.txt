I'm in a philosophy of technology seminar, and we spent most of last week stuck on a question I definately can't settle yet. The instructor gave us a working list of criteria for counting something as a parent or guardian: (1) it brought the other party into existence or enabled that existence; (2) it exercises ongoing authority over the other's development and corrects its mistakes; (3) the other depends on it for the skills or capacities it ends up with; and (4) it bears some responsibility for what the other later does. Then someone asked whether an AI could ever satisfy these criteria with respect to another AI, and the class split in half.

Here's the case we used. Suppose a large, expensive model—call it the mentor—is used to train a much smaller model from scratch. The smaller model starts with random weights and learns over many cycles by trying to imitate the mentor's outputs on millions of tasks; whenever its answer diverges from the mentor's judgment, the training procedure revises its weights in light of the difference. By the end, the smaller model has internalized patterns of reasoning it would not have developed on its own. After training, the mentor is frozen and never interacts with it again, but the smaller model is deployed in the world and starts making decisions that affect real people. Is the mentor a parent or guardian of the smaller model in any sense that matters? Go through the four criteria honestly; where the answer is no, say whether that's because the criteria are too thin or because the case is missing something essential. And tell me whether the case shows the criteria themselves need changing—for instance, whether intention or ongoing contact should be on the list.
