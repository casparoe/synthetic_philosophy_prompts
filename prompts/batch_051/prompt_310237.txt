I have spent enough time reading rationalist and economics blogs that I feel I am supposed to be an expected-utility fundamentalist. The von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, and in particular the independence or sure-thing principle, look to me like the only thing standing between decision theory and a pile of ad hoc patches. Violate independence, and I can usually be shown a money pump or a dynamic inconsistency; I want a criterion that says what stays constant across contexts, and expected utility seems to be the only candidate with clean answers.

Then I reread the passage in Maurice Allais’s 1953 Econometrica article on rational behavior under risk in which he introduces his two-pair choice problem. In the first pair, option A is 100 million old francs for certain; option B is a 10% chance of 500 million, an 89% chance of 100 million, and a 1% chance of nothing. In the second pair, option C is a 10% chance of 500 million and a 90% chance of nothing; option D is an 11% chance of 100 million and an 89% chance of nothing. I do not take this merely as a specimen of others’ behavior. I find the predicted violation in myself as a first-person normative pull: I want A over B, and I want C over D. The independence axiom says that A over B commits me to D over C, because the common 0.89 slice of 100 million in the first pair, and the common 0.89 slice of nothing in the second pair, should cancel out. But the cancellation feels like sleight-of-hand: the common slice sits differently in the two problems, and I cannot make myself treat it as irrelevant.

So I face the classic clash. But I want a negotiated verdict, not a side. Which should yield: the axiom or the intuition? Present the strongest case for treating my Allais preference as a cognitive artefact to be debunked—certainty effect, regret, framing, scope insensitivity, status quo bias, or whatever else you think is doing the work—and the strongest case for treating it as tracking something real about risk that expected utility misses, such as the special status of certainty or a failure of linearity in probabilities. Walk me through what each concession costs. If I keep the axiom, what must I believe about my own mind, about money pumps, and about why the intuitive difference between the pairs is not a reason? If I amend the theory to fit the intuition, what is the most conservative non-expected-utility model that does so, and what does it sacrifice in aggregation, dynamic consistency, and decision-theoretic clarity? I especially want your assessment of the standard rationalist move that calls the intuition a certainty-effect or scope-insensitivity artefact and says it should not be listened to. Is that a genuine debunking argument, or is it a refusal to take the content of the intuition seriously? Conclude with either a reading on which the axiom and the intuition can both survive, or a clear statement of which one you would give up and what would have to be true for you to reverse that.
