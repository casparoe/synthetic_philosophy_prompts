I am the student editor of a monthly high-school history magazine. Most submissions are short essays by students, and several times a year a teacher or parent complains that a published piece is opinionated or biased rather than objective. Our editors have no training in historiography, each contested passage can get only about twenty minutes of review, and our faculty advisor has final sign-off. We have studied the opposing views of Ranke — that history should show the past as it actually was — and of E. H. Carr and Hayden White, who argue that selection, narrative, and the historian's own standpoint shape every historical account. Turn that debate into a reusable procedure our editors can run on any disputed passage: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a satisfactory answer, which features of a passage can be treated as plainly factual, and which should be flagged as interpretive, with an explicit "this is a judgment call" step where the procedure cannot decide. Include three or four short test passages an editor could use for calibration, with the procedure's verdict for each. Then apply the procedure to two cases I expect to appear: a paragraph that gives precise casualty figures without naming any source, and a paragraph that calls a labor strike unrest in one sentence and resistance in another. Make sure at least one application reaches a conclusion our staff would rather not accept.
