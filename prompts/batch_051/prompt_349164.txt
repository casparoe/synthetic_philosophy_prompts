A professor in my philosophy of language class used Hofstadter's "A Person Paper on Purity in Language" to attack the "it's just neutral" defense of generic "he" and "man," and I've been turning it over since. Her version was: Hofstadter imagines a world where the unmarked word for human beings is race-marked rather than sex-marked, so people say "chairwhite" and "mailwhite" the way we say "chairman" and "mailman," and they defend lines like "All whites are created equal" as obviously including everyone. The pronouns are "whe" (the generic/inclusive one) and "ble" (for black people). When reformers in that world push to swap in "person" and a single neutral pronoun, the locals roll out the exact complaints we hear about "chairperson" and singular "they": it sounds ugly, it's redundant, everyone knows "white" includes black people too, the old way is just natural, the new pronouns don't even have a decent pronunciation. Then the kicker: someone in that world suggests replacing the race-based pronouns with sex-based ones ("he"/"she"), and the narrator treats that as obviously ridiculous — which is, of course, our actual system. The professor's conclusion was that "it's neutral and natural" in our world is just the fictional bigots' argument with the axis swapped, so it collapses, and we should stop fussing and accept the reforms.

I'm broadly sympathetic but I've been burned by arguments where an analogy does the work quietly. Can you audit it? First give me the correspondence table (sex ↔ race, man ↔ white, woman ↔ black, he/she ↔ whe/ble, reforms ↔ reforms, and whatever else you think maps). Then the disanalogies that actually matter, not nitpicks — places where the satire's world comes apart from ours. Then commit: once those disanalogies are in, does her conclusion still hold, or was the analogy smuggling something in? If a better analogy would do the same job more honestly, tell me that too. I'd rather you take a definite position and defend it than lay the options out.
