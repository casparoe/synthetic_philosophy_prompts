Consider a Westminster-style parliamentary democracy with a hereditary constitutional monarch, in which royal assent to legislation, the appointment of the prime minister, and dissolution of parliament are, by convention, exercised only on the advice of ministers. An amendment adds a single new power, to be exercised by the monarch personally. Once a bill has completed all legislative stages and awaits royal assent, the Speaker of the lower house may attach to it a certificate stating that, in the Speaker's opinion, the bill raises a substantial constitutional question. If a certificate is attached, the monarch may, within thirty days, either grant assent in the usual way or issue a suspensive veto. A suspensive veto does not defeat the bill: it delays assent for twelve months, after which the bill returns to the current lower house and may be passed again by a simple majority, whereupon assent is automatic and may not be vetoed again. If a general election is held before the twelve months have elapsed, however, the bill lapses and must be introduced afresh in the new parliament. The monarch's veto is not judicially reviewable, but the Speaker's certificate is reviewable on the ground that it is unreasonable.

Walk through how this arrangement would actually behave rather than how it is supposed to behave. What does it do to when governments bring forward constitutionally significant bills, to contests for the speakership, to the monarch's incentives and public standing, and to the strategies of oppositions and minor parties? Where is it likely to be gamed first — for instance through election timing, the splitting of bills, pressure on the Speaker, or pressure on the monarch — and can those failure modes be repaired without gutting the mechanism?
