As a journalist who covers economics and the behavioral sciences, I have been asked to lead a seventy-five-minute brown-bag session for the reporters on my publication's economics and data desks. The topic is the distinction at the center of ergodicity economics: ensemble averages versus time averages, and why the difference between them should change how journalists report on risk, expected returns, and inequality. My colleagues can compute an expected value and read a histogram, but none of them has worked through stochastic processes or nonlinear dynamics. I want a plan for the session, not the session itself.

Give me the order in which the ideas must be introduced and what each depends on: where the additive-versus-multiplicative framing has to come, whether and when to give the formal definition of ergodicity or to postpone it, and at what point the growth rate of a multiplicative process, understood through the logarithm of wealth, should be introduced. I want the session built around one carrying example: a fair coin that multiplies current wealth by 1.5 on heads and by 0.6 on tails, so that the cross-sectional mean wealth grows by 5 percent per flip while the typical, time-average path declines by a little over 5 percent per flip. Tell me what this example must contain and what it must avoid, and whether a different single example would carry the session better. Lay out the misconceptions this audience is likely to bring or form — conflating the ensemble average with the experience of a typical individual, writing off the example as mere loss- or risk-aversion, suspecting that the negative time-average growth contradicts the strong law of large numbers, or assuming that pooling many parallel bettors repairs the dynamic for each of them — and say at what point in the sequence each one should be anticipated and defused rather than corrected after it hardens. Give me a check I can run in the closing minutes that would show that the reporters have the distinction and not just the example: something they would have to reason through themselves and that would catch someone who has memorized the arithmetic but cannot transfer it. And be explicit about what I should decline to explain at this level, and how to say so without making the session feel truncated.
