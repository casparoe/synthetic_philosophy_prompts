I'm trying to work out what I actually think about threats and commitments in deterrence contexts, because my reactions are all over the place. Here are my verdicts on a set of cases, and I want to know what principle, if any, captures them.

1. A nuclear-armed state tells an aggressive rival that if the rival invades its ally, it will respond with nuclear strikes, even though once the invasion has happened the rational thing might be not to retaliate and thereby invite destruction on itself. I think issuing the threat is fine, maybe even the right thing to do, if it prevents the invasion; I also think carrying it out afterwards would probably be wrong. So: the threat is permissible but the act it threatens isn't.

2. Same state, but instead of threatening, it builds an automated retaliatory system that launches on detecting an invasion, with no human override. I think building that is wrong, even monstrous, though it makes the threat far more credible than the bluffed one in the first case.

3. A general burns the only bridge behind his own army, so retreat becomes impossible and the enemy knows his soldiers will have to fight to the death. I find this fine, even admirable, and I don't think he has wronged his own troops.

4. A leader tells an adversary: if you push matters to the brink, something terrible could happen and it will be on your head, but refuses to say what. My instinct is that this vague threat is more acceptable than making a precise threat, because no one is actually committed to a specific act; but I also suspect that's a self-serving distinction.

5. Saying "if you hit me, I will defend myself" doesn't even feel like a threat to me; it's just a statement of fact, and no special moral issue arises.

6. A great power promises a small ally that an attack on it will be treated as an attack on the great power. The deterrent works, but years later the ally is attacked and honouring the promise would now cost far more than anyone foresaw. I think the promise still binds the great power to intervene, though I also think leaders shouldn't make open-ended promises of that sort in the first place.

Is there a single principle about threats, promises, credibility, or something else, that gets all or most of these? If one of the verdicts has to go, which one? And what does your principle say about a case I keep going back and forth on: a state that deliberately makes its nuclear command and control less stable in a crisis, so that an adversary cannot be confident that a small provocation won't trigger a launch. That feels like a wierd engineered version of the vague threat, and I can't decide whether it's more like case 2 or more like case 4.
