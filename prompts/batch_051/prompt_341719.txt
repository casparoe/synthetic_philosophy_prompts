People who perform highly practiced skills often report that directing attention to the mechanics of the action makes it worse: the pianist who monitors her fingers stiffens, and the fielder who thinks through the steps of a catch drops it. I keep returning to a possible structural parallel with Bernard Williams's integrity objection, where Williams suggests that a man who saves his wife from drowning and whose practical reasoning runs through the general thought that it is permissible to prefer one's spouse has one thought too many; he should be moved by the particular fact of who she is to him rather than by a detour through principle. In both cases, inserting an explicit representation between the agent and the act seems to corrupt something that worked well without it. I cannot decide whether this is a deep structural parallel or merely a loose analogy. What would have to be true of both sides for results to transfer between them, and does the comparison break because moral action, unlike fluent performance, is answerable to justification in a way motor control is not?
