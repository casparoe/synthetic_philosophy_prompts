im a phd student working between philosophy of religion and formal epistemology, and I keep noticing a parallel that I can't tell wether it's deep or just a trick of the vocabulary. The hell debate has this proportionality problem: if damnation is literally infinite (eternal conscious torment), then no finite sin, however awful, could be proportionate to it, and that's a big part of why people end up annihilationist or universalist. The move is basically that desert runs on a finite scale, so plugging in an infinite penalty breaks the scale. Meanwhile in decision theory theres the infinite utilities problem: once you allow an outcome with infinite value or disvalue, standard expected value reasoning breaks down, because any act that gives that outcome a nonzero probability has the same infinite expectation, so every small risk of the infinite outcome swamps every finite consideration and pairwise comparison stops doing useful work. Pascal's wager is the famous case.

The parallel I keep coming back to is this: both cases are about what happens when you feed an infinite magnitude into a comparative framework that was designed for finite magnitudes. In both, the framework loses its ability to say that something is greater, less, fitting, or unfitting. The proportionality objection says finite guilt and infinite punishment are incommensurable in a way that makes the punishment unjust; the infinite utility problem says finite options and infinite payoffs are incommensurable in a way that makes expected utility theory unable to rank options. That looks to me like the same structural fact: one infinite value in an additive/comparative ordering dominates everything and the ordering degenerates.

But im not sure. In the hell case we're evaluating whether a punishment fits a crime, which is a desert relation, not a choice-theoretic one. In the decision case we're evaluating how an agent should choose under uncertainty. Those could be structurally different enough that the resemblance is only verbal.
