I have been trying to systematize my gut reactions to the kind of situation sometimes discussed under the label of the unilateralist's curse: any one member of a group can perform an action that binds everyone, and each member has her own view about whether the action is a good idea. I am less interested in the label than in the underlying norm. Below are my snap judgments on a set of cases. I suspect a clean principle will force me to give up at least one of them.

**The thermostat.** Four housemates share a house, and any of them can adjust the smart thermostat from a phone. One housemate lowers the nighttime setting from 68 to 62, partly to save energy and partly because he sleeps better in the cold. He knows another housemate has said she prefers 68. The change is trivially reversible. I think this is fine; mildly rude at worst, not the kind of thing that should have waited for a house meeting.

**The preprint.** Six researchers are coauthors on a manuscript. Five, including the lead author, think a possible confound should be checked before the work is shared. The sixth runs a quick robustness check, convinces himself, and posts the draft to a preprint server under all their names. Withdrawing it later would not remove it from the public record. I think this was wrong, and not only because he overrode the others; he imposed his own confidence on people who will be publicly associated with the paper.

**The three-person switch.** Three graduate students share a flat and a single electricity account. Any of them can log in and switch the household to a new retail provider. One of them spends a couple of evenings comparing plans, then makes the switch even though the other two had said they hadn't had time to look and wanted to wait a week. Undoing it costs a small fee and an hour of phone calls. I think this is fine; I would mildly prefer that he had waited, but I do not think he wronged anyone.

**The sixty-person switch.** A loosely managed sixty-member housing cooperative holds a single electricity account. Any member can log in and switch the whole cooperative to a new provider, and one member does so after a few evenings of online research. He knows many of the other members have not yet compared options and no one has had a chance to raise objections. The per-person stakes and the reversibility are close to the three-person case. I think this was wrong, though I find it hard to say exactly what he should have done other than wait.

**The rare-disease dataset.** Four researchers jointly hold a supposedly de-identified dataset about a rare disease. Any of them can upload it to a public repository. One does so, convinced the re-identification risk is negligible. The other three think the risk has not been adequately assessed. If they are right, the harm is severe and effectively irreversible. I think this was wrong.

**The interstellar transmission.** A dozen astronomers on a telescope time-allocation committee each technically have authorization to send a brief high-power radio burst toward a nearby star system, an idea the committee has discussed but never approved. One sends it over the others' objections, because he judges the scientific value to outweigh the risk. I think this was wrong.

I half-suspect the principle behind all of this is something like: you may act unilaterally when mistakes are cheap and reversible, and otherwise you must wait for agreement. But that has no room for why the sixty-person switch bothers me more than the three-person switch, when the stakes and reversibility are, by construction, almost the same. And it would need some extra clause about representing others, to handle the preprint without simply condemning the thermostat.

Is there a single precise principle that captures these verdicts, or nearly all of them? State the strongest version of the principle you can, indicating which cases motivate each clause. Then tell me what it says about a fifteen-person analogue of the electricity-switch case, where the would-be switcher is by far the best-informed member of the group. Finally, be explicit about which of my verdicts the principle cannot save, if any, and where I should most plausibly update.
