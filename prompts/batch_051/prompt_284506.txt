Last night I spent a long while arguing with a friend about whether losing a rare item in an online game is a real loss: she kept saying it is only a database entry, while I kept pointing at the surrounding institutions, and neither of us could produce a tidy account of what those institutions are committed to. The practice that now interests me more than our original question is this. A game company will restore a stolen account and its inventory, sanction players who obtain items by hacking, and treat the items in those cases as someone's property that can be taken. In the same breath, the same company's terms of service state that players have no ownership interest in anything in the game, and it will wipe an account's holdings for rule violations without compensation. Outside the company, courts in a few countries have treated the taking of virtual goods as theft, even though no physical object changed hands and the "owner" owns nothing under the platform's own rules.

I would like a reconstruction of the theory of virtual objects — and of virtual harms — that these mixed practices presuppose, and an honest statement of where the practice is doing no philosophically determinate work at all. What must a virtual item be, in order for it to be the sort of thing that can be stolen in one institutional context but not compensably lost in another? Does the theft-related practice strictly presuppose that the virtual object itself, or the loss of it, is real, or is it better explained as protecting something extrinsic such as the player's time, labor, real-money purchase, or settled expectations? Which parts of the practice would remain licensed if a thoroughgoing irrealism about virtual objects turned out to be right, and which parts would have to be reformed or abandoned?
