An essay I read argues that Darwin and Turing reached the same conclusion from opposite directions. As the essay presents it, Darwin's inversion was to explain apparent design without a designer: you do not need to know how to make a well-adapted organism in order for natural selection to make one. Turing's inversion was the same move for thought: you do not need to know what arithmetic is in order for a machine to do arithmetic. The author then offers an analogy meant to show these are one insight: natural selection is gradient descent carried out by nature. A population of organisms is a batch of candidate solutions; mutation and recombination are random perturbations of those solutions; differential survival and reproduction is the evaluation step in which the environment scores each candidate; and over generations the population moves through the space of possible phenotypes toward a well-adapted form, just as a model's parameters move toward a low-error region. The intended conclusion is that competence without comprehension is exactly what we should expect: hard problems can be solved by feedback alone, with no representation or understanding anywhere in the system, and so talk of an inner understander is dispensable even for us. I find the analogy vivid, but I cannot tell how much work it is actually doing. Audit it for me. Lay out an explicit term-by-term mapping; say where the correspondence is close and where it breaks; identify which features of gradient descent the analogy imports into natural selection without warrant (a loss function fixed before learning begins? a true gradient to follow? a designer who chose the task and the objective?); and then judge whether the Darwin-Turing conclusion is genuinely supported by the analogy, or whether it survives only in a weaker form once the disanalogies are admitted. If there is a better analogy available nearby, provide it and say what it captures that this one misses.
