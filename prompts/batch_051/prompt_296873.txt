In a conversation about whether the other side in a polarized dispute argues in good faith, someone repeatedly cut off claims that a campaign's messaging was deliberately deceptive by reciting the slogan "Never attribute to malice that which is adequately explained by stupidity." The cases at issue involved coordinated communications staff, rehearsed talking points, and patterns sustained over months rather than isolated individual errors, so the slogan looked as though it was doing more work than it can obviously bear. I want a precise map of what that one-liner could be asserting. Distinguish the distinct theses it might express—for example, an empirical generalization about the relative frequency of malevolence and incompetence, an epistemic rule about what to infer under uncertainty, or a standing moral requirement to presume good faith—and for each, say whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or false, and how it should respond to evidence of coordination and repetition. Finally, identify which reading would actually license treating an attribution of bad faith as out of bounds, and say whether that reading survives scrutiny.
