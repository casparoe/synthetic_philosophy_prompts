Hey—I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy and I keep tangling myself up over the phrase standing to blame. Could you act as an expert on the ethics of blame and moral responsibility and think through this carefully with me? Start by brainstorming the different things people seem to be tracking when they say someone has or lacks standing to blame, then work toward a single working definition (or a small set of definitions, if you think the concept splits into two), and test it against the cases below. Please write in American English.

By 'standing to blame' I mean whatever someone needs, over and above knowing that another person acted wrongly, in order to be in a position to blame them. I want a definition that gets the obvious cases right and gives some verdict on the murky ones.

Obvious yes: a woman whose parked car gets hit by a texting driver, and who has never texted while driving, blames him; a homeowner whose fence was destroyed by a neighbor's fire, and who is not negligent, blames the neighbor.

Obvious no: a guy who texts while driving every day blames another driver for texting; a manager who ordered an employee to falsify records blames the employee for falsifying them.

Hard cases: (i) a former texting-while-driving offender who quit seven years ago blames a current texting driver; (ii) a driver who constantly speeds but never texts blames a texting driver; (iii) someone who passively profited from a company's illegal dumping, but didn't know about it and didn't participate, blames the CEO for the dumping; (iv) a woman who commited the same offense once, confessed, apologized, and made amends, now blames someone who did the same thing; (v) a man blames his brother's attacker even though he wasn't there and wasn't hurt; (vi) a stranger with no connection to either party, who read about an assault in the news, blames the attacker.

Run the definition through all of these, say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and revise it. I'm fine with surprising verdicts as long as they're defended. My hunch is that ordinary talk about standing bundles together a moral-record condition (the blamer's own conduct) and a relationship condition (the blamer's connection to the victim or wrong), and the hard cases are where those come apart—but feel free to push back on that.
