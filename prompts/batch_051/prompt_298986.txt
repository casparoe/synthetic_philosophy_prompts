I've been teaching myself causal discovery and I've worked myself into an argument that feels too tidy, and I want it pressure-tested. My worry is that it gets causation out of a parsimony assumption for free.

A DAG satisfies the Markov condition with respect to a probability distribution when every conditional independence entailed by d-separation in the graph genuinely holds in the distribution. Call a DAG minimal if you cannot delete any edge without the remaining graph entailing some d-separation claim that isn't a true conditional independence in the distribution — so every edge is load-bearing. Here's the argument:

(1) The true causal graph over a set of observed variables satisfies the Markov condition with respect to the distribution those variables generate.
(2) So the true graph is among the Markov graphs.
(3) Searching for the sparsest Markov graph means deleting edges until no further edge can be removed while staying Markov, i.e., finding a minimal graph.
(4) The true causal graph is itself minimal. Its edges are direct causes, so deleting any one of them would, roughly, make the graph claim a false independence.
(5) Therefore minimality is what makes causal discovery work: pick a Markov graph with minimum edge count and you have recovered the true causal structure, up to orientation.

I keep thinking step 4 is where the trick is, smuggling in faithfulness under the cover of a definition, but I cant pin down exactly how. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and on the strongest reading of that step that makes it true, does the conclusion follow? If step 4 survives, tell me why step 5 doesn't overclaim — 'sparsest Markov graph' still sounds like it could hand you the wrong edges.
