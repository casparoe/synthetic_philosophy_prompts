A friend and I recently spent a long evening arguing about whether a device that alters a person's mental state can violate a right even if it never reads, records, or transmits a single thought. I want my students to confront that question directly rather than settle into a privacy framework by default. I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on neuroethics and the law, and I'm writing the final exam. Please draft one 60-minute essay question, with a model answer of roughly 700 words and a 20-mark marking scheme, built around the following case. The case is a closed-loop brain–computer interface that an employer requires of workers in a safety-critical job: an on-device sensor detects lapses in sustained attention and delivers a mild stimulation that restores focus. The device stores nothing, transmits nothing, and no other person ever learns whether it fired; its detection is done entirely on-device. The stimulation target—sustained focus on the task—is chosen by the employer, not the worker. The question should force students to say whether anything objectionable remains once mental privacy is, by stipulation, intact, and whether cognitive liberty must therefore be more than mental privacy or whether a suitably broadened privacy analysis can absorb the case. The model answer should not simply define terms; it should test a stretched-privacy analysis against the case, state an autonomy- or control-based principle, and apply it. The marking scheme should separate an A from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain and the specific mistakes that should cost marks, including the conflation of mental privacy with cognitive liberty. No citations or readings are expected; everything should rest on conceptual analysis.
