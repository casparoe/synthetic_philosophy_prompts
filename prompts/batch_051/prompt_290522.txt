I'm a philosophy graduate student sitting in on an archaeology seminar, and I keep noticing a move in papers and site reports that I don't have a name for. The pattern is this: an object, pit, structure, or deposit resists identification with any obvious practical function — it isn't clearly storage, refuse, production, dwelling, defense, or burial. Instead of treating that as a negative result, the write-up treats it as positive evidence about the society's beliefs: it gets classified as ritual, ceremonial, symbolic, or cultic. What bothers me is that this seems to convert an absence of evidence about function into evidence for exactly the thing everyone agrees is hardest to infer from material remains alone. I have seen a pit with unusual contents called a ritual deposit because it did not fit a domestic refuse profile, and a building with an odd floor plan called a shrine because it did not fit a household or workshop plan — in both cases without independent evidence about belief or practice, only the lack of a mundane fit.

There also seems to be a common-sense hierarchy operating in the background: technology is read off objects fairly directly, subsistence and economy come next, social organization is shakier, and religious belief is the most conjectural end of the scale — yet unexplained residues keep getting slotted at precisely the most conjectural end. My question is whether this pattern has an established name and definition. If it does, I want more than the label: I want to know exactly which inferential step the name picks out and how it differs from (a) an ordinary argument from ignorance, (b) an ad hoc auxiliary hypothesis, and (c) the legitimate inference of symbolic activity from positive features of an object, such as patterning, placement, or manufacture that a practical-function explanation handles poorly. If it has no established name, coin one and define it with the same care.

In "Existing candidates" (about 200 words), give the closest established concepts you know, including any standard critique of defaulting to ritual, and say precisely where each captures the pattern only imperfectly. In "A name and definition" (about 150 words), either defend an established term and give its proper definition, or coin one, defining the phenomenon and its scope conditions. In "Boundary cases" (about 150 words), describe at least two nearby cases that fall outside the phenomenon, to test the definition.
