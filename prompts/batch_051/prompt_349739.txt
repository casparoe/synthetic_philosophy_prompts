I am writing an essay on George Eliot's religious thought, and I want a careful critical reading of Middlemarch through the lens of her mature religion of humanity. Take as background the following: Eliot lost her evangelical faith as a young woman, translated Feuerbach's The Essence of Christianity in 1854, and came to hold a position on which the divine is a projection of distinctively human powers — above all the capacity for sympathy, moral imagination, and devotion — so that what is abidingly valuable in Christianity survives, transfigured, as an ethics of fellow-feeling rather than as a body of supernatural doctrine. Read the novel as her fullest attempt to show what happens when religious energy is redirected from God toward human life.

Focus your reading on these threads: (1) Dorothea Brooke's hunger for a binding theory of duty and an epic self-sacrifice, and how her marriage to Casaubon — whose Key to all Mythologies treats religion as dead matter to be catalogued — diagnoses an impoverished relation to the sacred; (2) Nicholas Bulstrode's evangelical language as a case study in how religious forms can rationalize wrongdoing even while the novel treats his need for atonement with seriousness; (3) the Reverend Farebrother as an alternative model, unorthodox and humane, against the official religious voices of the novel; and (4) the narrator's practice of sympathy as a way of knowing characters from the inside, together with the novel's closing claim that the world's improvement depends partly on obscure acts and faithful hidden lives that leave no public trace.

The reading must be honest in both directions: show where Middlemarch genuinely vindicates the humanist reduction of theology, and where the novel resists or complicates it — for instance, whether Dorothea's ardor loses something when its object is secularized, whether sympathy within the novel can bear the full weight the old religion once carried, or whether the narrative clings to something irreducibly Christian that the Feuerbachian lens cannot name. End by saying what the novel adds to the philosophical position that the philosophy alone could not state.
