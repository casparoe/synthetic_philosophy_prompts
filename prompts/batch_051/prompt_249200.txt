I'm helping run a meetup on the doomsday argument, and the last two sessions made it obvious that people are talking past each other because they're using the same words for different self-locating assumptions. I want to make a "which position on the doomsday argument do you actually hold?" quiz to hand out before we discuss it again. Eight to ten questions, please, each one a concrete case with two or three answer options, and a scoring key at the end that maps answer patterns onto positions. The questions should never use the debate's jargon or just ask people to pick a label. Use cases like: a coin flip puts either 10 or 1000 balls in an urn and you draw a ball numbered 7; you wake up in a room and are told the building has either 12 or 200 occupied rooms; you find out you're roughly the 70-billionth human; God flips a coin to decide whether to create a trillion observers in another galaxy before pressing go on our universe. That sort of thing.

The leaf positions I have in mind (though you should add others if the space demands them) are: the classic Carter-style self-sampling assumption with "all humans" as the reference class, so discovering your birth rank really is evidence that doomsday is sooner; the self-indication assumption, where your very existing is evidence that there are lots of observers; a version that keeps self-sampling but makes the reference class all observers or all observer-moments rather than humans; the flat-out view that indexical facts are never evidence, so the doomsday argument never gets off the ground; and the deflationary view that the likelihood shift is real but gets swamped by any reasonable prior. But build the tree from the logical space first rather than from this list: lay out the branching commitments—whether indexicals can be evidence at all, what counts as the reference class, whether the mere fact of your existence is evidence, whether the prior can swamp the likelihood shift—and then see which combinations of answers are actually occupied, named or unnamed. For any combination nobody seems to defend, say briefly whether it's incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored. Also flag which leaves your questions can't seperate and why.

The audience is a bunch of bright people who've read maybe one or two popular articles, so the questions themselves should be answerable by someone who doesn't know what SSA stands for; save the jargon for the scoring key. And don't make any question a disguised version of "do you think you're a random sample?"—make people commit through the cases, because half the group apperently says one thing in the abstract and the opposite once you hand them an urn.
