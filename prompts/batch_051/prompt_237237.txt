After a meetup about forecasting, I was standing around with two guys I know, Caleb and Andre, and they got into it over whether a big crowdsourced poll should be trusted over a panel of three domain experts to predict whether a new irrigation program will raise smallholder yields by at least 20 percent. I mostly listened, and I honestly couldn't referee it at the time, but I keep turning the argument over.

Caleb said the Condorcet jury theorem basically settles it: if each voter has even a slightly-better-than-chance probability of being right on the question, then as the group grows the majority becomes essentially certain to be right. So if you think the average person has at least a 51 percent shot at this, the big poll beats the experts. Andre said the theorem is doing no work here, because it's assumptions are false. First, voters aren't equally competent: most are guessing, some are below chance on a technical question like this, and a few are genuinely informed, while the theorem (as he remembers it) assumes every voter has the same independent probability above one half. Caleb pushed back that unequal competence isn't actually fatal: by the law of large numbers, the share of the crowd that votes correctly converges to the average competence, so if the average voter is above one half, the majority still becomes near-certain as the group grows. To reject the poll, he said, Andre would have to claim the mean voter is worse than a coin flip, which he didn't think Andre believed.

Andre replied that all of that still assumes independent votes, and that's the real failure. The voters read the same handful of articles, follow the same experts, and pass around the same posts, so their mistakes are correlated. If the crowd shares a bad model of how irrigation works, a million people will be wrong together, and adding voters just adds copies of the same error — the crowd ends up no wiser than its common information. Caleb answered that correlation isn't automatically fatal either: if the shared sources are right more often than they're wrong, then correlated voters are still tilted toward the correct answer on average, so a big majority might still track the truth. Andre said that's no longer the theorem he was citing, and citing Condorcet to justify the poll is just decorating a prior with a theorem.

Can you go through their exchange and say who is right about which specific point: the unequal-competence objection, Caleb's law-of-large-numbers move, the independence/correlation objection, and the "that's not the theorem" point? I'd also like to know what a correct appeal to Condorcet here would actually have to establish about the voters, whether Caleb's bottom line (the big poll beats the experts) survives Andre's objections, and who is more right overall.
