I am assembling a small set of poems to close a reading group on the philosophy of criminal law, and the topic we keep circling back to is the paradox of blackmail. The puzzle is that each ingredient seems, taken alone, perfectly lawful: a person is generally entitled to disclose a damaging truth if the disclosure itself violates no independent obligation, and a person is generally entitled to ask another for money. Joined in a conditional demand—pay, or the information will be disclosed—the same pair of lawful acts becomes the offense of blackmail. The group could not agree on whether the wrong lies in the coercive proposal, in the exploitation of the victim's interest in secrecy, in the leverage gained over information the victim cannot protect, or somewhere else entirely, and I want the poem to keep that disagreement alive rather than resolve it.

Please write a villanelle whose two refrains carry the two lawful ingredients: one refrain should voice the freedom to disclose the truth, and the other should voice the freedom to ask for money. Because each refrain returns throughout the poem, they should be lines that gain force with repetition. Each tercet should hold the two permissible acts close but still distinct, so that the reader feels the ingredients being held just apart; then, in the closing quatrain, the refrains should finally stand side by side as the poem's last two lines, so that the form itself executes the forbidden conjunction at the moment the poem ends. The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher who works on blackmail would recognize the argument in the structure, and good enough on its own terms that a reader encountering the problem for the first time would understand why the conjunction is disturbing.
