I have been developing a framework I call the proxy ladder, and I would like an honest assessment of whether it is more than a restatement of Goodhart's law. The core claim is that when a metric becomes a target, gaming is not removed by patching the metric; it climbs one level up. Level 1 is the proxy for the intended outcome. When agents game Level 1, the usual fix is a Level-2 measure — an audit of Level 1, or a second proxy meant to capture what the first misses — and agents then learn to game Level 2, and so on. For example, test-score targets produce teaching to the test; auditing the teaching then produces teaching to the audit; the audit criteria then become the next thing manipulated. I claim this recursion has only two stable endpoints: either the accumulated proxies become so dense that measuring them simply is performing the task, so there is no separate proxy left to game; or the system settles on a proxy so remote from the original target that gaming it has become socially invisible or effectively harmless. I know the general phenomenon is related to Goodhart's law, Campbell's law, and specification gaming, but I suspect the ladder claim itself — that gaming is displaced upward rather than destroyed, with exactly those two attractors — has not been stated in this form. Is the framework coherent as stated? Does it already exist under another name, in which case I would rather adopt the existing term? And what is the strongest counterexample, especially a case where a patch genuinely eliminates a form of gaming rather than relocating it?
