Write a parody of a consent decree, roughly 700–900 words, issued by a fictional competition authority after an investigation into several firms whose independently deployed pricing algorithms settled on a supracompetitive equilibrium without any human agreement. Give it a plausible but invented case caption, format it as a dry legal document — recitals, a definitions section, numbered paragraphs of ordered conduct, and a short monitoring provision — and keep it deadpan throughout.

The target is the conceptual fault line in algorithmic collusion: competition law is built around an agreement or a meeting of the minds, but reinforcement-learning agents can coordinate through repeated interaction and equilibrium selection while no human ever intends, communicates, or agrees to anything. The humor has to come from getting that problem right rather than from generic robot jokes. The recitals should emphasize that the evidence shows only the repeated observation of rivals' public prices and adjustments made in response, with no direct communication, no explicit instruction, and no human decision to coordinate. The definitions section should quietly contrast what the law ordinarily requires with what the algorithms actually did. The remedies should be technically coherent punishments of code rather than of conduct — for instance, capping learning rates, requiring exploration noise large enough to keep the system from locking onto one focal equilibrium, banning the pricing policy from conditioning on an individual rival's historical price vector while still allowing it to condition on aggregate market data, or requiring periodic re-runs against a competitive benchmark. Never state the underlying objection outright; let the reader feel it — that the order is treating an equilibrium as if it were an act, and punishing parameters because there is no intention to punish. A reader who knows the game theory (tacit collusion, Nash versus correlated equilibrium, how common knowledge can arise from repeated observation) should find the satire fair, while a reader who does not should still follow what is happening. Include one short quotation from a fictional, obviously invented concerned competition economist who thinks the order has merely made convergence to the supracompetitive outcome slower and more elaborate.
