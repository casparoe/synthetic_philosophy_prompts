In a discussion group I'm part of, we've been working through the old contrast between so-called shame cultures and guilt cultures, and last week one participant tried to settle the question of which emotion is the better moral emotion with an analogy. Shame, he argued, is like a security camera: it does its work only when someone is watching, or when you imagine someone might be; it responds to the exposure rather than to the wrong itself; and its sting depends on whose eyes you think you are under. Guilt, by contrast, is like an internal audit: it runs without any spectator, checks your conduct against your own books, and flags the discrepancy rather than the discovery. From this he concluded that guilt is clearly the superior moral emotion, that shame deserves its bad reputation, and that child-rearing practices and perhaps whole cultures should favor guilt—and that where a culture sits on this axis is therefore, to that extent, a choice.

I suspect the analogy is carrying more weight than it can bear, but I want it audited rather than simply dismissed. Please work through it in detail: supply the correspondence table between the two source pictures (the security camera and the internal audit) and the two target phenomena (shame and guilt); separate the disanalogies that matter for his intended conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic; and say explicitly what the analogy quietly imports about the internality of the standards being applied, the reliability of self-assessment, the role of real versus imagined audiences, and whether shame and guilt are actually competing for one and the same job. Then rule on whether the conclusion—that guilt is the better moral emotion and that cultures can or ought to choose it—survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and propose a better analogy if one is available.
