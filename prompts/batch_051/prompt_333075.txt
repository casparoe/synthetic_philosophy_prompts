In debates about the ethics of belief, William James’s essay The Will to Believe (1896) is often used against the Clifford-style principle that one should never believe without sufficient evidence. The most commonly cited part is his notion of a “genuine option,” but I want to focus on one of his supporting analogies. James writes:

> Finally, if I were Dr. Nansen and proposed to you to join my North Pole expedition, your option would be momentous, for this would probably be your only similar opportunity, and your choice now would either exclude you from the North Pole sort of immortality altogether or put at least the chance of it in your hands. He who refuses to embrace a unique opportunity loses the prize as surely as if he tried and failed.

I want an audit of this analogy as if it were offered today as a reply to evidentialism about religious belief. Give me the correspondence table: what maps onto the expedition, the invitation, the unique opportunity, the prize, the risk of death, the evidence for whether the expedition will reach the pole, and the losses associated with going versus staying. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for its argumentative work from those that are merely incidental. In particular, test whether the analogy imports a real inviter who knows the journey is possible and extends a genuine offer, a determinate prize that is lost by refusal because the offer is unique, and a practical asymmetry between joining and declining. Does James’s phrase “loses the prize as surely as if he tried and failed” surreptitiously convert agnostic suspension into an active refusal with the same downside as error? After you lay out the mapping and the breaks, tell me whether the anti-evidentialist conclusion survives once those hidden assumptions are rejected, or whether the analogy was carrying the whole argument. If the Nansen analogy fails, what better analogy could carry the same point?
