Here is an argument I keep returning to, reconstructed in Rawlsian terms. I want it stress-tested rather than summarized.

(1) The correct principles of justice are the ones that would be chosen by rational, mutually disinterested parties in the original position, behind a veil of ignorance that hides their place in society, natural endowments, and conception of the good.
(2) Because the veil hides every basis for assigning probabilities, the parties face a choice under complete ignorance, not merely under risk.
(3) Under complete ignorance, rational choice requires the maximin rule: rank alternatives by their worst possible outcome and choose the alternative with the best worst outcome.
(4) The worst possible outcome for a party choosing among rival principles of justice is to end up in the worst-off social position permitted by that principle.
(5) Therefore the parties will choose the principle under which the worst-off position is as good as possible.
(6) The difference principle states that social and economic inequalities are to be arranged so that they are to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged members of society.
(7) Therefore the original-position argument yields the difference principle.

I find (1) and (4) close to stipulative, and (2) seems to follow from what the veil is meant to hide, so the trouble must be in (3) or in the move from (5) and (6) to (7). But I cannot say precisely which step fails, or whether the argument is really valid once the right reading is made.

Is the argument valid? If it is, defend the step a critic should attack. If it is not, identify the first step that fails, explain the exact nature of the failure (false premise, equivocation, invalid inference, suppressed premise), and give the strongest repair of the argument that you think can survive. If the task as posed rests on a confused premise — for example, that decision theory can settle a question this way — say so plainly instead of working within a framing you think is mistaken.
