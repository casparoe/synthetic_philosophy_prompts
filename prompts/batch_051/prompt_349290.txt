I'm working through Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics for a research project on the ideal of self-sufficiency, and I keep tripping over the same passage in Book I, chapter 7. In the translation I'm using, it reads:

"The same conclusion also appears to follow from a consideration of the self-sufficiency of happiness—for it is felt that the final good must be a thing sufficient in itself. The term self-sufficient, however, we employ with reference not to oneself alone, living a life of isolation, but also to one's parents and children and wife, and one's friends and fellow citizens in general, since man is by nature a social being. On the other hand a limit has to be assumed in these relationships; for if the list be extended to one's ancestors and descendants and to the friends of one's friends, it will go on ad infinitum. But this is a point that must be considered later on; we take a self-sufficient thing to mean a thing which merely standing by itself alone renders life desirable lacking in nothing, and such a thing we deem happiness to be."

I'm confused about what "self-sufficient" is actually doing here. On one common reading, autarky sounds like not needing anything from outside: the self-sufficient life is one I can live off my own resources, without depending on other people or circumstances. But Aristotle immediately says the term is not to be applied to a solitary person: it includes one's parents and children and wife, and one's friends and fellow citizens in general. Then, just a few lines later, he says the self-sufficient thing is what "standing by itself alone" makes life desirable and lacking in nothing, which sounds like isolation again. I can't tell whether that last "alone" qualifies the life considered as a whole rather than the person, or whether Aristotle is genuinely pulled in two directions, or whether he is deliberately redefining the everyday idea of independence so that it has little to do with material or social independence. Could you walk me through what "self-sufficient" means in this passage—especially what work the "not to oneself alone" qualification is doing, and whether Aristotle's notion is compatible with depending on other people? And if someone cites this passage to defend a modern ideal of autarky, whether Stoic self-mastery or national economic self-reliance, which parts of Aristotle's meaning would that defense actually inherit, and which parts would it have to leave behind?
