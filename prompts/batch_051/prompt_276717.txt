I am a doctoral student in philosophy of biology, and our research group has been building a small database of case verdicts on biological individuality. One postdoc in the group, whose judgments we treat as a useful fixed point, recently filled out a yes/no questionnaire about which items she would count as a single Darwinian individual. Her recorded answers were: a domestic cat, yes; a honeybee colony considered as a whole, yes; a termite colony considered as a whole, yes; a Portuguese man o' war, yes; a single gastrozooid detached from a Portuguese man o' war, no; a buffalo herd, no; and a flock of starlings, no. She has declined to state the principle behind her answers, saying that the pattern should speak for itself.

Next week she will be asked to rule on a branching coral colony. In the species we have in mind, the polyps remain connected by living tissue and share a continuous gastrovascular system, but there is no sterile worker caste: each polyp retains the capacity to produce gametes, so the colony as a whole exhibits physiological integration without reproductive division of labor. I want a prediction of her verdict and, more importantly, a reconstruction of what she is doing. Specifically: which two or three defensible principles from the biological-individuality literature fit all of her recorded answers equally well; what each principle predicts for the coral colony; which recorded answer, if any, is doing the most work for each candidate principle; which principle you would bet she is running and why; and what additional case you would give her next to distinguish the candidate principles most cleanly. I am less interested in whether the coral colony is really an individual than in the diagnostic structure of her answers.
