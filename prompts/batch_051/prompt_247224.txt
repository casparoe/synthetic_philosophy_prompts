Last night I got into a long argument with a friend who works in formal epistemology about the problem of logical omniscience, and I want to settle something I could not articulate at the time. He laid the problem out in the standard way: possible-worlds semantics models an agent's beliefs as a set of worlds, but then the agent is automatically modeled as believing every tautology and every logical consequence of its beliefs, which is obviously false of any finite reasoner; he framed the interesting question as which repair is least bad — impossible worlds, awareness structures, distinguishing implicit from explicit belief, and so on. I kept wanting to object that, from where I sit, the whole difficulty looks like a type error, or a confusion between definitional equality and the propositional equalities no decider can be expected to know, but I could not make the translation precise in the moment.

I am a type theorist and work daily with proof assistants. I am fluent in the vocabulary I want the debate restated in: propositions as types, contexts and assumptions, proof terms as inhabitants, definitional versus propositional equality, canonicity and normalization, proof irrelevance, and the distinction between a proof term and the tactic or derivation that produced it; I can follow effect systems and resource-bounded computation, but they are not my home turf, and I have only an amateur's grasp of epistemic logic. Please restate the logical-omniscience debate in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, going through the main positions one by one — the original possible-worlds formulation, the closure principles that generate the problem, and the main proposed repairs — and for each say what translates cleanly, what has to be distorted to fit, and what has no expression at all. Then give me the residue report I actually care about: which parts of the philosophers' debate survive translation, and which parts fail to, and whether the residue is best explained as my vocabulary being missing something (for instance, a real notion of explicit, bounded, occurrent belief) or as the original debate being partly an artifact of possible-worlds modeling.
