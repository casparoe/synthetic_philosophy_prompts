I am giving a seminar next month to a mixed philosophy–computer science theory reading group on the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis, and I expect the question period to be hostile in a polite, technical way. My paper defends a deliberately unfashionable position: that the so-called physical Church–Turing thesis is not an empirical conjecture about the computing power of the physical universe, and that attempts to refute it with relativistic supertasks (spacetimes engineered so that an observer in finite proper time could witness an infinitely long computation), closed timelike curves, or other exotic machines misfire. I argue that the thesis earned its authority as a convergence claim — several independently motivated formalisms, including general recursive functions, the lambda-calculus, and Turing machines, pick out the same class of functions — and that its continuing role is regulative rather than descriptive: it sets a standard for when something counts as a procedure, an algorithm, or a computation at all. On my reading, a proposed counterexample never forces the conclusion that the thesis is false; it forces one of two things: either the process fails to specify a finite, surveyable procedure, or the word 'computation' has been stretched to cover something no finite agent could in principle execute or verify. The obvious cost, which I try to meet head-on, is that my view looks unfalsifiable, and I defend the claim that unfalsifiability in a regulative principle is not the same vice as unfalsifiability in an empirical hypothesis.

The audience is not uniformly on my side. It includes four philosophers of physics with a strong empiricist bent who think physics gets the last word on what is computable and who have worked on hypercomputation — the project of describing physically or logically possible processes that would decide functions beyond the Turing limit. It also includes two theoretical computer scientists who regard the Church–Turing thesis as settled informal mathematics and will be visibly irritated by anything that sounds like philosophy-of-science hand-waving, plus several logic graduate students who know the equivalence proofs but have not thought much about what those proofs establish. There is also one senior faculty member who thinks the thesis is best understood as a stipulative definition of 'effectively computable' and will push me to say what, on my view, is left to argue about at all.

Write me a Q&A prep sheet for this talk, not a defense of the paper. Give me the objections in roughly the order this audience will actually raise them, phrased as those particular people would phrase them rather than as a journal referee would, and ordered overall by expected damage — roughly likelihood times seriousness. For each objection, tell me what I should concede up front, what I should hold, a reply I could give in about two minutes under questioning, and a note on where that reply is softest. End with the question I should be hoping nobody asks, and the first move I can make if they ask it anyway.
