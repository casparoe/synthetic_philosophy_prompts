I follow a standing rule for philosophical debates that begin to look as though they may be verbal. When two careful interlocutors have charitably restated each other's positions and still disagree about a claim such as "Xs exist" or "Xs are nothing over and above Ys," I ask each of them to state what worldly discovery or argument would make them change sides. If neither can supply one, I classify the disagreement as a dispute over frameworks rather than facts, and I decline to take either side. I recently applied the rule to a debate over whether a table exists in addition to the particles that compose it: both parties failed my test, so I set the question aside as merely verbal. The rule has saved me from what I take to be dead-end arguments, but I am not sure it is anything more than a bias dressed up as a method. In particular, I worry that the test is tilted against substantive disputes that are not settled by observation — mathematical, conceptual, or metaphysical disputes in which a person could be right without being able to specify a confirming observation or a clear defeater. Is my rule rationally defensible as it stands? If it is not, is there a revised version — for example, one that permits me only to suspend judgment, or that requires me to produce an explicit paraphrase showing that the two sides are saying the same thing before I dismiss the debate — that keeps the benefits without the overreach?
