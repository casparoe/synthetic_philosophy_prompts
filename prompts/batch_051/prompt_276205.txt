I got interested in this after watching a piece of my hometown stay fixed long after the reasons for it disappeared: a bus route kept running because the depot, the schedules, and the drivers’ assignments had all settled around it, and by the time anyone asked whether it still matched the town’s needs, moving it had become expensive and politically difficult. Since then I have noticed that arguments about why things turned out the way they did fall back on “path dependence” as if it settled the question, when it really just names the thing to be explained. One friend says a keyboard layout survived because of an early typewriter-era choice, another says it survived because it was good enough and the cost of switching never paid for itself; a third says the same about a video format or a railway gauge. I want to stop leaning on examples and get a clear statement of what an adequate account of path dependence has to deliver.

Please write out the requirements: what any theory claiming that an outcome is path dependent must show, which cases it has to get right, which distinctions it must respect (an early accident versus a later lock-in, being caused by history versus being predictable from it, being difficult to reverse versus being impossible to abandon), and which regularities it has to accommodate. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it, and mark which are non-negotiable parts of the concept and which are only preferences of one camp or another — for example, that the surviving outcome must be worse than the alternative, that it could not have been foreseen, that increasing returns must be present, or that the lock-in must persist even when a majority would prefer to switch.

The part I most want to understand is whether the list can be satisfied all at once. It seems people want a path-dependent outcome to be contingent enough that a slightly different early run of events would have produced a different result, yet explicable enough that in hindsight you can see exactly why it persisted, and I do not know whether those two demands are compatible or whether one has to give way. Please flag any other places where the requirements pull in opposite directions — for instance, distinguishing path dependence from ordinary causation without classifying every historical event as path dependent — and say which single requirement, if you had to choose, does the most to exclude candidate theories. I am less interested in a scorecard of particular authors than in the list itself and the tensions inside it.
