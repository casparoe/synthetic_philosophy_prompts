Two friends of mine, Lena and Marcus, got into an argument at dinner that I still haven't been able to settle. Lena said that when she looks at a photograph of her grandmother as a little girl, she is literally seeing her grandmother—not just a picture that represents her, the way a drawing would. Marcus said that can't be right: her grandmother has been dead for years, so there is no person there to see; what she's seeing is a flat piece of paper covered in dyes, which she is only interpreting. Lena came back with stars: we say we see a star even if it blew up long ago, since the light from it is only arriving now, so seeing doesn't require the thing to exist at the same moment as you. Marcus said yes, but with a photograph the chain was broken—the light was absorbed by the film, developed, printed, and the light now hitting your eye comes from the print, not from the person. Lena said then by that standard you don't see through a mirror either, and what about a live security-camera feed on a screen? Those redirect or convert the light too. Marcus said a live feed might be like a mirror with a delay, but a recording is a trace, more like a footprint: when you look at a footprint you learn about the foot, but you don't see the foot. Lena answered that a footprint doesn't look like the foot, whereas a photograph carries the exact visual detail of the thing itself, so she's not inferring from evidence; she's seeing her grandmother's face. Marcus said, okay, then I can take the negative, retouch a tiny detail, and print it, and you'd look at it the same way and be unable to tell; one minute you were 'seeing her,' the next you're not, even though nothing visible changed for you—so the difference must be in the story you tell about the photo's history, not in anything the photograph does. Lena said that only shows you can be tricked about what you're seeing, not that you weren't seeing it before.

They both turned to me, and I honestly couldn't work out who'd won which point. I've thought about it alot since, and it still seems like both of them are right about different pieces of it. Could you walk through their argument round by round and say who is right about what, what (if anything) they're both missing, and whether overall the claim that we see through photographs survives?
