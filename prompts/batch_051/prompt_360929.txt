In my science classes we are expected to say that a wrong answer is simply wrong, but in practice everyone treats a pupil who gives the freezing point of water as 5 °C as less wrong than one who gives it as 80 °C. That gap between the official line and actual judgement has made me think about whether there is a principled way to say how close a false claim or theory is to the truth. I have since learnt that philosophers of science call this closeness 'verisimilitude', and that the obvious ways of measuring it run into trouble almost immediately. Before I read any particular theory of verisimilitude, I want the specification such a theory would have to satisfy.

Write the desiderata: what any adequate account of how close a false theory is to the truth must deliver. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it. Make sure the list handles at least these: two false theories where one is intuitively much closer to the truth than the other; the fact that adding an obviously true but irrelevant statement should not make a theory look closer; the fact that adding an arbitrary false statement should not be able to make a theory look closer either; and the worry that a measure should not change merely because the same theory is rewritten in a different vocabulary. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable, which are contestable, and which pair or subset you suspect cannot all be met at once. Then briefly run the simplest proposal—comparing a theory's stock of true statements with its stock of false statements—against your list, and say which single requirement excludes the most popular moves in this area. Please write the answer in American English.
