Right, I've just got back from a long, fairly circular argument with a friend about whether anything that can't be said can still be known — we went round and round on mystical experience, and by the end I realised I'd never actually written down the whole tangle of what I think. So here it is, roughly in the order I found myself defending it. I'd be grateful if you'd go through it and tell me whether the set actually hangs together. Don't just give me a yes/no: I want the tensions ranked by severity, which pairs only look like they clash but don't, and what the cheapest fix would be in each case — what I'd have to give up and what it costs me. And at the end, say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would push you to a different verdict about the worst tension, and where do you think your own reasoning is most liable to fail. Please write the whole thing in British English.

Here's where I've landed:

1. I think there are genuinley mystical experiences — not just strong emotion or brain noise, but states in which the person seems to be in direct contact with something real that our ordinary categories can't get any purchase on.

2. I think those experiences are ineffable in a strong sense: any attempt to say what was revealed distorts it into something false, and even the person who had the experience can't put its content into words later, not even privately to themselves.

3. But I don't think it's merely an emotional state. The experience is cognitive — the person comes out knowing something they didn't know before, not just feeling calmer or more at peace.

4. I hold that knowledge by acquaintance is a genuine kind of knowledge. Knowing what a taste or an experience is like is a real cognitive achievement even when you can't describe it, and I'm inclined to say that's what the mystical case gives you: knowledge of what that contact was like.

5. Except I also don't think knowledge can float completely free of judgement. Before I'll call a state knowledge, I want it to be able to make at least some difference to what someone thinks or does — to rule out at least one alternative, or help them tell one thing from another. Otherwise 'knowledge' is just a kind of compliment.

6. The old paradox doesn't bother me much — the one that says if it's truly ineffable you can't even say it's ineffable. Marking out where description gives out seems to me a seperate business from trying to describe the thing itself.

I honestly can't tell whether there's a real inconsistency in there or whether I was just too deep in it to see straight.
