Pascal's mugging, at least the version I first saw on LessWrong, runs on an agent with an explicit, unbounded utility function and a probability distribution that never fully rules out the mugger's story, so a tiny probability times a big enough payoff swamps any ordinary decision. The agents that now feel relevant — LLM agents trained by RLHF to approximate human preferences — don't obviously have either ingredient: no explicit utility function over arbitrary states of the world, and no well-defined prior over possible worlds, just a learned distribution over token sequences. Does the argument survive this shift? If an LLM gives in to a Pascal-style demand, is that the same failure mode or a different one (sycophancy, or taking the phrase "non-zero probability" in natural language too literally)? Or does the real mugging re-appear one level up, in the human deciding wether to trust the system's verdict? Say which premise is doing the work and whether there's a repaired version of the argument.
