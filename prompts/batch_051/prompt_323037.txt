I've been turning over an argument I heard at a reading group on AI safety, and I can't decide whether it is a genuine insight or a trick of phrasing. The claim is that the contrast between "worst-case" and "average-case" guarantees is largely a red herring: the former is simply the latter applied with a different, badly chosen probability distribution. The argument was presented in numbered form as follows.

(1) Any rational rule for accepting or rejecting a system must rank candidate systems by their expected value under some probability distribution over the situations the system will encounter.
(2) Average-case evaluation does exactly that: it estimates expected value under a distribution intended to match the situations the system will actually face in deployment.
(3) Worst-case evaluation is just expected value under an extreme distribution that assigns probability one to the single most unfavorable situation one can construct, and probability zero to everything else.
(4) That extreme distribution is not a credible forecast of the deployment stream. Nobody believes the world consists entirely of one adversarially engineered input repeated indefinitely.
(5) Therefore worst-case evaluation is not a stricter or more safety-conscious standard than average-case evaluation; it is an average-case calculation under an obviously false prior. If that is right, the demand for worst-case guarantees should be replaced by better sampling of the deployment distribution.

I felt step (3) was a caricature, but I couldn't pin down what "worst-case guarantee" means if not performance on the worst possible input. I also suspect step (1) is doing more work than it looks. Is the argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails, and on what reading of "worst-case" would the argument be strongest?
