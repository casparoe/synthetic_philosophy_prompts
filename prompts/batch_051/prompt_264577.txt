I'm not an academic; I came to these questions through online arguments about whether reducing the worst kinds of suffering should dominate moral decision-making, and I've ended up holding a view I can't easily reject. But when I tried to work out what the view implies about trade-offs, I built an argument that seems to show the view is either false or practically useless. I want to know where the argument goes wrong, if it does, and what I would be giving up by rejecting whichever move is at fault.

Here is the view. There is a level of suffering so bad that no quantity of milder pain, and no quantity of mild pleasure offered to other people, can compensate for it. One hour of that severe agony outranks a billion headaches and a billion pleasant afternoons. Below that line, pain and pleasure can be swapped back and forth in the ordinary way. Above the line, they cannot. I also find myself thinking that avoiding suffering, especially above that line, should matter more than producing happiness, rather than happiness simply canceling out the pain on the same scale. I'll call all of this the threshold claim.

Now here is the argument that worries me.

(1) If two sources of badness are commensurable, then some finite amount of the lesser one must be worse than a single instance of the greater one; otherwise they do not share a common scale at all.

(2) The threshold claim says no finite amount of below-threshold pain, mild pleasure, or comfort is worse than one period of above-threshold agony.

(3) Therefore, on my own view, above-threshold agony is not commensurable with below-threshold pain, pleasure, or comfort.

(4) But if two things are not commensurable, they cannot be compared. There is no answer to whether it is better to accept a small extra risk of above-threshold agony in exchange for a large reduction in below-threshold pain or a large gain in pleasure, and no way to choose except by guess or habit.

(5) A moral view that cannot rank those comparisons — the exact trade-offs hospitals, city planners, and ordinary people actually face — is worthless in practice.

(6) So the threshold claim leaves me with a view that is either false or worthless.

I would rather keep the threshold claim and the first two premises than abandon the idea that the worst pain outranks all milder payoffs. But if the argument is valid, one of the later moves must go. Which step is the mistake, if any? If no step is a mistake, which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it do to the rest of the view?
