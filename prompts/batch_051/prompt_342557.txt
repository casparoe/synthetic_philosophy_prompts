I am a PhD student in legal philosophy preparing a job talk for a criminal law theory workshop. The talk defends the following thesis: strict liability in modern criminal law is best understood not as an exception to the mens rea principle but as a distinct mode of liability whose legitimacy depends on the regulatory structure in which it is embedded. I plan to open with this passage from Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr.'s The Common Law: "The substance of the law at any given time pretty nearly corresponds, so far as it goes, with what is then understood to be convenient; but its form and machinery, and the degree to which it is able to work out desired results, depend very much upon its past." My argument is that debates about strict liability have focused too heavily on the first clause of that sentence—present convenience—while neglecting the second, and that attending to the inherited form and machinery of particular offenses explains why some strict liability offenses seem arbitrary in ways that others do not. The audience will be mostly criminal law scholars with strong doctrinal commitments, plus two moral philosophers who work on culpability and one legal historian. Prepare me a Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I should expect from this audience, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased the way this kind of audience member would put it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I should most hope nobody asks.
