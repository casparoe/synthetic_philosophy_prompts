I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I write a weekly column on why some piece of internet culture actually caught on — a meme format, a catchphrase, a conspiracy theory, a TikTok trend. I've read enough about the Darwinian theory of culture to know the machinery: variants, selection, retention, and the standing warning that most stories about why a thing spread are just-so stories. What I dont have is a reusable procedure for deciding, about a specific feature of a specific thing, whether its there because it helped the thing spread, because it's a byproduct of something else that spread, or because it's drift. The stakes are plain: if I call the wrong feature adaptive my editor runs a just-so story, but if I shrug too often the column has no thesis. Build me a procedure I can actually run in about an hour per column: questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to move on, where I stop and write we don't know, and which steps are really judgment calls I should own up to rather than pretend are method. Then run it on three cases: a meme template with a punchline slot that keeps mutating, a conspiracy theory that has lasted decades, and a slang term that spiked and died. At least one should end with the procedure telling me my pet adaptationist story is unsupported.
