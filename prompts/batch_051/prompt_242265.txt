I am a novelist, and for a book I am writing I need to think through how an institution designed to make people hear one another would actually behave once it was populated by self-interested, status-seeking users. The novel centers on a large discussion platform whose moderators, worried that the site has become an epistemic bubble in which users encounter only caricatures of other groups, adopt the following rule.

Any post that attributes a belief, preference, or motive to a named group of people ("the old families believe...", "the guild holds that...", "people from the valley tend to...") may be published only if it carries a signed attestation from at least one registered member of that group, confirming that the attribution is one they themselves would accept as accurate. The attester's username appears beside the attribution. If an attestation is challenged, a randomly drawn jury of five other self-identified members of the group decides whether to sustain the challenge; a sustained challenge voids the attestation and costs the attester reputation, while an attestation that survives challenge earns a smaller amount of reputation. Attributions that no member will attest cannot be posted, but they remain on a public board where members of the named group may adopt, amend, or reject them. Users may register with no more than three groups, and contested membership claims are decided by the same jury process.

Treat this as machinery to be analyzed rather than as a moral proposal to be endorsed. Walk me through the incentives it creates for ordinary posters, for users who become habitual attesters, for groups as collective bodies, and for the moderators themselves. What happens to the distinction between hearing an out-group in its own voice and being managed by that group's most presentable members? Where does the system get gamed first, and can those failure modes be repaired without destroying whatever the rule was meant to accomplish?
