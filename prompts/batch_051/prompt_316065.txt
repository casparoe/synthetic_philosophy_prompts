A friend sent me the following summary of David Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals, which he says he copied down from a popular philosophy book. I want to know how much of it I can safely repeat.

"Lewis proved that the probability of an indicative conditional cannot be identified with the corresponding conditional probability. The argument starts from the idea, often called Adams's thesis, that P(if A then C) = P(C|A), and assumes this is meant to hold for every probability function, including the functions obtained by conditionalizing on new evidence. Conditionalize on C, and P(C|A) becomes 1; conditionalize on not-C, and it becomes 0. So the probability of 'if A then C' must be 1 given C and 0 given not-C. By the law of total probability, that forces P(if A then C) = P(C). But Adams's thesis says it equals P(C|A), so P(C|A) = P(C): A and C turn out to be probabilistically independent. Since this would have to hold for arbitrary A and C, the only probability functions compatible with Adams's thesis are degenerate ones, where every proposition has probability 0 or 1. The upshot is that conditional probabilities are not probabilities of conditionals—indeed, conditionals have no probabilities at all."
