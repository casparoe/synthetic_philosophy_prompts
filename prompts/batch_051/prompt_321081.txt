I'm a novelist — historical, mostly Bronze Age Mediterranean, though I never trained in archaeology — and I've picked up enough over the years that my writing group has asked me to run a session on how archaeologists actually go from an object to a claim about past beliefs, rituals, or institutions. They need this because half of them keep writing scenes where someone digs up a little clay figure and immediately announces it's a goddess. So the audience is a dozen or so working novelists, smart and humanities-trained, no fieldwork, and deeply in love with the word "ritual." I get about forty-five minutes.

Don't explain the archaeology to me; plan how I should teach it. I want the order of ideas such that by the end they can feel, almost physically, the difference between what an object lets you assert and what it doesn't. What has to come first? (I suspect it's something like "objects don't carry their meaning around inside them," but I'm not sure that's actually first or that I've even got the framing right.) What one concrete find should carry the whole session, and what does it need going for it and what must it not have (I'd rather stay away from burial/grave stuff — feels like a crutch, and the moment grave goods appear they'll decide it's religion and stop reasoning). What misconceptions should I expect in what order? I know they'll say "ritual object" the moment a thing looks wierd, they'll assume form just is function, and they'll probably think more decoration means more sacred — but tell me where in the sequence to head each of those off, and what subtler wrong idea they'll form halfway through because of something I said. What should I refuse to explain at this level (stratigraphy? post-processualism?) and how do I say no without making it sound like I'm dodging the fun part? And give me an end-of-session check — a quick exercise or a single question — that would show me they've actually absorbed the inferential discipline and not just learned the word "assemblage."

Be committed about this: pick the order, pick the example, pick two or three misconceptions to spend real time on, and defend each choice — dont lay out the options for me.
