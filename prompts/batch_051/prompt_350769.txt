At my university's research ethics committee we are deadlocked over a proposal from a behavioural-economics group testing self-binding commitment contracts for smoking cessation. Participants recruited at the campus stop-smoking clinic would deposit £200 at enrolment and then, for six weeks, submit a daily breath sample through a phone-linked monitor; each positive or missed reading automatically forfeits £20 to the university hardship fund. The applicants argue that the design is built around hyperbolic discounting: it lets the present self bind the future self it can already anticipate, enforcing a preference the participant endorses rather than overriding it. Some members see a low-risk intervention that respects autonomy. Others reply that the consenting self is not the self that will feel the fine; that the future self, mid-craving, will experience it as an external sanction it never chose; and that any obvious safeguard, such as allowing participants to opt out once enrolled, would destroy the very commitment they joined the study to obtain. Draft the committee's decision memo: approve, reject, or approve with conditions. It should state a clear recommendation and deal honestly with the strongest consideration on the losing side.
