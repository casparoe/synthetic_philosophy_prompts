For a while now I have been noticing a pattern in how people — including me, on my more unreflective days — react to necessary, foreseen death depending on who is doing the deciding, and I cannot tell whether the pattern tracks something principled or just a superstition about politics.

Consider two kinds of cases. In the first, a clinician or incident commander faces a forced choice in which the right act will save several lives but certainly cost one: a triage doctor in a mass-casualty event sends an incoming patient away to die because treating him would cost two or three others; a fire chief decides which wing of a burning building to search first, knowing the delay will cost someone in the other wing their life. In these cases we do not expect the decision-maker to feel guilty. Grief and regret, yes; but if the triage doctor says that she did the right thing and does not feel she has done anything wrong, we take that as sound moral hygiene, not a failure of conscience. In the second kind of case, the same structure appears in politics. A prime minister orders a strike on a weapons cache hidden in a building where a child is almost certainly present, because allowing the cache to be moved will mean a massacre elsewhere; the strike kills the child and prevents the massacre. I, and I think most people, judge that she should have ordered the strike, and also that she ought to feel she has done something wrong — that she is in some way stained by it. If she later says she did the right thing and does not feel she has done anything wrong, we find that chilling. That is the asymmetry I want examined: necessary, foreseen lethal harm in a professional role such as medicine or emergency response leaves no moral remainder, while the structurally similar political act is expected to dirty the agent, so that the politician with clean hands strikes us as worse, not better.

I would like the strongest principled justification you can find for treating politics as special in this way — or, if there is none, a clear argument that the asymmetry should be given up in one direction or the other. In doing so, please test any candidate principle against nearby cases: a general ordering a diversionary attack expected to kill a platoon to save the main force; a police commander ordering a raid on a hostage-taker where a hostage may be killed; a public-health minister imposing an economic shutdown that will predictably cause some deaths through poverty; and an aid-agency director who signs off on a delivery route known to put a driver at risk. Please also address the obvious replies — that the doctor merely foresees or allows the death while the leader causes it, that consent and professional norms change the case, that the leader acts for a political community in a way the doctor does not — and say whether any of these, rather than politics as such, does the real work. On any interpretive question about what the problem of dirty hands does and does not claim, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on the topic, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. If the best justification turns out to force revision of the initial asymmetry — for instance, that the doctors should bear guilt after all, or that the leader should not — say so outright.
