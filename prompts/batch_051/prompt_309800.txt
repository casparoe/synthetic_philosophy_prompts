I have been trying to pin down a friend's views about which unobservable things in the history of science were real, partly because of the old worry that the history of science is full of successful theories that turned out to be false. She is firm that phlogiston was not real, that caloric was not real, and that the luminiferous ether was not real. She is just as firm that the chemical atoms of the nineteenth century were real, that electrons are real, that the gene of classical genetics is real, and that neutrinos are real. She also says atoms and genes count as real even though they turned out not to be the way their first defenders pictured them, so her principle is clearly not that the original theory had to be exactly right.

The new case I want to ask her about is dark matter. It has never been directly detected, but cosmologists treat it as indispensable for explaining how galaxies rotate and hold together, and they say its distribution predicted the pattern of temperature fluctuations in the cosmic microwave background and the large-scale clustering of galaxies, which later observations matched. If she is being consistent with her seven earlier verdicts, what should I expect her to say about dark matter: real, not real, or not yet decidable? What rule is she most likely running, and what other rules fit her earlier verdicts just as well and give a different answer on dark matter? Which rule do you think is her actual one, and why? Finally, which further case would best separate the rules you have identified — I have considered asking her about the Higgs field, gravitational waves, the multiverse, or the field that is supposed to have driven early cosmic inflation — and what would each rule say about that case?
