I have spent the past few months helping a family member think through a medical decision in which the evidence is thin, mixed, and slow-moving, and I have been struck by something that resists the usual probabilistic framing. Two situations can seem to support the same probability for the same medical claim — say, a 60 per cent chance that a treatment will help — and yet they strike me as entirely different guides to action. When the probability rests on one plausible mechanism and an unpublished case series, I want to move cautiously; when it rests on several independent trials with broadly consistent findings, I treat the same number as far more robust. This has led me to Keynes's view that the weight of evidence is something over and above the probability it supports.

My question concerns the proposition below, and I would like your credence in it together with the decomposition that produces it. My own credence is currently around 0.7, though I am not confident that the proposition is well posed.

Proposition: Keynes was substantially right that, for a wide class of practical judgements of the sort I have described, the rational state of uncertainty cannot be fully represented by a single precise probability, and that the weight of the evidence — the quantity, quality, and variety of the relevant evidence available — is a distinct determinant of how far one should rely on a judgement, not already captured by the probability itself, by a range or interval of probabilities, or by a probability plus a measure of how imprecise it is.

I would like you to assign a rough probability to each of the following sub-questions, and then explain how they combine to produce your overall credence in the proposition:

(a) The rational attitude towards such practical claims is best understood as a degree of belief grounded in the evidential relation between evidence and claim, rather than as a frequency or as a merely subjective willingness to bet.

(b) Even granting (a), there are practically important cases in which the rational attitude is genuinely non-numerical or only partially ordered, so that no single precise probability, and no interval with precise bounds, captures it fully.

(c) Even granting (b), the weight of evidence is a real further dimension: two agents can hold the same probability while one is rationally entitled to rely on it more, and this is not merely a difference in how aware they are of imprecision.

(d) This further dimension, if real, does normative work for an actual decision-maker: it cannot be absorbed without loss into a sufficiently sophisticated representation involving only probabilities and utilities.

Please give your overall credence in the proposition, state how (a)–(d) combine to produce it, and identify which of (a)–(d) your number is most sensitive to. If your number differs from mine, please tell me which sub-question the difference most likely turns on. Then tell me what evidence or argument would move your number the most. I am less interested in the bare number than in seeing where it is fragile, so please be explicit about the weakest sub-question. If you think the proposition is confused, or that my sub-questions are drawn in the wrong place, I would rather you said so plainly than produce a number for the sake of one.
