Question: Distinguish the inside view from the outside view and show how the distinction helps explain the planning fallacy. Then evaluate the following claim: when a high-stakes, one-off decision is being made, epistemic modesty requires the decision-maker to give lexical priority to the outside view.

Student answer: The inside view treats the project or decision at hand as unique and builds a forecast from its own features: its plan, resources, personnel, and the obstacles one can foresee. The outside view, which Tetlock introduced, sets those details aside and treats the case as an instance of a reference class of similar cases, basing the prediction on how that class turned out in the past. The planning fallacy illustrates the contrast: people estimate how long their own work will take by assembling the steps in the project and imagining completing them, while the relevant base rate from other people's similar projects is far more pessimistic; the persistent optimism of the inside view explains the mistake. Epistemic modesty is the recognition that one's own judgement is fallible and one's private information is limited. Since the outside view embodies information from many other people and cases, modesty demands deference to it. In a high-stakes, one-off decision, the cost of overconfidence is greater, so the case-specific information of the inside view must never override the outside view. Therefore, epistemic modesty requires giving the outside view lexical priority: whenever the two views conflict, the decision-maker should follow the outside view regardless of how strong the inside information appears to be.
