I keep noticing an asymmetry in how we treat space and time that seems hard to defend once it is pointed out. You do not think that a distant city is unreal until you travel there; you assume it exists now, at the same time you are reading this, merely somewhere else. Yet in our ordinary talk and in much of our philosophizing we treat the future as not yet real and the past as gone, with only the present genuinely existing. Special relativity makes this asymmetry uncomfortable, because it removes any privileged definition of now spanning the universe. For events far enough apart that no signal could pass between them, different inertial frames yield different verdicts about which one happens first, and there is a frame in which they are simultaneous; so there is no objective cosmic present, and whether a distant event is already real or still to come depends partly on how you are moving. Some people take this to show that past, present, and future are all equally real, and that the felt difference is an illusion. My question is narrower. Before accepting or rejecting that conclusion, I want to know whether the original asymmetry between the spatial case and the temporal case can be justified at all. What is the strongest principle that would let us say that distant places exist now while future events do not, and does it survive the case of an event that is future in my frame but simultaneous with me in some other frame? Please answer in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the way one would write a letter.
