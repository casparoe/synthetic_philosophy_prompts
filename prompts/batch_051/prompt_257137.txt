I'm giving a talk next month at a hospital grand rounds defending a regulated market in live kidney donation, and I want too walk in knowing exactly what the Q&A will do to me. The room will be mostly transplant nephrologists and surgeons, plus a couple of bioethicists from the hospital's ethics committee and maybe one health economist if he shows up. My position, roughly, is this: the current altruistic-donation system isn't keeping pace with the waiting list, and the black market is the worst of all worlds, so we should trial a single-state-agency model — one government buyer purchases kidneys at a fixed price, allocates them purely by medical need, and gives donors lifetime follow-up, an insurance top-up, and a fixed payment. No private buyers, no brokers, no haggling over price. I've got a decent sketch of the argument, but I know this audience sees donor complications up close and distrusts economists' supply curves; the department chair already warned me the surgeons hate the word "compensation" and will insist on calling it "reimbursement," which I think tells me exactly where their heads are at.

Give me a prep sheet: the six or seven objections I'll actually get, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way a transplant surgeon or an ethics-committee member would actually put them. For each: what I should concede on the spot, what I should hold, and a two-minute reply, plus a note on where the reply is softest. Include the question I'm praying nobody asks. And skip the generic undergrad ones — I don't need "what stops a rich guy from buying a kidney from a broke college student"; I need the version that comes from someone who manages a deceased-donor list every week and has watched live donors walk back in with hypertension and regret. If there's a point where their objection has real force and I should just say "yes, that's the cost we need to weigh," tell me where that is.
