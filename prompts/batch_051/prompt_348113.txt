Lately I've been giving probabilities as ranges instead of points, but only for messy real-world questions — if you ask me whether the bill passes, I won't say 40%, I'll say 25 to 55%. For coin flips or known-urn draws I'm happy to give a point. My rationale is that point probabilities sound precise in a way real-world evidence can't support: they paper over the difference between risk (known odds) and ambiguity (not knowing the relevant urn at all). But a friend says my policy mostly protects me from being scored, since a range is rarely wrong unless the event happens outside it, and since when I actually decide I pick an implicit point anyway. So I get the priviledge of imprecision without paying for it in action.

Is this policy defensible as a response to genuine ambiguity, or is it a sophisticated way of protecting my beliefs from challenge? Is there an honest version of it — say, one where I commit to how I'd bet from the interval, or specify in advance what evidence would narrow it — that keeps the benefit without the unfalsifiability? And how would I tell from the inside whether my range widths track real ambiguity rather than just tracking how much I want to avoid being pinned down?

Also: where you can, could you seperate what's actually settled among people working on imprecise probability from what's still genuinely contested, and not present one camp as the consensus?
