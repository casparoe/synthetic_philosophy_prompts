I'm a PhD student in philosophy of language, and I've been sitting in on a pragmatics seminar where the professor runs it like a common-law court: every week she rules on a batch of made-up speech-act cases from the front of the room and then absolutely refuses to state the principle she's applying. I can't tell whether shes an Austinian about felicity conditions, a Gricean about communicative intentions, or something even weirder, and the cases are starting to get under my skin. Her track record so far:

- Actor says "I do" in the wedding scene of a play: no marriage, because the stage isn't the right context for that bit of procedure.
- Someone says "I promise to pay you back Friday" with their fingers crossed behind their back: a promise was made anyway, since sincerity doesn't enter into it.
- Someone says "I bet you five dollars it'll rain" to a dog: no bet, because there's no uptake.
- Drunk wedding guest grabs the mic and says "I now pronounce you husband and wife": no marriage, they lack the authority.
- Four-year-old solemnly says "I promise I'll eat my vegetables": no promise, on capacity grounds.
- Someone clicks "I agree" on a forty-page terms-of-service box without reading it: a real agreement nonetheless.
- Chairperson says "I call this meeting to order" in a completely empty room: the meeting is open, because hearing isn't needed for that kind of declaration.

Next week's case is the one we're meant to argue about before she rules. A calls B, gets B's voicemail, and leaves a message: "I promise to repay you the $200 by Friday." The message saves fine. B's phone then breaks, so B never plays it and only learns what A said later from a mutual friend. Did A promise?

Predict how she'll rule, spell out the candidate rules that would each unify her prior verdicts, say which one you'd bet on and why, and tell me what further case—some tweak of the voicemail or a totally different speech act—would cleanly separate the live options.
