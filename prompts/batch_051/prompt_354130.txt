I am trying to work out, not from theory but from my ordinary moral reactions to cases, whether a bigger population—of a family, a city, or a species—is ever in itself better, and my reactions seem to pull in two directions. (1) A couple with two flourishing children are deciding whether to have a third. The third child would have a good life, but the two existing children would get somewhat less attention and the household would have somewhat less money, so each member would be slightly less well off than before, while the family's total well-being would be higher with three than with two. I think it would be better if they had the third child. (2) A well-run city of 500,000 grows to 750,000. Average well-being declines a little through crowding and longer commutes, almost everyone remains reasonably well off, and the city's total well-being rises. I think the larger city is the worse city. (3) A conservation program establishes a new breeding population of a songbird in a protected forest; the birds live short, watchful lives that are, I take it, on balance modestly positive. I think the extra thousands of songbirds make the world better, and I do not much care that average bird well-being is lower than it would be in a smaller, less crowded population. (4) Yet if I imagine filling an empty habitat with a short-lived invertebrate whose members have lives only barely worth living, I do not think any number of them, however vast, could outweigh one rich human life—and I am not even sure their existence is a good thing at all. (5) When I compare two amateur choirs, one small and warm with very high average enjoyment, the other much larger but more anonymous and slightly less rewarding per member, I prefer the smaller choir even though the larger one contains more total enjoyment and fellowship.

These verdicts seem to cry out for a single principle—perhaps something about additional lives mattering little below a threshold, or average mattering within groups that already exist while new good lives matter from a baseline—but I cannot make it precise. Is there a principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures all or most of these verdicts, and which of them, if any, must I give up? Please state the principle exactly, say what it predicts about a case I have not settled—a catastrophe that painlessly and unexpectedly removes a quarter of a population while leaving every survivor exactly as well off individually—and be explicit about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate.
