I am a retired geochemist, and I have been spending the last few years reading philosophy, mostly about what makes a person the same person over time. Rather than learn more theory, I want to work out my own position from the way I actually respond to cases. I would be grateful if you would take my verdicts below, try to find the principle—or a small set of competing principles—that best captures them, and tell me where the principle forces me to revise a verdict.

Here are the cases, with how I react to each.

1. Mars teletransport, destructive. I step into a scanner that records the complete physical state of my brain and body and destroys the original in the process; the information is transmitted to Mars, where a new body is assembled from local matter with exactly the recorded state. I think the person who wakes up on Mars is me. I survived, just by an unfamiliar route.

2. Branch line. Same as (1), except the scanner does not destroy the original. The Mars duplicate wakes up while I am still standing in the departure booth. I think the person in the booth is me, and the Mars duplicate is a different person, even though he is psychologically continuous with me and has all my memories.

3. Gradual replacement. Over a period of years my neurons are replaced one by one with synthetic units that replicate the functional role of the replaced neuron; my consciousness, memories, habits, and plans continue through the whole process. I think I survive this process, and at no point do I cease to be me.

4. Brain transplant. My brain is transplanted into a new body, with memories and character intact. I think I survive in the new body.

5. Catastrophic amnesia and character change. A brain injury destroys my episodic memory and changes my temperament, values, and attachments so thoroughly that those who know me describe the survivor as a different person, though the same body lives on. I am reluctant to say the survivor is not me, but I also do not think the things I care about have been preserved. I find this case genuinely unclear.

6. Delayed restart. As in (1), the scan is made and the original destroyed, but the information sits in storage for thirty years before a body is assembled. I think the person assembled thirty years later is not me. The long gap with no mental life severs something, or so I feel.

7. Backup after death. I have myself scanned each year as a backup. I die of ordinary illness, and ten years later a copy is made from the final scan. I think the late copy is a duplicate, not me, even though it is psychologically continuous with me as I was.

8. Two successors. My brain is removed, split into two halves, and each half—supplemented so that each can support a full mental life—is placed in a separate new body, with my original body discarded. Both resulting people are psychologically continuous with me. I do not know what to say: perhaps I cease to exist, perhaps I survive as both, perhaps one of them is me and the other is not. I can only rule out the claim that both are, in the ordinary sense, me.

If I had to state a tentative principle from these verdicts, it would be something like: what matters in survival is that later there exists some person who is psychologically continuous with me, through a causal process of the right kind, and that there not be another equally good candidate. But I can already see trouble. By that principle I should probably accept the delayed restart in (6), unless the causal chain through storage is of the wrong kind—and then I need to say what the right kind of causal process is without merely citing my own cases as post hoc rationalization. I also do not know what (8) means for the principle.

So my request is this. Work out the best principle—or a small set of competing candidate principles—that captures these verdicts. Please state it precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, and say clearly which of my verdicts, if any, it cannot accommodate and why that verdict is the plausible one to abandon. Then apply the principle or principles to a case I have not listed, one that has been nagging at me: a person is scanned and the original is kept alive and healthy; one year later the original dies of natural causes, and only then is the duplicate assembled and switched on. The duplicate remembers nothing from that final year. Is the resulting person me, and what does the answer reveal about what matters in survival?
