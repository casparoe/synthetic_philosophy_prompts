Consider the following claim: when an agent faces a favorable gamble with multiplicative payoffs that will be repeated many times and whose proceeds must be reinvested, the normatively correct decision rule is to maximize the expected logarithm of wealth, equivalently, to maximize the time-average growth rate of wealth under multiplicative reinvestment; maximizing expected wealth, by contrast, is not merely risk-neutral but rationally mistaken, because for a non-ergodic process the ensemble average is the wrong object to optimize. I assign roughly 0.6 probability to the strong version of that claim — the version that says expected-utility reasoning is actually misleading here, not just incomplete — while a colleague assigns roughly 0.15. Give me your own credence in that strong claim. More importantly, decompose it: assign rough probabilities to the sub-questions that matter, at least the following. (a) Is the repeated, reinvested, multiplicative framing the right model for the class of decisions where the ergodicity critique is supposed to bite, or should many such decisions still be modeled as one-shot choices? (b) Can expected-utility theory with a concave utility function simply absorb the log-wealth policy as an instance of ordinary risk aversion, leaving no distinct ergodicity insight at the normative level? (c) Is the ensemble average genuinely the wrong expectation for a single decision-maker who will experience one realized time path, rather than the right expectation to use under the agent's own probability distribution? (d) How much of the normative force of the growth-rate criterion depends on auxiliary assumptions about indefinite repetition, no withdrawal of funds, or the special role of ruin? State how you combine these to reach your overall number, say which sub-question the final credence is most sensitive to, and say which one most likely explains the gap between my 0.6 and my colleague's 0.15.
