I keep catching myself doing something weird with probability, and I want to know if it's actually defensible or just self-serving. When I estimate the odds my startup survives, I don't use the general startup base rate. I pick a much narrower reference class — my exact sector, founders who came from a big tech company, companies that went through the accelerator I got into — and the number comes out looking fine. But when a friend says he's opening a restaurant, I immediately reach for the broad base rate: most restaurants fail withing a few years. Same pattern in the other direction: the other day I got annoyed that my car insurer priced me by the broad class of drivers in my zip code instead of by my own clean record. So I want the narrow class when it flatters me, and the broad class when that flatters me. Is there a principle that actually justifies switching like that? If the narrow class is legitimate because it uses more specific information about a case, shouldn't I also use the specific facts about my friend's restaurant instead of the headline failure rate? And if the broad class is fair for the insurer, why is it unfair when I judge my friend? Give me the best justification you can for treating these cases differently, test it against a few nearby cases, and tell me whether I should keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or give it up.
