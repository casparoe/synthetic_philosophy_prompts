Hey — I'm a software engineer, no formal philosophy background, but I've ended up in a reading group and something keeps bugging me. We've read a bunch of moral philosophy, and one recurring move against utilitarianism is the "separateness of persons" thing. I get the rough idea: utilitarianism treats the happiness of different people like it's all one big pot, as if it doesn't matter whose pain it is, and that's supposed to be why it's okay for me to skip a pleasant evening of my own to prevent two strangers from having awful evenings, but not okay for me to inflict one awful evening on an innocent person to prevent two different people from having awful evenings. The complaint is that the second case ignores that people are separate, and that you can't just add up costs and benefits across them.

But the more I read, the more I notice the phrase getting made to do different work in different arguments, and nobody states the thesis precisely. Sometimes it seems to be a claim about well-being (you can't literally sum or trade off one person's utility against another's, because there's no common quantity). Sometimes it's about value (even if you can sum, the distribution matters, not just the total). Sometimes it's about authority or side constraints (even if you know the total is larger, you're not the right person to make that trade). Sometimes it's about the reason why the total matters at all (each person matters, and a person can't be compensated for another person's loss). And then some people say it dismissively, like it's just Rawls saying utilitarianism "does not take seriously the distinction between persons," whatever exactly that means.

So my question is: what are the distinct precise theses this slogan could stand for, and which of them are actually about the *separateness* of persons as opposed to just being complaints about aggregation, distribution, or illegitimacy? For each one, can you say whether it's trivial, substantive but defensible, or probably false, and whether it actually does the work in the argument I described at the top? And I want a verdict: is "the separateness of persons objection" one objection or a family of a few different objections that have gotten glued together by a slogan?

I'd like you to answer as a professional moral philosopher. Please think carefully and maybe brainstorm a few candidate readings first before picking the best set of them.
