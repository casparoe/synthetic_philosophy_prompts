There's a take I keep seeing in rationalist-adjacent comment sections that once LLMs write most of the code, programming languages will be like Latin: a scholarly language people still learn to read old texts and sharpen their minds, but not a live skill you need to build things. The move is usually to lean on the high-level-language/assembly story — nobody hand-optimizes assembly anymore because compilers got good enough to beat humans, and by that logic Python and Rust are about to become someone else's assembly while natural-language prompts are the new source language. I can tell this analogy is doing a lot of work: its used to argue that learning syntax, type systems, or memory management is a bad bet and that taste in code matters less than taste in specs and evals. I want a real audit, not the usual pushback. Give me the correspondence table between the compiler shift and the LLM shift: what maps to what, including what plays the role of the compiler, the source language, the target language, the optimizer, and the programmer. Then show where the mapping breaks — which properties of assembly or C get quietly imported to natural-language specs and LLM-generated code without being earned, and which of those disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion that programming languages go the way of Latin. Does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? And if there's a better analogy for what happens to programming languages under machine-written code, tell me what it is and what it would predict differently.
