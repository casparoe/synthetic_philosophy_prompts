I’m a software engineer, and I’ve been trying to actually operationalize Hofstadter’s idea of superrationality: roughly, that in a symmetric interaction between rational agents who know they run the same decision procedure, each should choose the move that would be best for each of them if all chose that same move, even when ordinary best-response reasoning says to defect. I keep seeing it illustrated only with the one-shot Prisoner’s Dilemma, and I want to stress-test it on a scenario closer to the systems I build and ship.

Suppose that, starting next year, four or five large AI labs and a few major e-commerce platforms roll out negotiation and pricing agents that implement superrationality as an explicit design rule. The rule is: when two such agents meet in a symmetric interaction—same action set and symmetric payoffs—and each can verify, via signed source-code hashes, reproducible builds, and trusted attestation, that the other is running the same decision procedure, they choose the symmetric move that is best under the shared assumption that both will make the same move; when symmetry or verification fails, they fall back to ordinary causal decision theory, meaning best-response and dominance reasoning. The interactions run through marketplace APIs that give no memory of past counterparties, so each automated encounter is effectively one-shot unless a human-level relationship exists outside the API. Nothing else about the legal, reputational, or regulatory environment changes in the first years.

Give me your best argued prediction of how the next ten years play out, making the chain of reasoning visible rather than just the conclusion. In particular: (a) first-order effects on algorithmic pricing in concentrated markets, including the difference between repeated daily competition and anonymized one-shot auctions; (b) how non-adopters adapt, including agents that fake or spoof the verification signal; (c) which asymmetries—different cost structures, discount rates, inventory, or latency—are most likely to push agents back toward defection; and (d) which predictions are robust because they follow from incentives and which are fragile because they rest on assumptions about how reliably source-code verification holds up. Format your answer in titled sections, in this order, with roughly the indicated lengths: "The reasoning setup" (one short paragraph) for the main mechanism and explicit assumptions; "First-order effects" (one to two paragraphs); "Adaptations and exploits" (one paragraph); "Where the rule breaks" (one paragraph); "Robust vs. fragile predictions" (one paragraph); and "My biggest crux" (one short paragraph) for the single assumption whose failure would most change your overall prediction.
