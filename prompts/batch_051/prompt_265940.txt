I am a doctoral student in early modern philosophy, and I have just been handed the task of leading a seminar on a book chapter about the legacy of Cartesianism. The chapter summarises Descartes' position on animal minds roughly as follows. Descartes held the beast-machine doctrine: nonhuman animals are mechanisms whose behaviour can be explained entirely by the disposition of their organs, without attributing to them a soul or any conscious states. His decisive argument, on the chapter's telling, was that language is the only outward sign of thought; since no animal arranges words or signs creatively so as to express meaning, animals cannot think. From this Descartes is said to have concluded that animals feel nothing whatsoever, and that their cries when injured are no more evidence of pain than the noises made by a clock. The chapter adds that this view was a straightforward consequence of Descartes' substance dualism, and that later Cartesians merely drew out its natural application to the practice of vivisection.

I am now unsure how much of this I can rely on when I present the material. I would like a careful accuracy audit of the chapter's summary. Going roughly sentence by sentence, could you say which parts a specialist would accept as a fair reading of the relevant stretches of the Discourse and the late correspondence, which parts are reconstructions presented as if they were Descartes' own claims, and where the chapter subtly overstates or flattens the position? My particular worries are these: whether language was in fact Descartes' only criterion for thought; whether he really denied animals all feeling, as opposed to drawing a sharper distinction between sensation and thought; and whether the beast-machine doctrine genuinely follows from substance dualism rather than from further commitments of Descartes' system. I am not asking for a reading list, just for a precise sorting of what is textual, what is defensible interpretation, and what is distortion.
