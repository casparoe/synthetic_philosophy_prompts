Before anyone builds, sells, deploys, or uses an interactive simulation of a dead person, I want a specification of what an adequate ethical theory in this area must get right. I will call the object at issue a griefbot: an artificial conversational agent trained or designed to imitate a particular deceased person, using that person’s writings, voice recordings, photographs, social media, or other digital traces. The theory’s job is to determine—not by a single intuitive judgment but through a set of adequacy conditions—when creating, offering, continuing, modifying, or deactivating such a system is permissible, impermissible, or required, and which interests a defensible verdict must track.

I keep seeing people argue about this by pointing at one vivid case—a widow comforted by a simulation of her husband, a mother using a bot of her dead child, a company selling chatbots of dead celebrities, a historical figure reconstructed for students—and then moving straight to a general verdict. I want the full specification first: the requirements a theory must meet before any of those verdicts can be trusted.

Please deliver two things.

First, a prose discussion that works through the requirements. For each requirement, give: (a) a one-sentence formulation; (b) the motivating case that reveals why it is a requirement; (c) why the theory cannot remain adequate if it drops or weakens the requirement; (d) whether it is non-negotiable, negotiable, or a demand that only one camp treats as a requirement; and (e) the distinction or distinctions it forbids collapsing. Do not stop at the obvious ones. Make sure the list includes requirements concerning: the dead person’s pre-mortem autonomy and posthumous privacy; the bereaved person’s autonomy, agency, and vulnerability; the distinction between representing a dead person and continuing or replacing them; the distinction between aiding grief and bypassing or prolonging it; the problem of false or fabricated statements attributed to the dead; the difference between private remembrance and public or commercial representation; the interests of third parties such as surviving family members or partners who did not consent; the position of children and other users who may not understand the boundary between simulation and person; rules for continued use when the bereaved wants the bot and others want it deleted; and the duties of commercial actors who operate the system. Treat any empirical claims about grief, attachment, or psychological harm as constraints that must be stated and marked as empirical rather than asserted as free speculation.

Second, give the joint verdict: whether the full set of requirements is jointly satisfiable, or whether apparent tensions in the list are themselves a result. Identify which single requirement does the most work to exclude popular permissive and prohibitive positions, and explain why.
