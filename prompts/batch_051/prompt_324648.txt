I am drafting a novel in which a central character, a lapsed Catholic who has become a strident rationalist, argues with his sister about whether religious belief can be intellectually respectable. In the scene, he draws on the distinction Freud makes in *The Future of an Illusion* between an error and an illusion. As I understand the passage, Freud says that an error is simply a false belief, whereas an illusion is a belief whose power and endurance are explained by the wish it fulfills rather than by its epistemic credentials. An illusion, he allows, can even happen to be true: the girl who believes a prince will marry her may, in the end, be right, but her belief is still an illusion because she holds it because she wishes it, not because she has evidence for it. The character takes this to be a devastating instrument against religion, since religious ideas seem to be sustained by wishes for a protective father, cosmic justice, and survival after death.

I want him to present the following argument, and I want it to feel stronger than a simple genetic fallacy while also visibly overreaching:

1. A belief is rationally discredited if the reason it has a grip on the mind is that it satisfies a deep wish, rather than that it answers to evidence.
2. Religious belief is sustained by such wishes: for a fatherly protector, for the sense that suffering is seen and repaid, for personal survival.
3. Therefore religious belief is rationally discredited.
4. The criterion in (1) cannot be applied only to religion. If it is sound, it must be applied wherever a belief’s staying power comes from its making life bearable: to the conviction that history bends toward justice, that one’s work has meaning beyond itself, that love is not merely a chemical accident, that civilization is a cumulative moral advance.
5. On inspection, many of these secular convictions are also held with the force they have because they answer to wishes for consolation and meaning, not because the evidence for them is especially good.
6. Even the character’s own faith that the universe is intelligible, or that submitting belief to evidence will improve life, may be sustained partly by the wish that the world be orderly and that honesty be safe.
7. Therefore, if the criterion in (1) is sound, it discredits not only religion but most of the rationalist’s own foundational posture.
8. But the character cannot accept that his rationalism is discredited in the same stroke as his sister’s religion.
9. So the argument seems to prove too much.

The scene should allow the sister to expose a mistake in this reasoning without merely accusing the brother of being cold-hearted or bad at identifying evidence. I want to know, for my outline, which premise or step a careful philosophical reader would reject, and what it would cost to reject it. If the problem is an equivocation or a slide between two senses of a phrase, I need to know exactly where the slide occurs and why it looked natural. If the argument is valid and the real question is which premise is least costly to abandon, I need to know which one and what the alternatives are. The novel does not need to include every position by name, but I want the diagnosis to be precise enough that the sister’s reply would feel earned rather than evasive.
