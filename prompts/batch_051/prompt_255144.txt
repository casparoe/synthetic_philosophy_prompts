I run an upper-level philosophy of biology seminar, about 14 juniors and seniors, 80 minutes. I want a card-sorting game that makes them actually feel the "what is an organism?" question falling apart, rather than me telling them it falls apart. The rough setup I have in mind: small groups get a deck of case-cards — a Portuguese man o' war, a huge aspen clone, a lichen, a honeybee colony, a coral colony, a termite colony, an aphid plus its Buchnera endosymbionts, a human plus their gut microbes, a dictyostelid slime mold at the fruiting-body stage, an oak tree, a cat — and they have to place each card into one of three boxes: ONE ORGANISM, MANY ORGANISMS, or REFUSES THE QUESTION / SOMETHING ELSE IS GOING ON. The sorting rule should force them to state a criterion, and then a later round (I don't know exactly how to structure this) should show the criteria pulling apart: something looks clearly one organism by genetic homogeneity but many by physiology, or vice versa, and the "refuses the question" box starts doing real work. What I want them to come away seeing is that "organism" is doing a couple of different jobs at once — roughly, physiological integration and unit of selection — and that holobionts, ant colonies, and aspen clones aren't puzzling merely because people haven't agreed on a definition; they're hard because these criteria genuinely conflict.

Could you write me the full activity: the exact card contents, how to group students, roles within each group, a timeline for the 80 minutes, the rule changes between rounds, and the debrief. The debrief should map specific moments in the game (e.g., the moment someone quietly changes their criterion to keep the man o' war or the aspen clone in ONE) onto the philosophical positions, and it should be honest about what the simulation can't reproduce — especially the fact that the game trades entirely on how the cards are written, so the phrasing of a card can smuggle in a position. No labels from the literature until the debrief, and no lecture up front. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each thing — e.g., students deciding it's "just semantics," or one loud student imposing a definition and everyone else going along.

One thing on the content: keep the settled biological facts clearly seperate from the contested philosophical moves. E.g., that a Portuguese man o' war develops from one zygote and is a colony of zooids, and that honeybee workers don't reproduce, are settled; whether a holobiont is a unit of selection, or whether an ant colony should count as an organism in the full sense, is genuinely contested. Don't run the contested stuff as consensus, and in the debrief flag which parts of the argument are still live.
