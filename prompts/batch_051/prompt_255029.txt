Compare the classical philosophical problem of akrasia, or weakness of will, with the behavioral-economic account of hyperbolic discounting (often described as present bias). The classical puzzle is roughly this: an agent judges that one course of action is best, all things considered, and yet voluntarily does another; how is such action possible at all, and in what sense, if any, is it voluntary or rational? The discounting account explains a neighboring pattern: agents weigh near-term rewards far more heavily than identical rewards further off, so their preferences can reverse as time passes, and they may plan to act one way yet later act another.

Does the discounting account solve the philosophical puzzle, dissolve it, or merely model one of its outward symptoms? In answering, consider at least these pressure points: (i) whether a preference reversal over time is the same phenomenon as acting against one's own present better judgment; (ii) whether the akratic agent's failure is best located in the discount function, in the agent's judgment, or in the connection between judgment and action; and (iii) what each framework must assume about the unity and continuity of the agent over time. Where do the two pictures of agency genuinely conflict, and what would count as evidence for preferring one over the other? Finally, separate what is settled from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers and behavioral economists working on these issues, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
