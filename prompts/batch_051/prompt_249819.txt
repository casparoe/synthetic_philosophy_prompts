I have been developing a view I call the single-currency account of moral judgment, and I would like an honest assessment of it.

By way of background: Moral Foundations Theory holds that moral judgment is built on several distinct foundations—care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, and sanctity/degradation—and that political differences arise because liberals and conservatives draw on these foundations to different degrees. The natural upshot is a kind of symmetry: each side has its own coherent moral psychology, and the psychology itself is neutral about which side is right.

My account rejects that symmetry. I suspect the foundations are not distinct currencies at all; they are different applications of a single currency, namely expected welfare or harm. On my view, care/harm is the base case, the direct small-scale welfare calculation. Fairness/cheating tracks the long-run welfare that depends on stable rules of cooperation. Loyalty/betrayal tracks the welfare of one's group over a longer horizon. Authority/subversion tracks the welfare that comes from coordination under settled hierarchies. Sanctity/degradation tracks very-long-run welfare protected by taboo, on the grounds that at that horizon explicit cost-benefit reasoning is too unreliable. On this account, what looks like a difference in which moral organ is triggered is actually a difference in two parameters: the boundaries of the moral community and the social discount rate applied to future welfare. A conservative and a liberal are running the same machinery with different settings.

If this is right, the psychological finding that left and right emphasize different foundations does not give both sides an equal claim to internally coherent moralities. It shows, instead, that the disagreement is really about whose welfare counts and how heavily future welfare should be discounted. The argumentative work shifts from the question of which foundations one ought to trust to the question of which parameters are justified.

I would like you to do three things. First, is this view already in the literature under a more careful name, and if so, what is the nearest existing position? Second, give me the three strongest objections to the single-currency account, and identify which, if any, are fatal rather than merely costly. Third, tell me whether the view actually helps with the question of who is right, or whether it merely relocates the disagreement from talk of foundations to talk of discount rates and membership—and, if it is a mere relocation, whether that relocation makes the disagreement more tractable or just more obscure.
