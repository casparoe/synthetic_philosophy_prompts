I sit on my county's algorithmic fairness review board, which must clear any automated decision system before a public agency deploys it. The transit authority asks us to authorize a six-month pilot that uses farecard and trip-pattern data to infer each rider's utility function from their observed choices — their revealed preferences — and then schedules personalized off-peak discount offers to maximize total inferred utility. Its proponents argue that utility just is what choice reveals, so the optimizer simply gives riders more of what they already choose and lets the board avoid imposing an external conception of the good. Two board members reply that this is a category error: revealed preference is a consistency condition on choice behavior — a way of asking whether choices could be represented as maximizing some preference ordering — not a measurement of welfare, and choices made under menu constraints, habit, and false beliefs can systematically depart from a person's interests, in which case the optimizer would amplify those distortions while labeling them "preferences." Draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Wednesday's vote. It should recommend approve, reject, or approve with conditions (with any conditions specified), give the board actionable reasons, and engage the strongest version of the losing side's argument rather than settling the dispute by definition.
