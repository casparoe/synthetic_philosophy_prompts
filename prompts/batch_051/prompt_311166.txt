I sit on the acquisitions board of a municipal archive whose collecting policy was adopted in 1979. Its opening clause reads: "The Archives shall collect, preserve, and make available for research the records of residents, organizations, and informal groups that document the social, cultural, and political life of the city. Recognizing that the historical record has always favored the prominent, the Archives shall take affirmative steps to identify and document the experiences of people and communities whose place in the city's history has been neglected." The policy was drafted as a deliberate corrective to silences in the sources, and its framers had in mind minute books, correspondence, leaflets, photographs, and oral histories.

The question before the board is whether that mandate reaches the following case. A tenant-led rent-strike campaign operated in the city for eleven months and left almost no paper. Its organizing, votes, and internal disputes happened in a group chat on a messaging platform; its public announcements went out as "stories" on a social app that deletes them after twenty-four hours; its lists of participating buildings lived in a shared document whose hosting account has since been suspended. The campaign's liaison asks us to preserve what remains: members' screenshots, exported chat logs, cached pages, and whatever can be recovered. But the material rests on servers the archive cannot compel anyone to produce, much of it was deliberately ephemeral, and two former members object to their private messages being archived. The policy predates every one of these features; in 1979 no third party could reach into a family's papers and erase them.

Does the quoted clause extend to this material, or does it stop at the letter and the era of its drafting? I would like a short board memo arguing the question from four angles: the ordinary meaning of the words read today; the purpose of the clause, especially its corrective commitment to document the under-recorded; what its drafters would most likely have said had this case been put to them; and what a reasonable member of the public would take the archive to have promised. Then say which approach should control here and why, and be candid about what that choice commits the archive to concerning custody, consent, and the power of private platforms over what is remembered.
