Bit of a niche one. I've been going down the "what is life?" rabbit hole and I keep seeing the mimivirus story cited as the thing that reset the debate — the giant virus originally mistaken for a bacterium and identified as a virus in 2003 — but no one ever actually runs it through the candidate definitions. I have a cached "viruses aren't alive" take and I want to break the cache, LessWrong-style, by tabooeing the word "alive" and looking at the gears.

You're a philosopher of biology who follows the giant-virus literature closely. Take the mimivirus episode as a real case study and analyze it through the lens of the project of defining life. I know the rough virology, so don't do a recap: mimivirus is about 750 nm, bigger than some bacteria, visible by light microscopy; its genome is ~1.2 Mb and encodes aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases and other translation-associated stuff even though it has no ribosomes and can't make its own proteins; it replicates inside Acanthamoeba; the related mamavirus gets parasitized by a smaller virophage (Sputnik), which is where the virus-catching-a-virus joke came from; and since then there's been a whole zoo of giant viruses. Also don't just give me the standard candidate definitions (metabolism/homeostasis, cellular organization, Darwinian evolution, the NASA-style self-sustaining chemical system capable of evolving) — use them.

For each major definition of life, say exactly where mimivirus lands, what the definition handles well, and what it has to stretch for or quietly ignore about the episode. Then turn the lens around: what does the giant-virus episode actually show about the definitional project? Is it a nice borderline case that confirms viruses really do sit at the edge, is it an anomaly for cell-based definitions that should push us to revise them, or is it evidence that "is X alive?" is the wrong question — that life is a cluster concept or a process rather than a binary property? Before you commit, brainstorm two or three different readings of the episode and then pick the one you'd defend. And please separate the uncontroversial virology from the genuinely contested stuff among philosophers of biology and virologists; don't present one camp's take as settled consensus.
