Over the past year I have been thinking about slow trade-offs—a job whose demands creep upward, a medication with accumulating side effects, a habit that ratchets by imperceptible degrees—and I have landed on a cluster of commitments that each seems right on its own but may not cohere as a package. I would like an audit of the package: which commitments pull against which, which tensions are merely apparent, and what the least costly repair would be.

The commitments are these:

(1) If a single step has a cost too small to be felt—no noticeable difference in energy, comfort, or time—and it yields a benefit I can appreciate, then I prefer the state after the step to the state before it, provided I understand the full sequence of which the step is a part.

(2) Rational preference should form an ordering: if I prefer A to B and B to C, I should prefer A to C. Letting preferences loop makes me a money pump, and I regard that as a decisive objection.

(3) I would refuse a single binding choice that traded my present state directly for the state reached after a long series of such steps, even if that single choice carried exactly the sum of all the small benefits. The end state is simply not worth it.

(4) Whether a course of action is rational should not depend on whether the same total change is presented as one choice or as a long series of choices. Bundling and framing ought to be normatively inert.

(5) There is no sharp threshold between the level of discomfort, fatigue, or lost time I would accept and the level I would not; any line I draw will mark a difference that does not, by itself, matter.

I am not asking for a general discussion of vagueness or transitivity. I want the specific faults in this set: the pairs that are genuinely inconsistent, the pairs that only look inconsistent, which commitment gives way most cheaply, and what that concession costs elsewhere.
