You are a professional philosopher who works on population ethics. Think carefully, and begin by sketching the logical space of positions and the diagnostic questions before you write anything final; do not start by listing named views and then writing a question for each.

I am building a "which population axiology do you actually hold?" quiz for a general-audience philosophy blog, aimed at readers who have never heard of Parfit or of the word "axiology." The quiz should sort readers by what they say about concrete choices involving populations, not by which label they happen to like. It should have eight to ten questions. Each question should be a short, concrete scenario with two or three answer options, and no question should use theory-laden words such as "utility," "welfare," "average," or "total." Readers should be able to answer each question on the strength of the scenario alone, without already knowing which position their answer points toward.

The scoring key should map patterns of answers onto at least the following leaves: total utilitarianism; average utilitarianism; a variable value view; critical-level utilitarianism; a narrow person-affecting view (only people who would exist under all the available choices count); prior-existence utilitarianism; and a negative or asymmetry view. If your questions naturally open a further cell that some named position occupies, include it; if a combination of answers has no standard occupant, mark it "unnamed" and say in one sentence whether that cell is empty because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

I especially want the quiz to probe the fault lines that standard discussions run together, and to make at least these cuts. One question should present, in plain terms, the move that generates Parfit's Repugnant Conclusion: it should ask whether a sufficiently enormous population of lives barely worth living can ever be better than ten billion lives of very high quality. Include a pair of "twin" cases that both agree that adding below-average happy lives is not an improvement, but that differ in whether improving the lives of people who will exist either way counts in the same way as adding genuinely new happy people, so that average views and person-affecting views come apart. Include one question on the asymmetry: whether it is wrong to bring into existence a child whose life will be miserable, and whether it is equally wrong to fail to bring into existence a child whose life would be wonderful. And include one question in which a small number of extraordinarily good lives is set against an astronomically large number of barely positive lives, so that a lexical view has something to say that an additive view rejects.

For each leaf, give one sentence on the most awkward commitment that the view cannot escape. For any two leaves your quiz cannot separate, flag that explicitly and say what an additional question would need to be like for the separation to be made. If this brief rests on a confused premise on my part — for example, if some of the distinctions I have asked you to draw collapse under pressure, or if the quiz format cannot track a view like prior-existence utilitarianism without smuggling in theory-laden phrasing — tell me that plainly and correct the brief, rather than producing a polished quiz that quietly papers over the difficulty.
