I moved here two years ago to direct a small maternal-health programme for an international NGO. This country is not my own, and I am still learning which unwritten rules are ordinary hospitality and which are something else. Our European funder’s compliance policy is absolute: no staff member may give an official a gift, in cash or in kind, worth more than about twenty euros.

Last week the regional health authority told us that our clinic’s licence renewal, pending for eight months, cannot be scheduled until after the holiday season. My deputy, who has lived here all her life, says this is simply how things work and that the way forward is to contribute to the wedding fund of the official who signs licences. The contribution would be public, registered by the family, roughly what a cousin would give, and not tied in any explicit way to a particular decision. The official has not asked for anything for herself; the deputy insists that not giving would be read as a deliberate insult rather than as a position of principle, and that our file would sit at the bottom for years. If I follow the funder’s rule, the programme stalls; if I follow local practice, I violate a rule I signed and, I suspect, a line I can feel but cannot yet articulate.

I do not want legal advice or a cultural anthropology of the region. I want help thinking clearly about whether going along with this would be wrong, and about whether the line my funder draws — a flat monetary threshold with no room for relationship, occasion, or public recognition — is the right line to impose here. Give me a way to reason about which gifts, favours, and relationships are wrong and which are what a decent person does, without retreating into a when-in-Rome reflex and without treating the funder’s rulebook as self-evidently universal. Keep the answer under 400 words and begin with your best judgment.
