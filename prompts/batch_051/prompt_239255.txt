Something's been bugging me about the standard contrast between presidential and parliamentary systems, and I want someone to either justify it or tell me honestly that it's a path-dependent inconsistency we've papered over.

Compare two cases. Case A: a prime minister puts her government's legislative programme to a vote, loses by one, and convention says she resigns or calls an election, because the government has 'lost the confidence of the House.' Case B: an American president stakes his whole agenda on a bill, loses by one in Congress, and nobody says he has lost the confidence of Congress. He stays, governs by veto and executive order, and everyone calls the next two years 'gridlock.'

The same underlying fact — the elected legislature has rejected the executive's programme — collapses the executive in one system and is just Tuesday in the other. I know the standard Bagehot explanation is that the cabinet is a committee of the legislature and therefore lives or dies on its support, whereas a separated executive draws its own tenure from a fixed term. But that restates the difference; it doesn't tell me whether the deeper norm is coherent. Either democratic authorisation means the executive may govern only with continuing legislative support — in which case the fixed-term president looks like unaccountable government between elections — or it doesn't — in which case the parliamentary resignation convention looks like over-identification of the executive with one transient majority.

Which horn would you take? And if you were designing a system from scratch, with the twin requirements of democratic legitimcay and stable government, is this asymmetry something you would build on purpose, reverse, or dissolve — and what would the replacement actually look like, given that you can't have both a fixed term and immediate removal for every lost vote without inviting constant instability?
