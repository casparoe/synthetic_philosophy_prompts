I am a novelist, and I keep a notebook of arguments the way other people keep sketchbooks. Last month two novelists I respect, Jonah and Priya, argued until the hour was late, and I sat to the side writing the exchange down rather than joining it. I could not have said who was right then, and I still cannot. Please adjudicate the argument as it was actually made.

Here is the exchange as I recorded it.

Jonah: "Consider the end of the species. Suppose we learn on reliable evidence that shortly after the last person now alive has died of natural causes, a catastrophe will destroy the human race, and that no presently living person loses a day. Compare that world with one in which the same people live the same lives, but humanity continues for many generations. I grant that the second world contains more people; if those missing generations are a loss, that is a loss of persons, and I will not argue the population question. What I deny is any third loss. Once the deaths are held fixed, the ending of the species adds no further badness. Harm needs a subject, and every subject who exists has the same life in both worlds."

Priya: "But human lives are not equally good in both worlds, even when their events and feelings are the same. I am planning a novel about three generations of lighthouse keepers. Part of what makes the plan worth undertaking is that the novel is addressed to readers I will never meet, some of them not yet born. In the asteroid world, the chain of readers stops with the present generation. I am not claiming that those unborn readers are the harmed parties. I am claiming that the activity of writing itself has a dimension—being entered into a practice that continues—and that dimension is removed in the asteroid world. That loss falls on the lives actually being lived."

Jonah: "If the loss never enters your thoughts, your life goes exactly as it would have gone. So the asteroid world is worse for you only if you know about it, and then the added badness is the distress of the knowledge—a familiar mental harm, and one that belongs to the content of a life, not to the extinction itself. You cannot claim the two worlds differ for existing persons while refusing to say where the difference lives. Either it is in their experience, or it is not in their lives at all."

Priya: "I refuse that alternative. A writer whose novel is plagiarized after her death is wronged even if no one, including her, ever learns of it. The wrong is a relational fact about her work and the world, not a feeling. In the asteroid world, the last generation's writers, teachers, and physicians stand in a relation to their work—they are the final holders of practices that go nowhere—that is worse than their counterparts' relation in the other world. That is a difference in their lives, though it need not surface as an experience."

Jonah: "The plagiarism case has an identifiable agent who violated a duty; the asteroid world has only a mute planet. And your relational fact either means that no future reader benefits, which is the absent-generations point again, or that the writer is related in thought to those absent readers, which is the knowledge point again. You are oscillating between two things your argument was supposed to avoid. A third reading has not so far been stated."

Priya: "Then let me state it. A life is not only its felt texture; it is also the shape it has in a story larger than itself. Being a beginning—an initiator of a practice that others take up—is different from being the final instance of a practice that closes with one. We can see that difference without adding any sentient beneficiary and without pretending that it is a private emotion; biographers discern such differences all the time. Extinction, on the scenario we are considering, makes every human practice the second kind at once. That foreclosure is a loss that lands nowhere in particular and yet lands on everyone, because it changes what all of their strivings amount to."

Jonah: "Then I need to know whether that loss is for the writer or merely for the world. If it is for the writer, it must make her life less good through something a life can contain; a relational property that never touches her experience and never benefits anyone else is, at most, a pleasing symmetry, not a harm. If it is merely for the world—an aesthetic preference for stories that continue—then it has stopped being about why extinction is bad for human beings. I can accept that the symmetry is missing while denying that anything of moral significance goes with it."

Priya: "You keep collapsing a life into its experience. A life goes better when it succeeds at the things that constitute it, and serving as a link in a continuing practice is among those things; it is a success-condition, not a form of felt satisfaction. We judge an otherwise beautiful final chapter by the fact that it leaves the story unresolved; its being last is part of the assessment whether the author intended it or not. Extinction makes every life an unresolved final chapter in a story that was still in motion. If you think that assessment applies only to fictions, then you have a picture of well-being too thin for the lives we actually lead."

The argument broke off there, with neither of them satisfied.

I want a committed verdict, not a menu of interpretations. Go through the exchange in order and say, for each significant move, whether it holds up, and where each speaker is right, wrong, or missing something they need. Do not lay out the possible resolutions and leave the choice to me; take a definite position and defend it. As the very last line of your response, write "Verdict:" followed by a one-sentence statement of your overall judgment about who is right about what.
