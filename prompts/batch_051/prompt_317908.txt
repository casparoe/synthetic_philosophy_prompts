I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've been mulling over an essay idea I would like feedback on. I recently encountered the term "Whig history," which I understand roughly as the habit of writing the past as a progressive movement toward the present, with past figures and ideas judged mainly by how closely they anticipated what we now take to be correct. It struck me that much popular history of computing does exactly this, and I want to write a short piece diagnosing the pattern for a technical audience.

My notes so far run as follows. Histories of programming languages are typically written as if each generation was reaching, imperfectly, toward something like the type-safe, memory-safe languages of today; features that survived are celebrated as prophetic, while languages that embodied coherent but different priorities are treated as mistakes or curiosities. Accounts of software process do the same thing: the movement from waterfall-style planning to agile or continuous delivery is narrated as though the field finally learned how to build software correctly, rather than as a response to changing economic, organizational, and technological conditions. Even broad surveys of systems—batch processing, time-sharing, personal computing, the cloud—read as a natural maturation, with abandoned alternatives mentioned only as side branches. My tentative thesis is that this framing hides contingency and path-dependence beneath a story of inevitable progress, and makes current arrangements look more necessary than they are.

I would welcome comments on several specific points. First, is this a fair application of the term "Whig history," or am I stretching it into something a historian or philosopher would wince at? Second, is the thesis actually interesting, or does it reduce to the banal claim that the past was more complicated than the textbooks suggest? Third, what is the strongest counterargument I should address—for example, that some technical changes really are cumulative improvements in a way the Whig label fails to capture? Finally, can you suggest how to structure the essay so it avoids the opposite mistake of romanticizing every abandoned technology as a lost golden age?
