I have been refining a view I call constitutive choice, and I want a candid check before I put it in a paper. The usual reading of Buridan's ass treats it as a pathological limit case: rational choice theory works whenever some inequality of preference breaks the tie, and the ass merely shows what happens when preferences run out. I think this gets the structure backwards. In important decisions — between careers, between ways of life, between two people one might marry — the options are often not comparable prior to the choice, and part of what is being chosen is the comparison itself: one is deciding not only what to do but what will thereafter count as a reason. I call such cases equilemmas, to separate them from mere indifference, where the options are equal and it does not much matter which way one goes. My central claim is that in equilemmas the preference relation is not prior to choice, as revealed-preference and decision-theoretic frameworks assume, but is partially constituted by it; asking what the agent really prefers before an equilemma is a category mistake, not just an epistemic difficulty. I can see that this runs close to existentialist accounts of radical choice, to work on transformative experience, and to debates about incommensurability, and I suspect the term equilemma already exists. Reconstruct the view in its strongest form, tell me what it borrows from or collapses into, and say exactly where it breaks or stops being distinctive.
