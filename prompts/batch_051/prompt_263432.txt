I keep going back and forth on the identity of indiscernibles. In the form that attracts me, it says that if a and b share every property, then a is b; it promises to rule out bare numerical difference, the idea that two things could differ while differing in no respect at all. That systematic virtue feels real. But I cannot shake the two-sphere universe: a world whose only occupants are two spheres, exactly alike in size, material, mass, and every other intrinsic respect, lying some fixed distance apart. It seems simply obvious that such a world contains two spheres and not one sphere described twice; if anything is possible, that is. So the plain principle appears false. The usual repair is to let relational properties count: each sphere has the property of standing at some distance from another sphere just like it, so they differ after all. But this repair only distinguishes them by building the other sphere into the property, which seems to presuppose exactly the numerical distinctness the principle was meant to illuminate, and it risks converting the principle into the triviality that two things differ by being two. I do not want to be told which side wins. I want a negotiated verdict: is the right move to restrict the principle to qualitative properties and grant that distinctness can outrun quality? Should the intuition about the spheres instead be debunked, and if so by what argument that does not simply assert the principle? Is the relational-property repair a genuine solution or a trivialization, and how would one tell? Where, in all this, is the load-bearing choice?
