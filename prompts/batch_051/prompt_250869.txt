I don't have any formal training in this; I've just been keeping a notebook of examples and trying to get the requirements straight before I read any more. The question is about self-knowledge — specifically, how much of your own mental life you are always in a position to know. Normally it seems obvious: if I'm cold, I can tell that I'm cold; if I'm angry, I can tell that I'm angry. But I've also seen cases where someone is in a state and doesn't know it — a person who is exhausted but sincerely insists he's fine, or someone who is angry but won't admit it even to himself. And there's a third kind of case that bothers me most: knowing something without knowing that I know it. I've had the experience of being sure I didn't know an answer, then hearing myself give the right one; it felt as if I knew it all along but couldn't tell that I knew it. So I've started to suspect there's a difference between being in a state and being in a position to know you're in it, and between knowing and knowing that you know.

The puzzle I want the specification for is this. Take a state that comes in degrees, like feeling cold. If you move gradually from feeling cold to not feeling cold, is there a last moment at which you feel cold and could know that you do? Saying there's a sharp cutoff seems wrong, but saying you could be cold and not even in a position to know it seems wrong too. I suspect the requirements for a theory of self-knowledge pull against each other here — that you can't have all of: if you're in a mental state you're in a position to know it; knowing requires that you couldn't easily have been wrong; and states like this have no exact boundary. What I'd like from you is the full list of desiderata: what any adequate theory of self-knowledge has to deliver, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. Say which requirements are non-negotiable and which are only one camp's preference, and then tell me which subset of the list cannot be jointly satisfied, and exactly why the conflict is forced. I care more about the criteria and the conflict than about any particular theory; don't worry about citing people or texts.
