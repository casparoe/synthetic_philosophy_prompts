Evolutionary biology has spent more than half a century arguing about the right level at which to represent natural selection, and the argument has not converged. The gene's-eye view associated with Williams and Dawkins holds that the gene is the unit that persists across generations, accumulates adaptations, and is the right point of view from which to explain them; organisms matter, but as ephemeral vehicles built by selection. Opposing it, the multilevel selection tradition associated with Price, Wilson, and Sober treats group selection as a real causal process that can sustain traits which within-group selection would eliminate, and it maintains that the gene's-eye perspective obscures real between-group causes. Both sides accept the same core population genetics, and both can write down the Price equation or Hamilton's rule. For simple additive traits the two formalisms look like rearrangements of the same covariance; how far the equivalence extends once interactions become non-additive is exactly where some of the recent technical fights have broken out. Around fifteen years ago a prominent group argued that inclusive fitness has serious mathematical limitations, and inclusive-fitness theorists replied that the critique depended on an unrepresentatively narrow definition of inclusive fitness. The two camps seem to be using the same mathematics and rehearsing the same objections, and neither moves.

My working hypothesis is that the debate is really three debates fused together: a partly verbal dispute about how to define a unit of selection and what makes one level of selection more real than another; a genuinely empirical dispute about how often between-group selection is strong enough, relative to within-group selection, to matter in natural populations; and a philosophical dispute about what one of two equivalent-looking formalisms has to add before it counts as the better explanation. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. Which of those components is doing the real work? Are some of them artifacts of the others, or are the two sides simply answering different questions? And on the basis of that diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a theorem, a kind of empirical data, a sharper distinction, or the conclusion that the original question was ill-posed?
