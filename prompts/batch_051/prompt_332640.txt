A problem that keeps coming up in my reporting on Bayesian epistemology is the Judy Benjamin problem. The setup is this. A paratrooper, Judy Benjamin, has been dropped at night into a territory divided into four sectors: Red Headquarters, Red Second Company, Blue Headquarters, and Blue Second Company. She knows the geography and has a well-defined prior probability distribution over her location, but she cannot tell which sector she is in. A radio message then reaches her; she regards the source as completely reliable, and she understands the message as imposing exactly one new constraint: if she is somewhere in Red territory, then her probability of being in the Red Headquarters sector, conditional on being somewhere in Red territory, is 0.8. The message does not tell her whether she is in Red territory, and it does not directly constrain any of her unconditional probabilities.

Please argue that there is no uniquely rational posterior distribution for Judy Benjamin to adopt in response to this message. The core of the argument should be that a bare conditional-probability constraint underdetermines a probability distribution, and that every concrete rule for turning such a constraint into a posterior — for example, leaving her unconditional probability of being in Red territory unchanged, or instead making the minimal information-theoretic adjustment among all distributions satisfying the constraint — amounts to a substantive interpretation of what the message means, an interpretation that the message itself does not force on her. Defend the stronger claim that this is not a computational limitation but a principled gap in the Bayesian treatment of evidence that arrives in conditional form.
