I'm giving a colloquium talk defending a qualified version of Milton Friedman's F-twist—the view that the realism of a model's assumptions matters little to its scientific value and that predictive success is the primary standard for judging a model. My qualified thesis is this: predictive accuracy is the principal but not the sole epistemic virtue of a scientific model, and a false assumption should be objected to only when its falsity is doing the predictive work in the wrong way, by making the model accurate for the wrong reasons or by blocking the model's improvement, rather than because false assumptions are intrinsically disqualifying. The audience is mostly philosophers of biology and climate science who think of models as causal-explanatory and intervention-guiding tools, plus two economists broadly sympathetic to Friedman and one philosopher of physics who holds that idealizations are admissible only when they can in principle be removed. Several members work on robustness analysis and already believe that agreement across a family of false models can warrant trust in a result. Draft a Q&A prep sheet for me: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience, phrased the way they would raise them, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft. Include what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and the one question I'm hoping nobody asks. Assume the room is friendly to modeling but hostile to instrumentalism about assumptions.
