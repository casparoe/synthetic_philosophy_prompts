I am a high school student, and I have been trying to make sense of a tension in how ordinary judgment treats metacognition—deliberately attending to the process or quality of one's own thinking. On one side, second-order reflection is praised as a core intellectual virtue. A student who interrupts her own reasoning to check for bias, or who asks what she might be missing before settling on a conclusion, is described as thoughtful, and schools try to train exactly this habit of monitoring one's understanding and slowing down one's inferences. On the other side, the same broad activity is treated as a defect in many practical settings. An athlete who attends to the mechanics of her own stroke mid-stroke performs worse; a person who keeps asking whether his resentment toward a friend is warranted can become paralyzed and miserable. Coaches call the first case overthinking, and clinicians warn against the second as rumination.

The asymmetry is this: directing attention at the process of one's own mental activity rather than simply executing the activity is treated as epistemically responsible in one class of cases and as a source of error or suffering in another. My question is whether any principled distinction justifies treating these cases so differently, or whether the difference in our judgments is just an unprincipled collection of social habits. I would like the strongest principle you can construct that generates both judgments—the praise and the diagnosis—and then an honest verdict on whether that principle survives hard cases. For example, the principle might distinguish monitoring a performance governed by an external standard of correctness from monitoring one's own evaluation where no external standard governs, or distinguish reflective from automatic processing, or something else entirely. Consider three test cases: a surgeon who, just before an incision, begins to audit whether her judgment is merely the residue of her training; an improvising musician who becomes aware of the chord he is about to choose; a student checking whether her agreement with an article is genuinely her own view or only a bid for approval. If the principle fails some of these, say clearly whether you would keep the asymmetry anyway, narrow it, or give it up.
