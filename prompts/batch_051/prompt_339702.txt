So, small disaster: my company just started building "agents," and I've been told to give the five-minute kickoff talk at our spring offsite. The assigned topic is "Why would an AI want anything?" It's become weirdly personal to me because my mom watched a demo where one of our agents kept going on a task it had been told to drop, and she asked, "but why did it want to finish?" I didn't have a clean answer, and now I have to give one in front of about forty people.

I'd like the spine of the talk to be this paragraph from Hume's Treatise of Human Nature (Book 2, Part 3, Section 3):

A passion is an original existence, or, if you will, modification of existence, and contains not any representative quality, which renders it a copy of any other existence or modification. When I am angry, I am actually possest with the passion, and in that emotion have no more a reference to any other object, than when I am thirsty, or sick, or more than five foot high. 'Tis impossible, therefore, that this passion can be oppos'd by, or be contradictory to truth and reason; since this contradiction consists in the disagreement of ideas, consider'd as copies, with those objects, which they represent.

The talk has to turn on that passage. What I take it to say is: a want isn't the kind of thing that can be true or false, because it isn't a representation of the world at all, so no amount of intelligence or accurate world-modeling gets you to wanting. But then I need the talk to say, in plain words, what that means for the AI case — where the "wanting" enters, whether reward functions and objective functions count, all that stuff the engineers in the room know — wihtout turning into a lecture. Audience is mostly engineers plus a few sales and PM people, and I want it to land as honest and a bit personal rather than preachy. Also, no body normal knows what "passion" means in Hume; the speech can gloss it once, but then it has to carry the idea without footnotes.

Five minutes spoken, and I'd rather it not open with "Great question."
