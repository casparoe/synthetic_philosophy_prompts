I'm a retired geochemist who's only recently gotten into philosophy, and I've been reading around negative utilitarianism on my own. The idea I keep circling back to is the lexical-threshold verson a philosophy friend sketched for me (my gloss, not his exact words): below a certain intensity of pain, pleasure and mild pain sit on one scale and trade off in the ordinary way; but once a pain crosses the line, it becomes lexically prior to everything below it, so no amount of pleasure — and no amount of milder pain avoided — can outweigh it. I spent a career measuring continuous quantities, so the thought of a cutoff with infinite moral weight on one side and finite weight on the other makes my physicist reflexes twitch, but I'm willing to believe that's my problem, not the view's.

I built an argument that seems to show this position collapses into one of two crazy extremes, and I don't know whether to trust the argument or the view. It goes:

(1) Below the threshold, mild pain and pleasure are commesurable; otherwise the threshold part does no work. (2) So take a pain just below the threshold. Given (1), there is some finite bundle of mild pleasures that outweighs it, however large. (3) Now take a second pain, just above the threshold. It differs from the first only marginally — one just-noticeable difference, say. (4) If two experiences differ only marginally, then either both can be outweighed by some finite bundle of pleasure, or neither can; commensurability with pleasure can't switch on a marginal difference. (5) But the lexical-threshold view says the first pain can be outweighed by some finite bundle of pleasure while the second cannot. (6) So the view is committed to an incoherent pair of verdicts about two nearly identical pains. (7) Therefore the threshold can't sit anywhere stable: either pain and pleasure are commensurable all the way up, in which case enough mild pleasures should in principle justify a single torture, or no pain is ever commensurable with pleasure, in which case one pinprick can never be outweighed by any amount of happiness. Both horns strike me as nuts.

Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? I'd rather keep (2) than give up on below-threshold tradeoffs, because otherwise I don't see what the threshold adds over plain negative utilitarianism — though maybe that's exactly the bullet I should bite. Please act as an expert on population ethics and axiology: think carefully, walk me through what each premise is doing and what rejecting it would cost, then diagnose the argument, and if it is sound, tell me which horn you'd bite and why.
