I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so bear with me. I've been drafting a book set in a small farming town that slowly ruins itself through blame. The inciting incident is a failed harvest: the town decides a widow cursed the fields and drives her out. Then, a season later, people start feeling uneasy about what they did to her — not because they have proof she was innocent, but because the exile didn't actually settle anything. So they pick the man who led the campaign against her and run him off too, for having poisoned the town against an innocent. Then they blame someone for that. By the final third of the book there's an entire calendar of blame-days and a standing class of designated culprits, and nobody can remember the original frost.

And in writing it I seem to have talked myself into a theory. I've been calling it second-order guilt, or sometimes the scapegoat regress. The claim is that ritual scapegoating only appears to displace guilt. Suppose the original rite actually works — the goat in Leviticus really does carry the people's sins into the desert, I'll grant the first round for free. The problem is that loading your sins onto an innocent animal, or an innocent neighbor, is itself a sin, so the community wakes up the next morning with a fresh debt: the guilt of what it just did to the scapegoat. And the only way it knows to handle that guilt is to find another scapegoat, which creates another debt, and so on. So scapegoating isn't a release valve, it's an engine for manufacturing guilt one step ahead of the community — the books are never balanced, the ledger just get longer. In the novel the only two stable endings I can see are either the town stops scapegoating all at once and has to face the whole accumulated pile, or it keeps a permanent scapegoat class it has to dehumanize hard enough that the second-order guilt stops registering at all.

I know there's a whole literature on sacrifice and scapegoating in anthropology and religious studies, and Im sure I've reinvented somebody's wheel. But I'd like an honest assessment anyway: is this a coherent mechanism or just a grim metaphor that happens to drive a plot? What's the nearest existing idea, and where does my theory actually break? I can already see one objection — if a community is good enough at justifying or dehumanizing the scapegoat, the second-order guilt never arises, and the regress never starts, which would make the whole thing collapse. Is that fatal, or just a boundary condition?
