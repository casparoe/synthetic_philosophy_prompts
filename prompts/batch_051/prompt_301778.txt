I run a reading group at the intersection of economics and formal epistemology, and last week we kept going in circles about peer disagreement. The question on the table was whether two thoughtful, numerate people who have looked at the same body of evidence and still assign different probabilities to some event — a recession next year, say — are both being rational, or whether rationality is strict enough that at least one of them has made a mistake. One participant kept deflating the discussion by saying that reasonable people can agree to disagree. When I pressed them on what, exactly, the phrase was claiming, they said it was just ordinary language and I was asking for too much precision. The complication I could not articulate well is that Aumann's agreement theorem appears to put exactly this ordinary thought under pressure: if two agents start from the same prior over the relevant possibilities — the common prior assumption — and their posterior probabilities for some event become common knowledge, then those posterior probabilities must be equal. Yet the conversational use of the slogan seems to be doing several different jobs at once, and I suspect some of those jobs survive the theorem while others do not.

I want you to take the slogan "reasonable people can agree to disagree" and separate the precise claims it could be making. Candidate readings seem to run along several dimensions: disagreement about values or taste versus disagreement about empirical probabilities; disagreement that can persist only between people who hold different private information versus disagreement that would survive full disclosure of all relevant evidence; disagreement that traces back to different starting priors versus disagreement that traces back to different responses to shared information; and a claim about the limits of argument versus a claim about what ideally rational agents would do under ideal conditions. For each precise reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or something false, and explain exactly how Aumann's theorem bears on it — in particular, which readings the theorem rules out only if the common prior assumption is granted, and whether the common prior assumption is best understood as a modest bookkeeping idealization or as the substantive, contestable claim doing all the work. Finally, lay out which reading the person in my group was most plausibly invoking when they shrugged off the whole issue, and whether that reading actually supports the shrug.
