I've been reading around the postwar trials for a seminar on international law, and I keep hitting a double standard I cant shake. Nearly every liberal legal system treats the ban on retroactive criminal law as close to sacred: no punishment without a prior law. It's not presented as a mere technicality; judges and jurists talk about it as a structural check on state power, the sort of rule you don't throw away just because the defendant did something despicable. If a government passed a statute tomorrow making yesterday's political activity a crime and then prosecuted a rival for it, we would call that authoritarian even if we detested what the rival did.

But after the Second World War, the Allies did something that looks structurally similar. At Nuremberg and at the Tokyo tribunal they prosecuted the vanquished for crimes against peace and crimes against humanity, categories that were not part of any enforceable criminal code with individual liability at the time the underlying acts were committed. The benches were drawn from the victorious powers; only the losers were tried; and broad swaths of Allied conduct, from city-bombing campaigns onward, lay outside the courts' jurisdiction. Yet my instinct, and I think the mainstream memory, is that Nuremberg in particular was a genuine advance for justice, not merely a piece of lawless victors' theater. Tokyo leaves me more ambivalent, but it purported to apply the same legal categories.

The standard defenses I've heard are roughly these: the defendants had fair notice because the acts were monstrously wrong on any plausible morality; a prewar treaty renouncing aggressive war already existed, so only the enforcement mechanism was new; the ban on ex post facto law exists to protect ordinary citizens from their own state, not leaders who turned the state into a killing machine; and in exceptional moments the demand that the worst wrongs not go unpunished simply overrides scruples about legality. But each of them cracks when I press it. Fair notice through obvious wrongness would let any legislature criminalize yesterday's sins; a treaty binding states is not an individual crime with a fixed penalty; the state-versus-citizen line seems reverse-engineered to reach a verdict already chosen on other grounds; and the exceptionality defense is close to admitting the rule fails exactly when it matters. So I honestly don't know whether the asymmetry is justified, or whether I should conclude that Nuremberg was a morally necessary but legally indefensible exception, or just victors' justice with nicer robes. Which way would you resolve it? Don't just pick a side — tell me what principle does the work and whether it can be stated without licensing retroactive punishment everywhere. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
