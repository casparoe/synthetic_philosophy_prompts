I'm on the oversight committee of a small grantmaking body that funds environmental restoration and land-return groups, and we're stuck on a case our grant agreement never anticipated. The agreement says a grantee forfeits their funding and is barred from future grants if they 'plan, carry out, or materially support terrorism,' but the term is never defined. Last month one of our grantees entered an active timber lease at night, cut the locks on two remote-operated logging machines, and shorted their control units so the machines bricked. Nobody was within a kilometer, and the group's statement stressed that the machines were chosen specifically because no one would be hurt; but the statement also said the point was to 'make every forestry company wonder whether its equipment will start in the morning.' One side of the committee reads this as obviously terrorism: politically motivated violence against the property of a civilian industry, intended to coerce an industry through fear. The other side says it can't be terrorism becuase terrorism is violence against persons, and what we have is sabotage with a political flavor. One member tried to split the difference by distinguishing 'instrumental sabotage'—damaging a thing to stop the specific practice you oppose—from 'communicative violence'—hurting or threatening a target to send a message of fear to a wider audience. I'm not sure that distinction does the work she wants it to.

Can you write the committee's decision memo? We need an actionable bottom line: does this conduct fall under the terrorism clause, and should we rewrite the clause either way. In the memo, please flag which parts of the definition of terrorism are genuinely contested among people who study this (state actors, property damage, whether an intent to intimidate a wider audience is essential) and which are fairly settled, rather than presenting one contested academic definition as the consensus.

For structure, use these sections in this order, with rough lengths: Recommendation (150–200 words); What the committee can treat as settled (100–150 words); What's genuinely contested (100–150 words); The strongest case that this counts as terrorism (250–300 words); The strongest case against (250–300 words); A revised clause and why (150–200 words).
