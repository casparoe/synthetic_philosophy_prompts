I've ended up defending what I take to be the common prior assumption, and I want someone to be honest with me about how exposed it actually is. The position I hold: if two ideal Bayesian agents have common knowledge of each other's posterior probabilities for some proposition, any remaining disagreement has to trace back to their having started from different priors; but there's no rational basis for that kind of prior difference, so genuine disagreement between fully rational agents should dissolve under enough shared reflection. What would have to be true for this to be false? I don't want just the standard objections. I want to know which load-bearing pieces — common knowledge of each other's beliefs, Bayesian updating as a model of actual reasoning, the idea that rationality uniquely fixes a prior, the assumption that ideal agents are even the right objects to think about — could in principle fail, and for each one what a live failure would look like rather than something merely logically possible. Are there observations, experiments, or formal results that would count decisively against the position, as opposed to just being awkward? What does it actually predict about persistent real-world disagreement that rivals dont? And have I insulated it in a bad way, so that any apparent counterexample gets dismissed as one party not really being rational or not really having common knowledge? If so, is that a defect for a view like this, or is that just the nature of a claim about ideal rationality? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones a defender would themselves concede as counting against it. To be upfront about my own standards: I'd find a worked case of two clearly reasonable but bounded agents whose disagreement survives full information-sharing pretty persuasive, but I'm not sure such a case can even touch a claim that's only about ideal agents.
