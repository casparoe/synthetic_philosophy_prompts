I've been trying to understand the problem of verisimilitude: the Popperian hope that although our serious scientific theories are probably all false, some false theories are nevertheless closer to the truth than others, so that science can make genuine progress without ever arriving at the truth. The usual gloss, both in the literature and in my reading group, is an analogy with an archery target: the complete true account of the world sits at the bullseye, a theory is an arrow that has missed, and a better theory is simply one whose arrow lands closer to the center. The analogy is doing real work, not just decorating a comparison we already know how to make—it is supposed to license rank-ordering two rival theories that are both false by their respective distances from the single true account. I want a careful audit of how far that picture actually holds.

Please give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the bullseye, the target face, the rings, the arrow, the archer, the shot, the measured distance? Then identify where the mapping breaks. I am especially worried about the following: the true theory is not a point in a two-dimensional plane but something more like a maximally structured set of claims; two false theories can miss the truth in orthogonal directions, while closeness to the bullseye assumes a single scalar distance; and in the target analogy the archer can typically see the bullseye, whereas we cannot inspect the truth directly. Say clearly which disanalogies matter for the intended use—licensing comparisons of rival false theories—and which are harmless. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion survives the disanalogies: can we keep a usable notion of one theory being closer to the truth than another, or was the analogy doing more of the work than it can bear? If you think it fails in an important way, suggest what a better analogy would need to look like. Keep the whole answer under 900 words.
