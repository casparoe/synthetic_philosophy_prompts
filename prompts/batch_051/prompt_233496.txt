I'm taking a philosophy of science course this semester, and we've spent several weeks on Feyerabend's argument that there is no single scientific method. As I understand it, the claim is that every candidate methodological rule—falsify, require independent confirmation, avoid ad hoc hypotheses, suspend judgment when evidence conflicts—has been productively violated somewhere in the history of science, so any rule rigidly applied would have blocked some genuine advance. Feyerabend concludes that the only principle that survives is the anything-goes slogan, though the seminar has been treating that as a deliberate provocation rather than as a literal method.

In another course we discussed a development that I can't line up with this. Suppose that in the near future most successful empirical research is done by automated discovery systems: software that proposes hypotheses from large corpora of results, designs experiments, and revises its commitments according to a fixed, inspectable algorithm. This looks like a case where science really does operate by a method, and one that could simply be read off from source code. But I am not sure whether that refutes Feyerabend, or whether it only refutes a caricature of him, since his target was a prescriptive rule that would hold for all inquiry, not a description of how one instrument happens to work. It also seems possible that the anarchy reappears one level up—in choices about training data, objective functions, evaluation benchmarks, and which findings are treated as discoveries in the first place.

What I would like to know, for a paper I'm outlining, is this: which premise of Feyerabend's argument does automated discovery actually put pressure on, and does the argument survive that development, need repair, or become inapplicable? If it survives, what exactly is left of the claim that there is no scientific method? If it needs repair, what is the strongest weakened version that remains defensible?
