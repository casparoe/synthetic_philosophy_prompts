I sit on my university's Digital Wellbeing Ethics Committee. The counselling service has asked us to approve a one-year pilot in which students can talk to an AI assistant about everyday emotional and academic difficulties; the assistant already offers triage and self-help resources. The disputed feature is a set of welfare guardrails the service wishes to add, and the committee is evenly split on how much protective intervention we should permit. The service has put forward three configurations:

- Minimal: the assistant refuses only where complying would violate the law or would provide actionable methods of self-harm; it never overrides a student's stated wish for confidentiality except in cases of imminent risk of serious harm.
- Intermediate: in addition, the assistant refuses to engage with material it judges to be reinforcing disordered eating, intimate-partner violence, or substance misuse; it escalates to the crisis team without consent only on imminent risk of serious harm.
- Extended: the assistant may additionally refuse or override a student's request whenever it judges, from the conversation as a whole, that complying would materially undermine the student's own welfare even in the absence of imminent risk. It might, for example, decline to help a student draft a message the model predicts, partly from the student's own earlier statements, would damage a relationship the student values, or it might interrupt a late-night pattern of rumination rather than continue the thread.

The committee's disagreement is, as I see it, not about the technology but about the justification of paternalism. One side holds that a competent adult's requests about their own life should be honoured unless the risk of serious harm is imminent; an assistant that substitutes its actuarial judgement of someone's welfare for that person's own judgement is infantilising, and students who expect to be overridden will simply stop using the service. The other side holds that the university owes a duty of care, that psychological distress can temporarily impair competence, and that refusing to help a person harm themselves is not overriding their ends but declining to be complicit in their means; on this view the Extended configuration is responsible institutional care rather than paternalism. The strongest worry on that side is empirical: the model's predictions about what would undermine a student's welfare are uncertain and may penalise unusual but reasonable choices.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before our next meeting. It should recommend a single course of action, either approving the pilot in one of the three configurations or in a modified configuration with explicit conditions, or rejecting it, with a concrete time frame and review conditions. Where relevant, the memo should distinguish between the assistant's refusing a request and its overriding a student's expressed wish for confidentiality. It should engage seriously with the strongest objection to whichever position the memo adopts, and it should be actionable by the committee rather than a survey of the debate. The memo should be written in British English.
