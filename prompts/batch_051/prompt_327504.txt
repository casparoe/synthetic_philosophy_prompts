Over the last couple of years I've been in a few arguments about Confederate monuments, and I'm tired of how scripted they are: one side says it's about heritage, the other says the statues went up during Jim Crow as a threat, and nobody says anything new. I'm reading Nietzsche's essay on history at the moment — the one with monumental, antiquarian, and critical history — and it's tempting to use it to sort what's actually going on in the debate. Monumental history grabs the past as a source of inspiring examples and usable greatness; antiquarian history wants to preserve and revere the past as an inheritance; critical history hauls parts of the past before a tribunal and breaks with them. The monument fight looks like it contains all three, but not in the clean way people would expect, and I suspect the framework partly fails in instructive ways.

Use those three categories to analyze the American fight over Confederate monuments — say from the Charleston church shooting in 2015, through the violence at the Unite the Right rally in Charlottesville in 2017, through the wave of removals in 2020. I want three things: (1) a careful mapping of who in the debate is practicing monumental, antiquarian, and critical history, including the weird cases where the same side switches modes; (2) an honest account of what Nietzsche's framework can't capture — that these are decisions about public space and democratic authority, that the monuments were themselves exercises in myth-making, and that a city can remove a statue without 'breaking with' the past at all; and (3) a verdict on what the episode shows about the three modes as a way of thinking about public memory — are the categories doing real work here, or am I just putting stickers on familar positions? If you think applying Nietzsche to this debate is a category mistake, tell me that straight instead of forcing it.
