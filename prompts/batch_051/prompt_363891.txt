I'm leading a two-hour seminar for final-year undergraduates on Brandom's inferentialism: the view that the content of a sentence is constituted by its inferential role—by what it follows from, what follows from it, and what it is incompatible with—rather than by what it represents or by the conditions under which it is true. The students have only taken a truth-conditional semantics course; they move easily among reference, satisfaction, and possible worlds, and they will arrive treating inference as something we do with already meaningful sentences, not as what makes sentences meaningful in the first place.

Plan the session's explanatory arc as a numbered list of exactly five steps, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Across the five steps, specify: the order in which the view's commitments must be introduced and what depends on what; the single carrying example and what it must and must not have; the misconceptions I should most expect from students with this exact background and where in the sequence each should be defused; the end-of-session check that would show they have taken up meaning-as-inferential-role rather than merely learned the slogan; and what I should explicitly decline to address at this level.
