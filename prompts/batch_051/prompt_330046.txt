In my philosophy club, two classmates—Marcus and Elena—were debating a problem our teacher called Parfit’s hitchhiker. A traveler stranded in a desert meets a stranger who will drive him to safety only if he promises to pay a large sum once they arrive. The traveler knows that, once he is safe in town, he will have no self-interested desire to pay, and he will judge that he has no reason to keep the promise. The stranger knows this too, so the stranger refuses to help unless the traveler makes himself trustworthy—for example, by taking a drug or using some device that will force him to pay later, even though, at that later time, paying would be contrary to what he then wants and judges best. The question is whether it can be rational now to bring it about that one acts irrationally later.

Marcus said yes, but with a qualification. He argued that if the traveler’s current aim is to survive, and if the only available way to survive is to bind his future self, then binding the future self is the rational choice now, even though the bound action will be irrational at the later time. He said we already accept this logic whenever people use commitment devices: a student deletes a distracting app before finals, or signs up for a course with a nonrefundable fee, knowing that her future self will dislike those constraints. The future self’s complaint, Marcus said, does not undermine the present self’s decision.

Elena disagreed. She said the hitchhiker case is different from ordinary commitment devices because the later action is not merely unpleasant; it is, by the traveler’s own future lights, something he has no reason to do. Deleting a distracting app is an attempt to help your future self achieve what that future self will still endorse—studying for finals. But the hitchhiker’s future self will not endorse paying the stranger. He will think, correctly, that the danger is over and the payment is a pure loss. Elena argued that if a person deliberately induces himself to act against his own future judgment, he is treating his future self as a mere tool. That seems disrespectful to oneself, and it cannot be called rational without stretching the word “rational” beyond recognition.

Marcus replied that Elena was conflating two levels. The action at the later time—paying—may be irrational. But the action now—taking the binding drug—is not the same action. It is a different action, chosen under different circumstances, and its rationality should be evaluated by the reasons available now. He said that if Elena refuses ever to cause future irrationality, she is implicitly adopting a principle like “never bring about a state in which you would act irrationally,” but that principle can be self-defeating: it would leave the traveler to die in the desert. So a rule that forbids all such self-binding is more irrational than the local irrationality it forbids.

Elena pushed back. She said that Marcus’s response proves too much. If it is rational to cause future irrationality by taking a drug, then it would also be rational to cause present irrationality by taking a drug right now, provided that doing so would achieve some current end. But a person who takes a drug to make herself steal a car because she currently wants the car is not rational; she is merely choosing to become an irrational instrument of a passing impulse. The difference, she said, is that the hitchhiker’s current desire to survive is clear and urgent, while the car thief’s current desire is trivial. But once you admit that the strength of a current desire can justify future irrationality, you have made rationality a matter of how much one happens to care about something at the moment. That undermines the very idea of rational constraint.

Marcus answered that Elena’s car thief example smuggles in morality. Rationality and morality are not the same thing: a person may rationally adopt means to a bad end, even if the end is immoral. The question is not whether the end is good, but whether the traveler’s present self has a reason to prefer the outcome in which he is rescued and later pays over the outcome in which he is not rescued. He does have that reason, because death is worse than paying. So the present decision is rational relative to the present self’s ends. The future self may wish the present self had not made the decision, but a later perspective does not automatically defeat an earlier one.

Elena conceded that death is worse than paying, but said that still does not answer the deeper problem. The traveler’s future self is not a different person; it is the same person at a different time. If you accept that the present self may bind the future self against its own judgment, you are accepting a kind of intra-personal paternalism. Elena argued that this is no more legitimate than one person forcing another to do something “for his own good” when the other does not agree. The traveler, by taking the binding drug, is essentially forcing his future self to pay a debt that the future self will reject. That is not a relation of mutual concern across time; it is domination across time. And if we think domination between persons is objectionable, we should also object to domination between temporal parts of the same person.

Marcus said that Elena’s analogy to interpersonal paternalism breaks down because the present self cannot consult the future self before acting, and the future self’s existence is conditional on the present self’s decision. In the desert, there is no future self who is being wronged, because if the traveler does not bind himself, that future self will not exist at all. The only way to bring the future self into existence is to make the promise and ensure it is kept. So the future self cannot complain that it was forced to exist on unfavorable terms, because its only alternative was non-existence. The comparison to one person forcing another for his own good assumes an independently existing victim, but here there is none.

Elena replied that this “future self owes its existence to the promise” argument is too strong. It would justify any self-binding that is a condition of survival, no matter what the future self would think. Imagine a traveler who can survive only by making himself, through a drug, into a person who will enjoy slavery. The future slave, by the present person’s current values, has a life not worth living. Marcus’s argument would say the future slave cannot complain, because he exists only as a result of the binding. But that is not a reason; it is an evasion. Existence is not automatically a benefit, and to say that a person cannot complain about the terms of his existence just because he would not otherwise exist proves too much.

The conversation ended with neither persuading the other. I would like to know who is right at each step, not just who wins overall. Specifically: Is Marcus right that the rationality of the present act should be evaluated separately from the rationality of the future act? Is Elena right that causing future irrationality is impermissible or at least irrational? Does the car thief example show that Marcus’s view allows too much? Does Marcus’s “future self would not exist” reply answer Elena’s domination concern? And does Elena’s slavery variant successfully refute that reply, or does it change the subject?
