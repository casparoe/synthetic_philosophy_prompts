I'm grading a midterm in a philosophy of history course and could use a second opinion on one answer. The question I set was: "Does the fact that small events—a lost nail, a delayed messenger, a general's fever—can appear to redirect the whole course of history show that history is not deterministic? In your answer, be careful to distinguish causal sensitivity, genuine contingency, and mere unpredictability." The student wrote:

The for-want-of-a-nail rhyme is usually taken to show how path-sensitive history can be: a trivial difference at the start (the nail is missing) ramifies into a large difference downstream (the kingdom is lost). This is basically the modern notion of sensitive dependence on initial conditions, sometimes called the butterfly effect. In both cases the system is chaotic, meaning arbitrarily small differences in initial conditions produce completely different trajectories. And chaotic systems are unpredictable in principle, because you would need infinite precision about the initial state and small errors grow faster than you can track them. Now, a deterministic system is by definition one whose later states are fixed by its earlier states; but these outcomes aren't fixed by anything we could ever know, so the nail episode seems to show that history is not deterministic, at least at the level of battles and kingdoms. The missing nail thus represents a real contingency: the outcome could have been different, because it turned on a fluke. The proper lesson is that historians shouldn't look for covering-law explanations of unique events, since the actual outcome wasn't forced by the antecedent forces—it was a matter of chance.
