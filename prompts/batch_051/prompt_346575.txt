I am a novelist. The dispute over advance directives turns on precedent autonomy: whether an earlier, capable self may bind a later self that no longer shares its values. That question seems to me to have an underexplored counterpart in the governance of a writer's work, and I want to think through a concrete proposal built on that counterpart rather than argue for or against it in the abstract.

Suppose a jurisdiction introduced a creative advance directive. While still capable, an author could file a binding instrument specifying whether unfinished manuscripts may be destroyed or embargoed; whether unfinished or unpublished work may be completed, edited, or released by another writer; whether the author's name, pen name, voice, or likeness may be licensed for sequels, adaptations, or synthesised continuations; and what is to happen to drafts, notes, and correspondence. The directive would be freely amendable and revocable so long as the author retained capacity. Once capacity was lost, or at death, it would become irrevocable, and courts would enforce it even against the author's later expressed wishes—for instance, a demented novelist who now cheerfully consents to a ghostwritten sequel or a licensed digital version of their voice.

Treat this as a mechanism. Analyse what it would actually do: the incentives it creates for authors at different career stages, for heirs and estates, for publishers and platforms, and for scholars and biographers; the ways it would be gamed or used strategically from the outset; the second-order effects on what authors write, label, and destroy while still well; and the points where the system would most likely fail first. Where you rely on debates about precedent autonomy, the authority of the earlier self, or posthumous rights, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on those topics, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Please answer in American English.
