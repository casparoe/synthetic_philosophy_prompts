I have been trying to work out a puzzle about the way people respond to sweatshops, and I would like someone to articulate the assumptions that response commits them to.

The practice, stated as neutrally as I can: a company sells goods made in a factory in a poor country. The workers are paid little by our standards, work long hours, and have few protections, but many of them choose the job freely and are better off with it than with their actual alternatives, such as farming that can fail in a bad season or no paid work at all. Nonetheless, consumers, unions, and the companies themselves often treat this situation as a serious wrong. Buyers boycott the brand, companies impose codes of conduct on their suppliers, and governments attach labor conditions to trade. What makes the puzzle interesting to me is that the same people who condemn these factories would rarely call a domestic worker exploited for taking a low-paying, unpleasant, insecure job; there, the job's unattractiveness is treated as something the person accepted.

So I want to know what theory of exploitation, or of consent, or of benefit, the practice presupposes. Condemning these arrangements seems to require the idea that a transaction can be voluntary, and better for the worker than any realistic alternative, and still be exploitative. But then several questions follow. Does the practice presuppose that poverty or desperation makes consent defective in a way that an ordinary unattractive job offer does not? Does it presuppose a standard of fair wages that applies regardless of what a worker agrees to, and if so, where does that standard come from and who is entitled to enforce it? Or does it presuppose that the company bears some responsibility for creating or maintaining the worker's bad alternatives, in a way that a local low-wage employer would not?

What I want is not a list of arguments for or against sweatshops, but a reconstruction of the implicit theory: what the practice strictly commits itself to, which rival views it quietly rules out, and where the practice is underdetermined because several different theories would license the same behavior. Please also say what would have to change in the practice if that implicit theory were replaced by the simpler view that a voluntary exchange leaving a person better off than their alternatives is always permissible.
