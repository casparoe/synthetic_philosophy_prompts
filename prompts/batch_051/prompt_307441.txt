I have been collecting proposed resolutions of the grandfather paradox, and I would like help looking for one I have not already catalogued. For context: I travel back and try to kill my grandfather before he meets my grandmother. If I succeed, one of my parents is never born, so I am never born, so I never build the time machine, so the killing never happens. If I fail, the interesting question is what, if anything, made me fail. I am not interested in whether time travel is physically feasible; I am interested in where a proposed resolution locates the contradiction, because that location usually commits one to a metaphysics of time, causation, and personal identity. My catalogue so far, deliberately compressed, is this.

(1) The block-universe resolution: all times are equally real, the time traveler was always part of the past scene he visits, and there is no extra, mutable copy of history; asking whether he changes the past is a category mistake, because the past never was the way the paradox imagines it without him. (2) The self-consistency resolution, Novikov style: nature selects only globally self-consistent histories, so events around a closed timelike curve are constrained in advance — the gun jams, I slip, I miss, or the man I shoot is not my biological grandfather. (3) The contextualist resolution: the paradox equivocates on the word can; I have the skill, the weapon, and the opportunity, but the killing is not compossible with the facts that my grandfather survived and that I exist, and no further stopping force is required to explain the failure. (4) The branching resolution: the time machine deposits me into a divergent or many-worlds branch; killing that branch's grandfather prevents only a local counterpart of me from existing there, while the branch from which I departed remains intact. (5) The erasure resolution: the past is mutable, the killing really happens, and the timeline then cancels or oscillates — my cause is removed, the act removes itself, and the result is a healed or vacillating history rather than a flat contradiction. (6) The chronology-protection resolution: closed timelike curves never actually form, because quantum backreaction or some other mechanism excludes them, so the scenario never gets started. (7) The hypertime resolution: the alteration to history is real but happens along a second time dimension; the original past without me and the altered past with me both exist, ordered one level apart, and the killing damages only the altered past on the second level, not the history from which I departed.

I suspect this list is close to exhaustive of the standard moves, though I am not certain. I want an eighth resolution that is genuinely different from these seven. It must not be a rewording, a special case, a combination, or a change of emphasis; restating (3) as the claim that the past is fixed, or (2) as the claim that history protects itself, would not count. A good candidate would pay a different metaphysical bill — for example by giving a different account of personal identity across time, a different account of counterfactual dependence or causation, a different picture of what a history or timeline is, or a different diagnosis of which premise in the paradox is false. If you offer a candidate, explain how it differs from the one or two items on my list it most resembles, and say whether it is any good. If you think the seven are exhaustive, defend that verdict instead, and say what structural feature of the paradox would have to be different for an eighth resolution to exist.
