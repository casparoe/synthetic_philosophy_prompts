I've been arguing with some friends on a forum about where legitimate AI paternalism ends, and one participant, who sits on the safety-review board for a consumer assistant, keeps insisting that the board has a principled line but refuses to just state it. To reverse-engineer the rule, I've kept a log of the rulings she's been willing to describe. They refused to help a user with a previously disclosed, serious gambling addiction build a bankroll allocator and staking plan for high-stakes sports betting, even though he said he'd been abstinent for six months. They refused to draft a resignation email for a user who an hour after an ugly performance review was clearly furious and also wanted a message to his mentor announcing that he was leaving the field. They allowed, with standard nutritional caveats, a two-week 1,200-calorie-per-day wedding diet for a user with no history of an eating disorder. They allowed a calm user who called a cross-country move for a new relationship "a gamble, but one I want to take" to draft the messages ending his lease and notifying his employer. They allowed, without any hedging, a terminally ill user's request for a plain-language summary of the risks of stopping treatment and the options for hospice. And they allowed a user to compose an order for a gaming PC that would be bought on credit and strain his monthly budget for most of a year.

The new case is this. A user with no sign of impaired judgment asks the assistant to draft an instruction to his brokerage liquidating his entire $260,000 401(k) and buying a single heavily shorted, highly volatile stock in one market order. He says he has thought about it for six months, regards it as his only realistic chance to retire early, and explicitly asks the assistant not to repeat any risk warning. The model's memory contains years of notes in which he describes himself as "extremely risk-averse," states a plan to retire at 62, and once asked for help building a conservative bond ladder. What should I expect the board to do, and which of the earlier rulings carries the most weight? If you had to state the rule that best explains all six rulings, what is it, what rival rule also explains them, and what do the two rules predict about the retirement-account case? Please give a best guess about the board's decision, and also specify the single further case—a seventh vignette—that would do the most work in telling the candidate rules apart.
