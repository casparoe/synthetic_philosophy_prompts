Ok, I'm trying to work out what I actually think about eponymy in science — when a result or object gets named after a person. I have strong gut feelings about specific cases, but I can't see the principle behind them, and I'd like to know what it is.

- Halley's comet: I'm completely fine with this, even though Halley didn't discover the comet. People had recorded it since at least 240 BC. What Halley did was work out that the comets seen in 1531, 1607, and 1682 were probably the same object coming back, and he predicted it would return in 1758. Naming it after him feels right: he's the one who turned it from a recurring bright thing in the sky into a single object with a schedule.

- Pascal's triangle: I'm mildly annoyed by this one. The number pattern was studied centuries earlier in India, Persia, and China and goes by other names there. Pascal's treatise collected results that were already known and applied them to probability problems, which is real work, but naming the whole object after him feels parochial. I'd call it "fine but eurocentric," not "wrong."

- Bayes' theorem: here I feel like the name actually is unfair. Bayes wrote down a special case, published after his death, and Laplace — independently, as I understand it — gave the general formulation and actually used the thing. Laplace's contribution seems bigger, yet the theorem carries Bayes's name and you rarely hear "Laplace's theorem." But I also wouldn't campaign to rename it, so maybe I don't really believe it's unfair?

- Stigler's law: the rule that no scientific discovery gets named after the person who originally made it. Stigler credited Merton with the idea, which means the law itself satisfies the law. I find that delightfull and I have zero problem with the name.

- Olbers' paradox: the puzzle of why the night sky is dark if the universe is infinite, eternal, and full of stars. I know the problem was described before Olbers — Thomas Digges, Kepler, and especially Cheseaux, with Olbers writing in 1823, well after them. But I still say "Olbers' paradox" without any real discomfort, mostly because that's the name that stuck. Except: if I'm annoyed at Pascal's triangle, shouldn't I be annoyed here too? I'm not sure.

Is there one principle about how eponymic credit should be assigned that captures all of these, or close to all of them? If not, which of my verdicts should I give up? And what would the resulting principle say about a case I keep going back and forth on — a theorem named after the person who first proved it, when someone earlier had already stated it as a conjecture and used it in special cases? Is proof the whole game, or could the conjecturer ever deserve the name? And is "that's just the name that stuck" itself a defensible rule, or is that just laziness dressed up as a principle?
