I'm a grad student working on optimization, and I've ended up in a running argument with a friend in evo-bio about whether natural selection has anything useful to teach people doing gradient-based training. We crowdsourced a list of proposed "lessons" from evolution, and they range from things that sound like real design advice to things that sound like a metaphor wearing a lab coat. Could you sort them into three tiers: (A) I'd actually lean on this as engineering guidance, (B) true-ish but mostly a productive metaphor, (C) misleading or actively harmful as a guide to practice. Give one sentence for each item explaining why it lands in that tier; if something genuinely doesn't fit the three tiers, add a fourth tier and justify it. Don't worry about citing anything—I care about the judgment, not a literature review.

1. Natural fitness landscapes aren't static: populations modify their own environments and the fitness of other types through things like niche construction and frequency-dependent selection. So the effective loss surface of a learning system should be expected to shift as the system itself changes, especially with human feedback or self-play.

2. Evolution produced eyes and wings without any gradient information. So backprop is an overrated convenience; with enough compute, selection on random-ish variation can solve the same hard search problems.

3. In the inclusive-fitness sense, selection tracks reproductive value—a proxy—rather than the organism's well-being. So every trainable metric is a proxy and will get gamed once the model is strong enough, and the practical lesson is to design proxies with the incentive structure you actually want rather than keep hunting for a "true" objective.

4. Evolution is greedy and has no mechanism for temporarily accepting lower fitness to reach a higher peak. So the local minima and saddle point worry in deep learning is overblown: SGD with some noise can get to good solutions without sophisticated escape tricks.

5. Sexual recombination matters because mixing partial solutions from different lineages is a more powerful source of novelty than point mutations. So optimizers should do more crossover-like operations between checkpoints than just averaging or fine-tuning.

6. Under the Baldwin effect, learned behavior can guide which variants get selected before any genetic change has fixed. By analogy, learned initializations and meta-learning can matter more than the outer optimizer that updates those meta-parameters.

7. Neuroevolution and NAS are basically blind variation plus selective retention in non-smooth spaces. Since natural evolution is that same family and is stunningly sample-inefficient even when it works, we should not be surprised they're slow—and their slowness is not by itself evidence that the whole approach is broken.

8. Evolution is open-ended in the sense that no fixed fitness function captures which novelty gets colonized next. So a fixed benchmark, no matter how hard, cannot produce open-ended learning; we'd need objectives that generate their own diversity.
