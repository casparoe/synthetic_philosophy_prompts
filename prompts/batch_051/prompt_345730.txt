I follow a rule about how I form beliefs concerning serious wrongdoing, and I would like to know whether it is rationally defensible. The rule is this: when I try to understand how someone came to do something harmful, I do not allow myself to settle on categories such as sadism, fanaticism, or innate cruelty until the more workaday explanations have been considered and specifically ruled out—careerism, fear of being troublesome, deference to superiors, thoughtlessness about the consequences, self-deception, the wish to appear competent and reliable. I adopted the rule after thinking about the trial of Adolf Eichmann and the argument, which I associate with Hannah Arendt, that what was striking about him was not a monstrous motive in the ordinary sense but something far more mundane: his evil appeared to travel through exactly those everyday channels. At first I confined the rule to large bureaucratic atrocities, where it seems to fit well; more recently I have extended it to everything from a colleague accused of sabotaging a project to my own small failures. A friend now says that I have not adopted an epistemic policy so much as installed a prior that makes exceptional cruelty the last hypothesis I will ever entertain, irrespective of the evidence. She says I have swapped the folk-psychological story about wickedness for an intellectual's version of the same bias. I can see her point, but I also think the usual narratives of evil are systematically overused. Is my rule defensible as it stands? If not, is there a narrower version that keeps whatever was worth keeping, and how would I tell from the inside whether I was applying even that narrower version badly? I would be grateful for an answer in British English.
