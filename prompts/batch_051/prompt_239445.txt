I'm on the conservation board for a small island country, and we're voting Thursday on whether to let a research consortium release the first gene drive our country has ever considered, targeting black rats on an uninhabited offshore island called Tefara. The rats are eating the eggs and chicks of a seabird that's down to about forty breeding pairs, and the consortium's proposal is a suppression drive: a CRISPR homing construct that biases inheritance so a female-infertility allele spreads and crashes the rat population in maybe three years. There's also a de-extinction group waiting in the wings with a proxy for a petrel that went extinct when rats arrived in the 1800s, and they won't release anything until the island is rat-free. The board is split three ways. Some want to approve the release now, because the models have the bird functionally extinct within a decade and twenty years of baiting and trapping haven't moved the numbers. Others want to wait three or four years for a second lab to finish a daisy-chain drive, one designed to lose its CRISPR components generation by generation and burn itself out after half a dozen rat generations instead of spreading forever; the worry there is the colony won't survive the delay. A third camp says we should refuse both on precautionary grounds: rats stow away on fishing boats all the time, so once a homing drive reaches the mainland it's effectively a decision to alter rat populations everywhere, with no recall button, and no one actually knows what happens to the predators and scavengers that now depends on the rats.

I've been asked to circulate a short written opinion before the vote. It should recommend one concrete course of action with a timeline, and it needs to engage the strongest consideration on the losing side honestly rather than strawmanning it. Keep it under about 900 words.
