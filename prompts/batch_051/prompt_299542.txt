My two housemates started a fight last week over the old digger wasp story, and I couldn't call it either way. You probably know it: the female wasp paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the burrow entrance, goes inside to check the burrow, comes back out, and drags it in. If you move the cricket an inch while she's inside, she drags it back to the entrance and does the whole check-and-drag sequence again, and she'll keep doing it instead of skipping the redundant check. People use that as a picture of mechanical rigidity; some people call it sphexishness.

Maya said the story gets used too loosely. The wasp is stuck because it can't represent its own routine as a routine. A person caught in a loop is different, because even if they can't stop right now, they can look at themselves and ask, "is this the same old pattern again?" -- and that's a second-order capacity the wasp simply lacks.

Jonah said that capacity is doing a lot of work. The question "is this the same old pattern?" also has to fire at a particular time, and either something inside triggered it or it didn't. The human is just a loop with a longer fuse: a loop that has an extra step called "check whether I'm in a loop." That step doesn't step outside the loop; it just adds another item inside it.

Maya came back that this makes "sphexishness" unfalsifiable. If a system can notice its own loop and change strategy and still counts as just a bigger loop, then the word no longer distinguishes the wasp from someone who leaves a bad relationship after noticing it's a pattern. Jonah said that was exactly his point: the difference is real, but it's a difference in how much of the world is inside the loop -- how far ahead the loop reaches -- not in whether there is a loop at all. Calling the human free and the wasp mechanical is re-describing a difference of degree as a difference in kind.

Maya said a difference of degree can become a difference in kind, becuase at some point the loop includes a representation of itself. The wasp responds to a cue; the person responds to the fact that the cue plus their habitual response is unreliable. That's not just one more input; it changes the kind of error the system can correct. Jonah said he'd only believe that if the self-representation weren't itself automatic. But the person who notices their pattern and still repeats it next week is functionally the wasp when the cricket gets moved a second time -- we just call one "rigidity" and the other "weakness of will," because we need free will to survive the data.

I kept going back and forth and didn't feel I could referee it. Can you go through their actual argument move by move and say who was right about what? Structure the response with these sections, in order, with roughly these lengths:

- What each side is actually claiming (~100 words): strip out the rhetoric and list each person's main claims.
- Point-by-point adjudication (~500 words): go through the exchange move by move and rule on each move.
- The shared assumption (~200 words): the thing neither of them questioned, and what happens if you drop it.
- Overall verdict (~150 words): who is more right overall, plus the strongest version of the other side's position.
