Please construct a thought experiment in which a Stoic committed to the Chrysippean cognitive psychology of the passions — emotions are assents to evaluative impressions, to be distinguished from involuntary first movements (propatheia) and from the sage's well-reasoned affects (eupatheia) — behaves in a way that looks floridly emotional, while a Romantic, who holds that felt emotion is partly constitutive of a well-lived life and should be followed as actually felt, behaves with visible restraint. Constraints: an ordinary setting, no physiological interference, and the Romantic's restraint must follow from what she genuinely feels, not from self-command or social performance. After laying out the scene, give the verdict of each psychology: for the Stoic, say whether each displayed response is a pathos, a propatheia, or a eupatheia and to what exactly he does and does not assent; for the Romantic, say which felt state, rather than which norm of decorum, yields the restraint. Then identify the feature of the case doing the work, say what the case collapses into if that feature is removed, and state how a defender of whichever party comes out looking worse would attack it. I plan to use this in a study group of retired scientists reading Stoic and Romantic treatments of emotion, where the aim is to move past the "Stoic equals unfeeling" cliché; a case that stipulates the verdicts rather than generating them from the two positions would defeat the purpose.
