I've spent the past few months noticing something at work, and it has turned into a paper I'm writing for a philosophy discussion group, which is why I want a case built to a precise specification rather than a general explanation. I work as a paralegal, and part of my job is telling clients whether, given the state of the file, we know enough about what happened to proceed. I've found that the same evidence satisfies me when we are only deciding whether to draft a routine letter, but not when a client's livelihood is on the line; stranger still, a colleague in an idle conversation will say that the client knows the very thing I would not have asserted in a strategy meeting. That pushed me toward contextualism about knowledge ascriptions and toward pragmatic encroachment, and I want an example engineered to separate the two.

Please construct a thought experiment in which contextualism and pragmatic encroachment come apart in the following direction. An ascriber, A, in an undemanding conversational context, says of a subject, S, that S knows that p. The contextualist says A's utterance is true, because the standards governing A's context are low and S's evidence is good enough by those standards. The pragmatic encroachment theorist says A's utterance is false, because S herself faces high practical stakes and therefore does not know that p, whatever A's context is like. The constraints are: an ordinary realistic setting such as a home repair, a medical test, or a lease; no more than three characters; no skeptical scenarios, demons, lotteries, or unusual physics; p should be a mundane proposition whose truth matters a great deal to S and almost not at all to A; and A must be fully aware of what is at stake for S, so that the disagreement between the theories cannot be explained away as A's ignorance. The case should not stipulate the verdicts; it should generate them from the two views as you set them out.

After presenting the scenario, walk through the demonstration the way you would in a paper. State p, S's evidence, and what is at stake for S, and explain why the pragmatic encroachment theorist denies that S knows. Then explain why the contextualist says A's sentence is nonetheless true. Say which structural feature is doing the work, presumably the separation of the ascriber's conversational stakes from the subject's practical stakes, and tell me what would happen if you collapsed the two: would the case collapse into the familiar pattern where both theories agree because the same person is both the subject and the ascriber? Finally, explain how a sophisticated pragmatic encroachment theorist would resist the case, for instance by saying something about third-person attributions, about how high the stakes have to be before knowledge is lost, or about whether A's utterance is best heard as a claim about what is good enough for A's purposes rather than as a full knowledge claim. Please write the whole thing in American English, in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write a letter or an email.
