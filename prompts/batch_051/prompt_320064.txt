In my graduate seminar on explanation and opacity, we have been separating two demands that are often run together: that an explanation of a model's output be causally faithful, and that it be accepted by a reasonable auditor. I want you to work through a legal-interpretation problem that forces that distinction to carry weight. The statute is invented for this exercise, and no knowledge of any real legislation is needed.

Section 9(b) of the (fictional) Interpretive Adequacy Act reads:

"Before deploying a high-impact model whose output materially affects any natural person, an operator must obtain, from a second model that is independent of the deployed model, a written explanation of the decision procedure by which the output was produced. The explanation must be sufficient to permit a reasonable auditor to understand why the output occurred, and must include a certification that the decision procedure is non-discriminatory. A certification obtained under this subsection is conclusive evidence of compliance with this section."

Now consider a case the drafters plainly did not anticipate. Meridian General Hospital deploys a high-impact triage model, T, that assigns emergency-room patients to priority queues. The hospital's vendor obtains the required explanation from interpreter model I. I was built by the same vendor, fine-tuned on synthetic cases generated by T itself, and is known to share much of T's internal feature geometry. I returns a fluent, confident explanation: T's output was produced by detecting a cluster of features I labels "subclinical hypoperfusion pattern," and no discriminatory variable was used. A human auditor could not independently recover this cluster from the visible inputs, but she finds the explanation plausible and well-organized. Subsequent ablation studies, however, fail to confirm that the cited features drive T's output: masking them changes the output by less than one percentage point, while masking other, uncited features produces much larger changes. The vendor maintains that the certification is nonetheless conclusive under Section 9(b).

Does Meridian General comply with Section 9(b)? Read the clause according to (i) the ordinary meaning of its text, (ii) the purpose of requiring an independent model explanation, (iii) what its drafters would have said had they anticipated an interpreter trained on the target's own outputs, and (iv) what a reasonable auditor is actually in a position to understand about T. State where these readings diverge, and argue for which should control in this case. Pay particular attention to the regress: if an interpreter is no more transparent than its target, and the auditor must take the interpreter's word for what T did, then what sort of explanation has the auditor received? Can compliance genuinely be made conclusive by a certificate that is the very thing in dispute? Be precise about whether "independent" should be read as causally independent, informationally independent, or something else, and what each reading would imply here.

After your analysis, give a brief structured summary: a table with one row for each of the four readings plus your overall judgment, and columns: "Approach," "What it asks," "Result in the Meridian case," and "Main vulnerability." The "Result" column should state in a short phrase whether the hospital has complied under that reading. Then close with a single JSON object with the following fields: "overall_compliant" (boolean), "controlling_approach" (one of the four readings or "hybrid"), "certification_conclusive" (boolean), "decisive_consideration" (one sentence explaining the controlling factor), and "open_problem" (one sentence stating what the case leaves unresolved about recursive interpretability).
