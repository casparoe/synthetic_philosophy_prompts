I am a graduate student preparing to present my own position on truth in a seminar on American pragmatism, and I want a consistency audit before I defend it. I find myself pulled toward both Peirce and James, while also wanting to keep a robust realism about the past, and I am not sure the package is jointly tenable. Here is the view as it currently stands:

(a) Truth is a property of beliefs (or of the propositions they express) and is the aim of inquiry.
(b) Peirce-style, I hold that truth is the opinion that sufficiently diligent inquirers would eventually converge upon, and that every genuine question has such a fated answer.
(c) James-style, I also hold that a belief is made true, over time, by its practical workings — its coherence with the rest of experience, its predictive success, and its guidance of action toward what we care about; apart from such workings, the word true does no explanatory work.
(d) I find correspondence formulations unhelpful: the image of comparing a belief with a reality that is not itself believed adds nothing and cannot explain how we ever tell truth from falsity.
(e) I also accept that there are determinate truths about the past that no possible inquiry will ever settle — for example, the exact number of hairs on Napoleon's head on the morning of Waterloo.
(f) Finally, I hold that the locution true for me but not for you is a confusion; truth is impersonal even though justification varies from person to person.

I cannot see exactly where the strain is, because each claim initially strikes me as correct, but I suspect the combination commits me to more than I realize. Please adopt the role of a professional philosopher working on pragmatist theories of truth, think carefully through the pairwise interactions before settling on your ranking, and deliver an audit rather than a general survey. Present your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: item 1 the severest genuine tension and what would have to give; item 2 the next severest; item 3 the next after that, or an explicit statement that there is no third; item 4 the most tempting apparent tension that dissolves on closer inspection and why it dissolves; item 5 the least costly revision that restores consistency and what it costs me.
