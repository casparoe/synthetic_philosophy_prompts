Question: People often dismiss an opponent's moral view as "just a bias" while treating their own as a legitimate moral preference. Is there a principled distinction between a moral preference and a moral bias? If so, what is it, and how does it apply to a disputed moral or political case?

Student answer: I think there is a distinction, and it comes down to responsiveness to reasons. A preference is a disposition to choose or endorse something that the person will revise when given good arguments and new information; a bias is a disposition that keeps operating even after the person has seen evidence that it is distorting their judgment. Take two people who both oppose rent control. One can point to data on housing supply and says she would change her mind if the data showed tenants were worse off; that is a preference. The other, when shown data that rent control helps current tenants, calls the data fake or just repeats a slogan from her neighbors; that is a bias. What matters is the causal history of the attitude, not its content. This is why calling something a bias is such a serious charge: it means the person is not tracking the facts. Some people use "bias" loosely just for a view they dislike, but that is a misuse. Strictly speaking, bias is an epistemic defect in how the attitude is held, and preferences are just evidence-sensitive evaluative stances. So yes, there is a principled distinction, and it does not depend on whether the view itself is right or wrong.
