I have been thinking about Abelard's account of sin, and I find its consent-only doctrine far more attractive than I expected. As I understand the view, sin consists entirely in consenting to what one believes one ought not to do. The inclination that precedes consent is not yet sin, since it can afflict even those who do not act on it; and the outward deed is not sin either, because the same deed, considered apart from consent, is indifferent and can be joined to very different consents. The deed neither constitutes sin nor adds to its gravity. Someone who consents to adultery and is prevented at the last moment is exactly as guilty as someone who consents and carries it out. One can sin without raising a finger, and one can act harmfully without sin so long as one is not acting against conscience; conversely, acting against conscience is sinful even when the act itself is harmless.

What attracts me is that this view supplies a single, definite criterion of culpability that lies wholly within the agent's control. It refuses to make guilt depend on whether a plan happens to succeed, on the opportunity or resistance the world supplies, or on the harm that happens to result. Two people with the same inner consent are, on this view, equally sinful no matter how differently events unfold. That refusal to let culpability be infected by luck strikes me as a genuine intellectual virtue.

And yet I have an intuition I cannot dislodge. If two people each consent to a killing, and one is stopped before he can act while the other completes it, I want to say that the one who killed has done something worse — and not merely something with worse consequences, but something more blameworthy, or blameworthy in a further way. The same goes for smaller betrayals and cruelties: I cannot persuade myself that the difference between almost doing and doing is morally inert. I find myself wanting the outward act to add something to the ascription of guilt, although I cannot say clearly what it adds, and I can see that saying so reintroduces exactly the luck the consent-only view aimed to exclude.

I would like you to press both sides at their strongest and then give me a negotiated verdict: should the intuition be debunked and surrendered, should the consent-only theory be amended or replaced, or is there a reading on which both can be preserved? Do not simply pick a side; tell me what would be required to justify each move, and how much confidence your verdict warrants. And if the task as I have posed it rests on a confusion — for instance if I am running together sin as a condition of the soul with the ordinary wrongness of what is done, or if I have misdescribed Abelard — tell me so plainly rather than forcing a verdict.
