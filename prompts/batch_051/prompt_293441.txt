I am a philosophy graduate student working on the intersection of philosophy of mind and cognitive neuropsychiatry. I have been reading about the two-factor theory of delusions. As I understand it, the theory distinguishes two contributing deficits. The first factor explains the content of a delusion by positing an anomalous experience: for example, in Capgras delusion, a disrupted autonomic response to familiar faces may generate the vivid feeling that a loved one has been replaced by an impostor. The second factor explains why the person accepts that strange idea rather than rejecting it, and it is usually characterized as a failure of belief evaluation, hypothesis assessment, or something similarly doxastic.

This is where I get stuck. The second factor seems to be described in belief-laden terms; after all, evaluating a hypothesis and accepting it sound like processes that issue in a belief. Yet much of the recent philosophical literature I have encountered debates whether delusions are beliefs in the first place. Some authors argue that at least some delusional reports are better understood as imaginings the patient mistakes for beliefs, as empty speech acts, or as avowals that are not supported by the rest of the person's behavior and attitudes.

What are the most important implications of taking the two-factor theory seriously for the question of whether delusions are beliefs? In your answer, please address the following. First, does the theory presuppose that delusions are beliefs, or is that presupposition dispensable under pressure from anti-doxastic accounts? Second, which parts of the theory would survive if delusions were understood as non-beliefs, and which parts would need to be reformulated? Third, what would the theory imply about how a clinician or an interlocutor should treat a patient's delusional utterance, for example whether to treat it as a sincere assertion of something the patient actually believes? Finally, distinguish what the theory directly entails from what requires additional assumptions, and say which implications you regard as most important.
