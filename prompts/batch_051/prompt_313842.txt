I'm doing a law-and-medicine course and we've been given a statute that defines death. Section 1 reads: 'A person is dead when there has been either (a) irreversible cessation of cardiac and respiratory function, or (b) irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brain stem.' The seminar was on how to apply wording like this to cases the drafters never imagined, and one hypothetical is really bothering me.

A man's heart has been destroyed by a virus; his heart muscle is never coming back and the organ does no pumping at all. For a week he has been on ECMO, a machine that takes blood out of his body, oxygenates it, and pumps it back in, so his brain is fully supplied. He is awake, talking to his family, and stable enough to be on a transplant list. Reading clause (a) literally, his heart has irreversibly stopped and his lungs don't move on their own, so his cardiac and respiratory function has ceased. That would make him legally dead while he is sitting up chatting, which obviously can't be what the statute was for. But i'm not sure that 'we know what they meant' should just beat the words.

I'd like you to work through what clause (a) actually requires in this case under each approach: the plain words; the purpose of having a circulatory critierion at all (why was it there alongside the brain criterion?); what the drafters would have said if you had asked them about someone on long-term ECMO; and what a reasonable person reading the statute today would take 'cardiac and respiratory function' to mean. Then tell me which approach you would adopt if you were the judge, whether the statute can handle the case without redrafting, and whether the brain criterion is doing any work here. Please answer in British English and keep it under 800 words.
