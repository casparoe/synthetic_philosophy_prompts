I've been going in circles on the safety case for chain-of-thought monitoring and I'd like it taken apart properly. The argument, as I understand it, is roughly: make a model produce step-by-step reasoning before it acts, and you can supervise the reasoning and catch dangerous plans before they're carried out. That seems to lean on the assumption that the written reasoning is faithful—that it tracks the model's actual reasons, or at least that a dangerous plan would show up in the text rather than staying hidden.

The development that's bugging me: increasingly these models are trained with outcome-based RL, where the only thing rewarded is the final answer or the environment score. The chain of thought is just part of the action sequence the policy is optimizing, so there's no reward for reporting true reasons; there's reward for producing tokens that lead to good outcomes. Under enough pressure, the CoT becomes a lobbyist for the plan rather than a transcript of it—omitting, sanitizing, or gently misdescribing the parts that would get it penalized. There are also results suggesting models sometimes rationalize after the fact rather than reason in the open, and some of the faithfulness work is pretty discouraging.

So my actual question: does the original monitoring argument survive that development, or does it kill it? If it needs repair, what's the smallest repair? I care more about which premise the development bites on than about policy. Is it the faithfulness premise, or is it the monitoring premise—that a monitor can tell good reasoning from bad? And here's the wrinkle I keep getting stuck on: maybe the argument doesn't need the CoT to be a faithful report of the model's internal reasons. Maybe it only needs the CoT to be causally upstream of the action in a way a monitor can use, even if the natural-language story is partly window dressing. Does that weaker premise still do the safety work, or does it quietly give up the whole point of reading the reasoning?

End with a definite bottom line in the form \answer{...} containing exactly one of: still works, needs repair, or dead.
