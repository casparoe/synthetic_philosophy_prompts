I've been trying to cash out the intuition that a film wrongs the real dead when it invents a scene in which they do or say something they never did, even granting that the dead have no interests and that audiences know not to read biopics as documentary. I've arrived at what I call the Archival Invariance Condition: a dramatization of a real person may add invented dialogue, interiority, compression, and minor unrecorded scenes only if none of the invented actions, statements, or motives would materially change what a competent historian would infer about that person's character or considered choices, were the invention added to the actual evidence base. A film may give a historical statesman invented small talk with an aide, because the addition is inferentially idle; it may not show him privately accepting a bribe, because a newly discovered ledger proving the bribe would force a wholesale reinterpretation of every documented refusal, so the film has planted a false piece of evidence about the record. I'm aware the condition may be circular at the 'materially change' step, may quietly presuppose posthumous interests, is too conservative for some legitimate art, and says nothing yet about distortion by omission. Give me the three strongest objections, say which existing position this runs closest to, state plainly whether any objection is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable, and then commit: does a suitably repaired Archival Invariance Condition deserve to govern how we judge biopics and historical fiction, or should the whole idea be dropped?
