Long-time reader, first time posting. I'm a journalist who covers misinformation and the psychology of belief for a science outlet, and over the years I've accumulated a stock set of things I say on panels. A collegue recently told me, kindly, that they can't all be true at once, so I wrote them down and I'd like an audit of whether the package hangs together. Here they are, roughly in my own words:

1. Most of us live in epistemic bubbles: we just don't see much of the other side, because attention is finite and feeds are sorted by our own past clicks. That isn't, by itself, an epistemic vice, and I don't blame people for it. What actually worries me is the echo chamber, where outside voices aren't merely absent but are actively discredited as untrustworthy before they can be heard.

2. Deference is unavoidable and usually rational. I outsource nearly everything I believe to a community I already trust — journals, researchers, editors I've worked with, people with a track record — because I can't check everything myself. The demand that I personally verify each claim is a non-starter.

3. The remedy for both bubbles and echo chambers, to the extent there is one, is more exposure to disagreement and better counterspeech, not deplatforming and not heavy-handed moderation. Counterspeech beats censorship, and part of my job is getting readers to sit with arguments they'd otherwise skip.

4. But I also think some groups — flat-earthers, say, or the slicker anti-vax creators — are so sealed and so bad-faith that engaging them wastes time and launders their claims into the mainstream. Nobody has a duty to debate them, and I've argued that editors shouldn't give them the 'both sides' treatment.

5. False balance is a real harm. When an outlet presents two sides as equally serious in a case where the evidence is wildly lopsided, it manufactures uncertainty. The climate coverage of the 2000s is my standing example.

6. And here's the debunking side of me: I think a huge amount of belief formation — mine included — tracks social identity and group loyalty more than it tracks evidence. This applies to left, right, scientists, journalists, everyone. And yet I still think I can end up with reliably true beliefs if I follow the norms I was trained in: check, source, correct, update when I'm wrong.

So: does this set hange together? I'd like the full audit. Which pairs are actually in tension and which only look that way? Order the real tensions by severity, and for each one say what would have to give and what the cheapest fix would cost. Don't pull punches; I'd rather find out now than from a reader correcting me.
