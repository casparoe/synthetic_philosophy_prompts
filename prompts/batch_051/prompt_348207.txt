I'm a novelist, and the book I'm drafting now has a subplot about a town vote on whether to allow a huge new development: if it goes through, the town's population roughly doubles, everyone's life gets a little more cramped and a little more anonymous, but the total amount of well-being across all the town's residents clearly goes up. My main character keeps treating the "everyone slightly worse off, but twice as many people" outcome as obviously worse, and I'm not sure she's right. I realize this is the same shape as questions I use in family scenes — is it good to have another child when it will thin the parents' attention but the child will have a life worth living? — and in a long digressive chapter about whether a species with more numerous but slightly worse-off members is a better state of nature.

So give me your actual credence in this claim, not just a "both intuitions are respectable" dodge: in ordinary, pretheoretic moral thinking about bigger families, denser cities, and larger species, the decisive standard should be total well-being rather than average well-being, provided the added lives are worth living. Decompose it for me. There are at least four subquestions I want separated: (a) whether our ordinary "that would make things worse" reaction to the town case actually tracks average well-being or has some other source, like fear of change or loss of community texture; (b) whether a life that's below the current average but still clearly worth living adds to or subtracts from the value of the whole; (c) how much independent weight inequality among residents should carry, even if it means preferring lower total welfare; and (d) whether there's any basic reason to bring new worth-living lives into existence at all, distinct from our reasons to improve existing lives. Give a rough probability for each, show how you combine them to get the headline number, and tell me which term your final number is most sensitive to. I currently put myself at about 0.3 on the headline claim and a novelist friend at 0.8 — say where you think the 0.3-versus-0.8 gap most likely lives, and what evidence would move that subquestion most.
