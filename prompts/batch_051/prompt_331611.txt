I've been trying to get clear about what statutes of limitations actually commit a legal system to, and the more I look the weirder it is. In the American system it goes roughly like this: simple misdemeanors have to be charged within a couple years, ordinary felonies within maybe five or seven, some frauds get extra time because the clock starts when the victim discovers the loss, certain sex offenses have been extended again and again until they can be brought decades later, and murder has no deadline at all. Civil claims over the same underlying act are often time-barred sooner than criminal charges are, so you can be done being sued but not done being prosecuted. If deadlines were just about evidence going stale—witnesses die, memories fade, receipts get thrown out—the pattern wouldn't really make sense, because evidence in a murder case can decay just like evidence in a burglary. So the graded schedule seems to be taking a view about whether blame itself fades with time, or about when a former defendant's interest in finality is allowed to outweigh the state's interest in punishment, or about which wrongs stay somehow open while property wrongs close. What I'd like from you is a reconstruction of the implicit theory. What view of the moral relevance of time does this pattern presuppose? Which parts of that view are strictly required by the practice and which parts does it merely fit with? And where is the practice genuinely underdetermined—where several different rationales could license the same deadlines? To pressure-test it: suppose a legal system actually believed time matters only epistemically, so the only reason for a deadline is that proof becomes unreliable over time. What would its limitations law have to look like, and which current features would be hardest to defend?
