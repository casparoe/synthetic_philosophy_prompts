My upper-level philosophy of collective action seminar has spent two weeks on why groups make bad calls, and everyone keeps reaching for groupthink or mob irrationality. I want to break that habit with a game that makes them feel the unilateralist's curse before I name it in the debrief. Logistics: 14 students, 75 minutes. Quick refresher in case your memory of it is hazy: the curse is about groups where any single member can act on behalf of everyone, and since each member has noisy private information, the group's decision ends up tracking the most optimistic member rather than the pooled judgment. Bigger groups can get more reckless even when every individual is reasonable and uses the same decision rule. I want that built into the rules, not said by me up front.

Here's the shape I have in mind, but change it if something works better. Split the class into a trio and a group of eleven, and give both groups the same fictional case: there's a shared resource, and anyone in the group can unilaterally approve an intervention for everyone. Maybe it's a residents' association deciding whether to try an untested well-cleaning method, or a lab deciding whether to publish a surprising result. Each player privately draws a card that tells them something about whether the intervention is actually good; the decks are identical in composition, and a player can only act if their card is a strong-good signal. The case itself is made up—the signals don't have to be real evidence, the point is the structure. Use a points pot rather than money: if the intervention goes through and it's actually good, everyone gains points; if it goes through and it's actually bad, everyone loses a chunk; staying put is zero. Run several rounds with reshuffled roles. Tune the deck so that on bad-intervention rounds the trio mostly stays put but the group of eleven usually has at least one person who sees strong-good and pulls the trigger, even though the pooled cards in the big group would mostly say no. Then debrief that gap.

After that, run a second condition that's meant to be a partial fix: either require a player to privately check with one randomly assigned other person before acting (the other person says their signal but can't veto), or introduce a paid sceptic who earns points for correctly arguing that the group should stay put. I want the bad approvals to drop in the second condition, and I want students to notice the point totals change on their own.

Write me the full plan: materials, exact timeline for the 75 minutes, deck compositions and payoff numbers, facilitation notes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the curse, explains why the majority or pooled-card answer would have been different, and why the second condition helps. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. My guess is some players won't act on a strong-good card in round one because they don't want to look reckless in front of the class—say how to handle that without spoiling the point. Then be honest about one thing the classroom version can't reproduce, maybe the irreversability or public embarrassment of real unilateral decisions, and how that limits what the game can show. End by saying how confident you are that this lands on the first run and which part of the design you're least sure about.
