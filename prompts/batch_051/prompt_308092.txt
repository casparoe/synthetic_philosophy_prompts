I sit on the disciplinary review panel at a midsize construction firm, and every few weeks we get a case where somebody broke the chain of command, refused an order, or went around their boss to flag a safety or quality problem. The stakes feel brutal in both directions; if we discipline someone who was rightly speaking up we teach the whole firm to keep its head down, but if we protect someone who was grandstanding or using 'safety' to settle a grudge we get chaos and copycats. We have about an hour per case, three of us on the panel, and none of us have a background in philosophy.

Lately I've been reading about how cultures tried to keep martial courage alive when there wasn't a war on, and it's struck me that a lot of what we call 'speaking up' at work is the same virtue pointed at a different danger. The tradition is subtler than our HR posters: it treats courage not as fearlessness but as a trained habit of judging which risks are worth taking, and it distinguishes the person who is steady because they reasoned about the danger from the person who charges in because they're fiery. The Greeks even had a word for that combative, spirited part of us—thumos—that has to be educated rather than either indulged or stamped out. I want that distinction turned into something our panel can actually run, not another mission statement.

Could you write us a decision procedure for deciding, in a disciplinary case, whether an employee's out-of-line act counts as courage we should protect or recklessness/insubordination we should sanction? It should be a checklist or flowchart in words: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a 'protect' or 'sanction', and where it has to hand the decision back to the panel because the question is a values call rather than a factual one. Be honest about which steps look objective but are really judgment calls, and about the kinds of cases the procedure will systematically mishandle. Then run the procedure on these three cases, and make sure at least one of them comes out in a way the firm will not like—that is, the procedure forces a 'protect' when we would rather sanction, or the other way around—and tell us what in the procedure forces that result. The cases: (1) a junior site engineer halted a concrete pour on a Friday afternoon because the batch temperature was just outside spec, without trying the supervisor who was on site; the delay cost around forty thousand dollars, and the pour probably would of been fine. (2) a quality manager refused to sign a client-facing report that overstated inspection coverage; his performance review that year was already poor and some panel members think he was just making himself unfireable. (3) an engineer emailed the whole project team and a regulator about a pattern of unreported near-misses; two of her five examples turn out to be overstated, but the underlying pattern is real.
