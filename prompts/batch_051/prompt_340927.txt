Working title: *Grammatical categories are real patterns, not natural kinds*

Draft:

The temptation is to treat grammatical categories — *noun*, *verb*, *accusative*, *preposition* — as natural kinds. The usual glosses encourage this: nouns name things, verbs name actions, accusative case marks the patient, prepositions express spatial relations. If that were right, cross-linguistic variation would be a matter of different surface encodings arranged around a fixed conceptual core, and the grammarian's job would be to discover, behind each language's accidents, the same underlying inventory of categories.

The trouble begins as soon as one demands necessary and sufficient conditions. The semantic criteria fail immediately: the noun *destruction* denotes an event rather than a thing, and the adjective *red* denotes a property rather than a manner or quality of action. The standard formal tests do not converge either. A form can look nominal in one environment and verbal in another; English adjectives share distributional properties now with nouns, now with verbs; Mandarin allows many roots to serve as nouns or verbs without a change in form. The straightforward natural-kind view does not survive contact with the data.

But the usual fallback also overcorrects. On that fallback, categories are language-particular, family-resemblance clusters, and cross-linguistic labels such as *adjective* are convenient fictions with no deeper content. The problem is that this makes the recurrence of roughly noun-like and verb-like behavior across unrelated languages look like an improbable coincidence. If categories were purely parochial, we would need an explanation for why unrelated languages keep rediscovering something like a noun–verb distinction, and why agent-like and patient-like marking keeps appearing in case systems and adpositional systems alike.

The claim I want to defend is that grammatical categories are neither natural kinds nor arbitrary descriptive artifacts. They are better understood as attractors in a design space: recurrent, partially stable solutions to a mixture of pressures — semantic expressiveness, predictability in online processing, learnability from noisy input, and the communicative loads a language actually carries. *Noun* and *verb* are not essences but basins in that space. A category is not a hidden joint in nature; it is a compressed description of a recurring equilibrium, and using it commits you to the recurrence of a cluster of properties, not to a common essence behind the cluster.

This reframes comparative grammar. The question "does this language have adjectives?" is less useful than the question "where in the space of property-word strategies does this language's grammar sit?" The same applies to case and adpositions. Case markers and adpositions are often alternative codings of a single underlying relational function — spatial location, instrument, path, recipient — with one language using inflectional case, as with the Latin ablative for instrument-like relations, and another using adpositions, as English does with *by* or *with*. Japanese approaches many of the same functions with postpositions. Treating *case* and *preposition* as two natural kinds gets the typology backwards: the interesting units are the relational functions and the parameters that choose how to code them, not the coding formats themselves.

The conclusion is deliberately deflationary: grammatical categories are real patterns, but they are leaky abstractions. They earn their keep as inferential tickets within a language — if I am told something is a noun here, I can predict a great deal about its distribution — but the abstraction leaks across languages, across constructions, and across diachronic change. The failure mode is not using such abstractions; it is reifying them into a fixed inventory that every language is then forced to fill.

I would appreciate comments on the following, in roughly descending order of importance:

4. Where would a working syntactician or typologist most plausibly object, and is there an easy repair?
5. Is the "leaky abstraction" conclusion too deflationary to be interesting? Should I commit to a stronger claim, and if so, which one?
