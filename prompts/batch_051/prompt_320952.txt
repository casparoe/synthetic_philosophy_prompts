I run a monthly seminar for working quantitative professionals — software engineers, data scientists, a few actuaries — who are comfortable with expected value and Bayesian updating but have rarely thought about how decisions compound across repetitions. Next session is on Kelly betting and geometric rationality, and I want to present it as a general lens on repeated choices under uncertainty rather than a bankroll-management rule for gamblers. Plan how I should teach it in about forty-five minutes, with a whiteboard, to that audience; do not write the lesson itself, but the design of the lesson. Work out the order in which the ideas must be introduced and what each step depends on — I have a tentative sequence (arithmetic expected value; wealth as a multiplicative process; the logarithm as the map that turns multiplicative growth into additive growth; the Kelly fraction as the maximizer of expected log growth; then, and only then, the relationship to utility and risk aversion) — and tell me where that sequence is right, where it is wrong, and where each step becomes vulnerable if a previous one is compressed. Choose one running example that should carry the entire session, and say what it must have and what it must not have in order to keep the arithmetic–geometric contrast visible. Map the misconceptions this audience will bring or form — for example, that Kelly simply assumes logarithmic utility, that risk-neutral or one-shot agents may ignore the geometric picture, that volatility drag is a kind of fallacy, that any positive edge warrants a position, or that the result is an alternative to expected utility theory rather than a reweighting within it — and specify the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off rather than corrected afterward. Also tell me whether to derive the Kelly fraction in full or merely argue for where it peaks, given the audience's mathematical comfort, and what must be in place before a derivation would help rather than distract. Finally, give me one end-of-session check that would separate someone who has grasped geometric rationality from someone who can only recite the formula, and say explicitly what I should decline to cover at this level.
