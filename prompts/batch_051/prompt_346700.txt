Last night a friend and I argued at length about the cosmological argument. He defends the contingency version, and I kept pressing him on whether the principle doing the work overreaches. Late in the argument he turned it around and presented the following; I've tried to write it out faithfully.

(1) Every contingent state of affairs has a sufficient explanation: a reason why it obtains rather than not.
(2) The existence of the universe — the sum total of all contingent things — is itself a contingent state of affairs.
(3) Therefore, by (1), the universe has a sufficient explanation for its existence.
(4) That explanation cannot be another contingent state of affairs: any such state would itself belong to the universe and would stand in need of the same kind of explanation, so the chain would either regress without end or circle back, and neither is a sufficient explanation of the whole.
(5) Therefore the universe is ultimately explained by a being that exists necessarily — a being whose existence needs no explanation outside itself.

(6) Now apply (1) to the fact that this necessary being creates this particular universe rather than another, or none at all. Given (2), this fact is contingent, so it too must have a sufficient explanation.
(7) Either that explanation lies entirely within the being's nature, or it does not.
(8) If it lies entirely within the nature, then the nature alone suffices for the creation, and since the being exists necessarily, the creation obtains in every possible world — so the universe exists necessarily, contradicting (2).
(9) If it does not, then there is some further state of affairs outside the nature that helps explain the creation. If that further state is necessary, it combines with the nature to make the creation, and hence the universe, necessary — again contradicting (2). If it is contingent, then by (1) it demands an explanation, and the same dilemma recurs. Unless the regress stops, no sufficient explanation has been given; if it does stop, it stops only by making the universe necessary.

So the very principle that carries us from a contingent universe to a necessary being seems also to carry us from a necessary being to a necessary universe. He put it this way: you can keep the principle of sufficient reason, or you can keep a genuinely contingent universe, but the contingency argument needs both — so one of them has to go.

I would most like to keep (1) and (2) and still avoid the conclusion that the universe is necessary, but I cannot find the step where this argument overreaches. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost?
