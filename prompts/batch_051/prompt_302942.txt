Here's an argument I keep running into in futarchy discussions and I can't tell whether it's doing real work or just making the whole thing sound safe. People talk about prediction markets as thermometers for the future—passive instruments that just report what's going to happen. Futarchy (the proposal to pick policies by letting prediction markets estimate, for each candidate policy, how well some agreed-on welfare metric would do if that policy were enacted) gets presented as just reading the thermometer before deciding. So if you're worried about letting markets run society, the thermometer story is meant to reassure you: markets aren't deciding anything, they're only measuring, the way a thermometer doesn't heat the room.

I'd like you to audit that analogy, not just say markets aren't literally thermometers. Do the mapping: what's the thermometer, what's the temperature, who's taking the reading? Then walk through where the analogy breaks. My hunch is the damning one is that a thermometer doesn't change the temperature it's measuring, but a prediction market can change the very thing it's supposed to predict, especially when the market's output determines which policy gets adopted. There's also the familiar worry that once a measurement becomes a target it stops being a good measurement, plus the fact that "welfare" isn't a single objective quantity like temperature. But I want to know which of these disanalogies actually matter for the reassurance the analogy is supposed to give, and which are just nitpicks.

So: what does the thermometer framing quietly import about prediction markets that may not be true, and does the conclusion—that futarchy is just letting a neutral instrument inform policy—survive the disanalogies once they're on the table? If the thermometer is the wrong analogy, what's a better one (thermostat? betting shop? something else?), and does the better analogy make futarchy sound worse rather than better?
