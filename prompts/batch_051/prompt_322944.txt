I am giving the opening address at a symposium marking the centenary of a state-sponsored massacre. The room will include historians, diplomats, descendants of victims, and descendants of perpetrators, and the audience is split between those who believe citizens today inherit their ancestors' moral guilt and those who deny that anyone alive bears any responsibility for the past. The organizers have asked me to take a definite position on collective responsibility. Write the address around a Jaspers-style fourfold distinction between criminal guilt, which attaches only to individuals for their own violations of law; moral guilt, which attaches only to individuals for their own choices and omissions; political guilt, which attaches to citizens as members of a political community for what that community does in their name and which persists through the continuity of the state rather than ending with the perpetrators; and metaphysical guilt, the sense of co-responsibility arising from human solidarity with wrongs done in one's presence or with one's knowledge. The speech should own political guilt and its demands of memory, repair, and institutional reform, while refusing to impute moral guilt to anyone merely for having been born into the community, and it should treat metaphysical guilt as something a person may take up but cannot be assigned. Let the distinction control the tone so that the address can admit responsibility without accusing the blameless, land with survivors' families as honest, and land with descendants of perpetrators as neither exculpatory nor defamatory. Keep it to around six hundred words.
