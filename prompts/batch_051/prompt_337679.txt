I've just come from a long argument with a friend about whether it is ever permissible to impose a risk on someone who has not consented. He held an actual-consent view, nearly as a matter of principle: no one may raise another person's chance of harm without that person's genuine, specific agreement. I kept pressing ordinary cases—driving on a shared road, a vaccination campaign, an apartment building's plumbing that might leak into the unit below, a hospital keeping records that could be breached—and he kept absorbing them by concluding that we simply live inside a great deal of unacknowledged wrongdoing. The argument stalled not on the cases but on what we were even asking a theory to do. Before I read any more proposals, I want the full specification. Write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of permissible risk imposition without consent must get right. Include the distinctions it cannot collapse—ex ante risk versus ex post harm; imposing a new risk versus failing to reduce a background risk; risks to identifiable persons versus purely statistical risks; small-probability catastrophic tail risk versus moderate-probability moderate harm; and unilateral risk versus a scheme of reciprocal risk that tends to benefit those exposed. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement. Then flag which requirements are negotiable and which are not, identify any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most work in excluding the obvious candidates: a strict actual-consent view, bare expected-value maximization, and an ex post compensation view. Score those three briefly against the list. A table is acceptable at the end.
