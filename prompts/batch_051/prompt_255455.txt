I am a doctoral student in political theory, writing on the political economy of the effective altruism movement, and I would like an incentive-level analysis of the following proposal. A donor network that has become concerned about measurability bias replaces its flat giving pledge with a counterfactual contribution schedule. Each member's required annual donation is computed as a base percentage of current income plus an opportunity-cost adjustment. A member who remains in a high-salary career pays the adjustment as a surcharge; a member who has left such a career for lower-paid direct or systemic work receives a remission calculated from the salary forgone. The adjustment is set by an elected auditing panel bound by a fixed formula. Foregone salary is counted precisely, from income records. The measurable outputs of a member's current work are counted using the same cost-effectiveness metrics that the network applies to charities. Unmeasurable contributions—political organising, institutional reform, movement-building, public persuasion—may be claimed only through a flat, capped allowance, identical for every member and not subject to the panel's discretion. Treat this as a mechanism rather than a moral position, and say how it would actually operate. What does it incentivise in members' career choices, in the auditors' behaviour, and among employers and colleagues who know of the audit? Where is it gamed first, and by whom? What second-order effects does it have on the division of labour between earning to give and direct work, and between measurable and unmeasurable causes? Can its main failure modes be repaired without collapsing the distinction between measurable and unmeasurable work or abandoning the counterfactual idea altogether? Please answer in British English.
