In W. E. B. Du Bois's 1926 essay Criteria of Negro Art, he appears to advance two positions that are difficult to hold together. At one point he argues, in substance, that all art is propaganda and that Black artists should judge their work by its usefulness in the struggle for racial equality. At another point in the same essay he refuses to let white audiences or white literary standards dictate what Black artists may depict, criticizing the pressure to produce only respectable or uplifting representations and insisting that the Black artist must be free to pursue beauty and truth as the artist sees them. Can these two positions be reconciled without turning one into a mere qualification of the other? What are the strongest candidate reconciliations—for example, different senses of propaganda, different audiences, or a distinction between a work's social function and its internal artistic criterion—and which of them is most faithful to the essay as a whole? If they cannot be reconciled, which claim should be read as Du Bois's deeper commitment here, and what does the tension reveal about the argument he is trying to make?
