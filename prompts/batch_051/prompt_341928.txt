I've been trying to figure out what my city's grandfathering rules are actually committed to. Here's the puzzle. A bar that opened in the 1970s in a building that doesn't meet current fire or accessibility codes can keep running exactly as-is; build the same building today and you'd be shut down. Same for a single-family house in a district that got rezoned commercial years ago: it stays, even though no new house could be built there now. The usual defense is that you can't punish people for doing something that was legal when they did it, and that forcing upgrades would be ruinously expensive. What view of path dependence does this practice presuppose, if any? In particular, does it presuppose that past choices legitimately lock in arrangements we wouldn't choose from scratch today, or is it just a pragmatic compromise about transition costs that says nothing about whether the old outcome was good? Reconstruct the implicit theory, and check it against the fact that many such rules let the old use continue but bar expanding it or rebuilding after a fire — is the practice's line between continuing and expanding coherent under the view you reconstruct?
