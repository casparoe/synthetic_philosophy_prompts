I am a high school student who has been reading about how medical systems decide whom to treat when there is not enough to treat everyone, and I have started collecting my own reactions to concrete cases. The reactions feel roughly consistent, but I cannot state the principle behind them, and I suspect one or two pairs of them are in tension. I have not learned the standard vocabulary for these trade-offs, so I will describe the cases in plain language. In each case, please assume that the patients are equally deserving, none is more at fault for their condition, none has dependents who should influence the decision, and the options cost the system the same amount unless I say otherwise.

1. A single intensive-care bed can go to a 19-year-old who, with it, would recover and live about 60 more years in good health, or to an 81-year-old who would live about 7 more years in good health. I would give the bed to the 19-year-old, and I feel confident about that.

2. The same bed can go to either of two 40-year-olds, each of whom would live 25 more years with treatment. One has long-standing paralysis from a spinal injury, uses a wheelchair, and reports that her life is satisfying. The other has no disability. I would not let the paralysis count against the first patient: a coin flip between them seems right, and a policy that automatically prefers the non-disabled patient seems wrong.

3. A prevention program would immunize a large population and, on current estimates, prevent five premature deaths each year, each death otherwise occurring around age 40. The same amount of money could instead fund an experimental treatment that would save the life of one identifiable six-year-old, who would then live a normal lifespan. Considered from a distance, the prevention program seems to me obviously the better use of the money. But if I imagine myself on the hospital committee deciding on the six-year-old's treatment, refusing it feels monstrous. I currently hold both reactions, and I do not know whether one principle can deliver both.

4. For the same cost, one treatment gives 100 patients one additional year of life each, and another gives a single patient 40 additional years. I would choose the treatment for the 100 patients.

5. I do not feel the same bedside pull in every case: I would be uneasy spending an equally large sum to extend a 90-year-old's life by a few weeks of very poor quality, even though the patient is right in front of me and would otherwise die.

I am unsure which of these verdicts are discardable reactions and which are tracking something I would endorse after reflection. The first two cases seem especially hard to hold together, and the third may involve a different kind of judgment altogether. Please try the obvious simple principles first, but do not stop there: if a principle captures most of my verdicts only by becoming complicated, say where the complexity is doing real work, and if a simpler principle forces me to overturn one of my verdicts, tell me which one and why. State the recommended principle precisely, link each clause to the cases that motivate it, and apply it to a case I have not considered: a treatment that restores vision without extending life to many patients, versus a treatment that extends the life of a smaller number of patients but leaves them in chronic pain. Finally, please end your answer with a line of the form \answer{...} giving a short label or phrase for the principle you endorse, or \answer{no single principle} if that is your conclusion.
