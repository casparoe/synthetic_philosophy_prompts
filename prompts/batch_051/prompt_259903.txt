I teach an upper-level undergrad philosophy of science seminar and next week were doing laws of nature. I want to make a handout that sorts the students into a position by what they say about cases, not by which label they'd pick for themselves.

Write me a decision tree for this. Start from a root like "do laws govern the world or just describe it?" and make sure the leaves include: naive regularity theory, Mill-Ramsey-Lewis / best-system Humeanism, an Armstrong-style view where laws are necessitation relations between universals, dispositional essentialism, and Maudlin-style primitivism about laws. If you think a coherent set of answers also leads to a "there are no real laws, just models and ceteris paribus patches" leaf, put it in.

Each branch node should be a question a student can answer without already knowing these positions. I want the tree built from the logical space of answers first, not from a list of names. Use at least these two probes somewhere: the cosmic mosaic question (if God wrote down every particular occurrent fact about the world, would he ipso facto have written down the laws?) and the twin-world question (could a world match ours in all particular facts but have different laws?). You can add others, like whether a law explains the regularity or just summarizes it, or whether copper's conductivity is part of what copper is.

For any combination of answers that no actual position occupies, flag it and say whether it's empty because it's incoherent, just unattractive, or merely underexplored. Also tell me which pairs of leaves the tree can't cleanly separate and where the residual disagreement between them lives — I suspect best-system Humeanism versus naive regularity gets slippery, and so does Armstrong-style necessitation versus primitivism once both say laws are extra facts.

Keep it usable on one side of one page, maybe eight to twelve nodes. I care more that the diagnostic questions actually bite than that every conceivable position gets a leaf.
