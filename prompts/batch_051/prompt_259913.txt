I spent my career in population genetics, which trained me to treat any readout as signal plus noise and to throw away the noise. In retirement I've been reading philosophy of emotion, and I've realized I've been running a policy for years without making it explicit: I don't let an emotional response count as evidence about whatever set it off. If I'm furious at someone over a paper's author list or a property line, the anger tells me about my blood pressure, my expectations, my childhood, or how much coffee I'd had—not about whether I was actually slighted. Last month an old collaborator sent an email that I read as insulting about my role on a paper we wrote together. The anger arrived fast, and my rule said: close the email, wait a day, then judge the words as if they'd been sent to a stranger. I did that, and concluded it probably wasn't an insult. But I can't tell whether I was taking the noise out of the measurement or talking myself out of a correct read.

My partner, who is not a philosopher but likes arguing, says that if emotions are something like perceptions of value, my policy is systematically discarding data; the anger might be detecting a real slight the way a Geiger counter detects radiation, and my next-day reconstruction might be the confabulated part. I've read enough to know philosophers divide roughly into three camps—emotions as judgments, emotions as perceptions, emotions as bare feelings—and each camp implies something different about whether my habit is defensible. I also know that even on the perceptual view some emotional responses (a startle reflex, a phobia, a bad mood left over from poor sleep) look like pure noise. I don't need a final answer on what emotions really are; I need to know whether this policy is defensible, and how I would tell from the inside whether I'm filtering noise or rationalizing away a real signal. Under what conditions—type of emotion, my expertise about the situation, time pressure, whether the emotion recurs—should an emotional response actually count as evidence? And is there a version of the policy that keeps the filtering without the assumption that emotions carry no information at all?
