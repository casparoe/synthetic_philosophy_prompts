You are an accomplished speechwriter who also moves easily in probability and the philosophy of chance. I am giving a five-minute toast at the wedding of two close friends, and I want you to draft it for me.

The couple met through a chain of coincidences that their families keep calling fate. They grew up three streets apart, went to the same summer camp in different years, once spent the same week in the same tiny beach town without ever crossing paths, and finally met as strangers when a missed flight and a rebooking put them in adjacent seats on a route neither of them had originally planned to fly. The room will be split. Half the guests are the groom's statistician colleagues and former students, who will privately wince if the toast uses "what are the odds" as if a small probability were proof of a cosmic plan. The other half are the bride's family, for whom the word "coincidence" already sounds like a cold dismissal of something wonderful.

I want the toast to build on a specific idea from the epistemology of anecdote: the birthday paradox. In a room of just 23 people, the probability that some pair shares a birthday is already better than even, even though any particular pair has only about a 1-in-365 chance; once there are many people and many possible coincidences, individually rare matches become collectively unsurprising. The point I want the speech to hold, without ever turning into a lecture, is that the probability of this exact meeting was genuinely tiny, while the probability of some striking meeting-pattern across the thousands of crossings in two busy lives is much higher than our naïve intuition expects. That distinction should do real structural work in the toast. It should let the speech be fully honest—no claims that the universe meant for them to meet, no suggestion that the odds prove destiny, no false wonder—without being deflationary—no "merely chance," no "statistically it had to happen to someone," no treating the couple as a generic pair. The relationship should emerge as more, not less, significant because of the honesty.

The speech must not read as a lesson or a probabilistic explainer; at most one image or joke should carry the arithmetic. It should land as affection and wit. It has to work for both halves of the room at once, and a good test is that the statisticians feel the speaker took their standards seriously while the family feels the speaker was warm and entirely on the couple's side.

Before drafting, think through the conceptual tension carefully and brainstorm two or three possible framings—for example, different ways to use the near-misses in the couple's story, or different jokes that quietly encode the birthday arithmetic. Then choose the strongest and write the toast itself, about 550–650 words, in a spoken register. Do not include quotations, citations, footnotes, or a bibliography; this is a speech, not an essay.
