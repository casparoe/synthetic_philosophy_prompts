Taurek argued that an agent choosing between saving one stranger and saving five strangers is not morally required to save the five. On his view the numbers do not by themselves settle the matter: the five deaths are not a single greater loss that any one person bears, and equal concern is owed to each potential victim individually. The argument was framed around an individual agent deciding a particular case in the moment. Now suppose an institution must instead choose in advance a triage rule for many future, anonymous cases—say, allocating scarce intensive-care beds or rescue resources before anyone knows who will occupy which position. From that ex ante standpoint, each affected person appears to have a higher chance of being saved under a rule that always saves the larger group, so the greater-number rule can be defended to each person individually without appealing to any impersonal sum of losses. Does Taurek's argument survive this shift to ex ante rule-making? Identify the premise under pressure, and either show how an anti-aggregationist can restate a coherent ex ante position or explain what has collapsed and what, if anything, remains of the original argument.
