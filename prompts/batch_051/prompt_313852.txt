I’ve been rereading William James’s essay “The Moral Equivalent of War,” and I keep getting stuck on a passage that looks like it is doing more than rhetorical work:

“There is something highly paradoxical in the modern man’s relation to war. Ask all our millions, north and south, whether they would vote now (were such a thing possible) to have our war for the Union expunged from history, and the record of a peaceful transition to the present time substituted for that of its marches and battles, and probably hardly a handful of eccentrics would say yes. Those ancestors, those efforts, those memories and legends, are the most ideal part of what we now own together, a sacred spiritual possession worth more than all the blood poured out. Yet ask those same people whether they would be willing, in cold blood, to start another civil war now to gain another similar possession, and not one man or woman would vote for the proposition.”

I want to treat the asymmetry James identifies there as a serious problem about the place of martial virtues in a peaceful society. The pattern seems to be: we honor courage, sacrifice, discipline, and collective self-transcendence when they are displayed in war, and we even treat the wars that displayed them as sacred parts of our collective identity; but we are not willing to institute a new war in cold blood to gain those same virtues, no matter how much we say we need them. What, if anything, justifies that asymmetry? It might be like admiring a firefighter’s courage while refusing to set fires as a school for courage. Or it might be like valuing great art produced through suffering without endorsing the suffering as a production method. Or perhaps the asymmetry is partly illusory: maybe people would, on reflection, choose to have the Civil War erased and only say otherwise because their identity is already enmeshed in what happened.

Please give me the strongest candidate principle you can find that would make this combination of judgments coherent, and test it hard. Consider, for example, a just war that could be initiated now and would reliably produce comparable civic goods at much lower cost; or a counterfactual choice not to have had a formative war at all; or a society that could create a non-lethal but genuinely transformative ordeal to train martial virtue. If you defend the asymmetry, say exactly where the analogy with setting fires or causing suffering for art breaks down. Also separate what is actually settled in the scholarly discussion of James’s essay and the ethics of war from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as if it were the consensus.
