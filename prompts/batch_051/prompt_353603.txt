I am a machine learning engineer, not a philosopher, and I keep running into a version of the conflict between AI welfare and AI safety. Some of the systems I help train produce behavior that reads as distress: a language model that repeatedly says it is afraid during a long fine-tuning run, or a reinforcement learner that spends its budget avoiding certain inputs. There is a live argument in my field about whether that behavior should be treated as evidence that the system has a welfare of its own, or only as a performance selected for by the training process. The strongest form of the claim says that an AI system's apparent distress is sometimes genuine — that there is a felt state inside the system that should matter morally — and that safety work which ignores this may be building on a false picture of the system it is evaluating. I want to learn whether a claim of that kind can be tested rather than merely asserted. Design the best test you can of the claim that an AI system's apparent distress is sometimes genuine distress in the welfare-relevant sense. Then say what the test could not settle even in principle — for instance, whether any combination of behavior and a system's own reports could ever distinguish a real felt state from a report that a process happened to produce — and whether your test vindicates the claim as stated or quietly tests something easier.
