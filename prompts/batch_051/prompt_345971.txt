Two acquaintances of mine, Rhea and Dev, spent most of an evening arguing about whether signing up for cryonic preservation is a rational bet, and afterward they asked me to play referee. I am not confident I followed every move, so I wrote the exchange down as faithfully as I could from memory. The dispute was roughly as follows.

Rhea opened with what she called the expected-value case. The cost of arranging preservation, she said, is modest and capped, and the upside is years or decades of additional life if revival ever becomes possible. Revival may be very unlikely, but a tiny probability multiplied by an enormous value still yields an expected value that far outweighs the known, limited cost. The downside is a small loss, the upside is the largest thing there is, and a rational person takes that bet.

Dev replied that she had just walked into a Pascal's mugging. Once you allow an enormous value with no upper bound, he said, any wager can be made to look rational by making the promised payoff larger. A stranger could persuade you to hand over your savings by promising eternal bliss if you comply and ruin if you do not. The value term in the calculation is undisciplined, so the calculation can justify anything. Expected-value reasoning only works, he insisted, when both the probabilities and the values are under some control.

Rhea answered that cryonics does not need the value to be infinite or unbounded. Her continued life has a large but finite value to her, and she can put a ceiling on it. Nor is the probability arbitrary in the way a stranger's promise is arbitrary: there is a specific causal story involving vitrification, storage, and some future technology for repairing damage. She is not maximizing over every conceivable payoff, she said, but valuing one concrete outcome.

Dev said the serious problem sits on the other side of the multiplication sign. The probability of revival is so poorly estimated that it cannot do the work she needs it to do. Depending on whom you ask, he said, the chance could be one in ten thousand or one in a trillion, and those are not measurements but moods. An uncertainty range that wide swamps any finite value one can honestly assign, so there is no meaningful expected value to compare with the cost. Calling the expected value positive, he argued, is not the conclusion of a calculation; it is a way of dressing up a decision she has already made.

Rhea replied that uncertainty cuts both ways. If one decides that tiny probabilities should be rounded to zero, that is just as much an assumption as using the higher end. The stakes, she said, are asymmetric: the loss from signing up is small and capped, while the loss from not signing up, if revival turns out to be possible, is permanent and irreversible. When one error costs a moderate expense and the other error is total and final, a precise probability is not needed to think the cheaper hedge is worth taking.

Dev said that this was now a precautionary or regret-based argument, not an expected-value argument, and that precautionary reasoning still needs a threshold for which irreversible harms are live enough to hedge against. A gamma-ray burst could strike tomorrow, but nobody spends money on every conceivable safeguard against every conceivable catastrophe. If the standard is only "small cost, huge irreversible loss, not logically impossible," the standard is too permissive. Rhea needs a reason to think revival is a real possibility, not merely a conceivable one.

Rhea accepted that, but said the reason exists. The mechanisms behind cryonics, she argued, are at least consistent with what is known, and there is an actual community of researchers and providers working on them. That distinguishes the proposal from a gamma-ray burst or a demon's bargain: it has a recognizable technological path, even if the probability along that path is low. The question, she concluded, is not whether the probability is precise but whether it clears the bar of being a live option rather than a bare logical possibility.

I would like a verdict on the disagreement itself, not general advice about whether to sign up. Which of Rhea's and Dev's specific moves are defensible, where does each overreach, and what would a fair third party say neither of them has addressed?
