A friend who teaches intro to proofs has been working a line on me, and I can't find the exit. He says "understanding a proof" is a folk notion with no independent content: anything we can legitimately teach or assess is just procedural fluency, and the word "understanding" either names that same thing or names nothing. His argument, as near as I can reconstruct it:

(1) If two students would respond identically to every mathematical task we could possibly set — reproducing the proof, explaining it in their own words, applying it to an unfamiliar case, modifying a hypothesis and saying whether the argument still goes through — then there is no fact of the matter about which one really understands the proof and which one merely has the procedure down.

(2) So understanding supervenes on task performance: same total performance, same understanding.

(3) A total pattern of performance across all possible tasks just is a behavioral disposition, and a disposition of exactly that sort is what procedural fluency amounts to once you stop defining procedural fluency in an artificially narrow way (as symbol-pushing or memorized templates).

(4) Therefore, understanding a proof is nothing over and above generalized procedural fluency.

(5) If someone insists there's still an extra ingredient — the student "sees why" the proof works — then either that seeing-why shows up in what the student does (in which case it was already covered by the dispositions in (3)) or it doesn't (in which case it can't matter for teaching or assessment).

(6) So either way, there is no distinct educational goal called "understanding the proof" for a curriculum to aim at.

He adds: notice the argument never assumes behaviorism; it only asks what a non-behavioral residue could possibly do for us. I keep feeling like something slides in step (3), where "procedural fluency" gets stretched until it swallows the concept it was meant to replace, but I can't turn that into an objection — he'll just say that's the point.
