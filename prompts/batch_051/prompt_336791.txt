You are a monetary theorist with a background in the history of economic thought. I have been having a recurring argument with friends about what actually makes something money: one friend holds that money must be, or be anchored to, a commodity with intrinsic value; another holds that money is simply whatever the state will accept in payment of taxes; a third insists that Bitcoin is money even though it is neither a commodity with non-monetary use nor issued by a state. The dispute has made me want to return to an older and, I suspect, underused framework.

For the purposes of this prompt, accept the following reconstruction of the eighteenth-century philosopher George Berkeley's account of money, drawn loosely from his economic writings. Berkeley held that the real wealth of a community is not any stock of precious metal but its productive power — roughly, the useful labour, skill, and provision it can command. Money, on his view, is best understood as a ticket or counter: a conventional instrument whose value consists in its ability to move and measure that underlying productive power, not in the material out of which it is made. Because the value of money is conventional and relational, he argued, a well-secured paper currency issued against a nation's real assets can do the work of money better than gold and silver, which can be hoarded, exported, or made artificially scarce in ways that crush trade and employment. Berkeley developed this case in the specific setting of eighteenth-century Ireland, but I want to set that historical setting aside and treat the underlying theory as a live option.

Please describe the most important implications of accepting Berkeley's account of money, reconstructed in that way, for three present-day debates. First, chartalism versus commodity theories: does Berkeley's framework cut cleanly in favour of the view that money is a creature of accepted convention, does it side with the commodity view, or does it force a reframing of that whole dispute? Second, cryptocurrencies: under Berkeley's test, would Bitcoin or a generic stablecoin count as money, as a legitimate circulating credit instrument, or as something else, and which feature of the account is doing the work in that verdict? Third, cashless payments and central-bank digital currency: what would a central bank that took Berkeley seriously be most concerned about getting right, and what would it regard as merely cosmetic? In answering, do not rely on facts from economic history or on any particular crypto-asset's price or adoption data; work from the reconstructed account itself, say plainly where the account is silent or underdetermines the answer, and make your inferences explicit. Think carefully, and if you judge that one of my three questions is ill-posed given the theory, say so and replace it with the question the theory is better suited to answer.
