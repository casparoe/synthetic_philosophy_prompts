I run the recognition program at a manufacturing company with two big offices, one in Copenhagen and one in Charlotte. Every quarter the office heads submit names for a companywide "MVP" award, and every quarter it goes wrong in a different way: in Charlotte the same lone-wolf closer wins three times running while the support staff stew about it, and in Copenhagen the managers quietly refuse to nominate anyone because, as one of them put it to me, nobody wants to be the tall poppy. So I've been reading a bit about Jantelagen — the Scandinavian "don't think you're special" script — and about tall poppy syndrome and its opposite, the American Dream idea that you should celebrate the self-made high performer. I'd like you to turn that contrast into a procedure I can actually run each cycle: for a given candidate and office, what questions should we ask, in what order, and what answers push us toward a public individual award, a team award, a quiet private recognition, or no recognition at all? It has to be usable by a non-HR manager in twenty minutes without a culture survey, and it should be explicit about the places where the manager's own cultural instincts are doing the deciding rather than the procedure.

Then apply it to three candidates: (1) a Copenhagen engineer who is genuinely the reason her six-person team shipped early, on a team that is tight-knit and whose lead consistently downplays her own contribution; (2) a Charlotte sales rep who broke the annual record on a deal where inside sales, legal, and support did most of the actual work; (3) a Copenhagen manager the C-suite wants to honor publicly for turning around a failing unit, even though his own team says he takes credit for their ideas. Make sure at least one of the three comes out in a way I won't like. And if you think the whole premise is wrong — that "celebrate versus downplay" isn't something you can standardize across offices without just building each office's local norms into the machine by hand — say so plainly and tell me what you'd use instead. Also mark the steps where you're least confident in the underlying psychology; I'd rather know which parts are judgment calls with a nice label than have them hidden as policy.
