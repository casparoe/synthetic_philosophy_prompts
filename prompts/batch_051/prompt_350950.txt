Development economics has developed a fairly stable professional norm about what counts as credible causal evidence concerning the deep determinants of national prosperity: institutions, geography, and culture. A cross-country regression of per-capita income on an index of institutional quality is treated as merely suggestive, while an analysis using an instrumental variable for institutions, or a natural experiment in which institutions changed for reasons plausibly unrelated to income, is treated as much more credible. For example, scholars have used differences in settler mortality as an instrument for the institutions established in former colonies, and have appealed to long-run reversals in the relative prosperity of colonized regions to argue that geography alone cannot explain the observed pattern.

Please reconstruct the implicit theory of causation, and of evidence for causation, that this methodological practice presupposes. In doing so, address at least the following:

1. What makes exogenous variation in institutions better evidence than raw correlation? Spell out the causal assumptions that must hold for an instrument or a natural experiment to identify the effect of institutions, including the assumption usually called the exclusion restriction.

2. Does the practice presuppose that institutions, geography, and culture are separable causes whose independent contributions can in principle be measured, or is it compatible with the view that, over the course of history, they are so entangled that the question of how much each contributes is not well posed? If the practice is compatible with several such views, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with.

3. The practice treats historical processes as if they could at least approximately mimic randomized assignment. What view of historical explanation does that presuppose, and what does it rule out? For instance, does it quietly exclude explanations in which a country's institutions and its later growth share a common underlying cause, so that no clean intervention on institutions alone is even conceivable?

4. If development economics adopted instead a mechanistic or process-tracing view of causation, where the aim is to trace how a particular institutional change sets off a specific chain of effects in a particular country, what would change about what gets published, what gets believed, and what gets learned? Is the current practice better understood as a genuine epistemological commitment, or as a pragmatic response to the fact that there is only one history and no counterfactual reruns in which institutions are altered while everything else is held fixed?

Finally, say how confident you are in this reconstruction, and single out the point in your answer about which you are least sure.
