Last night I spent hours arguing with a close friend about negative utilitarianism, and I came away genuinely unsure which of us conceded the right thing. I would like you to act as a careful philosophical referee on this dispute: take the role of a professional philosopher who works on population ethics and formal axiology, reason through the problem step by step, and begin by writing brief notes on the strongest version of each side before you deliver your final assessment.

I find myself drawn, on reflection, to a suffering-focused view. As I understand it, the view holds that suffering is the only thing that is ultimately bad, and that happiness matters morally only insofar as it relieves, prevents, or compensates for suffering. The theory has genuine virtues. It explains a real asymmetry: while we seem to have no obligation to create a merely happy person, we seem to have a strong obligation not to create a miserable one. It refuses to let a vast amount of mild contentment simply cancel out extreme torment. It yields a clean priority rule in trade-offs — alleviate the most severe suffering first. And it captures something I find hard to deny: the most urgent moral demand on us, when we can meet it, is to stop serious harm.

But my friend pressed the disturbing consequence I could not bring myself to accept. If there were a button that painlessly and instantly ended all sentient life, eliminating all future suffering, the view as stated seems to imply that a fully impartial agent should press it, or at least that the resulting empty world would not be worse. I find that conclusion almost viscerally wrong. A world containing much suffering alongside an enormous amount of flourishing seems to me better than no world at all, and pressing the button seems monstrous even if the transition itself harms no one. Yet in the argument I could not locate the flaw in my friend’s reasoning, and my friend kept insisting that my resistance is nothing more than status-quo bias — an inability to imagine the empty outcome from within rather than an insight into its value.

I do not want a scoreboard verdict. I want to know what would have to be true for the intuition to be the thing that gets debunked and dropped, what would have to be true for the theory to be the thing that gets amended or abandoned, and whether there is a reading on which both survive — for instance, by separating the axiological claim from the deontic claim, or by showing that the theory is not actually committed to the button case. Give me the strongest possible defense of each side before you negotiate between them, and make explicit which parts of each, if any, can be kept.
