William James, in "What is an Emotion?" (1884), imposes a deliberate restriction on his inquiry before defending his famous thesis. He writes:

"I should say first of all that the only emotions I propose expressly to consider here are those that have a distinct bodily expression. That there are feelings of pleasure and displeasure, of interest and excitement, bound up with mental operations, but having no obvious bodily expression for their consequence, would, I suppose, be held true by most readers."

I have been using this passage to pull apart two commitments that normally travel together under the name "Jamesian," and I want the distinction stress-tested. I call the first commitment Body Sufficiency: for the "standard" emotions James proceeds to discuss, the feeling of the bodily disturbance is the whole emotion — there is no non-bodily residue left over once the felt bodily changes are described. The second commitment I call Body Necessity: no state counts as an emotion unless it includes the feeling of a bodily disturbance; strip away every felt bodily element from fear, and what remains is not muted fear but a pale, non-emotional cognition that the situation is dangerous. My proposal is that James's real claim in the passage is Body Necessity, not Body Sufficiency, and that the two are routinely conflated in both the attack on and the defense of his view. Consequently, I suspect most contemporary appraisal theorists and perceptualists are really arguing about Sufficiency while implicitly accepting or dodging Necessity — and that Necessity is where the live debate should be.

I want to know whether Body Sufficiency and Body Necessity actually come apart once each is sharpened, or whether one collapses into the other. If they do come apart, does the quoted passage commit James to both, to Necessity alone, or only to a pragmatic decision about which cases he will discuss? Is this split already marked in the literature under other labels, or am I treating a real but unnoticed difference as a discovery? And the harder question: is Body Necessity a defensible criterion for the cases James sets aside — intellectual pleasure in solving a problem, aesthetic emotion, or moral response without any felt somatic signature? My fear is that I have accidentally reinvented the old "cognition plus feeling" view with new terminology, and that Necessity gives out exactly at the cases my distinction was meant to classify.
