I'm a high school student writing my final paper for a Comparative Government course, and I want to argue for a definition of a secular state rather than just describe four countries. I've drafted four criteria: (1) no denomination holds the status of an official state religion; (2) the state does not fund or subsidize religious institutions; (3) religious officials have no guaranteed role in the legislature or the office of head of state; and (4) the state's own institutions, including public schools, do not provide religious instruction or promote one faith. Against these, the United States passes all four, and England clearly fails (1) and (3) because the Church of England is established and 26 of its bishops sit in the House of Lords. So far the criteria seem to work.

The hard case is Germany. Germany has no established church and no bishops in the Bundestag, so it passes (1) and (3). But the state collects a church tax, a surcharge on income tax of roughly 8 to 9 percent, from registered members of certain denominations and remits the money to those churches; the only way to stop paying is to formally declare that you have left the church. Public schools also offer denominational religious instruction that is supervised partly by the churches, with an opt-out available. Under my criteria (2) and (4), Germany seems to fail, yet Germany is routinely called a secular state. The same tension shows up in Alsace-Moselle, where France, which I had treated as a clear pass, still pays clergy salaries under the old concordat. Please act as a political philosopher who works on religion and the state. Think through the criteria carefully, maybe by brainstorming possible refinements, and then tell me: is Germany a secular state under my four criteria? More importantly, which criteria do these cases show to be too broad, too narrow, or ambiguous, and how should I revise them if I want the United States, England, Germany, and France to come out in the order I expect? Keep the response to at most 500 words.
