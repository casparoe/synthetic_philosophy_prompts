A friend and I fell into an argument after we both read a popular essay about Malthus and the overpopulation warnings of the 1970s. My friend thinks the standard dismissal of Malthus — that he was simply refuted because agricultural technology kept output ahead of population — is too quick, and he laid out his reasoning like this. (1) For any prediction that hasn't come true, you can always say that it hasn't come true yet. (2) So the bare fact that a predicted event still hasn't occurred never refutes a long-run prediction; that fact is always compatible with the prediction's still being right. (3) A refutation would instead have to appeal to the timescale, but a defender can always reply that the timescale was only provisional — if the underlying mechanism operates slowly, then the prediction's not having played out on the original schedule doesn't refute it. (4) Malthus's claim was a long-run claim of exactly this sort: population tends to outgrow the food supply, and unless something checks it — famine, disease, or deliberate restraint — the gap eventually produces mass misery. (5) The usual objection to Malthus is that this mass misery keeps not arriving: the industrial revolution, the Green Revolution, and modern agriculture kept the food supply ahead of population. (6) Given (2) and (3), a defender of Malthus can always say the crisis is still in the pipeline and that Malthus was simply right but early. (7) So there is no evidence that can refute Malthus's long-run claim, and, by exactly the same reasoning, no evidence that can refute any long-run prediction; "right but early" collapses into "failed but not abandoned." I want to resist that conclusion because it seems to prove too much — it would make every long-run prediction, no matter how bad, effectively immune to evidence. But I can't find the step where his reasoning slips, and I worry my resistance is just sentimentality. Is the argument sound? If not, which step should go, and what would rejecting it cost? I'd most like to keep the possibility that Malthus identified a real mechanism on the wrong timetable. And whichever way you land, please tell me where your reasoning is most likely to fail and what argument or consideration would change your mind.
