I've been turning over a variant of Russell's theory of descriptions and I can't tell wether what falls out is a real problem or just the view doing its thing. The standard case is the present King of France when there isn't one: Russell says the sentence is false, not truth-valueless, because the existence condition fails. Now change the setup. France is a diarchy with two co-equal kings, Albert and Bruno. Albert is bald, Bruno is not.

Take the sentence The present King of France is bald. On Russell's analysis it's false because uniqueness fails—there is no x such that x is a king of France and every king of France is identical to x. So it comes out false even though one of the two kings is bald. Okay. Then take The present King of France is not bald. Work through the scope reading carefully. I get it as: negative taking the whole sentence (secondary occurrence of the description) gives true, since it negates a false sentence; negative on the predicate with the description primary gives false, since uniqueness still fails. Is that right?

The part that nags me is this: The present King of France is male would also be false, even though both kings are male. So when there are zero, two, or more satisfiers, the uniqueness clause swamps the predicate entirely—the predicate only gets a say in the exactly-one case. I can't decide whether that's a bug, a feature, or just a misunderstood prediction. Does Strawson's presupposition view do better, worse, or simply different here—especially when the conversational context makes one of the two kings salient? And does Donnellan's referential/attributive distinction earn its keep in the diarchy case: imagine someone points at Albert and says The present King of France is bald while wrongly believing there is only one king. What does each of the three views say about that utterance, and does the two-kings case push toward any one of them?
