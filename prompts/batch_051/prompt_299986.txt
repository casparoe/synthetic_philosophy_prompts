I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but I keep ending up in arguments with my editor about wether a scene reads as consensual, so I've built up a list of my own verdicts. I want to know if there's a single principle behind them or whether I'm just inconsistent. All the cases are straight couples, he/she for convenience.

1. He tells her he's had a vasectomy so they skip the condom. He hasn't. I can't write this as consensual. The act she said yes to isn't the act that happened.

2. He tells her he's in love with her to get her into bed. He isn't. I think she consented, and I wouldn't call it assault — he's a liar and used her, but that's not the same thing.

3. A boss tells an intern that if she won't sleep with him, he won't renew her contract and he'll bad-mouth her all over the industry. She says yes. I write that as non-consensual, even though nobody held her down.

4. A husband tells his wife the marriage is over unless they have sex more often. She agrees without enthusiasm. I honestly don't know how to write this one. It feels closer to the boss than to ordinary marital negotiation, but she isn't facing ruin.

5. Two people at a party, about equally tipsy and equally into it. Fully consensual, no problem.

6. Same party, but one person is basically sober and the other can barely stand, and the drunk one keeps saying yes. I write the sober one as having done something wrong, even with the yes.

7. A man lets a woman assume he's single when he's married, and she wouldn't have slept with him if she'd known. I keep going back and forth and the scene stays unfinished.

Is there a principle that gets cases 1 through 6, and which of my verdicts does it force me to drop if it can't? Then tell me what it says about case 7, and about a person who agrees because they badly need money. Lead with the principle, then test it against the cases, then tell me which of my verdicts I should overturn. Keep it under 400 words and in plain English — no citations.
