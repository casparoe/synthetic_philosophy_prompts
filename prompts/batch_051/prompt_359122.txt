I'm on the board of a small family foundation — we give out about $1.2 million a year in grants between $5,000 and $50,000, and we get maybe 400 unsolicited proposals a year, of which we can fund around twenty. I keep hearing people talk about evaluating causes by importance, tractability, and neglectedness, but every time I try to apply it I end up with three vague scores and no idea what to do with them. I want a screening procedure our all-volunteer, non-expert board can actually run on each proposal in about twenty minutes, before we decide whether it deserves a full board vote or a polite no.

The procedure should be a sequence of questions in order, with what counts as a good-enough answer to move to the next question, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is a values call rather than a facts call. The people using it are a retired dentist, a high school teacher, and two small-business owners, and none of them will read background material first. We can't hire a program officer and we can't do site visits; the screen runs on a one-page summary from the applicant plus a short phone call.

I've seen people multiply the three numbers, I've seen people add them, and I've seen people just eyeball it, so I don't just want a scoring rubric. I want to know how the three considerations actually bear on each other in practice: when neglectedness matters at all, how high importance with poor tractability should be handled, and whether a tiny, neglected cause that no one can do anything about should pass. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and which cases the procedure should seperately flag for the board rather than decide.

Then run the procedure on three proposals: (1) a summer math camp for gifted low-income kids in our county, run by the founder's daughter; (2) a pilot to open public cooling centers in a nearby city that's having more frequent extreme heat; (3) a lab studying a rare genetic disease that affects roughly two hundred people a year.

Tell me where the procedure gives an answer the board won't like, and what it can't do.
