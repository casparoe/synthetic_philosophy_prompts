In a reading group I run on early modern theories of personal identity, we have spent three sessions on the exchange between Locke and Reid, and while everyone now agrees on the facts of the two positions, we appear to be stuck. The disagreement concerns how to weigh a passage in Locke. In Book II, Chapter XXVII of An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, after arguing that the identity of a mass of matter consists in the same particles, Locke writes: ‘In the state of living creatures, their identity depends not on a mass of the same particles, but on something else.’ One camp in the group reads this as evidence that Locke already treats identity as a sortal-dependent relation and not as strict numerical sameness; they think this gives him resources to accommodate the intransitivity involved in Reid’s brave officer. The other camp reads it narrowly: for organisms the ‘something else’ is the organised life, and for persons it is consciousness, and the resulting relation is still presented as an identity; on their view, the sortal move does not help because Reid’s objection is that consciousness, unlike life, is not a transitive relation across whole lives. The debate has stalled at exactly this point: both sides agree about what Locke says and about Reid’s objection, but they keep repeating the same move and counter-move. I would like a diagnosis, not a verdict for one side. Specifically: is this a verbal disagreement about what ‘identity’ means in this context, a substantive disagreement about what a criterion of identity must do, or two groups asking different questions—one about metaphysical constitution, one about forensic responsibility and memory? Is there a hidden assumption about transitivity that one side holds and the other rejects? And, given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress—a distinction, an argument, a reading of the text—or should we stop expecting the debate to resolve? Please answer in British English, and keep the response structural rather than adjudicating the two readings.
