I am a PhD student in the philosophy of computation, and I need help disentangling the epistemic status of the Church-Turing thesis from that of the physical Church-Turing thesis. Please explain the distinction carefully, and in particular address the following. First, in what sense is the original thesis a claim about the informal notion of an effective method or algorithm, and why does that status prevent it from being either a formal theorem or an ordinary empirical hypothesis? Second, what exactly does the physical Church-Turing thesis add to the original one: is it the claim that every finitely realizable physical process can be simulated by a Turing machine, the claim that no physical process can compute a non-Turing-computable function, or something else, and why does this second claim appear to carry empirical risk in a way the first claim does not? Third, suppose a physical process were discovered that computes a function outside the Turing-computable class — for instance, a device that exploits a relativistic Malament-Hogarth spacetime to complete infinitely many computational steps in finite proper time and thereby appears to decide the halting problem. Explain whether such a discovery would refute the original Church-Turing thesis, the physical thesis, both, or neither, and whether the correct response would instead be to refuse to classify the process as executing a finite procedure. Finally, say whether any finite body of physical evidence could settle the physical thesis in either direction, and what kind of claim remains if it is treated not as a falsifiable conjecture but as a regulative principle about what is to count as a physical computation.
