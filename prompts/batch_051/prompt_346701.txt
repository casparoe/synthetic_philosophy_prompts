I have been working on a project on institutional design, and I have been reading Lewis on convention — in particular, the idea that a convention is an arbitrary but self-sustaining solution to a recurring coordination problem, kept in place by common knowledge and precedent rather than by the content of the rule itself. A group I work with is considering a proposal that tries to engineer exactly this from the top down, and I want to understand how it would actually behave as a mechanism rather than whether it sounds appealing in the abstract.

The proposal is for a municipal Office of Convention Revision. Each year the Office draws up a registry of recurring local coordination problems: greeting customs, who gives way on narrow footpaths, how shared laundry machines in apartment buildings are reserved, and similar low-stakes repeated interactions. For each registered norm, the Office publishes two things — an empirical estimate of the current regularity (what most people in the relevant situation actually do) and a single proposed replacement rule, chosen by lottery from resident submissions. It then runs a coordination referendum restricted to residents who self-report encountering the situation at least twice a month. A replacement rule is certified only if it wins a two-thirds majority of those voters and at least 85 percent of reported regulars pledge to follow whatever passes; otherwise the incumbent remains certified. Crucially, every proposed replacement — winning or losing — is published for a full year in a public Convention Gazette, along with the vote totals and pledge rates. Official signage, newsletters, and school materials then teach whichever rule is certified as the active convention.

Analyze this as a Lewisian would. Does the mechanism tend to produce genuine conventions in the relevant populations, or does it mainly produce official-looking regularities that fail the common-knowledge or conditional-preference conditions? What does it incentivize for residents, for proposers, and for the Office itself, and how would each of them game it? What happens to coordination when a losing alternative is deliberately given that much publicity? Trace the likely second-order effects after three or four cycles, and say which failure modes could be fixed without gutting the scheme and which could not.
