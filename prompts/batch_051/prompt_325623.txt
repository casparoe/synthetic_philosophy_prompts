I put roughly 0.35 credence on the following proposition: the standard willingness-to-accept/willingness-to-pay gap reflects a real, welfare-relevant attachment to things one owns—or to reference points more generally—rather than a decision error or experimental artifact. She puts roughly 0.8 on the same proposition. I would like your own number, but more importantly the decomposition. State your credence that, after the best available experimental controls, the residual endowment effect is better explained by genuine reference-dependent preferences that welfare analysis should respect than by residual bias or artifact. Then decompose it into at least these terms: (a) the probability that the gap is not largely an artifact of transaction costs, strategic misreporting, subject inexperience, or experimenter demand; (b) the probability that, conditional on a real gap surviving, it reflects a pattern of valuation the subject would endorse on reflection rather than a framing effect she would repudiate; (c) the probability that a distinction between true and merely constructed preferences is coherent enough to carry the bias-versus-legitimate-preference distinction here; and (d) the probability that, if genuine, reference-dependent ownership valuation should count as welfare-relevant for policy rather than as an input to be debiased. Give a number and a one-line justification for each term, show how they combine into your overall credence, and say which term the final number is most sensitive to. Then tell me which of the two people at 0.35 and 0.8 most plausibly disagree about, and what evidence or argument would most move your own number.
