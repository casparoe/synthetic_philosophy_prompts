In A System of Logic, Book III, John Stuart Mill advances two positions that are harder to hold together than he acknowledges. In Chapter VI, on the composition of causes, he maintains that in mechanics the joint effect of several causes is the sum of the effects each would have produced separately, so that the law of a system can be derived from the laws of its parts together with the law of their combination. But he denies that this holds throughout nature: in chemistry, he says, the properties of a compound are not an additive function of the properties of its constituent elements, and he calls the laws that govern such cases heteropathic. In Chapter V, however, he has already defended the law of universal causation, according to which every event has a cause and the uniformity of nature licenses inductive inquiry. Reconstruct the apparent conflict between Mill's admission of non-additive, heteropathic laws and his commitment to universal causation. Lay out at least two candidate reconciliations—for example, one that treats the composition principle as a domain-limited empirical generalization, and one that treats heteropathic laws as an epistemic label rather than a causal exception—and evaluate them. Is there a reading on which the two chapters are consistent without making one claim merely verbal? What distinction between composition of causes and causal uniformity must Mill draw to make the position stable, and does that distinction do its work without special pleading?
