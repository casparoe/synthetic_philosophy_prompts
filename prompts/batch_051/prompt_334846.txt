I'm a software engineer on a platform team, no philosophy background, and we're arguing about our merge policy. Right now any engineer can approve their own PR and merge if CI passes. For most code that's fine, and keeping it that fast matters. But for shared infrastructure—auth, schema migrations, API contracts—one person's decision can break everyone, and unlike a normal bug it's hard to unwind. We have about forty engineers, so even if each person only merges these when they privately think it's justified, at the group level the bad ones feel basically guaranteed: it only takes one confident person on one bad Friday.

I've been trying to write down when to switch from anyone-can-act to need-several-independent-approvals. My rough test for the danger zone is five criteria:

1. The actor captures most of the benefit or convenience, but the cost of being wrong lands mostly on others.
2. Reasonable people genuinely disagree about whether the action is good, and each has private information others dont.
3. The action is cheap for the actor, so there's little natural brake.
4. The bad outcome is hard or expensive to reverse.
5. Waiting for a second or third opinion is cheap relative to the downside.

The borderline case we keep litigating: a senior engineer unilaterally merged a sweeping refactor of our logging library over a weekend. It wasn't urgent. It touched about two hundred files and configs, broke downstream dashboards CI couldn't see, and ate a week of cleanup. But the refactor itself was arguably right—the old API had caused real outages—and the senior dev had a ton of private context on why the old design kept failing. By my criteria I genuinely cant tell whether this is a should-have-required-sign-off decision or just a normal autonomous action that happened to go wrong, where the lesson is better tests rather than more process.

So: does that case qualify under my criteria? Walk through each criterion, tell me where the criteria underdetermine the verdict, and say whether the case shows I need to add, drop, or sharpen a criterion. I'd also like to know if criterion 5 is quietly doing all the work and smuggling in the conclusion.
