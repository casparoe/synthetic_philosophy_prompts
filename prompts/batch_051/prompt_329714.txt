In my bioethics class we've been doing a unit on how death gets legally defined, and our teacher gave us a handout with the part of our state's law that says when someone counts as dead. It reads: "A person is dead when at least one of the following has happened: (a) the person's heartbeat and breathing have stopped and cannot start again, or (b) the person's whole brain, including the brain stem, has stopped working and cannot start again." I know its copied from somewhere, but the teacher says the exact wording is the point.

Then she gave us a case the people who wrote the law definately did not have in mind. A patient gets a disease that destroys his heart muscle and scars his lungs so badly that neither will ever work on its own again. He's hooked up to an ECMO machine that pumps his blood and oxygenates it outside his body, so his circulation and breathing keep going even though his own heart and lungs are done. He's awake the whole time, talks to his family, and is basically just waiting to see if he can get a transplant. Reading (a) literally, his heartbeat and breathing have stopped and can't restart, so he'd be dead—but that seems absurd because he's sitting there talking to you. Kids in my class said the machine is doing his circulation and breathing, so those functions haven't actually stopped. But then I don't see why you can't say the same about a brain-dead person on a ventilator: a machine is doing their breathing too, but everyone still says they're dead because of (b). Are we just letting machines count for the heart and lungs but not the brain, or is there an actual principled reading that separates the two?

We have to write a response laying out how the plain words, the law's purpose, what the drafters would have said if you could ask them, and what a reasonable person would take the law to mean each come out on the ECMO case, and then say which reading is best. I keep going in circles, so I'd really like someone to actually work through it.
