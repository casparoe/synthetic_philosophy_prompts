Consider the older cyber-libertarian argument that territorial speech law is the wrong model for the internet. As originally made, it ran roughly like this: online expression has no single location, or is everywhere at once; a state that objects to some content can at best filter it at the border, while the speaker and the server remain beyond its reach; so online speech should be governed by the platforms' self-regulation and by user choice, not by the speech laws of the countries where the content happens to be read. Since then, major jurisdictions have reversed the premise by regulating foreign platforms through their local users, advertisers, or assets; platforms generally comply by geoblocking the offending content or the whole service rather than exiting the market, effectively building jurisdictional borders as artifacts of compliance. Does the original argument survive this development, survive only in a repaired form, or is it dead? Identify the exact premise the new enforcement environment bears on, and say what still holds once that premise is revised—in particular, whether the argument's normative objection to territorial regulation does any work independently of its factual claim about the internet's borderlessness. State how confident you are in the diagnosis and which claim you are least sure of.
