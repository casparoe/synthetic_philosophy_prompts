Give me a working definition of an object-given reason for an attitude — the sort of consideration that counts in favour of the attitude itself because of what the attitude's object is like, as opposed to a state-given reason, which counts in favour of having or acquiring the attitude because of what having it would do for you. Test the definition against each of the following cases, and revise it, or defend the surprising verdict, where it misfires.

- Admiring a mathematician because her proof is elegant.
- Admiring a powerful official because he will approve your visa only if you genuinely admire him.
- Believing a friend is innocent because the alibi evidence supports that belief.
- Believing a friend is innocent because the alternative would leave you too despondent to help.
- Fearing a harmless snake that is, to you, indistinguishable from a venomous one.
- Fearing an aggressive dog that has bitten before.
- Being angry at someone who maliciously humiliated you.
- Being angry at someone because you know the anger will make you a more effective negotiator in an unrelated meeting.

I regard the first member of each pair as object-given and the second as state-given, but the snake case undermines every definition I try, and I am not sure that the last two cases really fall on different sides. State your definition, run it through all eight cases, and indicate where it yields a surprising verdict or no verdict. Please answer in British English.
