Two members of my history seminar recently argued about whether Nazi Germany could have happened without Hitler, and they asked me to referee. One defended a structural explanation and the other defended the residual importance of individual agency, and I honestly could not decide: each seemed to win some exchanges and lose others. I would like you to go through their exchange move by move and say who is right about what.

The structuralist opened. By the early 1930s, she said, Weimar was already failing: the Treaty of Versailles, the hyperinflation of 1923, and then the Depression had radicalized voters and shredded the democratic center, while the civil service, the army, and much of industry had never accepted the republic in the first place. Some authoritarian, nationalist regime was overdetermined. So the question whether there would have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler conflates the broadly predictable outcome — an authoritarian, revanchist dictatorship — with the particular party that happened to fill that slot. Dwelling on Hitler's individual qualities is the Great Man fallacy.

The individualist replied that this was itself the conflation. The interesting counterfactual, he said, is not whether Weimar would have survived or whether some right-wing dictatorship would eventually have appeared, but whether the specific Nazi regime would have appeared — its ideology, its strategic risk-taking, and the genocide. Those features were not in the background conditions; they were supplied by a particular movement under a particular leader. Without Hitler, the party could plausibly have remained a fractious sect: he built it, broke or expelled his rivals, and made his own authority central to its appeal. Conditions can open a window without determining who climbs through it or what they do once through.

The structuralist answered that this treats biography as an independent force. Everything Hitler used, she said, already existed before him: the ethno-nationalist and anti-Semitic traditions, anti-Bolshevism, the ambition to overturn Versailles, and the elite project of using a mass movement to crush the left and install an authoritarian state. If Hitler had died in 1930, those materials would still have been there, and there is no strong reason to think the right could not have found another figure to organize them. The slot was waiting. The absence of one man does not delete a structural situation. What mattered was the selection mechanism — the Depression plus the conservative elites' willingness to hand power to a movement they believed they could control — not one man's charisma.

The individualist came back that this is where the argument slips. A slot is not an organization, and an organization can fail to find a leader who both unifies its factions and wins mass support. Without Hitler, the German right could plausibly have coalesced around a conventional military-authoritarian government, which would have revised Versailles but remained cautious about another two-front war, and would have been driven by national restoration rather than continental conquest and racial extermination. You might well get an authoritarian, repressive Germany; you do not automatically get the 1939 war or Auschwitz. Structure may explain the range of live possibilities, but it does not by itself explain which possibility in that range was realized, or how it was executed. The claim is only that Hitler was a necessary part of one particular causal complex, not that he was sufficient to produce anything from nothing.

Please adjudicate the disagreement. For each of the four moves, rate it on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify the rating. Then give an overall verdict: which question each side is really answering, whether and where they are talking past one another, and what, if anything, neither side has addressed.
