A university's assessment code contains the following regulation, drafted in the early 2000s:

"Regulation 7.1: In any assessment, a candidate shall not knowingly present as the candidate's own work the words or ideas of another without due acknowledgement."

Consider two candidates.

Candidate A, facing a take-home essay, asks a general-purpose language model to produce a complete essay plan: the thesis, the order of argument, the examples, and the replies to likely objections. The model supplies all of this; Candidate A then writes the whole essay in their own sentences, following the plan closely. No sentence resembles the model's output, and no published source is copied. Candidate A submits the essay with a cover sheet that states only that the candidate has read the assessment regulations; nothing on the sheet asks whether an AI was used.

Candidate B does exactly the same. When a tutor afterwards asks, "Did you use an AI assistant in preparing this essay?", Candidate B answers, "No — I only used it to check my spelling."

The drafters of Regulation 7.1 were concerned with unacknowledged quotation, paraphrase of published authors, and essays bought from other people; the language model was not among the cases they had in mind.

First, set out what Regulation 7.1 requires in each case under four approaches: (i) the plain words; (ii) the evident purpose of the rule, namely to ensure that an examiner's judgement attaches to the candidate's own intellectual performance and that examiners are not misled about authorship; (iii) what the drafters would most plausibly have said had they been asked; and (iv) what a reasonable examiner would take the regulation to promise. Then assess whether the regulation, read as a general principle about deception of examiners, catches one candidate but not the other, both, or neither.

Then address the underlying moral issue: should the Code treat the silent candidate and the mendacious candidate differently, and does the distinction between lying and merely misleading bear that weight? If one candidate has done something morally worse, locate the difference — in the false assertion itself, in the greater abuse of the examiner's trust, in the fact that the lie forecloses further inquiry, or in something else — and say whether it is a difference of kind or of degree. If the regulation fails to catch the silent candidate, try to repair it in no more than two sentences without also catching a tutor's substantive feedback, a friend's objections, or the ordinary influence of one's reading; then say whether the repair succeeds, and what the need or failure of repair shows about what Regulation 7.1 was ever a rule against.

Answer in British English, and distinguish your degrees of confidence: say which interpretive step or empirical assumption your verdict most depends on, and what consideration would most seriously weaken or reverse it.
