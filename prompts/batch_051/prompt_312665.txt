A couple of guys on my team got into an argument during lunch last week about a boids-style flocking sim one of them had been messing with all weekend to imitate starling murmurations. He'd coded up the usual three local rules -- seperation, alignment, cohesion -- and was showing off how they generate these lifelike flock patterns: the flock turns together, splits around obstacles, and re-forms on the other side. Then he said the thing that kicked off the whole argument:

(1) "So the flock is nothing but the individual agents running those three rules. There's no flock-force, no separate flock-thing over and above that."

The other guy pushed back:

(2) "But you can't actually predict what the flock is going to do from the rules without running the sim. All the regularities you bother to point at live at the flock level. Even your own code comments say 'flock turns here,' not 'agent 437 adjusts its heading.'"

The first guy came back with:

(3) "That's just shorthand because we can't hold a few thousand velocity vectors in our heads. Every fact about the run is a fact about where each agent is and which way it's pointing. Nothing floats free of that."

Then the second guy said:

(4) "But the same flock-level pattern can be generated by different agent-level rules. If I swap out your alignment rule for a different one and still get the same macro behavior, the flock pattern survives while all the micro facts are different. If the flock fact and the micro facts were literally the same thing, that couldn't happen."

The first guy answered:

(5) "Those aren't the same thing realized two ways. They're two different sims that happen to look alike to a lazy observer. You're mistaking a resemblance for an identity. Real starlings don't have a separate flock force either, so what's left for the flock to be?"

The second guy said:

(6) "I never said the flock was an extra force. I said it's a real pattern, and it's the pattern you actually intervene on. If you want to keep the flock from splitting when it hits an obstacle, you don't hand-edit agent 437's velocity; you tune a global parameter like how strongly agents steer toward the average heading. That's not laziness, that's the level at which the system is controllable."

The first guy tried to respond but lunch ended, and by then I'd lost track of who was ahead. They both turned to me to settle it and I just sat there like an idiot. Its the third time they've litigated this and neither has moved an inch.

Adjudicate this move by move: for each of the six moves, say whether it's clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, and briefly say why. Then say what, if anything, both of them are missing about the underlying question. Don't just tell me it's a "levels of explanation" thing and leave it there -- I want to know which specific moves survive and which don't.
