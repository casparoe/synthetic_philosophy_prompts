Write the desiderata for a theory of value across infinite populations. By "infinite" I mean worlds in which there are countably infinitely many welfare-bearing locations — lives, persons, or moments of experience — and not merely an indefinitely large finite number, so that familiar totalizing rules either diverge or depend on an arbitrary order of summation. The purpose of the theory is to rank such worlds for an aggregative consequentialism, without falling back on the judgment that all infinite worlds are equal or that every choice among them is indifferent.

I want the full specification of what such a theory has to deliver, before I look at any particular proposal. For each requirement, state it precisely and give the case that forces it. The list should at least take a position on:

- reduction to ordinary total utilitarianism on any finite world;
- weak and strong Pareto over individual welfare, and the case of one world being better for every individual than another;
- invariance under permutation of locations, and the case of two worlds that differ only in who occupies which position;
- insensitivity to the order in which contributions are summed, and the case of a non-absolutely-convergent welfare stream whose total changes under rearrangement;
- the distinction between a world that contains extra good lives and one that merely rearranges the same lives;
- robustness to changes at finitely many locations;
- completeness of the ranking, versus an acceptable partial ordering.

Mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular family of approaches — for example, value-density proposals that compare worlds by limiting average welfare per location, or hyperreal aggregation that assigns differently sized infinite sums — or any other classification you find useful. Also state which distinctions the theory must not collapse: for instance, the difference between an aggregate being undefined and its being infinite-but-equal-to-everything, and the difference between the quantity of well-being and its density.

Then answer two questions. First, which subset of the requirements, if any, cannot be jointly satisfied? Give the conflict at the level of a concrete case, not a slogan. Second, if you had to enforce a single requirement from the list, which one does the most work in eliminating otherwise attractive candidate theories — and, briefly, which families it eliminates. Keep the scorecard short: the criteria, and the tensions among them, are the deliverable.
