Last week at my philosophy reading group, two members locked horns over whether it's wrong to use a community observatory without paying the membership dues, given that nobody checks membership at public viewing nights and one of them (Pete) goes all through the summer. I just listened, but I've been stewing on it.

Diane said it's obviously wrong because it's unfair: you're taking the benefits of a cooperative scheme without doing your part, and that's a kind of exploitation even if you can't point to any particular person who is harmed. Pete replied that that's exactly the issue—if no one is made worse off by your one non-payment, then there's no victim, and 'unfairness' without a victim is just a primative feeling, not a reason. Diane came back that if everyone thought like him the observatory would close, so he's only able to free ride because most people don't take his view; he's making himself an exception. Pete said 'what if everyone did it' is a different test from 'what does my act do,' and only the latter matters: his single non-payment changes nothing, so the act isn't wrong, whatever would happen under a different general policy. Diane then said that on that logic he can't complain when the observatory folds for lack of funds, and that fairness is about whether you could accept the principle behind what you're doing, not about marginal harm. Pete answered that he can regret the loss of a good without having been obliged to produce it when his contribution would have been inert, and that the causal blame for a collapse lies with whoever's contribution would have made the difference.

I honestly couldn't referee it. Can you adjudicate move by move—which points are right, which are overstatements, what both of them are missing, and who, if either, is more right overall?
