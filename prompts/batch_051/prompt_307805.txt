Lately I've been arguing with my brother about whether "democratic backsliding" is a real thing or just a label people use for governments they dislike. I have gut verdicts on a bunch of cases, and I'd like someone to test whether there's one principle holding them together, or whether I'm just sorting countries by my own politics.

Here are the cases as I feel them.

In Hungary, the governing party has won repeatedly and used its supermajority to rewrite the election rules, amend the constitution, put its people on the constitutional court, and buy up most of the media. I call that backsliding, clearly.

In Poland, the government forced a bunch of supreme court judges into early retirement so it could replace them with judges it picked. Backsliding, also clearly.

In Turkey, after the failed coup the president used emergency powers to purge tens of thousands of judges, teachers, and journalists, and later jailed a main rival. Backsliding, obviously.

In the US, a president lost an election, claimed without evidence that it was stolen, pushed state officials to produce enough additional votes to flip the result, and a mob stormed the legislature. Backsliding, even though the result wasn't overturned.

Now the ones that get murky.

A governing party passes a voter identification law that it says privately will reduce turnout among groups that lean toward the opposition. My gut says backsliding, but less confidently, since a lot of democracies have some version of this.

A new government replaces a winner-takes-all system that had systematically manufactured huge majorities for the previous ruling party with a proportional system that gives small parties fair representation, including its own. Self-interested, but I don't call it backsliding.

A newly elected government adds four seats to a constitutional court that keeps striking down its laws, and fills them with allies. I call that backsliding, even though changing the size of a court isn't unprecedented.

During a pandemic, a government postpones local elections by six months and rules by decree under a law with a written sunset clause, then restores the elections. I don't call that backsliding.

An opposition leader is convicted of corruption on what look like genuine financial records, but the prosecution is politically very convenient. I genuinely can't decide. It's not a frame-up, but it also isn't clean.

The principle I keep trying out is something like: it's backsliding when the people in power change the rules of the game to entrench themselves, not when they just win under the rules or make the rules fairer. But that makes the voter-ID case fuzzy, and it doesn't explain why the corruption prosecution bothers me.

So: is there a coherent principle behind most of these verdicts? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop or revise? And what would that principle say about a goverment that strips a court of jurisdiction over a case that might implicate its leader, or about a party that loses a referendum but then uses its parliamentary majority to pass a watered-down version of the same proposal? Also, is there any non-partisan way for an outside observer to draw these lines, or is my brother partly right?
