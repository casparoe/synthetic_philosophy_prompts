As a novelist, I earn my living by manufacturing, in readers, an uneven distribution of care: a few hundred pages of point of view and interiority can make one invented life matter more than a trainload of unnamed extras. I want to submit that professional fact to philosophical pressure.

Consider the familiar trolley problem, changed in one respect. The single person on the side track is neither a loved one nor an unadorned stranger. She is a real person with whom you have no history of mutual commitment, but for the past year you have been writing a novel built around her: you have interviewed her, reconstructed her motives, discarded the details that did not fit the story, and narrated her from the inside until your sense of her is richer than your sense of most of your friends. The five on the main track are known to you only as a number and an occupation. The switch, the numbers, the causal structure, and the absence of further consequences remain exactly as in the standard case.

How does this change the analysis? I am asking for the anatomy of the reasoning, not a one-word verdict. In particular:

1. Does this one-directional, partly invented intimacy ground the kind of special obligation or permitted partiality that is usually claimed for a spouse or child, or is it closer to the parochial favoritism that impartialists diagnose as bias? Which view of partiality—as a virtue of attention or as a bias—better explains the distinction, if there is one?

2. Which of the standard verdicts on the original case—impartial consequentialism, a deontological constraint against killing, an ethic of relationship-grounded partiality, a virtue theory—survives the variation, and which is forced to introduce distinctions its official framework did not originally contain, for example between reciprocal and one-sided intimacy, between a person and one's narrative of that person, or between invented and discovered character?

3. A novelist can produce, deliberately and quickly, an outsize concern for one life. Does that ease of production debunk partiality by showing it to be an artifact of arranged attention rather than a response to value, or does it show that the earned/unearned contrast was doing less work than the standard debates assume?
