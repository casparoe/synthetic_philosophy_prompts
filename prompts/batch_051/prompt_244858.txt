A professor of philosophy summarized Wittgenstein on religious belief as follows. He said that for Wittgenstein religious utterances are not ordinary factual claims but belong to a distinctive form of life: they express a picture one lives by and an attitude toward the world rather than a hypothesis one might verify. On this reading, evidence can neither support nor undermine genuine religious faith from the outside, because the belief is not held as an evidence-sensitive claim in the first place. That is why the believer and the atheist are not genuinely contradicting one another; they are operating within different language-games with different standards of sense and justification. It follows, he concluded, that on Wittgenstein's view religious statements can be neither true nor false, and that philosophy's only legitimate business with religion is to describe its grammar, never to criticize what believers say.

This summary sounds both clarifying and a little too neat to me, but I cannot locate where it goes wrong. Which parts would a careful Wittgenstein scholar accept, which parts are subtle overstatements, and does the final conclusion about truth and about philosophy's role actually follow from the accurate parts?
