I run the due-diligence function at a small foundation that makes grants and recoverable investments in alternative-governance projects — mostly charter cities and special economic zones in lower-income countries, but with a growing share of seasteading ventures and land-based "network state" proposals. We see on the order of a dozen serious proposals a year, with asks running from around $100,000 for feasibility work to several million dollars for early infrastructure. Our board, which signs off on anything above a quarter million dollars, has told us that our current case-by-case verdicts are too inconsistent and wants a durable process. The team is me, one junior analyst, and part-time outside counsel; we can spend about a week on each proposal, and anything we send to the investment committee has to fit in three pages. I have been reading the rationalist and adjacent commentary on exit, competitive governance, and charter cities for years — Tiebout sorting, voice versus exit, the worry that a private city is just a small state with better marketing if its customers cannot fire their government — but I have never had to turn that reading into an instrument we could actually run.

Please do that translation. The principle I want operationalized is roughly this: a project gives someone a real option to exit from existing states only if a resident can choose among genuinely independent providers of the services states monopolize — above all security, adjudication, and rule-making — and can walk away from any of those providers without losing what made them stay. Turn that into a checklist or decision procedure: the questions to ask in order; for each question, what counts as a satisfactory answer; where the procedure stops with a no; and where it must hand the decision to a person because the issue is one of values, political risk, or otherwise beyond what the procedure can settle from facts. Mark any step where the procedure is really a judgment call wearing a checklist costume. Then run the procedure on three proposals: (a) a charter city in a developing country created under a special-economic-zone law, with a 99-year master lease held by a private developer and commercial disputes enforceable in a foreign court; (b) a seastead whose security and arbitration would both be supplied by one private contractor under a five-year renewable agreement, with the platform registered under a flag state; and (c) a land-based network-state project in the rural United States that owns its land outright and organizes services among members but has no distinct legal jurisdiction. Make sure at least one of the three gets an answer my board will not like — for example, rejecting the proposal we currently find most exciting — and end by being explicit about what the procedure cannot do, no matter how well it is run. Please write the answer in American English.
