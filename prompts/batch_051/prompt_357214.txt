Suppose a jurisdiction establishes a National Risk Authority with the following mechanism. Each year the Authority auctions a capped number of tradeable 'risk permits', each denominated in one expected death per year, with one million micromorts to the permit. The cap is set below the expected annual mortality currently produced by all regulated hazards and falls by two per cent each year. Every regulatory agency — food safety, medicines, road transport, occupational health, environmental protection and the like — must hold permits equal to the expected annual deaths its current rules can be predicted to allow, estimated under a common methodology that converts injuries, chronic illness and lost life-years into mortality-equivalent units and includes a fixed rule for how distributional effects are counted. An agency that tightens its rules and thereby lowers expected deaths may sell its surplus permits; an agency facing unusually expensive reductions may buy them. Auction revenue is paid into consolidated government revenue, and a public register records each agency's holdings, baselines and models.

Analyse how this institution would actually behave. What incentives does it create for agencies, regulated firms and the staff of the Authority itself, and how might each game the scheme? What second-order effects would likely appear — in the selection of which hazards get controlled, in how baselines are chosen, in the treatment of concentrated as against dispersed risk, and in how the public and the courts respond to a market in expected deaths? Does the mechanism allocate society's tolerance for risk efficiently, or do the practicalities of measuring, comparing and trading risk defeat that aim? Indicate which failure modes could be repaired by amending the design and which are consequences of the underlying idea.

Please write your answer in British English.
