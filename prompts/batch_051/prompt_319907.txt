I spent forty years as a physical oceanographer before I retired, and I've been reading philosophy of science in the last year or two. The thing that's been circling my brain is what I now know people call the F-twist: the idea that a model's assumptions don't have to be true — they don't even have to be realistic — so long as the model's predictions pan out. I did exactly this all my career. I ran models that assumed constant vertical mixing, ignored friction at the seafloor, treated the shelf as perfectly homogeneous, that sort of thing. Some of them predicted the seasonal temperature cycle beautifully and then blew up when a student used one to estimate oxygen drawdown under a different stratification regime.

Here's my question. I want you to design the strongest test you can of the claim that a false assumption can be harmless to a model — that the model succeeds despite the falsity rather than because of it. In particular I keep muttering to myself about the difference between an assumption I could add back in, term by term (friction, a spatially varying mixing coefficient), and one where there's nothing clear to add back because the model's internals don't correspond to any real process. Concretely: if I hand you a model with a known false assumption and a good track record in one regime, what observation or experiment would tell you whether that assumption is load-bearing or innocent? Tell me what result would count for the F-twist and what would count against it. And then be honest about what your test still leaves in the dark — maybe even a model that passes it only predicts, and doesn't explain. Can you seperate "harmless idealization" from "lucky fudge" at all, or is that a distinction tests can't reach?
