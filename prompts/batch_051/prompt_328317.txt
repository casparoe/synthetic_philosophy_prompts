I cover science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and while reporting a feature on the state of AI interpretability I have repeatedly come across a courtroom analogy deployed against automated interpretability — the increasingly common practice of using one model to generate and score explanations of what another model, or a component of it, is doing. The critics put it like this: asking a model to explain a model is like asking one defendant to testify about another defendant's state of mind. Each party can produce a fluent, plausible, self-serving story; neither can be subjected to genuine cross-examination; and the fact that the two accounts hang together proves nothing, because they share a training environment rather than a common access to the truth. The conclusion routinely drawn is that recursive interpretability is circular in a corrupting sense, and that a model's explanation of a model should be treated as further behavior to be explained, never as evidence about the target.

I would like an audit of that analogy, carried far enough to decide whether the skeptical conclusion survives it. Please begin with an explicit table of correspondences: what plays the role of the crime, the defendants, the witness, the testimony, the motive to mislead, the cross-examination, the jury, the corroboration, and the verdict. Then identify the places where the mapping breaks, and sort the disanalogies into those that are merely accidental to the courtroom image and those that track real differences between persons and models — for instance, that a model can be rerun under controlled interventions, ablated, fine-tuned on held-out data, and scored against ground-truth tasks in ways that a human defendant cannot. Finally, say whether the critics' conclusion survives the disanalogies or whether the analogy was doing the argumentative work, and, if the courtroom is the wrong source, what a better analogy would be and what it would license instead.
