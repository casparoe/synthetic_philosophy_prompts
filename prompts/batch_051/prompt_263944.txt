Consider the following version of the Death in Damascus problem. An agent must choose to go to either Damascus or Aleppo, and wants above all to avoid Death. Death, an extremely reliable predictor, has already formed a prediction about which city the agent will choose and will wait in the predicted city. If the agent goes to the city where Death is waiting, the agent dies; if the agent goes to the other city, the agent lives. The prediction was made before the choice and is not causally affected by the choice, but it is highly correlated with the choice because Death is very good at predicting exactly this kind of deliberative outcome. Apart from avoiding Death, the agent is indifferent between the two cities.

I am compiling a taxonomy of the main responses to this problem, and I would like you to contribute a new entry. The responses I already have are these four:

(1) The evidential decision theory verdict. Acts are evaluated by the news they would carry about Death's location. Choosing Damascus is strong evidence that Death predicted Damascus and is waiting there, so that act has near-worst expected utility; symmetrically, choosing Aleppo is strong evidence that Death is waiting in Aleppo. In the limit of a perfect predictor, the two pure acts tie at roughly zero expected utility, so the theory sees the agent as facing near-certain death no matter which pure act he performs and offers no usable way out.

(2) The causal decision theory verdict. The prediction is a fixed, pre-existing feature of the world, so the agent should evaluate acts using unconditional probabilities of Death's location rather than probabilities conditional on the act. If nothing selects between the cities a priori, the two acts tie at a positive expected utility and the agent may choose either; if the agent has independent evidence about where Death is waiting, he should choose the city less likely to contain Death. The known difficulty is that this recommendation cannot be combined with a tie-breaking procedure without becoming predictable: however the agent resolves the tie, that fact itself becomes evidence about where Death is waiting.

(3) The ratifiability diagnosis. A decision is ratifiable only if it remains optimal conditional on having been made. Conditional on having settled on going to Damascus, the agent would prefer to go to Aleppo, since having settled on Damascus makes it very likely that Death is waiting in Damascus; and symmetrically for Aleppo. Neither pure act is ratifiable, so the criterion directs attention away from pure acts and toward mixed strategies.

(4) The randomization response. Since the lethal correlation runs through the accuracy of the prediction, the agent can break it by selecting the city with a genuinely chancy device whose outcome Death could not have predicted. The choice then ceases to be decisive evidence about Death's location, and the agent secures survival with probability one-half rather than near-certain death. If Death is assumed to be able to predict even the randomizer's outcome, this response simply recedes one step without resolving the underlying issue.

I would like a fifth response: an argument, decision principle, diagnosis, or challenge to the problem's setup that is not one of these four and not a restatement, special case, or combination of them. It may, for example, deny some premise of the puzzle, revise the notion of rational choice at stake, or exploit a feature of the decision situation that the four responses above do not. Explain how the proposed response relates to (1) through (4), and give an honest assessment of whether it solves the problem or merely relocates it.
