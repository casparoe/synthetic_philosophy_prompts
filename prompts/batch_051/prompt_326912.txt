I'm putting together a unit on group decision-making and I want a worked case of the unilateralist's curse — the thing where several people each get to act on their own private read of a situation, and someone ends up taking a chance the group would have rejected if they'd compared notes. I dont want the usual hand-wavy version with alien civilizations or doomsday physics. Build me one from scratch, to this spec.

Three agents, same prior, same values, each gets a private but noisy signal about whether some ordinary action is worth taking — posting an allegation, forwarding a file, making a bid, something like that. Each uses a sensible individual rule: act iff your posterior expected value clears the threshold you'd use if you were the only person deciding. Make the true value of the action negative, and engineer the signals so exactly one of the three gets a misleadingly flattering signal and acts, while the other two, on their signals, correctly sit tight — and the action causes the harm anyway. The catch: all three knew in advance that each could act unilaterally and that each would be deciding on a private signal alone, and if they'd sat down together beforehand they would all have endorsed a joint rule that waits until the private signals are pooled before anyone acts. So the bad outcome comes from the gap between the individual rule they each actually use and the joint rule they would each endorse, not from anyone being reckless or stupid.

Constraints: ordinary-life setting, stakes a normal person could face (a damaged reputation, a wasted few thousand dollars, an awkward public scene — nothing existential), no more than three people, no perfect predictors, and the scenario itself should be no longer than a paragraph. Also make sure the case doesn't collapse into a volunteer's dilemma: the failure here is that someone acts, not that nobody does.

After the scenario, walk me through each agents step-by-step reasoning. Then give the ex ante joint deliberation that would have rejected the same act. Say which single structural feature makes the individual rule and the joint rule come apart, and whether removing that feature — for instance, by requiring two agents to sign off before acting — dissolves the case into ordinary group deliberation or leaves a residual curse. Finish with the best argument a defender of the individual rule could give for why the joint rule overcorrects, and tell me how strong you actually find it.

The case is for a class exercise, so the verdicts need to fall out of the mechanics. Don't stipulate them.
