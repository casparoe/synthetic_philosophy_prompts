I have been thinking about this on my own for a few years and would like a professional check on it. The thought started with the sentence "I forgive you." It does not seem to describe a private feeling, because a person can say it while still feeling raw and it still does its work; and it is not straightforwardly true or false the way "the door is closed" is. But saying it changes what the two people can later say to each other. I have been calling the split the difference between mirror sentences and seed sentences. A mirror sentence is assessed against the world: it is meant to match, and it fails when it does not. A seed sentence is assessed by what it plants: it creates, updates, or cancels a fact between the people involved — a debt, a license, a status, an expectation — and it fails when it is not permitted to plant what it tries to plant. My central claim is that no sentence is a pure mirror. Every assertion carries at least a thin seed, because by saying that something is the case, a speaker also plants the fact that they are now answerable to the audience if it turns out not to be the case. Some sentences, such as "I promise" or "You're fired," are seeds on the surface, and I think they work by disguising themselves as mirrors: they look like a report of an inner state or of a fact about the company, but their real job is to install the very thing they seem merely to describe. The test I use is to ask which question the sentence is being heard as answering. If it answers "How are things?" it is doing mirror work; if it answers "What is now the case between us?" it is doing seed work. I suspect ordinary talk is full of seeds wearing mirror clothes, and that a lot of conflict happens when a speaker means a mirror and the listener hears a seed.

I have vaguely heard that philosophers talk about "performative utterances" that are more like actions than descriptions, but I have never read any of that literature. I want to know whether my mirror-and-seed idea is just that in homemade vocabulary, or whether anything in it is new, and where it goes wrong.

Please answer as a philosopher of language who specializes in speech acts, and assume I have no academic background. Before settling on your assessment, work through the hard cases and stress-test my distinction; do not just translate my terms into jargon and call it an objection. Structure the response as follows:

1. Reconstruction — restate my view in its strongest, clearest form, separating claims I have run together and filling in gaps I left. (about 150 words)
2. The existing terrain — tell me the ordinary names for these ideas, how close my view comes to each, and where I am merely rediscovering something standard. (about 250 words)
3. Objections — give the four strongest objections or counterexamples, from the most awkward to the fatal, each with a concrete sentence and situation. (about 400 words)
4. Verdict — say plainly whether the mirror/seed distinction is salvageable, what it would take to keep it, or what I should replace it with. (about 150 words)

I would rather hear that I have reinvented a standard idea in plain words than protect the theory.
