I have now run a faculty seminar on "moral revolutions" often enough to notice something uncomfortable: the core dispute has become circular in a way that no longer feels productive. The question is how practices that were once entrenched, morally serious, and enforced by honor—Atlantic slavery, Chinese footbinding, the duel—came to an end with surprising speed. One camp says the efficient cause is moral argument: abolitionists and their successors made the better case, and once the arguments percolated through the culture the practices became indefensible. Another says argument is mostly retrospective rationalization; what really does the work is material interest and institutional change—practices fall when they become economically or administratively costly to powerful people. A third says both of these miss the mechanism: following Appiah's account of honor and the line Bernard Williams takes about the end of the duel, they hold that such practices are sustained by a status economy and collapse only when honor is redefined so that the practice becomes shameful or ridiculous; the moral arguments and the interests were both available for a long time and did nothing until that status shift occurred. Each camp has a paradigm case it handles cleanly and a standard story about why the others' cases are exceptions; all the arguments are known, and nobody moves.

I do not want an adjudication of which camp is right. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Is this a verbal dispute about what it means for a norm to "end"—legal abolition versus extinction of the practice versus a change in its social meaning? Is it a dispute about which cases count as data and how the cases are selected? Is it a dispute about explanatory level, so that what sustains a practice, what triggers its collapse, and what people later say about it are three different questions being fought under one heading? Is a hidden counterfactual doing the work—would dueling have ended without the ridicule, would slavery have ended without the abolitionist arguments? Or is the question "why did norm N end?" ill-posed in a way that guarantees the impasse? My own suspicion, offered only as a hypothesis, is the third: the camps are answering different questions under a single heading. Tell me whether that hypothesis holds up against what each side actually says, and then tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis—a distinction, a differently designed comparison, a different question—or why no progress should be expected. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and without restating the question.
