My ethics reading group got into an argument about whether simplicity is actually a virtue in moral theorizing, and someone built a reductio that's been bugging me ever since. I'll lay it out as he gave it:

(1) Simplicity is a theoretical virtue in ethics, at least as a tiebreaker: other things equal, the more parsimonious theory is to be preferred.
(2) A moral theory is more parsimonious when it posits fewer fundamental principles and fewer basic kinds of moral reasons.
(3) By that measure, the simplest possible moral theory is the error theory: zero fundamental principles, zero basic moral reasons, plus the claim that all positive moral judgements are false.
(4) But the error theory fits our moral intuitions terribly; it says they're all systematically in error.
(5) That's no obstacle to the simplicity fan, because once simplicity counts as a theoretical virtue, the fit of your intuitions can be bought off by gains in parsimony, and there's no principled stopping point to how much fit you may trade away.
(6) Whenever a pluralist theory adds another kind of reason to accommodate some recalcitrant intuition, a simpler theory can instead diagnose that intuition as bias, distortion, or self-interest.
(7) So favoring simplicity always pushes toward positing fewer reasons and explaining away more intuitions, with the error theory as the limiting case.
(8) Therefore, if simplicity is a virtue in ethical theory, the best theory is the error theory.
(9) But no one thinks parsimony should force us to conclude there are no moral reasons; that can't be what a theoretical virtue does.
(10) So simplicity isn't a virtue in ethics after all.

Here's the part that makes it feel like it proves too much: the same move would seem to rule out using simplicity anywhere in theory choice, since in philosophy of science too you can always "simplify" a theory by explaining away recalcitrant data as noise rather than positing more mechanisms. So either the argument's wrong somewhere or we've been irrational to ever care about parsimony.

I'd prefer to keep (1), because I do think a one-principle theory beats a seventeen-epicycle theory when both fit the cases equally well, but I don't see how to keep (1) without sliding all the way down to (8). Which step goes wrong, and what do you pay for denying it?
