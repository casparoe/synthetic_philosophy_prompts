I'm in a running argument with a lawyer friend about whether clicking an 'I agree' button on a standard consumer app actually counts as agreement in any morally serious sense, and I want to locate where we actualy diverge instead of just trading intuitions. Set aside the totally predatory cases where the terms are hidden or impossible to find — I mean the ordinary one: the button is right there, the terms are one click away, forty pages long, you don't read them, you click. The claim I want a credence on is this: in that ordinary case, the click constitutes genuine agreement, in the sense that the user has a real (defeasible) obligation to actually comply with the terms, not just that a court can enforce them. My credence is about 0.35; my friend is around 0.8.

Give me your own number, but mostly give me the decomposition. I can see it spliting into at least these sub-questions, though add others if you think they belong:
- is agreement fundamentally a mental act (actual informed assent) or a social/performative act (what you reasonably communicate by clicking)?
- does the user know enough about what's in the terms for acceptance to be genuine, given that nobody reads them?
- does the take-it-or-leave-it structure undermine voluntariness enough to matter?
- does the practice earn independent legitimacy from its consequences — keeping services cheap, the company's reliance, the stability of millions of contracts — even if the individual elements look shaky?

For each sub-question give a rough probability and one line of justification, then show how you combined them into your headline number (don't just average if the structure matters — say how the conditional dependencies run). Then tell me which single sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, and roughly how many points your credence would move if you got decisive evidence on it. Last, tell me where you think the 0.35-vs-0.8 gap most likely lives, and what evidence would move you the most.
