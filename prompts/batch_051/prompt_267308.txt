I'm a software engineer by training, and I recently came across a discussion of sphexishness — the idea that some behaviours look intelligent from the outside but turn out to be fixed, reflexive routines. The canonical example is from Jean-Henri Fabre's The Hunting Wasps. As I understand the passage, Fabre observed a Sphex wasp that had paralysed a cricket and dragged it to the entrance of its burrow. Before pulling the cricket inside, the wasp would first go into the burrow alone, apparently to inspect it. If Fabre moved the cricket a few centimetres away while the wasp was inside, the wasp would come back out, haul the cricket to the threshold again, and then go inside to inspect once more. Repeating the intervention made the wasp repeat the entire cycle, over and over, without it ever seeming to learn to skip the inspection step or otherwise alter the routine. Fabre took this as evidence that the wasp's behaviour is mechanically rigid.

I'm trying to figure out whether that reading is right. The conceptual claim I want to test is something like: the wasp is executing a fixed sensorimotor routine, and therefore its behaviour is mindless or mechanical in a way that flexible, goal-directed behaviour is not. But an alternative is that the wasp still has a goal — get the cricket into the burrow — and simply has a very limited behavioural repertoire plus a strong, local trigger for the inspection step. Those two readings make the same observable prediction in Fabre's setup, but they differ conceptually.

How would one test the claim that the wasp's behaviour is mechanical rigidity rather than limited but still goal-directed flexibility? I'd like a concrete experimental design, preferably one that could also be implemented in a simulated agent or a robotics control architecture. What result would favour each reading? And what part of the distinction — if any — could no experiment settle, because it is not really an empirical difference but a difference in what we mean by mindless, mechanical, or goal-directed?
