I've ended up fairly convinced by something like Hacking's view of psychiatric classification, and I want you to pressure-test it for me. The view is this: psychiatric categories are interactive kinds rather than indifferent ones, and looping effects explain a good deal of why the categories keep shifting. Once a label like ADHD or dissociative identity disorder gets into circulation, the people sorted under it learn it, respond to it, re-describe themselves through it, sometimes conform to it or resist it, and the category itself moves as a result. The thing being studied changes in response to being studied, in a way a chemical element doesn't. What would count against this? I don't want the standard list of objections critics always trot out. I want a genuine map of it's exposure: what observations, case histories, or arguments should actually move me away from it, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and which ones a committed looping theorist would themselves concede as counting against them rather than dismiss as confused. I'd also like an honest assessment of whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability — whether every possible outcome just gets described as a looping effect after the fact — and if it has, whether that matters for a view of this kind. You can draw on things that have arguably already happened in the history of psychiatry, and I'd especially like to know what the view predicts that its rivals don't. Write it in British English, and keep it as continuous prose: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just paragraphs like an email.
