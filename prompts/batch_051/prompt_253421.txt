Analyze the Gulf of Tonkin incident of August 1964 and the Johnson administration's public justification of the subsequent escalation in Vietnam through the lens of Hannah Arendt's account of lying in politics. By the incident I mean the following: a first engagement between the destroyer USS Maddox and North Vietnamese torpedo boats took place on August 2; a second engagement was reported for the night of August 4; and within days the administration obtained the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, which it then used as authorization for major escalation. The salient facts for the analysis are that the weight of later evidence, including material in the Pentagon Papers and declassified signals intelligence, indicates that no second attack occurred, that the administration presented that attack as established while privately receiving ambiguous and contradictory reports, and that a draft resolution authorizing military force was prepared before the incident.

Arendt's account, developed in her reflections on the Pentagon Papers, distinguishes a limited kind of lying from a distinctively modern form. The limited lie concerns particular facts, is told by someone who knows they are lying, and remains answerable to a public that still measures claims against reality. The modern political lie, by contrast, is an exercise in image-making: it constructs a coherent alternative picture of events, addresses itself to a public rather than to an identifiable opponent, and tends to loosen the image-maker's own grip on the facts. On this account, the deeper danger is not the occasional false statement but defactualization, a process in which the image replaces reality as the basis for judgment and action, and the people maintaining the image come partly to believe it themselves.
