I'm in a philosophy of intellectual property course and we spent a week on orphan works and abandonware, and I can't get an asymmetry out of my head. When a person dies with no will and no findable heirs, we don't just leave his house behind a fence for 70 years. The state eventually gets involved: the estate gets administered, creditors get paid, the house gets sold or is claimed by the state, and in a lot of places someone who openly occupies land the owner never returns to can, after enough years, gain legal title. We treat an ownerless physical asset as a problem to be cleared away and reallocated. But if the same person leaves behind a novel, a photo archive, or a software project, and the rights are held by heirs nobody can find—or by a company that dissolved without assigning them—the work just sits there. Nobody can republish the novel, digitize the archive, patch the software, or keep the server running, because the rights are still technically owned by a phantom. In the US and UK a recent literary work is locked up for the author's life plus 70 years, and there's no escheat or adverse-possession style mechanism for copyright; the orphaned rights just stay stranded.

The justifications I've tried—incentivizing creation, respecting the author's wishes, moral rights, not letting strangers profit off someone else's work, and the idea that idle physical stuff imposes costs while idle rights merely fail to benefit others—all seem to me to apply just as much, or as little, to the house. If we respect the dead owner's wishes, why do we sell his house to pay his debts? If clearing dead assets matters, why do we leave the book dead? So: is there a principled justification for treating ownerless intellectual property so differently from ownerless physical property, or is this just a bias toward matter over information that the law should of corrected? If you defend the asymmetry, defend it against the close case: a self-published author dies leaving one finished but unpublished manuscript and a small utility program he wrote and maintained by himself, never open-sourced, with no traceable heirs and no company behind either. What should happen to the manuscript and the software, and why wouldn't that same reasoning reassign his house too?
