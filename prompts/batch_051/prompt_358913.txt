Last weekend I had a long argument with a friend about whether AI alignment is one problem or several. I defended the position that it is, at bottom, one problem: reliably getting a system to do what its operators actually intend, with everything else — outer and inner misalignment, specification gaming, robustness, interpretability — being facets of that single gap between intention and behavior. My friend defended the opposite view: that alignment is a family-resemblance term covering several problems with different structures and failure modes, and that a mechanism aimed at one of them will be defeated by the others. The argument was productive but stalled at the level of taxonomy, so we agreed to test the disagreement against a concrete institution that deliberately treats alignment as one problem and then see how it behaves.

Here is the institution. Call it the Standing Specification Escrow.

1. Any developer deploying a general-purpose AI system above a capability threshold set by statute must place in a public escrow a bond whose size is set by an independent capability auditor. The bond scales with the system's estimated capacity to cause harm, as measured by proxies such as inference compute, interaction bandwidth, and the degree of autonomy the system has in real-world actions.

2. Before deployment, the developer must file a specification with a standards body: a formal statement of what the system is intended to do and not to do. The standards body must certify that the specification is non-vacuous and reasonably complete before deployment is permitted; the developer otherwise writes the specification itself.

3. After deployment, any person or organization may file a claim that the system committed a specification violation. A claim may concern an output or action that fails to do what the specification says, including cases the specification did not foresee, provided the claimant can argue that the specification's own stated principles imply that the outcome was wrong.

4. Each claim is heard by a randomly drawn jury of lay adjudicators. The jury is shown the specification, the system's action, and the parties' arguments; the standard of proof is the balance of probabilities.

5. If a claim is upheld, the claimant receives the entire escrow as a bounty. The developer must post a new bond double the previous amount before resuming deployment; the doubling decays back to the baseline only after a two-year period with no further upheld claims.

6. The developer may avoid the doubling by proving that the specification itself was defective — that the specification, though certified, could not reasonably have covered the case — and by filing a certified amendment that closes the gap.

7. The scheme deliberately makes no distinction among kinds of misalignment. Inner-misalignment failures, specification gaps, reward hacking, and safety-relevant externalities all count as specification violations, with no separate track.

Please walk through how this mechanism would actually play out in practice, rather than assessing it in the abstract. In particular, discuss: what it would incentivize developers, the standards body, the capability auditors, the adjudicators, and potential claimants to do; how it would be gamed, and by whom; where it would fail first; what second-order effects it would have on the kinds of systems that get built, on what gets written into specifications, on the market for capability estimates, and on public trust; and which of its failure modes, if any, could be repaired by amendment without producing new ones. Throughout, use the mechanism's behavior to adjudicate the argument I had with my friend: does the scheme succeed in treating alignment as a single problem, and does its behavior suggest that alignment is, underneath, one problem or several?
