I've been teaching a grad seminar on psychiatric classification this term, and we keep running up against a claim I want to pin down before I write the next lecture, because I suspect the students smell a dodge in it. The claim, roughly, is Hacking's idea that a classification doesn't just track the people it classifies: it loops back and changes them, so that the people classified start to fit the classification better over time, and the classification then gets revised to keep up with the people it has partly produced. The old multiple personality story is the usual illustration, and so is the way borderline personality seems to have settled into a recognizable style of distress once the label was widely available. A colleague in the psych department says this is either unfalsifiable grandstanding or just a pretentious name for the boring fact that labels affect behavior, and she's challenged me to say what the looping effect actually predicts. So here's what I want. Give me the strongest testable version of the looping claim you can defend, and then design the best study you can to test it against the weaker label-affects-behavior reading: specify the population, what you'd compare, what you'd measure, and what result would favor each reading. Then say exactly what a positive result would show and what it would still leave open — in particular whether any experiment can get past the worry that we've only measured changes in how people describe themselves rather than changes in the people, and whether there's a residue of the looping claim that no test could settle in principle. And if the answer is that the claim partly collapses into an empirical hypothesis once sharpened and partly doesn't, say where the line falls. I'd like this as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — just a few solid paragraphs like you'd write to a colleague.
