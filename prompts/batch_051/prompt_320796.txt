For a philosophy-of-games reading group, I'm writing up my views on whether anything we do to video game characters matters morally, and I'd like an audit of whether the set hangs together before I circulate it. I'm inclined to accept all of the following:

1. It is morally permissible to commit mass murder in an open-world video game, and enjoying that sort of play does not reflect badly on a person's character.
2. Creating or consuming wholly computer-generated depictions of child sexual abuse, where no actual child is involved at any stage, is morally wrong.
3. Fictional characters do not exist, so they are not the kinds of things that can be wronged or that have moral claims on us.
4. Moral assessment of an action should track actual harms to, and the flourishing of, actual persons, not bare imagery or representation as such.
5. The social meaning of a practice — what it normalizes or signals — can make the practice wrong even when no actual person is harmed in the act itself.
6. A gut feeling of disgust is not, by itself, a good reason to think something is wrong.

Go through these and identify which commitments genuinely pull against each other and which tensions are merely apparent, dissolving once the commitments are properly understood. Rank the genuine tensions by severity, say for each what the least costly revision would be, and then identify the single revision that would do the most to make the set coherent. I don't want a survey of the literature; I want to know specifically where my own position is unstable.
