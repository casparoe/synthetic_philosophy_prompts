I've been reading Thomas Robert Malthus's An Essay on the Principle of Population (1798), and in the opening chapter he frames the whole argument like this:

"It has been said that the great question is now at issue, whether man shall henceforth start forwards with accelerated velocity towards illimitable, and hitherto unconceived improvement, or be condemned to a perpetual oscillation between happiness and misery, and after every effort remain still at an immeasurable distance from the wished-for goal."

I keep coming back to that "perpetual oscillation" line, because it seems like the most honest statement of what he actually expects: not immediate famine, but a ceiling that any improvement eventually bangs into, so average conditions get dragged back down. My working view is that Malthus was right but early. The negative feedback loop is real—more food or better conditions let more people survive and reproduce, and the surplus gets spread over more people, so average well-being tends to fall back toward subsistence. But he hardcoded two things that should have been variables: the ceiling on output, and how people respond to higher income by having fewer kids. Tech moves the ceiling, and the demographic transition changes the population side of the loop entirely. In software terms its the right feedback loop, with two constants where there should have been parameters, so the model oscillates when the actual system started climbing.

I want to stress-test that "right but early" take, not just hear the usual response that he was wrong because technology or institutions changed. What would actually count against it? I'm looking for: (a) facts, data patterns, or arguments that would make "right but early" just false; (b) reasons to think it's unfalsifiable, a rescue that can absorb any failure and is therefore empty; (c) things that would shrink it to a trivial claim not worth calling a theory. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Which ones would a committed defender of the right-but-early reading quietly have to concede, and which would they dismiss as holding him to anachronistic standards of prediction? And be straight with me about which parts of the Malthus debate are settled versus genuinely contested among people who study this—don't present a contested take as the consensus. If you think the "right but early" reading is basically insulated from evidence, say so, and tell me whether that's a feature or a bug.
