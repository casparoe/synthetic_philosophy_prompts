After retiring from a career in experimental biology, I have been reading around in legal epistemology, and I have settled into a cluster of views about statistical evidence and standards of proof. I suspect the cluster may not be consistent, but I cannot locate the fault line myself. I would like a rigorous audit of the following commitments. I am not asking for a survey of the field or for sources; what I need is a tracing of what these positions jointly commit me to.

1. In civil cases, the preponderance standard is a probability threshold. A plaintiff should prevail just in case the evidence, all things considered, makes the defendant's liability more probable than not (strictly greater than one-half). The relative costs of the two types of error can explain why the threshold is set where it is, but once it is set, nothing beyond the probability should determine sufficiency.

2. In the blue-bus case, I believe the injured plaintiff should lose. As I understand the case: the plaintiff can prove only that the bus that caused the injury was blue, and that the Blue Bus Company operates eighty percent of the blue buses on the route in question. I accept that the probability that it was a Blue Bus Company bus is 0.8.

3. In the gatecrasher case, I believe the rodeo operator should lose. As I understand it: one thousand spectators attend a ticketed rodeo, 499 paid, the operator selects one spectator at random, and there is no stub, witness, or other evidence tying that particular spectator to nonpayment. I accept that the probability that the selected spectator did not pay is 0.501.

4. I hold the parallel view in criminal cases: proof beyond a reasonable doubt is a higher probability cutoff, not a different kind of standard. Yet I would not convict on a naked statistic even when I accepted that it crossed the cutoff — for instance, a defendant whose only inculpatory evidence was that he had been drawn at random from a group of one hundred, ninety-five of whom committed the offense.

5. I do not believe there is a sharp epistemic difference in kind between statistical and individualized evidence. From my former life in the laboratory, an eyewitness identification is just a fallible measuring instrument with a sensitivity and a false-report rate, and it gets its force only by placing a person in some reference class or other: those matching the description, those in the area at the time, and so on. I therefore resist the suggestion that the blue-bus and gatecrasher plaintiffs should lose simply because their evidence is "merely statistical."

6. Finally, I think the choice of reference class should not by itself drive outcomes. A diagnostic test's positive predictive value means nothing until one fixes the population to which it is applied, and nothing in the facts selects one of the indefinitely many possible reference classes as uniquely correct. If an unforced selection of a reference class were all that stood between liability and no liability, I would not think the law should let that selection settle the case.

Please work through these commitments and tell me whether they hang together. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating every pair as equally problematic. For each tension, say whether it is genuine or merely apparent, identify exactly which commitments conflict, and state the least costly revision and what it would cost. Also identify any pair I might wrongly think is in tension but is not, and say why. Finally, if more than one revision is possible, tell me which single revision would restore consistency at the lowest cost — or, if none would, say so and explain what the deepest obstacle is.
