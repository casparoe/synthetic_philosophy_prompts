Over a semester, one of my teachers has been developing a view of creativity that she describes as knob-twiddling. On her picture, any work sits inside a conceptual skeleton, and creative work consists in knowing which knobs to turn, how many of them, and how far. She has issued the following rulings on a series of cases; each is a verdict about whether a work is creative, not a grade for its quality.

1. A pianist records a baroque cello suite on a synthesizer, preserving every note, rhythm, and accent and changing only the instrument's timbre. Her verdict: not creative — the piece itself is untouched.

2. A student paraphrases a journal article by substituting synonyms and reordering the clauses while preserving every claim and every step of the argument. Her verdict: not creative — the underlying structure is unchanged.

3. A parodist rewrites the lyrics of a familiar song while keeping the melody, harmony, rhythm, and formal structure identical. Her verdict: mildly creative — one knob turned, though a large one.

4. A translator renders the argument of a sonnet into another language using a completely different stanza form and meter, dropping the original rhyme scheme. Her verdict: creative — the poetic problem was re-solved, not merely re-worded.

5. A composer builds a new piece entirely from short samples of existing recordings, but recombines them into a new formal architecture in which none of the sources is recognizable. Her verdict: highly creative — the resulting structure is far from its materials.

6. A forger paints a new picture in the exact compositional manner and style of a celebrated painter, intending it as a tribute rather than a deception. Her verdict: not creative — a style transfer with no new knob turned.

Next week's case is a jazz musician who writes a brand-new melody over the exact chord changes and form of a well-known standard, leaving the harmony, tempo, and groove unchanged, while the melody itself has a highly distinctive contour and rhythmic phrasing.

Predict her verdict on this jazz case. Begin by stating the candidate rules about creativity that could jointly rationalize her earlier rulings, including at least two rules that diverge on the jazz case. Say what each candidate rule predicts about the jazz case and why. Then give your best guess about her actual verdict, with reasons, even if the case is genuinely underdetermined. Finally, specify the one further case that would most decisively settle which rule she is running, and explain what that further case would have to look like.
