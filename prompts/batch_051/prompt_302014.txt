I manage a small sports-betting operation: three analysts, a bankroll of about two million dollars, and between five and twenty wagers a week across major US and European leagues. Our models produce win probabilities we trust, but we have no principled staking rule — sizing has been instinct plus a loose "one or two percent per play" — and after a bad quarter I want it rebuilt on firmer ground. The framework I keep hearing about is Kelly betting and geometric rationality: because the bankroll compounds multiplicatively, the quantity that matters for long-run growth is the expected logarithm of terminal wealth rather than expected winnings, and stakes should be set to maximize that. I can work with the single-bet formula; what I need is a working procedure, not another derivation.

I would like a staking procedure the desk can run on every proposal before it goes into the book, in the form of an ordered sequence of questions and calculations. For each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, what to do when the answer is missing or is only a rough estimate, where the procedure returns "no stake," and where it must stop and hand the decision to me because the question is no longer one for geometric rationality. We have at most ten minutes per proposal between a model output and the bet deadline, and I have to sign off whenever the procedure is overridden. Be explicit about which distinctions in the theory must survive translation — uncertainty about the edge estimate, correlation between simultaneous wagers, the gap between growth-optimal and what the bankroll or I can tolerate — and which can be dropped. Then run the procedure on three proposals: (1) a single bet at decimal odds 1.91 on a team our model gives a 56.5% chance to win; (2) two correlated wagers, a team to win the first half and the same team to win the match, each of which shows an edge on its own; and (3) a heavy favourite at decimal odds 1.08 whose true probability our model estimates at 92%. Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer we will not like, and close by saying plainly what the procedure cannot do for our desk.
