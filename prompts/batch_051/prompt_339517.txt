An acquaintance who works in Confucian role ethics likes to say that a person is like a note in a melody. On this analogy, to be a self is to answer to the roles one actually occupies—daughter, teacher, neighbor, friend—and just as a note has no identity apart from the melody that contains it, there is no inner core left over when all roles are abstracted away. She uses the analogy to argue that moral cultivation should be understood as improving one's responses within relationships rather than polishing a hidden inner nature, and that there is no ethical remainder behind the roles.

I have been reading Mencius and came upon this passage in the Jin Xin I book, in James Legge's translation:

Mencius said, 'When we get by our seeking and lose by our neglecting - in that case seeking is of use to getting, and the things sought for are those which are in ourselves. When the seeking is according to the proper course, and the getting is only as appointed - in that case the seeking is of no use to getting, and the things sought are without ourselves.'

She says this passage is friendly to her analogy: what is "in ourselves" is just our situated capacity to respond well from where we stand, while what is "without ourselves" is success, recognition, and outcomes. But I read Mencius as marking a stronger distinction, in which the self has its own field of cultivation that does not reduce to a function of its relationships.

Audit her melody analogy. Give the correspondence table explicitly: what maps to the note, the melody, the musician, the performance, and the surrounding notes? Then identify where the mapping breaks, which features of a melody the analogy quietly imports into ethics—for example, that a note's value is exhausted by its contribution to the whole, that there is a composer or score, that notes are synchronous rather than temporally stretched—and which disanalogies matter for her conclusion as opposed to being harmless. Finally, tell me whether Mencius's distinction between what is in ourselves and what is without ourselves can be absorbed into the analogy without changing her view, or whether the passage gives me good reason to resist the analogy's intended moral. If a better analogy would do less smuggling, propose one and say what it buys.
