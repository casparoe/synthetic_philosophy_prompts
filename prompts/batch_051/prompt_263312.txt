I keep getting stuck on Augustine's move in On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins, Book I, where he writes:

For if God granted to the clothes and the shoes of the Israelites that "they waxed not old" during so many years, what wonder if for obedience it had been by the power of the same [God] allowed to man, that although he had a natural and mortal body, he should have in it a certain condition, in which he might grow full of years without decrepitude, and, whenever God pleased, pass from mortality to immortality without the medium of death?

The way I read it, he's arguing that death wasn't built into human nature; it only became the default after sin. That lets him say mortality is inherited as a penalty, not just as a biological fact. But then I can't tell how far the "guilt" part is ment to go. Please answer in American English. Also, if the ranking depends on what Augustine means by "guilt" versus "penalty," say where you think the passage settles that.
