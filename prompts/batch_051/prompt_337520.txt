I retired a few years ago from a career in the experimental natural sciences, and I have been spending my retirement working through epistemology. The part of my former craft I keep circling back to is the discipline of deciding when to trust a piece of apparatus. Some readings we accepted almost by default once an instrument had been set up and calibrated against a standard: to reject one of those readings, we needed positive evidence of malfunction, not merely the absence of independent confirmation. Other devices, especially new or improvised ones, had to earn trust. We checked them against instruments we already trusted or against known references, and only after a sufficiently consistent track record did their output count as evidence. This practical contrast seems to me the working twin of a philosophical debate I have been reading about: the dispute between reductionists and anti-reductionists about testimony. As I understand the positions, the reductionist holds that a hearer is warranted in believing what a speaker says only if the hearer possesses adequate reasons for regarding the speaker as reliable, reasons that ultimately trace back to the hearer's own experience of how reports and facts have lined up in the past. The anti-reductionist holds that testimony is a basic source of warrant, much like perception: an assertion can justify belief by itself unless the hearer has specific grounds for doubt, and the hearer need not possess any positive track record of the speaker's reliability.

I would like you to carry out, rigorously, the translation experiment I have only managed informally. Restate the reductionist and the anti-reductionist positions in the vocabulary of scientific instrument practice — calibration and standards, independent cross-checking, channel reliability, signal-to-noise, earned trust versus default trust in a properly set-up device — as faithfully as you can, without surreptitiously assuming that laboratory practice already settles the philosophical question. Then give me a residue report. Which elements of each position translate cleanly into the instrument vocabulary? Which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in that vocabulary at all? I am especially interested in one suspicion of mine: that the anti-reductionist's default trust, once translated, may start to look like reductionism practiced at the level of institutions and shared standards rather than by individual hearers — and I want to know whether that is a real insight or a distortion imposed by the vocabulary. The part I most want is the point-by-point account of where the mapping breaks, not a final verdict for either camp.
