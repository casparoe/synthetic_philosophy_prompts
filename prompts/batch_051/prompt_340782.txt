I recently signed a lease on a storefront in a mid-sized town, intending to open a small bookstore café. While applying for a sign permit, I learned that a local ordinance passed in 1962 requires every commercial sign in the historic district to be painted directly onto the building's masonry, and prohibits the sort of mounted, externally lit sign that nearly every other business on the street has used for decades—including the previous tenant of my own storefront. The ordinance has not been enforced in roughly half a century, and the town has routinely approved mounted signs for other applicants until quite recently. Last month, however, a newly appointed code enforcement officer announced that, going forward, the town would enforce the 1962 rule against new sign applications only. Existing signs may remain untouched, but I and other new applicants would be required to comply. The clerk told me, half-apologetically, that the ordinance had never been repealed and that she was not free to ignore it.

I am not looking for legal advice about my particular case, and I know a lawyer could advise me about any formal challenge. What I want is to understand the principle at stake. Is a law that has been openly and universally ignored for decades still binding in the same way as a law that is actually enforced? If the town now applies it only to new applicants while grandfathering everyone else, is there a principled objection beyond its simply feeling unfair? And what is the right way, here, to respect the rule of law: does the rule of law require officials to begin enforcing the old rule now that it has been noticed, or is there a sense in which a legal obligation can lapse through sustained, open non-enforcement?
