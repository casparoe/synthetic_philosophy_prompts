I'm in a philosophy of biology course and we've been comparing natural selection to optimisation algorithms like gradient descent. One thing is really nagging me. In the ML lectures people write off gradient descent as a greedy, stupid hill-climber that gets stuck in mediocre local minima. But on the biology side we're told natural selection is this blind, mindless process that nevertheless produces all this finely adapted 'design'. If selection is basically gradient ascent on a fitness landscape — just sloppier, with nobody computing the gradient properly — why doesn't it get stuck on local peaks or drift into junk more often? It feels like either the ML folks undersell what stupid hill-climbing can do, or the biologists oversell what its capable of. Where exactly does the analogy break, and is calling evolution an optimizer itself part of the problem? Could you also flag which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on it? I don't want to go into the seminar defending someone's pet view as if it were the consensus.
