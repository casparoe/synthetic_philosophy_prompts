ok so I just had a long argument with a friend about Davidson's line in What Metaphors Mean — he basically says that when Romeo calls Juliet the sun, the sentence only has its literal meaning (which is false: Juliet is not a giant ball of gas) and whatever else it does is just an effect on the listener, like a picture or a nudge, not anything you could call content. My friend was defending this and kept treating every objection I made as if it were the same complaint. After an hour I realized that a lot of the standard objections really do blur into one move: "no, metaphors do carry content." Now I'm putting together a handout of objections to Davidson's denial of metaphorical meaning, and I want the entries to be genuinely distinct, not one point restated five times.

So far I have:
(1) dead metaphors — things like "mouth of a river" started out metaphorical and then hardened into new literal meanings, and it's hard to see how that hardening works if the original metaphor never had any content to transmit;
(2) the Lakoff-style systematicity point — conceptual metaphors like LOVE IS A JOURNEY or TIME IS MONEY generate whole families of expressions ("we've hit a rough patch," "don't squander your time") and license systematic inferences, which looks like a conceptual mapping doing real work, not just a free-floating nudge;
(3) novel metaphors — a fresh line like "my boss is a bulldozer" tells you something reasonably specific about the boss on first hearing, and a view on which the sentence is merely literally false plus subjective associations has troubld explaining how speakers converge;
(4) truth and aptness — we treat metaphors as better or worse, as getting the target right or wrong, and we argue about them roughly the way we argue about assertions, which is awkward if there is no content to be right about;
(5) cognitive usefulness — science and philosophy are shot through with metaphors (electrical "current," "pressure" in economics) that seem to be doing epistemic work, not just decorating, and a purely non-cognitivist account makes that mysterious.

I think these basically exhaust the usual "but metaphors carry cognitive content" moves, so the thing I want from you is one more objection with a different structure entirely: not a rewording of any of the five, not a special case or combination of them, and not a sixth way of saying metaphors have content. It could attack a different step in his argument — the meaning/use distinction, his examples, his picture of what a metaphor does — or come from an angle the list doesn't touch. And say briefly what the new objection shows that the five don't.
