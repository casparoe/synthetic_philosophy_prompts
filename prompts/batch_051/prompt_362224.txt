I retired from a career in the natural sciences, much of it spent arguing about how to divide nature into kinds, and I have recently been reading philosophy with the unsettling habit of testing every category I meet against the standards I used to impose on species, minerals, and geological formations. The generational labels that dominate journalism and office training — Baby Boomers, Generation X, Millennials, Generation Z — now strike me as a good test case. I have reconstructed the skeptical argument against taking them to be real kinds, and I would like to know whether it goes through.

(1) A grouping counts as a real kind only if its members share properties downstream of the grouping itself that support reliable projections, and its boundaries track genuine discontinuities in an underlying causal process rather than mere conventions. (2) Generational groupings sort people by birth-year brackets whose endpoints are conventions: Boomers are said to run from roughly 1946 to 1964 and Millennials from roughly 1981 to 1996, with the exact figures varying from one source to another; the endpoints were chosen by demographers, pollsters, and editors, not discovered by observing sharp transitions at 1964/1965 or 1996/1997. (3) The causal processes that might make a birth cohort share downstream traits — economic cycles, wars, technological change, family policy — vary across birth years, but their breaks are either smeared across many years or cut across the conventional brackets: the digital transition did not respect the Millennial/Gen Z boundary, and the disruptions of 2001 and 2008 struck people distributed across several brackets rather than lining up with any boundary. (4) If a grouping's endpoints are conventional and no underlying causal discontinuity coincides with them, then the projections it licenses are artifacts of that convention rather than detections of real structure. (5) Therefore the generational categories are not real kinds.

I half-suspect this argument is sound and that "generation" talk is mostly market segmentation wearing a sociological costume. My philosophical friends tell me instead that I am importing a natural-science conception of real kinds into a domain where kinds can be real in a different, classification-dependent way. I want to know exactly where — if anywhere — the argument fails. Is it valid as it stands? If not, which step fails, and on what explicit reading of that step? If it is valid, what exactly does the conclusion amount to, and should someone who wants to defend the reality of generations push back on premise (1) or premise (4) rather than accept (5)?
