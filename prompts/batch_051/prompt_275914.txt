A popular science book I'm reading gives the following account of why time has a direction. The microscopic laws of physics are time-symmetric, so no fact about particles picks out the past from the future. The only fundamental source of temporal asymmetry is the Second Law of Thermodynamics: entropy, which the book defines as the number of microscopic arrangements consistent with a system's macroscopic appearance, can only increase, never decrease. Since the universe began in an extraordinarily low-entropy state—the "past hypothesis"—entropy has been climbing ever since, and this single entropy gradient is what ultimately distinguishes the past from the future. Memory works because the past was lower in entropy: a record is a low-entropy structure that carries information about an earlier event, and the same is true of the psychological arrow, the arrow of causation, and the arrow of information. Boltzmann showed that this increase is a strict law for macroscopic systems, so the only genuine remaining mystery is why the initial state had such low entropy. Once the past hypothesis is granted, the book concludes, every other arrow of time reduces to the thermodynamic arrow, and the idea that time "flows" adds nothing beyond the entropy gradient.

I find most of this plausible, but the claim about causation and the flat statement that entropy can only increase make me uneasy. Is this a fair summary?
