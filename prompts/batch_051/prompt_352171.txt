I just spent an evening arguing with a friend about whether epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness is self-defeating. He kept returning to Thomas Henry Huxley's 1874 essay "On the Hypothesis that Animals are Automata, and Its History," where Huxley suggests that states of consciousness are related to the working of the nervous system only as a collateral product, like the steam-whistle of a locomotive, which accompanies the engine's work without influencing the machinery. My friend's version of the worry was this: if that picture is right, then my own judgment that phenomenal states exist and lack causal power is itself produced entirely by physical processes, with the felt character contributing nothing; the very evidence I would cite—the felt pain or the felt conviction—is causally irrelevant to my arriving at the epiphenomenalist conclusion, and so the conclusion cannot be rationally grounded in that evidence. I want a number: what is your credence that this self-undermining objection gives us a decisive reason to reject epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness? Decompose it. In particular: (1) How likely is it that phenomenal properties are causally relevant to the formation of our judgments about them? (2) Given that they are not, how likely is it that our epistemic practice still permits knowledge of them through some kind of constitutive or non-causal relation? (3) How much weight should we give to the fact that accepting epiphenomenalism would make our own acceptance of it explicable irrespective of its truth? Assign a probability to each term, show how they combine, and tell me which sub-question you are least confident in and what evidence would move it most.
