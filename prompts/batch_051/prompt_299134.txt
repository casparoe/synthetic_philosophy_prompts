I got into an argument with my officemate last week about whether what you expect can literally change what you see, and it's been bugging me since. Her view is that perception is cognitively penetrable: that beliefs, desires, and background knowledge can reach down into the perceptual system and alter the experience itself, not just the interpretation you slap on it a second later. I keep replying that every alleged demonstration of this is really showing a shift in judgment, memory, or response bias — the person sees the same ambiguous stimulus and only the later labeling changes. Our running case is a hiker who expects a bear: does she genuinely see the bush as a bear, or does she see a vague shape and then fear makes her classify it as a bear extremely fast? Since both stories predict she'll say she saw a bear, I don't see how you'd ever pull them apart.

But I'm not sure that's right either, and I want to know what the best possible test would look like. Suppose I want to test the specific claim that cognition changes perceptual experience itself, not post-perceptual judgment. What should I measure, what pattern of results would count in favour of penetration, and what would count against it? And here's the part I really care about: after the best experiment anyone could run, what would a positive result still leave open? In particular, is there a residue — the inner feel of the experience having changed — that no behavioral measure could settle even in principle, no matter how clever the design?
