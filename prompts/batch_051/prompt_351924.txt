Suppose you are advising the grants committee of a private research initiative in philosophical logic. The initiative will fund a single five-year program of roughly six researchers, and it has already decided to support work on dialetheism and paraconsistent logic, construed broadly enough to include the substructural systems that now surround that debate. The committee does not want a survey of the field; it wants a defended allocation of effort. Your task is to answer one question: which three or four open questions, or lines of work, within this area are most worth prioritizing, and in what order?

The committee has been urged to treat the following as candidate priorities, though you are free to argue that some matter more or less than commonly thought, or to propose others.

One candidate is the Curry shift. The Curry paradox can trivialize several otherwise paraconsistent systems unless they restrict structural contraction (the rule that a premise may be used more than once) or adopt a nonstandard conditional. Some now argue that the real lesson of the paradoxes is not that some contradictions are true but that classical structural rules or the usual conditional must go. The committee wants to know whether the program's center of gravity should therefore move from paraconsistency toward substructural logic, and whether such a move undermines the original dialetheist motivation of keeping the unrestricted truth schema (a sentence is true just in case things are as it says) or unrestricted set comprehension (every predicate determines a set).

Another candidate is the just-true problem. If some contradictions are true, the dialetheist still needs to say of some sentences, including the sentences of dialetheism itself, that they are true and not false, and to express genuine disagreement with opponents. Can a paraconsistent language express this without introducing a negation or exclusion operator strong enough to reintroduce explosion?

A third candidate is the metainferential question. Some recent work suggests that the dispute between classical and paraconsistent accounts of the paradoxes is settled less by which individual inferences are valid than by which principles about validity itself hold. How much does a defensible dialetheist theory of truth depend on taking a stand at this metainferential level, and what would such a theory look like once that level is made explicit?

A fourth candidate is rational belief under contradiction. Standard probabilistic and belief-revision frameworks assume that an agent's beliefs are consistent. If a dialetheist genuinely accepts some contradictions, what should updating, decision-making, and theory acceptance look like? Is a paraconsistent epistemology a prerequisite for the rest of the program, or a downstream application that can wait?

A fifth candidate is recapture. Paraconsistent logicians often recover classical reasoning in consistent domains by adding consistency assumptions. Is this a principled explanation of why classical mathematics and science keep working, or a sign that the paraconsistent program has retreated from offering a genuine alternative to classical logic?

A sixth candidate is paraconsistent mathematics. How much of ordinary mathematics, or of naive set theory, can a paraconsistent framework actually recover, and which mathematical losses should be treated as unacceptable?

For your answer, do four things. First, state which three or four priorities you would fund first and rank them. Second, for each priority, explain which other questions it is upstream of, meaning what it would answer, reframe, or render moot. Third, assess its tractability for a group whose main strength is formal logic rather than professional mathematics or computer science, and indicate what a plausible progress report after two years would show. Fourth, make explicit the criteria behind the ranking, such as how many other problems depend on the question, tractability, neglectedness, and risk, and say what evidence over the next two years would lead you to reorder it.
