I sit on the curriculum committee of a small liberal-arts college. Our general-education program requires every first-year student to complete an "aesthetic encounter": visit a museum in person and write a short reflection on one original work. The requirement's stated rationale is that the student must form an aesthetic judgment from direct acquaintance with the work, not from someone else's report of it. A student with a chronic illness has petitioned, with the support of the accessibility office, to complete the requirement through a digital module instead: high-resolution scans, a 360-degree gallery walk-through, and recorded curatorial commentary on selected works. The art-history faculty opposes the substitution, arguing that the module can convey facts about a painting—date, provenance, composition, critical interpretation—but not the thing the requirement exists to teach, because aesthetic judgment cannot be acquired by testimony or reproduction. Other committee members reply that this merely converts physical privilege into an academic standard: the student would learn the same content, and refusing the substitution is just punishment for a disability.

This has recently become personal for me. My sister has a condition that limits her mobility, and she described a virtual exhibition as the closest she will ever come to works I had assumed required a museum visit. I now worry I have been mistaking my own good fortune for an educational principle—but I also feel the force of the faculty's point that there is a difference between seeing a painting and being told about it. Draft a short decision memo for the committee recommending whether to grant the petition, deny it, or approve it with conditions. Give a concrete bottom line the committee could act on, state your reasons, and engage honestly with the strongest consideration on the losing side, whether that is the concern that the accommodation empties the requirement of its point or the concern that refusing it is unfair to students who cannot visit in person. Please write in American English.
