I keep coming back to an argument that seems to show that constitutional precommitment is either toothless or a disguised form of rule by the dead. I cannot accept the conclusion, but I cannot find the step that fails, and I want a diagnosis rather than a defense of either horn. Here is the argument as I would present it.

(1) Ulysses's ropes are a genuine precommitment because he cannot undo them in the moment of temptation: his crew are under orders not to untie him, however sincerely he pleads once the sirens sing. If he could revoke the binding at will, exactly when his tempted self most wants out, it would not be a precommitment but a resolution.

(2) A constitution is meant to be a polity's version of this: an entrenched constraint that a people imposes on its own future self against predictable episodes of panic, populist rage, or factional spite. Take an entrenched free-speech guarantee as a representative case.

(3) For that guarantee to work as a precommitment, it must stand to later majorities as Ulysses's ropes stand to him: it must keep them from undoing the constraint at the moment they most want to.

(4) But a later majority can, through the amendment process, undo or narrow an entrenched guarantee; few entrenchments, if any, are insulated from every avenue of legal change. It is true that amendment is harder than ordinary legislation, requiring supermajorities, delays, or repeated votes. That only relocates the difficulty: either those hurdles can in principle be cleared by a sufficiently determined later majority, or they cannot.

(5) If they can be cleared, then the constraint is ultimately revocable by the very agent it was meant to bind, and it holds only until clearing the hurdles matters enough. Ulysses's ropes would be worth little if the crew would untie him once he asked long enough or gathered enough hands.

(6) If the hurdles cannot be cleared, the situation is worse: citizens alive today cannot change the rule even when they are resolved to, and they are governed by a decision made over their heads by people no longer alive. That is precisely the kind of control by the dead that we otherwise find objectionable in hereditary rule and in the legal devices by which a long-dead owner dictates the use of property forever.

(7) So constitutional precommitment seems to be either legitimate but largely illusory, or real but illegitimate. I would most like to keep both of the following: an entrenched guarantee genuinely constrains a later majority tempted to violate it, and a well-designed constitution is not merely one generation ruling its successors. But I cannot see how to keep both unless some step above gives way.

Which step do you reject, and why does it look so plausible before it gives way? What does rejecting it cost—for example, must I also revise what I said about Ulysses's ropes, or give up the assumption that precommitment works by preventing the agent from acting on its later preferences? And if you think I have built the dilemma on a false contrast from the start, or begged a question in describing precommitment, say so plainly and correct the setup rather than answering inside it.
