I run a Saturday workshop on democratic institutions for about 18 adults, mostly law students and junior public-sector staff; I have 90 minutes and can offer small prizes. I want a self-contained activity that makes participants feel the force of constitutional precommitment before any of the relevant vocabulary is introduced — no reading circulated, no opening lecture, and no terms like Ulysses contract, entrenchment, or dead hand until the debrief. By the end, I want them to see two things at once: why a group might rationally want to bind itself, and why the very rule that solves that problem immediately creates the problem of binding people who did not choose it — losing voters inside the assembly, and replacement members who join later.

Please design the activity in enough detail that I could run it from your instructions alone. I would like it built around a repeated public-goods game with real but small stakes, using tokens redeemable for prizes, in which each round every participant anonymously chooses how much of a fixed endowment to keep and how much to contribute to a common fund that the facilitator multiplies and returns in equal shares. Set the multiplier so that universal cooperation is better for everyone than universal defection, while unilateral free-riding is individually tempting. The game should run in five stages:

1. Three or four unregulated rounds, so participants experience the temptation to free-ride and the erosion of contributions under anonymity.
2. A constitutional convention at which the assembly may, by simple majority, adopt a binding contribution floor for all later rounds, together with an amendment rule of its own choosing, including a threshold and a delay before amendments take effect.
3. Several regulated rounds under the adopted floor, so participants experience both the benefit of the rule and the fact that it binds those who voted against it.
4. A replacement stage at which a few original players leave with their tokens and are replaced by newcomers who had no vote on the floor.
5. An amendment vote under the adopted threshold, so the newcomers experience inheriting a rule they never ratified.

Give exact rules, numeric payoffs for an 18-person group, ballot wording for the convention and amendment votes, a minute-by-minute 90-minute schedule, and a debrief that maps concrete moments in the game onto the conceptual pieces: the free-rider problem as the motivation for self-binding, the difference between binding oneself and a majority binding a dissenting minority, the amendment threshold and delay as entrenchment, and the replacement members as the dead hand problem. In the debrief, show where each move could have been resisted and what it would cost to resist it — for example, refusing any floor costs the group the public good, while accepting a high threshold costs later majorities their flexibility. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run, such as the group cooperating too well in the unregulated rounds, voting for a floor out of social desirability, or an amendment vote that removes the floor and dissolves the dilemma, and explain how a facilitator should handle each failure. End by being explicit about what the game cannot reproduce: real constitutional coercion, genuine intergenerational timescales, the multiple incommensurable values at stake in actual constitution-making, and the difference between a facilitator enforcing a rule and a state enforcing law.

Make sure the activity is runnable with nothing more than printed slips, tokens, a ballot box, and a whiteboard. Do not include any readings, citations, or external materials, since the discussion must stand entirely on what happens in the room.
