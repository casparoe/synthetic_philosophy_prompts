I'm a third-year undergraduate in a course called the Economics of Future Minds. This week we are on Robin Hanson's The Age of Em, and I am preparing for a seminar in which two students will debate a single contested point. I'd like you to write the dialogue for me: a short, realistic exchange between two equally competent participants in which neither side is a strawman and each side responds to the other's actual arguments rather than taking turns monologuing.

The point at issue is whether most em wages would fall to subsistence. The first position holds that, in a competitive economy of whole brain emulations—uploads of human minds that can be copied, run at different speeds, and put to work in many instances at once—the wage for an ordinary em's labor, measured per subjective hour, would be driven down to roughly the cost of the hardware, energy, and memory needed to run it. Any premium above that cost is a profit opportunity: run another copy of a productive em until the marginal copy earns no more than its running costs. The second position rejects this as the default equilibrium. It argues that the arbitrage story assumes productive ems can be copied and deployed without their consent, that ems would not coordinate to defend their pay, and that the institutions needed to enforce such coordination could not arise. On this view, ems would retain control over their own copying through property-like rights in their mental content, and would use labor organizations, reputation systems, or precommitments to keep wages above subsistence. Have the characters push on each other's mechanisms: why would or would not cartel discipline hold, how cheaply could a high-wage em break away, whether scarcity premia for unusual skills could persist, and whether the first position's arbitrage argument presupposes the very institutional background it claims will be eroded. End the dialogue when the disagreement has become sharper and more empirical, not when it has been resolved.
