I'm a backend engineer and I've never taken a philosophy course, so maybe this already exists under some name. I've been trying to settle when it's okay to say an AI system "believes" something, and I came up with what I call the patch test.

The test: you can say a system genuinely believes P if some part of its internal state stands for P, and if you could surgically overwrite just that part with a false value while leaving everything else alone, the system would then act the way you'd expect something that believed the false P to act. I don't mean one neuron literally; just a stable, localizable chunk. It's like debugging: flip one variable at runtime and watch what happens downstream. If flipping it changes behavior in a way that makes sense relative to the patched value, that variable was functioning as a belief. If there's no such variable, or flipping it does nothing, or the behavior you get makes no sense relative to the new value, then it didn't have the belief.

Chess engine: if the eval says +4 and I flip it to -4, and the engine starts playing wild, unsound attacking moves instead of consolidating, I'm fine saying it believed it was winning. Self-driving car: the scene graph has an object tagged "pedestrian"; if I delete or shift that entry and the car stops braking and drives on, it believed a pedestrian was in front of it. A thermostat doesn't believe anything because the curled strip of metal isn't a stored variable, it's just the sensor reading itself being reshaped by the temperature; there's no separate claim about the world I could patch while leaving the input alone.

The hard case is LLMs. I've had one explain to me at length that it believes there's a mug on the table in a story, but I can't find any localizable "mug on table" chunk in the weights to flip, so by my test I'm forced to say it doesn't believe there's a mug there—it just outputs text that's consistent with believing it. That feels right to me but also weirdly strong. I also half-remember someone saying you should treat a chess computer as if it had beliefs because that predicts its moves—something about an intentional stance—and I never knew wheather that was meant literally or just as a trick.

So: is the patch test coherent? Does it already exist under another name? Take it in its strongest version and tell me where it genuinely breaks, and whether it forces me to say LLMs don't have any beliefs at all. And please say how confident you are in your answer, and flag the one point you're least sure about.
