Last night a friend and I spent several hours arguing about whether a language could do without pronouns — without words like "I," "you," "here," and "now" — replacing them everywhere with descriptive phrases and pointing. The argument kept circling a distinction I could not name, and I want to know whether that distinction has an established name and how far it really extends.

The pattern is this. "I" does not behave like a name or a description. If it meant "the person speaking this very sentence," then "I am in danger" and "the person speaking this very sentence is in danger" ought to say the same thing. But they seem to differ in how they connect to action. Someone who realizes "I am being followed" will behave one way; someone who realizes "the person speaking this very sentence is being followed" — without independently taking themselves to be that person — may simply study the sentence and do nothing. The descriptive phrase can be true without producing the same response, while "I" seems to grip the person using it in a different way. The same difficulty arises with "now." A person can sincerely believe "the meeting is at noon," wonder whether it is now noon, and miss the meeting; plausibly they cannot believe "the meeting is now" and remain in that same state of wondering. Yet "now" seems to carry no more descriptive content than the time at which it is uttered.

My friend maintains that all of this is a dispensable feature of English, and that a well-designed language could eliminate such words with no loss except convenience. I found myself unable to say precisely what his language would be giving up — whether it is a kind of meaning, a kind of information, a role in explaining action, or something else — and that is what I want to pin down.

So my question is not merely whether there is a word for "I" and "now" as a class. It is whether there is an established term for the specific difference I am describing: a content, or state of mind, that resists replacement by a description even when the description picks out the same object, because it is tied to the act of the one who says it or thinks it. And is there a standard argument, carrying a standard label, for the stronger claim that no language can do without at least some expressions of this kind? Or is that stronger claim still genuinely open? If nothing settled corresponds exactly to what I have described, I would prefer to be told where the nearest concepts begin and end than to be given a label that blurs the thing I tripped over.
