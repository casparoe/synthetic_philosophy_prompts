I work as a senior policy analyst for a state legislature's judiciary committee, where several times each session we must take a position on bills that would waive the state's sovereign immunity for a narrow class of claims. The committee chair has asked for a single reusable decision procedure rather than a fresh argument for each bill, because the debate in hearings keeps sliding between "this family deserves something" and "we cannot open the treasury to every mistake," and neither reflex settles anything. I have no research staff, and the procedure must be runnable by me in under two hours and explainable by the chair to constituents who are not lawyers. The chair will use it as the committee's default recommendation but wants to be told clearly when the procedure is handing her a discretionary call rather than a conclusion.

The deeper issue we keep circling is the question of whether the state itself can do wrong at all, or whether wrongs are always done by individual officials with the public treasury merely picking up the bill. That difference seems to matter: some bills concern an institutional failure that continued after warnings and is naturally described as the state's own wrong, while others concern an official's error inside a system that was otherwise sound. I want that distinction operationalized without the committee having to use the phrase "institutional failure." The procedure should proceed through ordered questions, specify what counts as a sufficient answer to each, indicate where it stops with "recommend waiver," where it stops with "recommend no waiver," and where it must stop and hand the decision to the chair because the remaining question is one of value rather than analysis. It should also say explicitly what the procedure cannot decide and where it is likely to misfire. Then apply it to three bills: (1) waiver for a man whose conviction was overturned after it emerged that a state forensic analyst overstated the reliability of hair-microscopy evidence; (2) waiver for injuries caused by a state-maintained road after inspectors had flagged the hazard for four years without repair; and (3) waiver for harm to a child resulting from a foster-care placement decision that was reasonable on the information available at the time it was made. At least one of these should come out against the committee's political instincts; explain which feature of the case forces that answer, and say whether that is a defect in the procedure or a place where the procedure is correctly overriding sympathy.
