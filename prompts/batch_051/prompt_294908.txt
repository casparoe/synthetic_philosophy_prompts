In The Methods of Ethics, Henry Sidgwick presents a family of observations that have come to be called the paradox of hedonism. As he formulates it, the reflective pursuit of one's own pleasure tends to fail on its own terms: the person who keeps asking whether an experience is enjoyable often weakens the enjoyment, while absorption in some other end is, for many people, a more reliable route to pleasant experience. Write a dialogue between two contemporary philosophers who disagree about how much weight to give this thought. One defends a view close to Sidgwick's: that the paradox reveals something deep about self-defeating ends, and that the best strategy for a rational egoist may be to avoid focusing on pleasure directly. The other resists this conclusion, arguing that the supposed paradox is really an empirical point about attention and affect, or that a sophisticated hedonist can adopt indirect policies without abandoning hedonism. Have them engage each other's actual moves: for instance, one might press the other on whether the indirect strategy is still genuinely hedonistic, while the other might press on whether the paradox survives if pleasure is understood as a felt quality rather than an object of propositional desire. Ensure neither character is a strawman, and end with each identifying the strongest point the other has made.
