A colleague who thinks Popper was right the first time about Darwinism — that natural selection is not a testable scientific theory but a metaphysical research programme — gave me this argument, and I can't find the slip.

(1) A theory is scientific only if some possible observation could count against it.
(2) For natural selection, no observation counts against it: whatever turns up, a Darwinian can redescribe it as adaptive after all, or as a byproduct, or as drift, or as a response to a changed environment, so the core claims of natural selection survive untouched.
(3) So natural selection is not scientific.
(4) But the same protective maneuver is available in every science: any apparent anomaly can be absorbed by adjusting auxiliary assumptions about instruments, background conditions, or unobserved entities, so the core of any theory can be kept from falsification.
(5) By the reasoning in (2)–(3), no theory whatever is scientific — which is absurd.
(6) Therefore (1) is false.

I would like to keep (1) in some form, and I also want to keep the conclusion that Darwinism is science. So I need to know which step to reject, and what rejecting it costs. My colleague says if I reject (2) I am underestimating how much of the Darwinian tradition has been just-so storytelling; if I reject (4) I am denying a point about how real science handles anomalies; if I reject (6) I owe an account of why the same form of argument is valid against Darwinism but not against physics. Where does the argument go wrong, and what is the least costly way out? As you answer, separate what is settled about Popper's stated views and about falsificationism from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of biology, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
