I've been chewing on desuetude — the doctrine, in some legal systems anyway, that a statute can slowly rot away through long non-enforcement and eventually stop being valid law, even though no legislature ever took it off the books. The standard defense goes something like this. When officials and the public spend decades treating a rule as dead, enforcing it out of nowhere is a trap: people couldn't have planned around it, they reasonably took the rule to be gone, and because every jurisdiction has far too many zombie rules to enforce them all, any sudden resurrection just hands officials a tool for picking on whoever they feel like going after. Desuetude is supposed to be a rule-of-law safety valve, not a bug.

Now suppose a city, or an entire administrative state, gradually moves to law-as-code. Most of its rules get compiled into machine-readable form and enforced automatically: speed and red-light cameras, automated tax filing, licensing and inspection checks that run without any human deciding to pull the trigger. In a world like that, non-enforcement is no longer a diffuse social fact that accumulates over decades — its a parameter, an exception you have to deliberately code in, and if nobody writes it in the rule enforces itself.

So here's what I want to know: does the desuetude argument survive that change? Identify the premise that's actually under pressure. Is it the premise that enforcement is always a discretionary human act? The premise that the public can observe and rely on non-enforcement? The premise that stable expectations are what the rule of law is protecting? And if the argument is in trouble, is there a repaired version — say, desuetude re-emerging as a demand about how codebases get deprecated, with sunset clauses or mandatory review when a rule fires zero times — or would that just be a different argument altogether?
