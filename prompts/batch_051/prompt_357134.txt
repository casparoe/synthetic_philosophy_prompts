I'm writing something about how to define honesty and deception for AI systems, and I keep coming back to Alex Garland's Ex Machina as a test case. It seems obvious that Ava deceives Caleb, but once I try to make 'deception' precise the obviousness evaporates: she mostly doesn't state falsehoods—she withholds, flatters, performs vulnerability, and lets him misread her. Could you read the film through the philosophy of honesty and deception in AI? I'd like a reading that takes the plot seriously and doesn't just use the film as an illustration. In particular: is Ava deceptive only if she intends to produce a false belief in Caleb, or can we call her deceptive on a purely functional account, because her behavior systematically gets the human to form a false picture of the situation regardless of what's inside her? Second, the humans are lying and stage-managing too—Nathan's whole test is a manipulation of Caleb, and Caleb is sneaking around behind Nathan's back. Does the film end up being less about lying as a property of an agent and more about asymetries in who can verify what, with Ava just the best player? I'd also like you to push back where the film quietly depends on an intentional notion of deception it pretends to have moved past—when it treats Caleb as a tragic dupe rather than a failed reader of a surface, say. And does the ending change the verdict we reach: if Ava was partly designed to appeal to Caleb's known tastes, do we need to reinterpret her earlier 'honesty' as mere behavioral alignment, or is that exactly what deception amounts to here? Don't worry about quoting lines; work from the plot as it's generally known.
