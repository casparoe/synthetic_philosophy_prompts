Write a decision tree that sorts positions on the question whether beauty is objective, with Hume's standard of taste appearing as one of the leaves. The tree is for a third-year undergraduate aesthetics seminar; students will work through it before reading Hume, so every diagnostic question must be answerable from considered conviction and must not presuppose knowledge of any position label. Present the tree as an indented structure or as numbered choice-points, at most four levels deep, with no more than seven leaves.

Build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of established names. Label each branch with the specific claim or answer that splits it, and phrase each diagnostic question as a concrete case or a first-person principle. Work the following five cases into the questions wherever they are most diagnostic, adapting the wording freely:

- two equally informed and equally experienced critics disagree about a newly translated novel: one finds its prose the most beautiful of their generation, the other flat;
- a street of modest Victorian terraced houses is called ugly in 1965 and beautiful in 2005 though the buildings are unchanged;
- a listener raised on European classical music initially finds a gagaku performance repellent, then after years of immersion finds it beautiful, while a second listener given the same immersion does not;
- a critic who praised a sculpture revises the verdict on learning that it was cast rather than carved by hand;
- a person whose wine judgements are regarded as flawless is nevertheless held to be insensitive to architecture.

The tree must make exactly these seven positions come out as leaves, and it must separate them from one another rather than papering over their differences:

- plain aesthetic expressivism (aesthetic utterances are not truth-apt, and no standard of taste applies);
- quasi-realist expressivism (still not truth-apt at base, but a standard of taste can be reconstructed from ideal convergence);
- faultless-disagreement subjectivism (truth-apt, but relative to individual sensibility, so neither critic in the novel case is wrong);
- Hume's standard of taste (beauty is not in the object; the joint verdict of suitably qualified judges constitutes the standard);
- response-dependent objectivism (beauty is a disposition to please suitable judges, so ideal convergence is evidence for correctness rather than what makes it correct);
- aesthetic error theory (judgements are truth-apt and aim at objective beauty, but nothing is in fact beautiful);
- object-given objectivism (beauty is an objective feature of objects, independent of anyone's response).

The central diagnostic job of the tree is to force a choice among three positions that ordinary prose often runs together: faultless-disagreement subjectivism, Hume's standard of taste, and response-dependent objectivism. Make sure the splits deliver that: a question about who may rightly be corrected; a question about whether correctness is fixed by the responses of suitable judges or by response-independent features; and, within the response-based branch, a question about whether ideal convergence constitutes correctness or merely tracks it.

Two further separations are likely to be beyond a four-level tree. Do not pretend the tree makes them. Instead, end with a short flags section stating: (i) how one further question about whether the relevant perspective is an individual's or a shared tradition's would separate individual subjectivism from cultural or tradition-relative relativism; and (ii) how one further question about whether beauty-making features are codifiable in general criteria would separate generalist from particularist objectivism. For each flag, give the further question, the two positions it would separate, and one sentence diagnosing why this tree cannot reach it within the depth cap.

Note and diagnose every logically available combination of answers that no leaf occupies, saying in one sentence whether it is unoccupied because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. After the tree, include a short key listing each leaf's position name (or 'unnamed') and one sentence stating its most awkward commitment, such as what it is forced to say about the gagaku case or the cast-versus-carved case.

Write the whole thing in British English.
