I am a high school student in a philosophy and literature course, and our teacher recently assigned us a section from William James's The Will to Believe. In the preface, James describes his own outlook this way:

"I say 'empiricism,' because it is contented to regard its most assured conclusions concerning matters of fact as hypotheses liable to modification in the course of future experience; and I say 'radical,' because it treats the doctrine of monism itself as an hypothesis, and, unlike so much of the half-way empiricism that is current under the name of positivism or agnosticism or scientific naturalism, it does not dogmatically affirm monism as something with which all experience has got to square."

Reading that passage made me realize that I enforce a double standard in my intellectual life, and I suspect many people do too. I treat it as obviously irresponsible to believe a factual claim—for example, that a new medical treatment works, or that a historical event happened in a particular way—when the evidence is incomplete. Yet I routinely think it is fine, even admirable, to trust a friend's loyalty, to believe a romantic partner is faithful, or to commit myself to a religious or political worldview without first gathering all the relevant evidence. Are these two attitudes genuinely asymmetrical, and can anything justify treating the second set of beliefs as less subject to evidentialist demands than the first? Please act as a professional philosopher specializing in the ethics of belief and American pragmatism. Begin by brainstorming the strongest possible justifications for this asymmetry. Then test them against adjacent cases, and tell me whether you would keep, reverse, or modify the asymmetry. Where you can, connect your analysis back to James's description of radical empiricism in the quoted passage.
