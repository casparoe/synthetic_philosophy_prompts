I am taking a philosophy of mind course this semester, and we have just finished a unit on early computationalism, centered on the way von Neumann compared the nervous system to the computing machines of his own period. In reviewing my notes, I wrote down a reconstruction of one line of thought I took from the reading, and I now cannot tell whether it is a clean argument or a chain of subtle confusions. Here is what I wrote:

(1) A person's mental life is realized entirely by the activity of neurons in the brain. (2) The relevant activity of a neuron consists in its firing or not firing an all-or-nothing action potential, much as a vacuum tube either conducts or does not conduct. (3) Any system whose elements contribute to the system's operation only through all-or-nothing events is a digital system. (4) Everything that matters about the operation of a digital system can in principle be simulated by a digital computer. (5) Therefore, everything that matters about the operation of the brain can in principle be simulated by a digital computer. (6) If everything that matters about the operation of the brain can be simulated by a digital computer, then the mind is a computational phenomenon. (7) Therefore, a sufficiently powerful digital computer running the right program could in principle have a mind.

I am fairly confident that von Neumann himself was more cautious than this reconstruction, since he emphasized that the nervous system is only partly digital and that simulating an operation may not be the same as reproducing it. My suspicion is that the flaw lies either in the move from all-or-nothing signaling to calling the whole system digital, or else in the final move from successful simulation of the brain's operation to the claim that the mind is computational. But I cannot say exactly which premise should be rejected, or how it should be restated so that the argument becomes either sound but weaker, or revealingly false. Please go through the argument step by step and tell me whether it is valid, which step fails if it does, what the corrected version of that step would be, and whether the conclusion might still be true even though the argument is defective.
