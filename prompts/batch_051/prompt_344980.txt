I'm the student rep on my school's science fair ethics committee, and I have to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's meeting. A junior, Maya, wants to test whether crayfish learn to avoid the side of a tank where they get a mild electric shock, and whether they still avoid it a week later. She's framing it as "invertebrate learning and memory." Under our school's science fair manual, animal welfare review is only required for vertebrates, so invertebrates are basically auto-approved and by the rulebook there's nothing to discuss. But the committee is split anyway. One group says crayfish don't have a real brain, just a bunch of ganglia, and their behavior is reflexes with no one home; asking for review is just sentimental. The other group says crayfish have nociceptors, learn avoidance, and show pain-like behavior after injury, and that drawing the ethical line at "has a backbone" is arbitrary; if there's any real chance they suffer, we shouldn't approve it as-is. Some of them keep saying the actual question is which computations matter morally, not which body parts an animal has -- if it links injury signals to behavior and learning, that should count, not the spine. I have to write the memo and I can't just summerize both sides.

Can you draft the memo for me? It should recommend a concrete decision: approve as-is, reject, or approve with conditions (and if conditions, say exactly what they are). It should engage the strongest argument for whichever side you don't take, and say enough about the underlying uncertainty that both groups can see their view was actually considered. I need something the committee can act on at the meeting, not a philosophy essay. Aim for about a page, and make the very last line start with "Verdict:" followed by approve, reject, or approve with conditions (plus a short phrase if there are conditions).
