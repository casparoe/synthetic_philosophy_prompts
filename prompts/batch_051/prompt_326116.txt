I'm rereading Plato's Timaeus and Critias for a course on ancient historiography, and I keep getting stuck on the section where Critias recounts what Solon heard from the Egyptian priests at Sais—roughly Timaeus 22b–23d, with the fuller narrative in Critias. The priest tells Solon that the Greeks are like children with respect to their memory of the past, because they preserve only the memory of a single great flood, whereas Egypt has maintained a continuous written record through repeated cycles of destruction. He explains that the Nile protects Egypt from the alternating devastations by fire and water that periodically destroy other peoples, and he claims that Athens was founded nine thousand years before the conversation and once fought a great war against an aggressor from beyond the Pillars of Heracles. That, as far as I can tell, is the seed of the Atlantis story.

I am not interested in whether Atlantis existed. What I want to understand is what this speech is supposed to establish within the dialogue itself. Is the priest's account presented as literal history, as a likely account or probable tale—the thing Timaeus later calls eikos muthos—or as something closer to a thought experiment about an ideal city under stress? What criteria does Plato give in the dialogue for distinguishing an account that demands belief as a historical record from one that functions as a mythic vehicle, and is that distinction stable once the Atlantis narrative gets going? I would also like help understanding the rhetorical work of the Egyptian setting and the claimed ancient written records: does Plato need an external archive to make the story plausible, and does the text give readers any internal way to evaluate that claim?

A close reading would be ideal, focused on the demarcation problem rather than a survey of later Atlantis theories; if the later reception illuminates the demarcation issue, a short mention is fine.
