EXAM QUESTION
From a broadly consequentialist standpoint, does the suffering of wild animals generate an obligation for human beings to intervene in nature? Present the strongest form of the case for intervention, then present and evaluate what you take to be the strongest objection to that case.

STUDENT ANSWER
Wild animal suffering matters because consequentialism does not care who causes a pain. A pain is bad because of how it feels, not because the cause was a human being, a pathogen, a predator, or the weather. So the deer that slowly starves over the winter matters just as much, pain-for-pain, as the deer shot by a hunter. Once you accept that, the scale is startling: there is far more animal life in the wild than in human care, so the total amount of suffering in wild nature probably dwarfs most other sources of suffering. That by itself creates a strong prima facie duty to reduce wild animal suffering wherever we can.

The strongest objection, I think, is that natural suffering is not something anyone has to fix because nature left alone is good for animals and for ecosystems. Predation keeps populations healthy, and animals die naturally, so their deaths are not a moral problem. This objection mistakenly puts the good of species and ecosystems above the good of individual sentient creatures. From a consequentialist point of view, what ultimately matters is suffering itself, not whether the process that produced it looked natural. Since the appeal to nature's overall functioning is just a version of the naturalistic fallacy, the objection fails, and the prima facie duty becomes an all-things-considered obligation to intervene.
