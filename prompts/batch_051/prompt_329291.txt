I've increasingly come to think that the honor-based account of major moral revolutions associated with Kwame Anthony Appiah is roughly right: practices such as dueling among men of rank, footbinding in China, and chattel slavery in the Americas did not end primarily because better arguments against them appeared or because the balance of material interests shifted; they ended because the practices lost their honor and came to mark their participants as contemptible. I mean the strong version of the thesis: honor is not one causal factor among several, but the mechanism through which arguments and interests had to pass before they could move anyone, so that the timing and suddenness of a norm's collapse are best predicted by asking when and for whom it became dishonorable. Tell me what would have to be true for this to be wrong. I want an honest map of the view's exposure rather than a standard defense or refutation, with defeaters sorted from decisive to merely awkward. Specifically: can the American slavery case really be told as an honor story when it ended in a war in which interests and coercion were plainly doing the work? Can the view be distinguished from a material-interest account once "honor" is allowed to track economic and political standing? What would show that argument, not honor, was the primary engine in at least one major reform? I also want to know which concessions a committed defender would make—what they themselves would count as disconfirmation—and what the view predicts about which norms survive that an interest-centered or argument-centered rival would not predict. Finally, tell me whether, pressed by these objections, the thesis retreats into unfalsifiability by redefining honor to include whatever in fact changed, and whether that should worry me for a claim of this kind or is the expected cost of a thesis about social meaning.
