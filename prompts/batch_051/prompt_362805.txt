You are a professional philosopher of mathematics who works on the epistemology of consistency claims. Think through the question carefully before you answer, and begin by brainstorming the relevant evidence and the structure of the problem; only then settle on your considered, numerically explicit view.

I want your actual credence in the following proposition:

(P) First-order Peano arithmetic is consistent: there is no finite derivation from the usual Peano axioms, including the full first-order induction schema, of a contradiction such as 0 = 1. Equivalently, there is no formula φ in the language of arithmetic such that both φ and ¬φ are derivable.

Some clarifications. I am not asking whether PA can prove its own consistency; Gödel's second incompleteness theorem shows it cannot. I am not asking about ZFC, and I am not asking whether mathematicians would abandon classical arithmetic if a contradiction turned up. I am asking for your epistemic probability that P is true, understood as a determinate mathematical fact about which you may nevertheless be less than certain. If P were false, classical PA would prove every sentence in its language, so this is not a marginal technical fault.

Please give your answer as follows.

1. State a single number: your credence in P.

2. Decompose it. Identify the sub-questions your credence rests on, assign each a rough probability, and give a sentence or two of justification for each. Then show how the pieces combine. Your decomposition should address at least these factors, though you may add to or restructure them:

(a) The probability that our canonical concept of the natural numbers, zero and successor together with the completed sequence of all naturals, is coherent and determinate enough to serve as the intended structure for PA, so that its quantifiers have a definite range.

(b) The probability that, conditional on such an intended structure existing, every Peano axiom is true in it, with particular attention to the unrestricted first-order induction schema rather than the visually trivial axioms for zero and successor.

(c) The historical base rate. Frege's Basic Law V seemed self-evident and was inconsistent. How much should that make a reflective reasoner worry that our current, apparently self-evident arithmetic axioms might hide a contradiction too?

(d) The evidential weight of no contradiction having been found. Conditional on PA being inconsistent, how likely is it that a contradiction would already have been found through ordinary mathematical use, automated theorem proving, or deliberate search? For example, the logician Edward Nelson once circulated a claimed inconsistency proof that was found to contain an error; factor in whatever you take that episode to show about detection difficulty or about how seriously the possibility is taken.

(e) The weight of relative consistency proofs, such as Gentzen's proof of PA's consistency using transfinite induction up to ε₀, given that such proofs employ principles whose own consistency is not provable in PA.

A natural way to combine the pieces, which you may adopt or replace with your own explicit structure, is: P(consistent) = [P(intended structure exists) × P(PA axioms true in it | intended structure exists)] + [P(PA not sound) × P(consistent nevertheless | PA not sound)], with the final term informed by (d) and (e). Whatever structure you use, make the arithmetic of the combination visible.

3. Say which single sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, meaning where a modest change in a component probability would move the final credence most.

4. Say what concrete evidence or mathematical development would most change that sensitive component, and give a rough estimate of how far it would push your final credence.

Do not substitute a discussion of ZFC, constructivism, or paraconsistent logic for an answer about PA; those are relevant only insofar as they actually bear on P.
