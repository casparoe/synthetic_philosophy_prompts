For most of my career I worked in the natural sciences, where a good definition is one that survives contact with awkward cases. I have lately been reading political philosophy, and I have become absorbed by the strand of thought that treats the modern state as a kind of corporation — a continuing legal person constituted by a charter or constitution — and that treats the great chartered trading companies of the early modern period, such as the East India Company and the Hudson's Bay Company, as partly sovereign bodies rather than as ordinary commercial firms. From this reading I have assembled the following six criteria for counting something as a state in the corporate-sovereignty sense, as opposed to a private corporation operating inside someone else's state:

1. Continuing legal personality. The entity is a single legal person, distinct from its changing membership, capable of owning property, suing and being sued, and surviving the departure of any of its members.

2. A constitutive charter. It has a founding instrument that creates its organs, distributes powers among them, and prescribes a rule for amending or replacing the arrangements.

3. Territorial jurisdiction. It claims and exercises exclusive jurisdiction over a defined territory and the people within it, rather than authority over a narrow class of persons such as members, shareholders, or employees.

4. General law-making and adjudication. Within that jurisdiction it can issue binding rules of general application and run courts or similarly binding dispute-resolution bodies.

5. Legitimate coercion and fiscal power. It commands the coercive apparatus that is treated as the sole legitimate enforcer inside the jurisdiction, and it can impose general taxes or levies rather than merely charging for services.

6. External standing. It can deal with other states in its own name, as a counterpart, rather than only as a private litigant or the subordinate agency of a host government.

Now consider a deliberately constructed case, though it borrows from real practices such as free ports and special economic zones. The Meridian Port Company holds a 99-year charter from a recognised coastal state to administer a fifteen-square-mile free port. The charter gives the company the exclusive power to legislate the port's commercial, civil, and criminal codes; to run its only courts; to finance and command a port constabulary; to levy taxes and tariffs; and to operate border and immigration controls. It may negotiate operational arrangements with neighbouring states about customs and public-health inspection, and its travel document is accepted by those states for visa-free entry. No state, however, treats the company as a diplomatic counterpart. The host state retains formal title to the land and continues to call itself sovereign, although the charter cannot be amended or revoked without the company's consent, and in practice the host state exercises no administrative power inside the port.

Does the Meridian Port Company qualify as a state in the sense captured by my six criteria? I am less interested in the bare label than in the following. Work through each criterion in turn and say whether it is satisfied, violated, or left underdetermined by the case. Then say whether the criteria themselves need revision: in particular, are 'territorial jurisdiction' and 'external standing' marking something independently real here, or is the first a proxy for effective administrative control and the second a downstream effect of the other five? Finally, which single additional fact about the case would most change your overall answer?
