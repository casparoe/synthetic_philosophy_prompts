I write historical fiction, and the longer I do it the more I think my craft problem and the historians' presentism problem are the same shape, but I cant tell whether thats a real structural fact or just the word "perspective" doing too much work. Heres the mapping as it looks to me. When I write in free indirect style, I let the narration take on a character's moral vocabulary without me or the narrator endorsing it — the prose says "the girl was ruined" not because I think she was, but because that's the only shape the thought can take in that character's world. Historians who say we should understand an age "in its own terms" seem to be trying to do the same thing: present actions under descriptions available to the agents without thereby ratifying those descriptions. In both cases a writer keeps their own standards out of the representation, and an audience has to infer the writer's actual attitude from the gap between what's said and how its said. My question is whether this is a genuine structural identity or a loose analogy. If its genuine, does anything transfer? Narrative theory has a whole toolkit for unreliable narrators and for how an author can hold or lose ironic distance from a viewpoint character; would a historian who goes native in a past moral world be unreliable in the same technical sense, and would the same failure modes and fixes apply? If its not genuine, where exactly does the mapping break — is it that I invented my characters' world, so there's no independent fact about what their "own terms" were, while the historian's escape is constrained by evidence? And give me one concrete prediction the parallel makes about one side that turns out false about the other, so I can see where the analogy gives out.
