I teach formal epistemology and decision theory, and I serve on the methods advisory group of a state agency's program evaluation office. The office's analysts decide, several times a month, whether to commission an evaluation study before recommending that a program be continued, expanded, redesigned, or terminated. The programs under review have annual budgets from a few hundred thousand to the tens of millions of dollars. The analysts have no formal training in decision theory; the director signs off on every recommendation and is openly skeptical of spending money on studies when a decision could simply be made; and the legislature expects every study to be justified by its effect on a decision, not by curiosity or thoroughness. Studies cost between $30,000 and $500,000 and take two to twelve months, and because the funding calendar is fixed, commissioning one almost always imposes a delay cost of its own.

The office has latched onto the slogan that information can never hurt in expectation — the result usually called Good's theorem — and treats it as a license to commission any study that is not obviously useless. The theorem, as I understand it, says only this: a cost-free signal cannot reduce the expected utility of an expected-utility maximizer, because the decision maker can always ignore the signal but may, with luck, use it to match action to state better. It says nothing on its own about costly information, delay, signals that could not change the chosen action under any outcome, or decision makers who only partly resemble that idealization.

I want you to turn Good's theorem into a reusable procedure for these analysts: a step-by-step decision procedure, in the form of a checklist or an ordered sequence of tests, that an analyst with no decision theory can follow to decide whether a proposed study is worth commissioning for a particular decision. The procedure should work through the questions in order of increasing difficulty: (i) which decision the study is meant to inform, and whether its findings will arrive before that decision must be made; (ii) whether any possible finding could change the recommended action, and if not, why the study's value for that decision is zero however precise or inexpensive it would be; (iii) how to extract the value of the information from a rough model of possible findings, available actions, and outcomes, treating the study as a chance node whose result feeds a later choice; (iv) how to add the study's price, the cost of delay, and the non-instrumental benefits of evaluation, such as transparency and legislative accountability, which the theorem does not price; and (v) where the procedure should stop and report that the theorem cannot settle the matter rather than producing false precision.

Then show the full run of the procedure on these three applications: (1) a process evaluation of a literacy program the office is legally required to continue regardless of findings, which the director wants anyway as due diligence; (2) an impact evaluation whose results would arrive only after the program's sunset date but before a decision about a much larger successor program; and (3) a $400,000 randomized trial that would delay by nine months a decision about a politically sensitive program whose outcomes the analysts can describe only as better or worse and not yet as utilities. At least one of these must produce an answer the director will not like; say plainly which one, and why the procedure's discomfort is part of its value. Conclude by stating what the procedure cannot do — what it must take from outside itself in the way of values, probabilities, and a decision rule — so the analysts do not mistake it for a machine that turns studies into decisions.
