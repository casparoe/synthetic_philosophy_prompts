I am a novelist, not a philosopher, and I would like an expert to stress-test a piece of reasoning I have written into a character's mouth. In the scene, a former population epidemiologist named Alma argues over dinner that humanity should not expect a long, galaxy-spanning future. I want the scene to be intellectually honest: if her argument is valid, I will let it stand; if it has a hidden flaw, I need to know exactly which step fails so that another character can expose it convincingly later.

A philosopher friend who read the scene tells me Alma is groping toward something called the doomsday argument, but he will not tell me which formulation is the defensible one, so I am asking you.

Please act as a professional philosopher with expertise in formal epistemology, Bayesian confirmation theory, and anthropic reasoning. Think carefully. Before giving your final verdict, brainstorm the possible failure points and the strongest repair available for each step, and consider whether the argument can be made valid on some reading. Then answer questions (a) through (d) in order.

Here is Alma's argument as I have drafted it:

(1) I know, roughly, that I am approximately the one-hundred-billionth human being to have lived.
(2) Lacking any special information about my place in the human story, I should reason as if I had been selected at random from the set of all humans who will ever live, with each position from first to last equally likely to be mine.
(3) Consider two live hypotheses: SOON, on which the total human population will be about 200 billion because we become extinct within a few generations; and VAST, on which the total will be at least 100 trillion because we spread through the galaxy over millions of years.
(4) If SOON is true, the probability that I would find myself among the first 100 billion humans is about one-half.
(5) If VAST is true, the probability that I would find myself among the first 100 billion humans is at most one in a thousand.
(6) So my actual position, somewhere among the first 100 billion, is about 500 times more likely if SOON is true than if VAST is true.
(7) Therefore, given my actual position, SOON is about 500 times more likely to be true than VAST.
(8) Unless I already had independent reason, before considering my position, to regard VAST as hundreds of times more plausible than SOON, and no one at this dinner party has any such reason, I should conclude that SOON is probably correct and that a long human future is unlikely.

Questions:

(a) Is the argument valid as written? If it is not, identify exactly which numbered step fails, and be explicit about the difference between an invalid step and a merely contestable premise.

(b) Give the strongest reading of each step on which the argument can be repaired. State the repaired inference explicitly and what additional assumptions it needs, especially any assumptions about priors and about the reference class assumed in step (2).

(c) If the repair requires assumptions the original argument slides over, which assumption is the most natural place for a critic to push back, and how much of the argument's force survives that pushback?

(d) For the novel, I am considering a revision in which step (2) says instead that Alma should reason as if selected at random from the humans who have lived so far. Would the argument still support any doomsday-style conclusion? Explain why or why not.

I can follow formal notation where it makes a step precise, such as conditional probability, likelihood ratios, and Bayes's theorem, but please do not let notation substitute for the explanation.
