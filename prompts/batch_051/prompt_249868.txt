Here's the view I want you to stress-test. In most of my courses I teach the standard line that Zeno's paradoxes are basically solved: once you have the modern theory of limits, the convergence of geometric series, and the "at-at" theory of motion — the idea that motion just is being at a continuum of places at a continuum of times — the Dichotomy and Achilles and the Tortoise stop being genuine paradoxes. Play it straight and tell me what would have to be true for that standard line to be wrong. Which assumptions go into it — about space and time, about what an infinite sequence or "completing" one could mean, about whether mathematical convergence is even the right kind of answer to a problem about getting from A to B — and which of those assumptions could in principle fail? For each, what would the world (or our concept of motion) have to look like, and which escape routes are actually live options rather than merely logically possible? I want an honest assesment of how exposed the standard resolution actually is, not just the usual "Zeno was confused" line. Be willing to conclude either way, but wherever you land, say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would most plausibly make you revise the verdict, and where is your reasoning shakiest. You're a professional philosopher with a background in the philosophy of physics and mathematics — think carefully, and maybe start by brainstorming different readings of what Zeno's paradoxes are really after before you commit.
