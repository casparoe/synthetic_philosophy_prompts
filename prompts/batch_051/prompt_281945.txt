So in my reading group (half philosophers, half not) we've spent months going in circles on whether there is any real moral difference between lying and merely misleading someone. We agree on the easy stuff: deception is usually wrong, some deception is justified (murderer at the door and all that), and in the cases we actually fight about, the speaker intends the same false belief and the hearer ends up with the same false belief. The only difference is whether the speaker utters something they believe to be false, or a true sentence picked precisely so the hearer will infer the false thing anyway.

The two camps have hardened. One side says the literal falsehood matters because it's a different kind of act: in lying you vouch for something you don't stand behind, you assert what you disbelieve, whereas in misleading you stay narrowly truthful and the hearer takes the last step to the false beleif themselves — so at least some of the responsibility is theirs. The other side says this narrow-truth excuse is a moral illusion. If you engineer the context so the hearer is going to end up with p, which you think is false, you've knowingly brought about the same wrong with the same intent; caring about whether you said the false thing or merely steered them to it is like caring whether someone was shot or shoved off the platform when you meant for them to die either way.

No one has moved in months, every session ends with restating the same points. And I've realized I don't want a verdict on which side is right. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this even is.

My working hypothesis is that we're not having one moral disagreement at all. I think the two sides have picked different objects of evaluation: one side is evaluating the act itself (the speech act and what the speaker commits herself to), the other is evaluating the outcome the speaker knowingly produces (the hearer's false belief). If that's right, new cases definately won't settle anything, and we've been talking past each other for months.

Is that diagnosis correct? Or is there a substantive question underneath — for instance an empirical/psychological one about whether hearers actually experience the two differently, or whether the "they took the last step themselves" story is false because the speaker controlled the whole inference, or whether the distinction between what's said and what's implicated is doing more work than it can bear? Diagnose the disagreement: verbal or substantive, where exactly the camps diverge (facts, standards of evidence, bedrock intuitions, different questions), and say what would count as progress given the diagnosis. Lead with your diagnosis in a sentence or two, then defend it, and keep the whole thing under 600 words.
