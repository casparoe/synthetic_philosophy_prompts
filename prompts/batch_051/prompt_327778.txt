I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine. I recently noticed a resemblance between two bodies of work I encountered on different assignments, one from cognitive science and one from legal theory, and I cannot decide whether it is a genuine structural parallel or an accidental echo. I would like you to adjudicate that.

The first body of work is Douglas Hofstadter and Emmanuel Sander's Surfaces and Essences. Their claim is that analogy is the core of cognition: a concept is not a definition or a set of necessary and sufficient conditions but a bundle of remembered situations bound together by perceived similarity. Words such as chair, open, and mother pick out categories built up from exemplars through analogical mapping. The difference between literal and metaphorical speech, on their account, is only a difference in how deeply an analogy has become entrenched; the literal is simply the analogy we no longer notice. Thinking is mostly the retrieval of a similar past situation and the mapping of its structure onto the present one.

The second body of work concerns common-law legal reasoning. There, too, a concept such as offer, negligence, or reasonable expectation is not fixed by a definition. It is learned from and modified through cases: a judge compares the present facts to previously decided ones, follows or distinguishes precedents, and the announced rule is in practice a compressed summary of those past decisions rather than a formula that settles its own application. The common lawyer's principle that a prior case controls a new one only when the two are sufficiently alike is an explicit, institutionalized version of that same procedure.

So the parallel, stated plainly: in cognition, concepts are frozen analogies; in common law, legal rules are frozen precedents; and judicial reasoning is analogy-making made public and answerable.

Please tell me whether this parallel is deep or superficial, and be precise about the mapping. If it is real, I want to know what would have to be true on both sides for results to transfer in either direction — for example, whether Hofstadter and Sander's claim that there is no principled line between the literal and the metaphorical implies that there is no principled line between applying a legal rule and extending it by analogy, and whether that in turn means judges are less in the business of discovering law than of making it under the cover of resemblance. If the parallel breaks, tell me exactly where: what the law has that cognition lacks, or cognition has that the law lacks, that makes the analogy fail.

When you answer, please write continuous prose, as you would in an email or a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
