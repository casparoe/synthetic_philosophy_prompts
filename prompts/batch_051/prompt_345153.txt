Suppose that tomorrow morning a proof goes up on the arXiv, machine-checked in two independant proof assistants, that first-order Peano arithmetic is inconsistent: the usual axioms for the natural numbers, with induction as a scheme, derive 0=1. Within six months a dozen of the best proof theorists have gone through the derivation by hand and nobody finds a gap. It is short enough to teach in a graduate seminar, and it doesn't use anything exotic — just the ordinary induction schema and a clever diagonal argument. Don't waste any time objecting that this can't actually happen; in the scenario the proof is correct and the community ends up treating it as a formal fact.

I want a reasoned prediction of how the next twenty years play out — not a story, but a chain of mechanisms, incentives and constraints, with explicit confidence attached to each link. Structure the reply as follows:

1. Immediate aftermath (0–2 years), about 200 words. What the logic community, the proof-assistant community and the mainstream press each do first; the initial attempts to patch things, e.g. hunting for the axiom that is doing the damage, retreating to weaker arithmetic, or reaching for paraconsistent or intuitionistic variants.

2. Formal methods and computer science (2–20 years), about 250 words. Formal verification, programming language semantics, cryptography, and the rest of the market for machine-checked 'proofs'. Which of these carry on working anyway, which quietly collapse, and why.

3. Working mathematics (2–20 years), about 250 words. Do working mathematicians actually change their day-to-day practice, or does it mostly pass them by? Work through exactly what the PA result implies for ZFC and set-theoretic foundations, given that ZFC interprets arithmetic; flag any assumption that inference depends on. Then say whether any of this reaches the way people publish proofs.

4. Philosophy of mathematics, about 200 words. Which established positions (platonism, formalism, logicism, finitism, structuralism, and so on) become easier or harder to defend, and where do the debates actually move over the next two decades.

5. Robust vs. guesswork, about 250 words. Go back through your prediction and sort the links: which effects survive most variations in how the proof works, which are sensitive to the specific proof, and what would have to be different about the proof — e.g. which fragment of PA it uses, whether it is constructive, whether the contradiction survives in weaker or intuitionistic systems — for your overall picture to be wrong.
