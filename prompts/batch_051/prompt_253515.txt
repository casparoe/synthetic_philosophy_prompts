Exam question:
State Molyneux's problem and explain Locke's negative answer to it. Be precise about what the newly sighted person is being asked to do and about what, on Locke's account, the relevant experience is supposed to supply.

Student answer:
Molyneux's problem asks whether a man born blind, who has learned to tell a cube from a sphere by touch alone, would be able to tell which is which by sight alone immediately after gaining vision, before touching them again. Locke answers no. He reasons that the man already has tactile experience of how a cube and a sphere affect his touch, but he has not yet had the experience linking those tactile feelings with the corresponding visual appearances. So he cannot know with certainty which visual appearance belongs to the cube and which to the sphere. This illustrates Locke's empiricism because it shows that recognizing a shape through vision is something we have to learn by repeatedly associating what touch tells us with what sight tells us. Moreover, as Locke emphasizes in the same discussion, the newly sighted person's visual field is at first nothing but an unorganized spread of light, color, and shadow, and he cannot even see the two objects as distinct solid bodies until he has learned to interpret cues such as shading and perspective. Thus even the basic perception of a solid object, apart from judging it to be a cube or a sphere, is an acquired habit rather than an innate capacity.
