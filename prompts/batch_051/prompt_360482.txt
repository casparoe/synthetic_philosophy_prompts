I am the risk officer for a regional flood-control district. Each spring I recommend which projects go into a bond proposal, and the board votes on my recommendation. The stakes are large, the projects are hard to reverse once built, and the board has no formal training in probability. Our hydrologists no longer give single probabilities for flood events; they give intervals, and different models give different intervals. I am coming to think this is the situation imprecise probability is meant for: I hold a set of admissible probability distributions rather than one, and I should not collapse the disagreement to an average just to make the spreadsheet easier. But I do not know how to turn that set into a yes-or-no recommendation.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure I can run in a week on a spreadsheet. It should state the questions to ask in order; say what counts as a sufficient answer to each; say where the procedure returns "yes," "no," or "collect more information"; and mark the step at which a remaining problem is a values judgment for the board rather than a modeling question. I especially need it to handle two recurring situations: an interval of expected net benefit that straddles zero, and two modeling teams that produce non-overlapping intervals but each defends its own. Also tell me how many of those probability distributions I am committed to: only the distributions the analysts literally supplied, every distribution compatible with each interval, or also all probability-weightings between them, and whether that choice changes the recommendation.

Apply the procedure to two cases. The first is a canal-widening project costing $12m whose expected avoided-damage benefit, across accepted models, is $7m–$25m. The second is a buyout of thirty floodplain homes costing $9m; one modeling team puts expected benefit at $14m–$18m, another at $4m–$6m, and neither team will concede that its model is worse. Please say what the procedure returns for each, including any recommendation the board will resist.

Present the answer as a numbered list with exactly five items and no introduction or conclusion. Items 1 through 4 are the procedure. Item 5 gives the procedure's result for the two test cases. Keep each item to a sentence or two.
