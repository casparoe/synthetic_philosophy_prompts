I am the student member of my university's research ethics committee, and this term I am also taking an upper-level seminar on consent, deception, and coercion. The seminar has made me realize that our committee's decisions often turn on a principle we have never written down explicitly, so I am trying to reconstruct it from our past cases before our next vote.

Our precedents:

1. A psychology study told participants it measured memory for word lists when it actually measured whether the order of the items affected recall. Every task was accurately described in the consent form, the risk was minimal, and participants were drawn from the general student body. The committee approved it, with an immediate debriefing requirement.

2. A study of a topical gel described the gel in its consent form as having "no significant side effects" when the investigators' own pilot data showed a roughly 2% rate of severe skin reactions. The committee rejected it, on the ground that a reasonable person would want that fact before agreeing.

3. A low-risk survey of laboratory culture recruited participants through department chairs, who e-mailed their graduate students to "encourage" participation. The committee rejected it, holding that the students could reasonably believe their funding or evaluations would be affected, and that consent given under that pressure was not free.

4. A minimal-risk study enrolling participants with mild cognitive impairment was approved, but only after the team added a simplified consent script and three teach-back questions; anyone who could not restate the purpose, the procedures, and the fact that participation was voluntary was excluded.

5. A study offering three hundred dollars for a forty-five-minute session of moderate exercise was approved. Some members worried the payment might induce students to ignore discomfort, but the committee held that an attractive offer is not coercion; the protocol included a medical screen, a safety stop, and the right to withdraw.

6. A study filmed participants during a group discussion that was described as being "for analysis of conversation style," when the footage was also going to be played in a class to train interviewers. The committee rejected it, holding that what would be done with the recordings was part of what a reasonable participant would want to know.

The new proposal is from a political science group. The consent form accurately describes the session: twenty-five minutes, three short online puzzle games whose difficulty varies, a mood questionnaire, and a survey item in which participants read a hypothetical campus petition and say whether they would sign it. The form says the study concerns "games and self-reported attitudes." The researchers have told the committee, in confidence, that the real aim is different: they want to know whether mild frustration from the games increases willingness to sign the petition, because they are building a model of when people are most persuadable. Nothing about the tasks is concealed, the frustration is just an incidental feature the form already mentions, the data are stored without identifiers, and participants are debriefed immediately afterward. The only deception is about the purpose of the study.

If the committee wants to be consistent with its own precedents, how should it vote, and which earlier decision is the closest analog? I can see a reconstruction of our precedents on which the proposal should be approved with the debriefing condition, and a rival reconstruction on which it should be rejected. I would like to know what principle each vote would attribute to the committee, which principle better coheres with the full set of past decisions, and what further case would do the cleanest job of separating the two principles the next time one comes up.
