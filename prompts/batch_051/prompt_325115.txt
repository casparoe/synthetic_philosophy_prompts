I am going to spend the next few months reading work on welfare conditionality and the category of the undeserving poor, and I do not want to begin until I know what an adequate view in this area is actually being asked to do. Write the desiderata: the full specification of what a theory of conditionality in the welfare state has to get right. By conditionality I mean the rules, tests, and sanctions that attach behavioral requirements to benefits — such as looking for work, accepting offers, attending appointments, or reporting income accurately — together with the normative view of why such requirements may properly be attached to some claimants and not others.

Give me the list of requirements, with the case or consideration behind each. In particular, I want:

- the cases any such theory has to get right, including at least these: the claimant who deliberately misreports income; the claimant who could work but refuses available work; the claimant in an area with genuinely few jobs; the caregiver whose capacity to take paid work is limited by dependents; the claimant with a disability or illness that is difficult to verify or that the system misclassifies; the claimant whose earnings are real but unstable and irregular; and the claimant who has already been sanctioned and is now worse off.

- the distinctions the theory is not allowed to collapse. I have in mind at least these: culpable noncompliance versus incapacity; fraud versus simple noncompliance versus administrative error; the justification of the scheme as a whole versus the justification of how an individual official treats an individual claimant; need-based and contribution-based logics; ex ante incentive effects versus ex post punishment; and entitlement to support versus deservingness in the merit sense.

- the empirical and institutional constraints it cannot contradict. At minimum it has to cohere with the existence of sizable non-take-up among eligible people, with the reality of wrongful denials and administrative error, with the fact that conditions are applied by officials with limited information rather than by a neutral judge of character, and with whatever the strongest version of the evidence shows about the effects of sanctions on employment, poverty, and health. Where the evidence is contested, say what a theory would have to predict and how the dispute matters for the theory, rather than treating one side of the empirical debate as settled.

- a clear statement of which requirements are non-negotiable for any adequate theory and which are preferences of particular camps — for example, a reciprocity requirement, a work-neutrality requirement, a priority for the worst-off, or an insistence that conditionality is justified only if it does not stigmatize.

- a flag on any subsets of the requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied. I suspect there is a real tension — perhaps a genuine impossibility — between full sensitivity to individual circumstance, low administrative discretion and cost, and strong protection against moral hazard. If some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, tell me exactly which requirements conflict and whether the conflict is in principle or only at feasible cost.

Then, briefly, how the main families of view — strict workfare and reciprocity views, contributory insurance views, needs-based unconditional views, and participation or capability views that broaden what counts as contribution — do against the list. Keep that scoring brief; the criteria and their motivating cases are what I most want.

Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this area, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Write the whole thing in American English.
