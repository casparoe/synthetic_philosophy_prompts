As part of my doctoral work on moral progress, I keep coming across a particular analogy doing serious argumentative work: the expanding circle, in which moral concern is pictured as beginning at the self and radiating outward, like ripples from a stone dropped into a pond, through kin, tribe, nation and species, until it takes in all sentient beings. In the literature this image is used not merely to describe the past but to underwrite a prediction: that the circle will continue to widen unless something blocks the mechanism, and that resistance to further widening is a failure of the moral point of view. I would like a careful audit of how far this analogy holds. Set out what corresponds to what between the pond and the moral circle; identify where the mapping comes apart, such as ripples attenuating with distance while moral demands do not, or the pond's fixed origin as against a centre that is itself morally contested; say which features of the pond image are silently imported into the target—a privileged centre, distance as the principal obstacle, smooth and continuous expansion—and which of those imports matter for the intended use; and then judge whether the prediction that concern should and likely will keep widening, perhaps to encompass non-biological minds, survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether some better analogy would serve instead.
