I am a retired natural scientist — my career was spent on the experimental side of the biological sciences — and I have only lately begun to read philosophy with care. I have been trying to make explicit the principle behind my judgements about causation by omission, and I would be grateful for help in testing and repairing it.

Let me set out my verdicts on a number of cases.

1. An entomology-room curator switches off the dehumidifier for cleaning one Friday and forgets to switch it back on. Over a hot, damp weekend the pinned specimens are overrun with mould. I judge that her omission caused the ruin of the specimens.

2. I had agreed to open the ventilators of a colleague's experimental glasshouse each morning while he was abroad. One day I stayed at my desk writing and did not go. The glasshouse overheated and his seedlings died. I judge that my omission caused the seedlings' death.

3. A visiting researcher unplugs the chest freezer in the specimen room so that he can run a projector for a seminar, and never plugs it back in. The tissue samples thaw. I judge that his failure to reconnect the freezer caused the thaw, even though he had no standing responsibility for the room.

4. A retired ecologist had promised to send the baseline vegetation maps of a long-term study plot to the person taking it over. She never sent them. Without the maps the new custodian cannot tell whether the vegetation has changed, so twenty years of comparison are lost. I judge that her omission caused the loss of the comparison.

5. A referee notices that a submitted paper's method is undermined by a calibration result known in his own laboratory. He says nothing about it because he regards it as outside the remit of the review. The paper is published, and a junior group spends two years building on the method before the problem is discovered. I find that I do not judge his silence to have caused the wasted effort, although the effort would have been spared had he spoken.

6. A chemist borrows a colleague's reflux condenser and never returns it. When the colleague later performs a synthesis, he substitutes an open beaker, and the hot solvent ignites. The fire would not have happened if the condenser had been returned, but I am inclined to say that the failure to return it did not cause the fire; at most it was a background condition for an accident that was separately produced.

7. A visitor to a herbarium sees water beginning to drip through a ceiling onto a tray of dried plant specimens. He could easily move the tray without risk, but he does not, because the specimens are not his responsibility. The tray is ruined. I judge that his omission did not cause the ruin.

My working rule has been roughly this: an omission is a cause of a harm when the agent was under a specific obligation or prior undertaking to attend to the relevant state of affairs, or had themselves brought about the state of affairs that needed attention, and the harm would not have occurred had the omitted act been performed. The rule handles cases 1, 2, 3, 4 and 7 comfortably. But case 6 troubles me: the chemist was plainly under an obligation to return what he had borrowed, and the fire counterfactually depended on his not doing so, yet I still resist calling his omission a cause. Case 5 also makes me uneasy, because a referee does have some professional responsibility for what appears under review, so the rule might classify his silence as a cause against my judgement unless the obligation is read very narrowly.

I would like to know whether there is a coherent principle here, or a small set of candidate principles, that I should adopt. Which clauses does it need, and which of my judgements does it force me to abandon? I am less attached to keeping all seven verdicts than to ending with a rule I can state honestly, so please say plainly if some verdicts must be surrendered.

Please also say what the principle predicts for two further cases that I have not been able to decide:

8. A project scientist notices that the automatic backup of a long-running experiment has quietly stopped, but says nothing because she assumes the information-technology officer has seen the same error message. The disk later fails and a year of data is lost.

9. A technician hears from a colleague that a batch of pH buffer has been contaminated, but does not pass the warning on to the person who will use it that afternoon. The resulting measurements are invalid and an experiment has to be repeated.

Would you be able to decide cases 8 and 9 from the principle alone, or do they require further facts about the duties of the people involved?

Please organise the answer under the following headings, in this order, with the approximate lengths indicated.

1. Summary of my judgements (about 150 words): restate the pattern in your own terms and identify the hardest tension within it.
2. Candidate principles (about 450 words): state two or three defensible rules that would capture most of my verdicts, and say where each fails or needs a special clause; a table is welcome here if it helps.
3. The rule I should adopt (about 350 words): state the most defensible rule precisely, and show how each clause is motivated by specific cases.
4. Predictions for my further cases (about 250 words): say what your preferred rule yields for cases 8 and 9, and whether extra institutional facts are needed.
5. Verdicts I should revise (about 200 words): state which of my seven judgements your rule forces me to abandon, and whether the remaining fit is satisfying.

Please write the answer in British English.
