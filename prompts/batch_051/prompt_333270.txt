I keep circling around this question and I think a real legal case would force me to actually land somewhere. The case is Jacobellis v. Ohio (1964). Quick version: a theater manager in Cleveland Heights was convicted under Ohio law for showing Les Amants, a Louis Malle film, which the state said was obscene. The Supreme Court reversed, but it couldn't produce a majority opinion; you had Brennan and Goldberg saying the film just wasn't obscene, Black and Douglas saying no censorship ever, Stewart saying only hard-core pornography is outside the First Amendment and he couldn't define it but knew it when he saw it and this film wasn't it, and two dissents. The court was all over the place, and the obscenity standard stayed messy until Miller in 1973.

I want to use this as a case study for the epistemicism vs. supervaluationism debate about vagueness. So here's what I'd like from you: treat 'obscene' and 'hard-core pornography' as vague predicates and the film as a borderline-or-not case. Walk me through what an epistemicist and a supervaluationist would each say the Supreme Court was actually doing when it asked whether this film was obscene. Who or what is the 'player' in your mapping — the individual Justices, the Court as a body, the predicate itself, the precedents? What does each theory predict about the multiplicity of opinions, the fact that a reversal happened without a majority rationale, and the role of intuition in Stewart's position? Where does each theory capture what was going on, and where does it strain or go silent? Finally, tell me whether Jacobellis gives us any reason to definately prefer one theory over the other for legal adjudication, especially since courts can't just shrug and say it's indeterminate — they have to affirm or reverse. I'm less interested in a summary of the theories and more in a disciplined mapping that runs both directions, case illuminating theory and theory illuminating case.
