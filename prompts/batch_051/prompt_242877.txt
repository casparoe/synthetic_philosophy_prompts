I'm helping run a professional development day for about twenty high school physics teachers, and I'm tired of sessions where someone stands up and says "students have misconceptions" and everyone nods, then goes back to their classroom and treats wrong answers as laziness or sloppiness. I want a ninety-minute activity, no lecture up front, that makes the teachers themselves make the same kind of systematic error their students make, then have that little moment of "oh no, I did it too," and then debrief what it means that a wrong answer can feel so obviously right. Don't say the words "misconception," "alternative framework," or anything like that until they've already been through the experience. I'd like the activity to use a simple physics task—maybe the thing where a ball is shot through a curved tube and you have to draw the path it takes after it comes out, which everyone gets wrong in the same way—but if you've got a better item, use that. The important part is that most of the adults in the room should commit to the intuitive-but-wrong answer, and the setup should make it safe to be wrong, so no one gets called out or put on the spot individually.

Also include a debrief that maps their moment of confidence onto the bigger idea that students aren't making random errors but reasoning from internally consistent, theory-like accounts of the world — and be honest about what the activity can't show them, like how stable those student theories are or how they respond to disconfirming evidence. I need a materials list and instructions clear enough that a teacher who's never led a PD can run it in a regular classroom. It'd help if the activity has some small social or game-like pressure so the wrong answer doesn't just feel like a quiz: maybe people vote, bet points on predictions, or have to defend a choice to a partner before seeing what happens.
