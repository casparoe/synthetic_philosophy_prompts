I'm a retired physicist, and a few of us from the old lab have been reading our way through the philosophy of computation. One of the group, who spent thirty years writing compilers, has been pressing an argument that seems to show the Church-Turing thesis is vacuous, and worse, that the so-called physical Church-Turing thesis is vacuous in exactly the same way. He's layed it out below; I can't find the exit. I'd love a diagnosis: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?

(1) The Church-Turing thesis (CTT) says every effectively calculable function is Turing-computable. (2) Effectively calculable means calculable by a finite, mechanical procedure, the kind a patient clerk could carry out with paper and pencil, following exact rules, without insight or invention. (3) Turing's analysis of a human computer is often taken to show that following such rules is just what a Turing machine does; that's the real content of the thesis. (4) A counterexample to CTT would be a function that's effectively calculable but not Turing-computable. To show a function is effectively calculable, you'd have to exhibit an effective procedure that calculates it. (5) But an effective procedure is exactly the kind of thing a Turing machine can execute, so the function would be Turing-computable after all. So CTT is trivially true: any proposed counterexample supplies its own refutation. (6) If CTT is trivially true, its just a definition with no empirical content; it tells us nothing about real machines, physical systems, or minds. (7) Now take the physical Church-Turing thesis (PCTT): no physical system, however exotic, can compute a non-Turing-computable function. This is normally treated as a substantive empirical claim about the world. (8) But the same reasoning applies. A purported physical hypercomputer, a box that solves the halting problem, say, would have to be identified by a description of what it computes. To describe what it computes, you'd need a rule that determines, for each program-and-input pair, whether the program halts. But a rule that determines the correct answer in every case just is a decision procedure. So the description of the box would itself be an effective procedure for the halting problem. But the halting problem is undecidable; no such procedure exists. So the box isn't a possible refutation of PCTT; it's a contradiction in disguise. (9) Therefore PCTT is also true by definition, not a testable claim about physics. If somebody built a halting oracle, we couldn't even coherently say what it would be for it to work; we'd have to say we'd misdescribed it. (10) That can't be right. A black box that reliably answered every halting question correctly would refute PCTT, full stop, even if nobody understood its internals. An empirical hypothesis shouldn't be immune to a black box.

I'd like to hang onto (7) and (10) if I can, but I'll pay whatever the argument forces me to pay. And please write the answer in British English.
