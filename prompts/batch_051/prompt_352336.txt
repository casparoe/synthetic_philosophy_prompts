I’ve been collecting ordinary judgements about population size for a course I’m designing, and I keep finding a pattern I want pressure-tested. People routinely say that a world with more flourishing lives is better than a world with fewer, even if the average quality is slightly lower; but the same people resist the idea that a city is improved by doubling its population at the cost of a small decline in average wellbeing. They also treat choosing to have a child as good for the child, but hesitate to say the state should promote larger families simply because the extra lives would be good. I’ve started calling this the “two registers” view: ordinary moral thought has a register of lives-added, which treats each new life above a “decent floor” as an addition to value, and a register of average-flourishing, which treats the quality of the lives as the relevant thing whenever the question is about managing an existing population. The view has a threshold—new lives count only above the floor—and an asymmetry: adding lives is one kind of reason, lowering average is another.

I’m not sure whether this is just a set of inconsistent intuitions being given a name, or a coherent position. It may already exist under some label I don’t know: critical-level utilitarianism, an average-total hybrid, person-affecting versus impersonal considerations. But I don’t want a bibliography. I want a candid assessment of the view itself: reconstruct it charitably, say what exactly it commits me to when comparing a bigger family, city, or species, and show where it breaks—especially whether the threshold is doing hidden work or whether the asymmetry can survive cases like adding lives that are below the floor but not bad, or replacing a population with a larger one of lower average. Is the view coherent enough to teach, or does it collapse into one of the standard positions under pressure? Please answer in British English.
