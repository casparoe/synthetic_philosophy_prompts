I am a philosopher of mind deciding how to spend the next three years. I have a postdoctoral researcher and a modest research fund, and I want to put both to work on the question of which computations matter morally: the problem of saying when a system made of different materials, or organized quite unlike us, has internal processing that amounts to felt experience, and how confident we need to be before we treat it as a moral patient. The field seems to me to contain several candidate fault lines and no settled view about which one is upstream of the others. Should the priority be the demarcation problem, the attempt to identify which architectural features of a computation are sufficient for experience rather than merely correlated with it — for example, whether valenced affect, the global availability of information to the rest of the system, integrated information, or a self-model of one's own states is what turns signal into suffering? Or should it be the attribution problem proper, the question of what degree of belief in sentience can be warranted by behavior and internal organization in novel cases, and how to trade off the moral costs of false attribution against the costs of missed patients? Or does the more urgent question lie nearer the substrate, in whether the right computational profile is enough regardless of implementation, so that a whole-brain emulation would count as a patient while a neuron-by-neuron description of a pain episode replayed like a recording would not? And somewhere below all of these sits the gradedness question, whether sentience is all-or-nothing or admits of partial and intermediate forms, which looks as though it is presupposed by every answer to the others. I am asking for a defended agenda rather than a survey, and certainly not a reading list or set of citations: rank two or three of these clusters, or ones I have not named, in the order you would fund them, and defend the ordering. Be explicit about the criteria you are using — importance, tractability with current conceptual and empirical tools, how many other questions depend on this one, and how likely an answer is to change our treatment of actual systems — and about where the criteria pull against one another. I would rather have a well-defended ranking than an even-handed tour. Then tell me where your ranking is most likely to fail: which argument or finding would have to land for you to demote your top choice, and what would most plausibly change your mind about the ordering. Write the whole thing in continuous prose, as you would a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold type, or tables.
