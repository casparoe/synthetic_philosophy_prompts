A teaching assistant in a logic and computation seminar I sat in on spent a whole lecture pressing one analogy. His line was that, under the Curry-Howard correspondence, you should think of a proposition as a type and a proof of it as a program that outputs a value of that type. Implication becomes a function type; conjunction becomes a product type; disjunction becomes a tagged union; the universal quantifier becomes a dependent function type, one whose result type can depend on the input. And proof normalization, the process of eliminating detours in a derivation, just is program execution. From this he drew a strong conclusion: constructive logic is, for all practical purposes, the logic of computation. Every constructive proof is an algorithm that computes a witness, and if a statement has no constructive proof, there is no algorithm for producing one. He used that to draw a sharp line in the seminar—constructive mathematics is where the algorithms live, and classical reasoning is a non-constructive layer bolted on top.

I could follow each step, but the conclusion seems to be carrying more weight than the mapping can bear, and I want to know exactly where it gives out. Please give the correspondence table explicitly—what plays the role of the type, the program, the function, the data structure, and the execution trace on the logic side—and then audit the disanalogies. In particular, tell me where an intuitionistic proof might fail to deliver something we would normally count as an algorithm, and where a classical proof might nonetheless have computational content (for example through double-negation translations, continuation-passing style, or control operators). Say which features of ordinary programs the analogy quietly imports—termination, efficiency, modularity, totality—and whether they reliably hold on the proof side, and which of the disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion he drew rather than being side issues. Finally, tell me whether the sharp line survives honestly: is "constructively provable" coextensive with "computable" in the sense he claimed, or is the analogy doing more work than it can support? If it is, what more accurate replacement would you put in its place?
