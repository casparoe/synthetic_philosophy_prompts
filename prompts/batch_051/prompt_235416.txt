Our neighborhood runs a mutual-aid network with a labor-time currency. Members offer tasks—mending, tutoring, hauling, baking for events—and are paid in hours: one hour of work earns one credit, and one credit buys one hour of anyone else's work. The founders described the credits as bookkeeping for labor already performed, not as objects of value in themselves.

A new member, who trades currencies for a living, noticed that nothing forbade trading the credits themselves. He now buys credits from members who want cash, then sells them back at a markup in the weeks before the harvest festival, when everyone suddenly needs childcare and baking at once. He holds several hundred credits—claims on other people's labor—without ever having worked an hour in the network. When the coordinators objected, he replied: "You accept my credits when you do the work, so how can you say I didn't earn them? Every trade is just buying low and selling high."

I am one of the coordinators. We have to decide whether his credits should be treated the way credits earned by labor are treated, and, if not, what rule would mark the difference without collapsing into vague talk of "real work." I am not asking whether what he does is legal, and I am not asking whether he is a bad person. I am asking whether credits earned by speculation differ in kind from credits earned by labor, and whether that difference, if it is real, justifies treating them differently. I would also like the strongest statement of his side rather than a straw man, and an honest account of how much of our initial reaction survives that statement.
