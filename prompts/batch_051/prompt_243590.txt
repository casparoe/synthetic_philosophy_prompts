I spent my working life around probability distributions, and now that I'm retired and poking into philosophy I've drifted into a reductive view about the language of possibility. Here it is: a proposition is possible — relative to a setup, a body of knowledge, whatever the right background is — just in case it has nonzero probability under the appropriate distribution. Necessary is probability one. Impossible is zero. 'Possible' and 'probable' aren't two different faculties, one tracking worlds and one tracking credences or frequencies; they're the same scale, and the modal vocabulary is just the coarse-grained end of it. I don't mean that 'possible' is a synonym for 'likely.' I mean the facts that make a modal claim true are probabilistic facts: when we say a drug could cause some side effect, we're really claiming something about the tail of a distribution.

I want to know what would properly count against this. I'm already unmoved by the usual showstoppers: the spinner that has probability zero of landing on exactly 0.5 but still seems possible, mathematical uses of 'possible' where there's no distribution in sight. Those feel like idealizations or changes of subject, not refutations. So set those to one side. I'll also admit I don't have a crisp account of what makes a distribution the appropriate one; I'm treating the background as fixed by the questioner's interests for now.

Tell me about the less obvious exposure. Which cases, formal results, or explanatory failures does this view live or die by? What does it predict that its rivals don't? Which of those would a committed reducer like me have to concede count as genuine evidence againts the view, and which would I get to shrug off? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me honestly if the position is so hedged by now that it risks nothing — and wether that insulation is a feature or a bug. The defeater I'd personally find most persuasive would be a matched pair of cases that agree on every relevant probability distribution but still demand different modal verdicts.
