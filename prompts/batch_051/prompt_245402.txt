I direct the curriculum unit at a nonprofit that provides adult English-language instruction. Each summer I approve the scope-and-sequence for the coming academic year, and the most contested decision is which grammatical structures receive one of our limited explicit-grammar slots and which are left to be acquired through the communicative, input-rich activities that fill most of our classroom hours. The stakes are concrete: a course lead's decision about a single grammar point commits roughly twelve classroom hours and shapes four cohorts of adult learners, and an error in either direction means either drilling a structure that exposure would have handled painlessly or expecting learners to absorb a structure that the available input never actually justifies. My instructional team is split between two camps—teach every structure explicitly, or trust acquisition and nearly eliminate explicit grammar—and both sides currently argue from intuition rather than a shared decision rule.

I know enough linguistics to see that the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument should be doing the work here: learners converge on grammatical knowledge that is not fully determined by the sentences they encounter; they receive positive evidence (actual sentences of the language) but little dependable negative evidence (information that a candidate sentence is ungrammatical); and certain properties of grammar appear underdetermined by that positive evidence in ways a general-purpose learner could not reliably overcome. I want this logic translated into an operational decision procedure, not a slogan. Please write me a procedure that a course lead with no formal training in linguistics can run in under forty-five minutes on a single candidate grammar point to decide whether it is a plausible candidate for acquisition from classroom input alone or whether it warrants an explicit-teaching slot. The procedure should ask its questions in a fixed order; specify what counts as a sufficient answer to each; stop clearly with a verdict where it can; flag the steps that remain judgment calls; and define any technical term in a single clause.

Then run the procedure on three points from our working list: (1) third-person singular -s on present-tense verbs, as in "she walks daily"; (2) the choice between the indefinite and definite article when introducing and then re-mentioning a referent in a short narrative, as in "a dog entered the yard; the dog looked hungry"; and (3) yes/no question formation with complex subjects, as in "Is the man who is waiting tired?" versus the ungrammatical "Is the man who waiting is tired?" For each case, say how far the procedure gets before it stops, where it stalls or hands the decision to a human, what verdict it returns, and how that verdict bears on our current practice—in particular, flag any case where the procedure's answer would surprise one of the two camps.
