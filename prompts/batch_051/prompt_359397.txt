I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been reporting on a streaming company that wants to replace its arbitration clause with a rule borrowed from bargaining theory, the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution. Under the proposed procedure, if the company and a creators' collective cannot agree on a new revenue-sharing contract, an arbiter first fixes a disagreement point: the payoffs each side receives if talks collapse for good. The arbiter then computes each side's ideal point, meaning the best payoff it could obtain under some agreement that gives the other side at least its fallback. The imposed outcome is the efficient point on the frontier at which both sides have advanced the same fraction of the distance from their fallback to their ideal point. The company's public case is that this is fairer than ordinary winner-take-all arbitration; that case is not what I need assessed.

I'd like you to analyze how the rule would actually behave once both parties know it is the rule and adapt to it. Start from the clean benchmark in which the total pie is fixed at V and the only question is the split. There the arithmetic is exact: if the claimed fallbacks are c and p, each side's award is half the pie plus half of its own fallback minus half of the other party's fallback. That already makes the fallback less an objective floor than a strategic bid. Where does that lead? Consider what each side would do to the evidence of outside options it presents, the side contracts it signs with third parties, the timing of near-collapse or walkouts, and the definitions of revenue that determine V itself. Then go beyond the fixed pie: what breaks when the pie's size depends on effort after the agreement, when more than two parties sit at the table, when the feasible set is nonconvex, when fallbacks are privately known, or when the arrangement can be reopened a year later? For each failure mode, say whether a patch exists — auditing fallbacks rather than taking them from the parties, locking the definition of revenue in advance, imposing a standstill on side deals — and whether the patch can be applied without quietly giving up the property that attracted the company to this rule in the first place, namely that both sides' progress toward their ideal outcomes is measured on a common scale. Finally, mark which of your conclusions are forced by the rule's formal structure and which rest on empirical assumptions about how firms and labor groups actually behave, and tell me which of those assumptions, if it proved false, would most change your overall verdict on whether the scheme would survive contact with real parties.
