A colleague has asked for a one-page statement of my considered views on the ethics of deception, to circulate ahead of a seminar where the group will stress-test one another's positions. Here is the profile I sent. (1) The wrong of deception lies in what it does to the target's agency: the deceiver induces a false belief and thereby gets the target to choose on the basis of considerations the deceiver knows are not real; once that manipulation and the intent behind it are fixed, I do not see how the particular route by which the false belief was induced adds a further wrong. (2) All else equal, lying is morally worse than merely misleading: asserting what one believes false is graver than inducing the same false belief through carefully chosen truths. (3) Lying is constituted by the assertive act — one lies just in case one asserts what one believes false and thereby invites the hearer's trust — and whether the hearer is taken in is neither necessary nor sufficient for a lie to occur. (4) Merely allowing a false belief one did not create to persist is generally less culpable than actively inducing it, even when one benefits from the error. (5) Where a special relationship imposes a duty of candor, as between doctor and patient, misleading can be exactly as bad as lying: a doctor who tells a patient that the scan shows no additional tumors, expecting the patient to infer that the disease is in remission, has not lied but has wronged the patient no less than had she said outright that the disease is in remission. Audit this profile: for each pair of commitments in genuine tension, say how severe the tension is, what the cheapest repair would be, and what it costs; say which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection and why they only look like tensions; and identify the single revision that would do the most to make the whole set hang together.
