Write a satirical memo, about 650 words, from a government advisory body called the Committee on Intergenerational Accounting Standards, recommending that agencies adopt a positive pure rate of time preference when discounting climate damages and other long-run costs. Format it as numbered bureaucratic guidance and keep the tone deadpan. The humor should come from the committee treating its preference for present people over future people as a neutral technical parameter—like choosing a depreciation schedule—while the document inadvertently exposes the ethical commitments that doing so requires: that later welfare counts less simply because it is later, that the impatience of the living is a legitimate input into public policy, and that the unborn are rather like creditors with poor credit history. The satire should land the Ramsey-style objection to pure time preference, namely that discounting later welfare merely because it is later is hard to justify ethically, without the memo ever stating this straightforwardly. Take a definite satirical stance and do not hedge. Conclude with a note of no more than 120 words identifying one place where the piece exaggerates or relies on a contested interpretation of discounting.
