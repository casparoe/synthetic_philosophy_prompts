I'm a software engineer and I don't have any formal philosophy background, but I've ended up having to build a preference-inference module for a recommendation-ish product, so I've been reading bits of the IRL / revealed preference stuff. I realize I don't actually have a crisp rule for when a model has legitimately infered a preference from behavior versus when it's overreading. So I wrote down a bunch of cases and my gut calls. I want to know if there's one principle that gets all of them, or close, and what the principle says. If some of my verdicts have to go, tell me which ones and what the cheapest repair is. Then run whatever principle you land on against the bonus case at the end, which I keep going back and fourth on. Keep the whole answer under 550 words.

Here are the cases. In each one, a model observes someone and spits out a preference.

1. A person never changes defaults on anything—apps, privacy settings, installers. In surveys they say they care about privacy. The model infers, from the settings they leave as-is, that they don't actually value privacy. My call: bad inference. The defaults are doing the work.

2. A person says they only want to watch documentaries, but 90% of their actual watch time is reality-TV clips. The model infers they prefer reality TV to documentaries. My call: good inference. Actual watch time beats self-report.

3. A teenager starts smoking because his friends do, hates it, has tried and failed to quit multiple times, and still smokes a pack a day. The model infers the teen prefers smoking to not smoking. My call: bad inference. He's stuck, not choosing.

4. A person can pick a $7.99 subscription or a $9.99 one that adds offline downloads, picks the $9.99 one, and then uses offline downloads a lot. The model infers they value offline downloads at more than $2 a month. My call: good inference.

5. A person gives $500 to a coworker's charity after an in-person pitch, and has never given to that charity before or since. The model infers they value that cause at $500. My call: bad inference. Social pressure explains it better than a stable preference.

6. A person always picks the coffee shop with the longer line even though the coffee is basically identical, and when asked says the longer line is a signal the shop is better. The model infers they prefer the longer-line shop because they treat the line as information. My call: good inference. The behavior is strategic, not mindless.

The bonus case: someone sets up screen-time limits for themselves, then hits "snooze" on the limit about as often as not. The model infers they prefer the extra screen time to whatever they said they wanted when they set the limit. I honestly don't know whether that one is more like case 2 (go by the behavior) or case 3 (don't count the behavior because something is overriding them).
