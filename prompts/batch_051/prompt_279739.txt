I'm a novelist working on a story about a woman who forages on public land, and I want the property intuitions in the book to have some teeth. I've been reading Locke's Second Treatise of Government, Chapter V, and I keep getting stuck on a passage where he is trying to explain how property begins without everyone's consent. He writes:

"Was it a robbery thus to assume to himself what belonged to all in common? If such a consent as that was necessary, man had starved, notwithstanding the plenty God had given him. We see in commons, which remain so by compact, that it is the taking any part of what is common, and removing it out of the state nature leaves it in, which begins the property; without which the common is of no use."

This is one of those lines I think I understand until I start listing my own gut reactions, and then it falls apart. Here are my verdicts. A hiker filling a water bottle from a spring is fine and the water is hers. A woman picking enough wild blueberries to fill a couple of pails for a week of jam is also fine, even if she sells some, so long as the patch isn't stripped. But a company that sweeps public weather data, cleans it up, and then claims exclusive rights to the processed dataset feels wrong to me, even though they added real labor. Somebody who taps a sugar maple on remote public land and claims the whole tree for the season feels okay for the sap, but not the tree itself; they can't exclude hikers from standing under it. And if a group claims a whole valley by being the first to put up a few cairns and a trail marker, I don't think they own the valley, not even as a campsite, even though they've mixed their labor with the route.

I can't tell whether Locke's principle of mixing labor really delivers all of these, or whether I'm smuggling in some independent idea about enough-and-as-good that isn't in the passage. What principle, if any, captures my pattern of verdicts? And which of my verdicts, if any, would Locke's passage force me to drop?
