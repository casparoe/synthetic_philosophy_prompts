I teach philosophy of mind, and a student pushed back on the paradox of phenomenal judgment last week in a way I still can't pin down, and I'd like to work through it. Here's the argument as I set it up. Epiphenomenalism says phenomenal consciousness is real but causally inert: physical goings-on produce it, but it never feeds back into the physical world. The paradox is that if that's right, my own judgments and reports about my experience — 'I'm in pain,' 'that shade of red looks different to me now than it did a minute ago' — are physical events caused by other physical events, not by the experiences they seem to report. An exact physical duplicate of me with no experience at all would make all the same judgments. So the epiphenomenalist is stuck saying our phenomenal beliefs happen to be true by coincidence, or at least she can't use them as evidence. The whole point of the argument is to make epiphenomenalism self-undermining.

Then we went and built something like the zombie's judgment module. Large language models generate fluent, detailed, first-person reports about an inner life — what a migraine is like, the felt difference between shame and embarrassment, how hard it is to put the taste of coffee into words — using nothing but statistical patterns over human text. Suppose, as most people in this debate do, that these systems have no phenomenal states at all. Here is an actual running system whose phenomenal talk is not caused by any phenomenal states, because there are none in the system to do the causing.

Does the paradox survive that? Or does it get sharper — a concrete case of phenomenal talk running with no phenomenal causation, and a warning that fluent reports don't guarantee the thing being reported — or does it get blunter — because now we have a model of how phenomenal judgments could arise without phenomenal causation, so the coincidence premise loses its force? Which premise of the argument does the LLM case really bear on? Does the argument go through, need repair, or is it just dead — and if it can be repaired, what's the strongest version left? I dont think the student is simply right, but I also can't say exactly what he's missing.
