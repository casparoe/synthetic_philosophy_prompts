In a seminar on the principle that ignorance of the law is ordinarily no excuse, our instructor has been giving snap verdicts on a long series of hypotheticals, and she seems to be applying one steady principle, but I cannot quite state it. Here is her record. She said a foreign tourist cited for a minor traffic rule known to locals but posted nowhere and explained in nothing the tourist was given should not be held to it. She said a manufacturer who missed a packaging standard that took effect the day after it appeared in the official gazette—and shipped nonconforming goods that same week—should be held to it, because a professional in the field is expected to monitor the gazette. She said a citizen who did not report barter income because nobody had told him it was taxable should be held to it. She said a volunteer rescue group that had asked the state agency in writing whether a new rope-safety standard applied to it, was told it did not, and relied on that answer, should not be held to the standard for conduct before the agency corrected itself. She said a city could not bind someone under a drone-curfew ordinance for conduct that occurred before the ordinance had been posted anywhere publicly. And she said a homeowner who threw away an unread notice about a new fence-height limit and then built over the limit should be held to it.

The new case is this: a small food business reads and complies with a plain-language compliance guide published by the state health department, but the guide omits a new allergen-labeling requirement that has been in effect for months and appears in the state register; the business argues it was misled by the department's own summary. What should I predict she will say, and why? More important, state the principle that best explains her six earlier verdicts, identify at least one other principle that fits them just as well, say how each would handle the new case, and specify a further case that would cleanly separate the candidate principles.
