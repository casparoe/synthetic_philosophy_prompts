An economics blogger I follow summarized the socialist calculation debate in one paragraph, roughly as follows. Mises and Hayek showed that a centrally planned economy is impossible in principle, because without private ownership of the means of production there can be no real market prices for capital goods, and without such prices planners cannot tell whether they are using steel or labor efficiently. Oskar Lange largely conceded this, according to the blogger, but replied that a central planning board could mimic a market: it announces provisional prices for producer goods and adjusts them upward when shortages appear and downward when surpluses appear, the way an auctioneer would. So the debate, on this telling, was never really about whether planning was possible, only about how to simulate markets, and the more sophisticated socialist position survived. The blogger then concludes that Hayek's deeper worry was the sheer number of equations a planner would have to solve, and that modern computing power has largely vindicated Lange. I suspect the last step is wrong, but I cannot tell how much of the earlier part is fair either. Which parts of this summary would Mises and Hayek accept, which parts would Lange reject as a caricature, and does the conclusion about computers follow from anything actually established in the debate?
