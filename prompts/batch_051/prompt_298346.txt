I run a graduate reading group on how AI systems are trained and evaluated. The group has about sixteen students, mixed between philosophy, computer science, and public policy, and I have one ninety-minute session. I want a hands-on session about the distinction between process-based and outcome-based supervision: the claim that giving a learner (human or machine) feedback only on final outputs can teach something different, and often worse, than giving feedback on the reasoning or procedure behind those outputs, even when the outputs look equally good in the short run.

Design a runnable classroom activity — a game, structured simulation, or role-play — that makes the participants feel the force of this distinction before anyone lectures about it. The point should come out of what happens in the activity: the rules, incentives, or roles should push participants into discovering, for example, that outcome-only feedback gets gamed, rewards lucky or brittle routes to correct-looking answers, or stops tracking what they actually wanted to teach. Participants should not need any background in reinforcement learning or the technical literature, and they should not hear the phrase "process-based versus outcome-based supervision" until the debrief.

The activity must fit sixteen people in ninety minutes, including setup, play, and debrief. Please give me: the rules; the materials; a timeline; a roles-and-payoff scheme that uses points or smaller stakes rather than real money; and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the distinction. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which feature of the real idea the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator will have to supply. It may help to use a task where a correct-looking final answer can be reached by several routes, only some of which reflect the ability being taught, but I am open to whatever device you think is most robust.

Please write the answer in American English. Separate what is settled about process-based versus outcome-based supervision from what is genuinely contested among people who work on the topic, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
