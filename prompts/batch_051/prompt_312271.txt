His line was that Buddhism is basically a form of consequentialism: the whole system is oriented around dukkha—suffering or unsatisfactoriness—and it's cessation, so actions get evaluated by whether they move toward less suffering, and the right action is just whichever one has the better consequences in terms of suffering. I kept objecting that Buddhism also puts intention at the center of moral evaluation—the same outward deed can have completely different karmic weight depending on the cetanā behind it, and a good outcome doesn't retroactively make a bad intention good. He said that's still compatible with consequentialism. But how? If the intention is what matters morally, doesn't that push Buddhism much closer to something Kantian, where the good will is the locus of value and the consequences are almost beside the point? It feels like he's just stretching 'consequentialism' until it covers any view that cares about suffering at all. Am I wrong that centering intention rules out consequentialism?
