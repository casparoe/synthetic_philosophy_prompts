I'm trying to work through a proposal a friend made for a city that's stuck on the boundary problem — who should get to vote in its referendums, when lots of non-residents are clearly effected (commuters, landlords, remote workers, people who'd live there if it weren't for its zoning) and plenty of residents are barely touched by a given measure. The proposal: scrap the fixed voter roll for ballot measures and give each referendum its own demos. In the one-week registration window before each measure, any natural person in the world can register to vote by paying a fee equal to 0.5% of their gross income from the previous month (self-reported, but randomly audited against whatever income records they have) and writing a one-sentence claim about how the measure would affect them. All the fees go into a pot, which is then distributed in equal shares to every registered voter right after the vote, no matter the outcome. Each registered voter then casts one equally weighted vote. The city itself never keeps a cent.

Treat this as a mechanism, not a moral position. Walk me through what it actually does: who gets pulled in, who gets shut out, how registration stops being about affectedness and becomes something else, what organized groups do first, what happens with measures that matter enormously to a few people and only a little to everyone, how the payout skews turnout across income levels, and where the whole thing gets gamed hardest. Also tell me whether any of the failure modes can be fixed without just turning it back into a fixed residency-based electorate.
