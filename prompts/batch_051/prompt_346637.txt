I teach an animals-and-ethics seminar and run a tiny philosophy podcast on the side. In a couple weeks I'm interviewing a philosopher (I won't name her, but she's a serious defender of large-scale intervention in wild animal suffering) about a new paper in which she defends what she calls "interventionist biocentrism." Her view in brief: the scale of suffering from predation, parasitism, disease, and starvation among wild animals is a moral catastrophe; declining to research interventions isn't neutrality but complicity; and we should begin with small, reversible measures like vaccination, contraceptive baiting, and supplementary feeding, then move toward larger genetic and ecological interventions as the modeling improves. She's quite good at deflecting the usual objections as "status quo bias" or "just an empirical bet, not a principled objection."

Draft eight to ten interview questions that push past that. I want at least one question on each of the following: (1) whether predation itself is the problem, or only the suffering it causes, and whether a predator-free ecosystem is even a coherent goal; (2) how she aggregates across predator and prey interests — say, a gazelle fawn eaten alive versus the lioness who needs to eat — especially since the prey are far more numerous; (3) the baseline problem: why is the existing level of wild suffering the thing we're allowed to leave in place, when we'd never permit its equivalent in a zoo or a lab; (4) the epistemic risk objection: interventions like cane toad introductions went badly, so shouldn't the burden of proof run against intervening rather than for it; and (5) the coordination problem: who decides which species get managed, and by what authority, when ecosystems cross borders. For each question, include a follow-up that anticipates her most likely reply. So, for instance, if she'd answer the aggregation worry by saying the numbers are overwhelming, ask whether that commits her to eliminating predators rather than merely managing their numbers; if she'd answer the baseline worry by saying "do no harm" is question-begging, ask what principle stops her view from implying we should turn Yellowstone into a managed garden. End with one question about what evidence or model result would actually change her mind. No generic questions ("what drew you to this topic?" or "summarize your view"), and make sure the questions turn on the actual content of her view, not just on her likely rhetorical replys.
