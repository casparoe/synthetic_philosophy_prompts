For most of my working life I was an experimental scientist, and only in retirement have I turned to philosophy. The question that keeps pulling me back is how to understand dispositions—fragility, solubility, toxicity, and the like—because the laboratory encourages one answer and the philosophical literature seems to think that answer has died a quiet death. I would like you to tell me how exposed my answer actually is.

The position I hold, provisionally, is this. A disposition ascription such as "this sample is soluble" is a counterfactual claim under an explicitly controlled idealization: were the sample to be subjected to the relevant stimulus under specified background conditions, its characteristic manifestation would occur. I grant that the standard counterexamples are real. A fink is an arrangement in which the disposition would be removed by the very stimulus that is supposed to elicit it, so that the naive conditional comes out false even though the ascription is true. A mask, or antidote, is an arrangement in which the disposition persists but something intervenes to block the manifestation, so that the naive conditional again fails. My view is that these cases do not refute the conditional approach; they show that the antecedent was underspecified. One controls for a fink by stipulating that the causal basis of the disposition persist through the stimulus; one controls for a mask by stipulating that the causal pathway from stimulus to manifestation be unobstructed. The crucial commitment is that these stipulations can, for each well-posed ascription, be cashed out in non-dispositional, physical vocabulary, and that they can be given in advance rather than improvised after a counterexample is proposed. Where they cannot be given, I am prepared to say that the original ascription was not determinate, rather than that the conditional analysis is false.

What I want is not a survey of the literature but a map of what would count against this view. Which observations, cases, arguments, or formal results should move me away from it? Please separate what would be decisive from what would be merely awkward, and be clear about which items a defender of a conditional analysis would themselves concede as damaging. I would also like an honest verdict on whether the position, as stated, has been insulated against refutation: whether the move of enriching the antecedent can always be repeated for any proposed counterexample, and if so, whether that is a problem for the view itself or only for my formulation of it. Having spent a career distrusting theories that could not be made to fail, I want to know whether this one can fail, and how.
