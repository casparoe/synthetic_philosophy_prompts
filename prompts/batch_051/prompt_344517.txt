The roots of x^2 + 1 = 0 are the two square roots of -1. In actual mathematics, which root gets called i is treated as arbitrary: complex conjugation swaps them while fixing every real number, and no purely algebraic statement distinguishes one from the other. Now suppose a counterfactual history. Around 1700, the European mathematical community adopts and enforces a convention that one of the two roots is the distinguished root, call it j, and that j and its negative are not interchangeable. This is not treated as a harmless notational choice. Treatises define addition, multiplication, exponentiation, and the elementary functions so that j occupies the role our world gives to i, and any proof that fails to respect the distinction between j and its negative, or that states a result only up to an arbitrary choice of root, is rejected as incomplete. By 1750 the convention has hardened into a general norm that the roots of any polynomial with rational coefficients must be presented in a fixed canonical order, and a result stated only up to permutation of the roots is unpublishable. Give me your best prediction of how mathematics develops under this regime from 1700 to 1900, compared with actual history. I want an explicit chain of reasoning, not a story. In particular, address: which developments would have proceeded essentially unchanged because they depend only on the algebraic structure of the complex numbers; which developments would have been delayed, duplicated, or driven down blind alleys because the convention obscures the conjugation symmetry; and where and when the convention would most likely have broken down or been exposed as empty. For each link in your reasoning, state your confidence and what would have to be true for that link to fail. Finish by saying what the comparison indicates about the original question: does it matter how you label the roots, or is the arbitrariness of the labeling doing no mathematical work?
