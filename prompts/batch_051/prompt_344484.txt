I write about science and ideas for a general audience, and next month I'm running a public ideas lab at a science museum: one facilitator, about 20 adults, 75 minutes, no philospohy background assumed. I want the group to feel the endurantism vs perdurantism debate before they ever hear those words. The rough contrast: endurantism says a thing persists by being wholly present at each moment it exists, while perdurantism says a thing is a four-dimensional worm stretched through time, and what we meet at any instant is only a temporal part of it. I'd like you to design a runnable activity or game — props or roles or small stakes — where participants run into the pressure points themselves, especially the problem of temporary intrinsics (one and the same thing having different properties at different times) and the creeping intuition that maybe there are parts of things across time after all. Don't let it turn into a lecture; the distinction should show up in the rules or the incentives, not in the facilitator explaining it. Then give me a debrief script that maps specific moments in the activity onto the two views, flags what will predictably go wrong when it's run and how to handle it, and is honest about what the activity can't reproduce and has to be supplied by me afterwards. The takeaway I want people to leave with: whether you count a thing as wholly present at each moment or as stretched through time changes what it means for it to be the same object from one moment to the next. Keep the materials cheap and the whole thing doable by one person. And please no reading list or citations — just the activity. I'm not too worried about them already knowing some jargon; the point is to make it viseral.
