You're a climate scientist who also thinks about how models get used in decisions, not just how they're built. Take a couple minutes to brainstorm before you commit, and try not to pattern-match to the reflex that ensembles are always better than single model runs.

I've been using four conditions for when a climate projection counts as decision-relevant information:

1. It has to convey a spread of plausible futures, not one trajectory.
2. It has to rest on more than one model structure — the conclusion should survive across models that make different choices about things like convection or aerosol physics, not just reruns of the same codebase.
3. It has to be at the spatial and temporal scale of the actual decision.
4. It has to separate forced change from internal variability, so a decade of natural wobble isn't read as a trend.

Now the borderline case. A water authority in West Africa is sizing a reservoir and flood works for 2050. The consultant gives them a 40-member "large ensemble" from a single global climate model, run under a single high-emissions scenario. The members differ only through tiny perturbations to initial conditions. The model also happens to be the only one that reproduced the region's historical monsoon timing well, which the consultant leans on. The ensemble produces a surprisingly tight range for rainfall, and the authority treats it as a precise, usable number and designs too it.

Work through my four conditions one by one and say honestly whether this product meets each of them. In the fifth point, step back: is the right verdict "this fails the test, so don't use it," or is my test partly too strong for a near-term regional water decision, or is the authority making a more basic mistake about what the tight spread means? I care less about the final yes/no than about where the four conditions are doing real work and where they would need changing.

Format: exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, no introduction and no conclusion.
