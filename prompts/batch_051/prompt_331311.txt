I've been steadily talking myself into Turing's old bet that the route to machine intelligence is less like writing down an adult mind and more like raising one: start with something childlike and plastic, then educate it, not just with supervised training on cleanly labeled examples but with the messier loop of feedback, correction, and the kind of unlabelled immersion human children get from their environment. I don't want to relitigate the classic question of whether machines can think, and I don't need citations or a history of the idea; what interests me is whether that specific architectural bet, the child-machine view, is actually testable, and what would count against it.

What would the world have to look like for it to be false, or at least badly wounded? I want the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to seperate the evidence that a defender of the view would themselves concede as counting against it from the stuff they could fairly dismiss as changing the subject. Concretely: which of its predictions can in principle fail, say about how much built-in structure is needed, how sample-efficient the learning has to be, or what gets learned rather than supplied? Which rival approaches doing unexpectedly well would count as evidence against it? And has the position been softened over the years into the unfalsifiable claim that of course it's not literally a child, it just means a general-purpose learner, and if so is that a real problem or just the normal fate of an architectural idea? I'm trying to get a handle on how much I'd have to see before I should give it up.
