I write about science and tech for a living, and i've drifted into a publishing habit I want pressure-tested. Whenever I'm about to write something that could hurt a company's or a person's reputation, I nudge the risky claim from fact to opinion or forecast. Instead of "the product doesn't work as advertised," I write "in my view, the product fails to deliver"; instead of "the model will fail here," I write "I'd forecast poor performance in this setting." My rough theory is that defamation law only punishes false statements of fact, so opinions sit in a safer zone, and ratings and star scores are the safest form of all. But this obviously changes what I'm actually telling readers. Is this policy defensible, or am I laundering factual claims through the fact/opinion distinction? More specifically: under American law, are ratings and forecasts genuinely protected as opinion, or is that contested? And does the legal fact/opinion line track anything real, epistemically speaking, or is it just a practical carve-out? Please separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on First Amendment and media law, and don't present a contested view as the consensus. American English, please.
