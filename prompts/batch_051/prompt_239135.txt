I am a philosophy professor, and I chair the Clinical Ethics Committee of my university's teaching hospital. We are deciding whether to approve a six-month pilot of an autonomous vasopressor-titration system in the medical ICU. The system — call it AutoTitrate — uses a proprietary continuous-learning model to adjust norepinephrine infusion rates within preset bounds in response to real-time vitals. A critical-care nurse supervises up to six patients on the system, and an intensivist can override it at any time, though the vendor's own simulations suggest that many overrides would occur too late to prevent an adverse event. If a patient dies from a wrong titration, it will be genuinely unclear whose fault it is: the vendor trained the model and set the bounds, the hospital purchased and deployed it, the nurse was supervising too many patients to catch the error in time, the intensivist approved the protocol, and the system's own recommendation was the proximate cause. Several committee members argue that this diffusion of responsibility is a decisive reason not to approve: approving would mean knowingly creating a situation in which, after a bad outcome, no determinate person will deserve blame or be answerable for it. Others reply that responsibility is not disappearing but being reallocated — to the vendor by contract, to the hospital as an institution, and to the supervising clinicians — and that a pilot with a projected mortality benefit should not be blocked by a philosophical worry. The committee is deadlocked, and I have been asked to produce the decision memo the full board will vote on. I want the memo to treat the responsibility-gap objection as a serious moral problem, not as a technicality or a public-relations issue, and to assess the case on ethical grounds rather than legal liability.

Please draft the memo with the following sections, in this order and with these approximate lengths:

1. Summary (150 words): the question before the committee, your recommendation, and the principal reason for it, in one paragraph.

2. The proposal and the objection (300 words): state precisely what the pilot would involve, and explain why some members think it would create a responsibility gap — distinguishing backward-looking answerability and blame from forward-looking duties to prevent harm, compensate victims, and learn from failures.

3. The case for approval (400 words): the strongest reasons to approve, including the projected clinical benefit, the claim that responsibility is reallocated rather than eliminated, and the institutional advantages of locating answerability in the hospital and the vendor.

4. The case against approval (400 words): the strongest version of the responsibility-gap objection, including the concern that a gap is morally objectionable even when liability and compensation have been arranged by contract.

5. My recommendation (350 words): a clear bottom line — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with any conditions that would make the gap ethically tolerable, such as revised supervision ratios, mandatory override logging, freezing the model during the pilot, or an independent no-fault review process for adverse events.

6. The strongest consideration on the losing side (200 words): state the single consideration that most counts against your recommendation, and explain why, on balance, it does not prevail.

The memo should be written so the committee can act on it directly, and it should not require the reader to consult external legal, regulatory, or medical literature.
