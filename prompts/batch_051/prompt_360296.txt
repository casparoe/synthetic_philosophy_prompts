I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a reported essay about whether adversarial debate between AI systems could ever substitute for directly checking what is true. The proposal, as I understand it, runs roughly as follows: two comparably capable language models are instructed to argue opposite sides of a factual question before a human judge; the judge and the debaters are then trained together, so the judge learns to see through bad reasoning and the debaters learn to produce better reasoning, with the expectation that the more accurate side will tend to win even when the judge could not have established the answer on her own. The objection I keep returning to is the obfuscated-argument problem. On questions where the correct answer is complicated, unintuitive, or technical, a capable but dishonest debater may be able to overwhelm an honest one with superficially rigorous noise: spurious distinctions, cherry-picked evidence, technically valid but irrelevant detail, misleading framing, and arguments too intricate for the judge to evaluate without already knowing the answer. The deeper worry is that further training may not repair this, because the judge's standards are themselves forged on exactly the features a skilled obfuscator can manipulate.

I want a credence, not a summary of the discussion. Specifically: what probability do you assign to the claim that obfuscated arguments are a decisive obstacle for human-judged debate, in the following sense — for an important class of empirical questions on which the judge cannot independently verify the answer, a dishonest debater of equal underlying capability can defeat a truthful debater more often than not, and no realistically available amount of further training or protocol design eliminates that advantage. Decompose the claim into the sub-questions it depends on: at least, how often truth is genuinely harder to present than a specific falsehood; whether judge-training transfers to unseen forms of obfuscation; whether the truthful debater has offsetting advantages; whether procedural safeguards such as cross-examination or grounding in verifiable sources cheaply neutralize the asymmetry; and whatever else you take to be load-bearing. Give each sub-claim a rough probability with a sentence of justification, show how they combine into your overall figure, and identify which term your final number moves the most when you vary it. Finally, tell me which sub-claim you are least confident in and what concrete evidence — an experiment, a formal result, a deployment — would most shift it. For calibration, I am somewhere near 0.35 and a researcher I interviewed is near 0.75; I would like to know where the disagreement between us most likely lives.
