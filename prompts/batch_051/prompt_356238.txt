My friend has surprisingly firm views about when you owe it to people to be legible—to not hide what's actually going on or put on a front. Track record: she says a guy with depression who acts cheerful at the office so his coworkers don't have to cope with him is fine, not deceptive. She says a trans woman who lets new coworkers assume she's cis and never corrects them is also fine; they're not entitled to that. She defended a colleague who answers emails in a bright, professional tone while going through a divorce—fine, that's just compartmentalization. But she got angry about a man who tells his wife "nothing's wrong" for weeks when he's actually upset, just to avoid a fight: that's a betrayal, you owe your spouse the real stuff. And she says a lecturer who wraps simple ideas in jargon so students think he's deep is doing something wrong—students paid for clarity and he's withholding it.

The new case: a woman with chronic pain hides it behind a cheerful front at work, and also hides from her husband how bad it actually is, not to avoid a fight but because whenever she's honest he hovers and trats her like a patient and she can't stand it. Do you think my friend will say she owes her husband the truth about the pain? I'm less interested in a yes/no than in the rule my friend seems to be running, what other rule fits her earlier verdicts just as well, what each predicts here, and which further case would best settle which rule she's actually using.
