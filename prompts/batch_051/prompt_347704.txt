I've been sitting in on a reading group that meets in a law and philosophy department, and for the better part of a year we have been going around in circles about causation by omission. The examples are always the same, and nobody disputes the underlying facts: the plant dies because no one waters it; the child starves because the caregiver does not feed him; the inspection report is never filed and the dam collapses; the patient's sepsis goes untreated. What we cannot settle is what to say about such cases. One faction insists that omissions simply are not causes. An omission, they argue, is not an event, a mechanism, or even a thing in the world; it is an absence, and absences cannot transmit energy, push, pull, or make a difference except as a shorthand tied to our interests and norms. On their view, to say the caregiver's omission caused the death is a piece of contrastive or explanatory shorthand, useful for assigning blame but not a description of any real causal relation. The other faction replies that this is philosophy gone fastidious: in tort law, in medicine, and in ordinary life we say, and seem to mean, that a failure to act caused the harm, and a theory of causation that cannot say this has abandoned the data rather than explained it.

The impasse is made stranger by the fact that the two sides agree on all the counterfactuals. Everyone concedes that if the caregiver had fed the child, the child would not have died; that if the plant had been watered, it would not have wilted. The divide is over whether that counterfactual dependence, keyed to an absence, is sufficient for causation. There is also the selection problem: for any given harm there are indefinitely many omissions—no one fed the child, no one called a doctor, no one had built a hospital nearby, no one had invented a treatment—and yet we single out one failure as the cause. One side takes this to show that omission-causation is really responsibility-talk, driven by salience and norms rather than by the world; the other side answers that ordinary causal selection is always interest-laden, so omissions are no worse off than positive causes. My working hypothesis is that the debate has stalled because it is several disagreements wearing one name: a metaphysical disagreement about whether "cause" denotes a single worldly relation that absences can instantiate, a semantic disagreement about whether ordinary causal talk is answerable to that metaphysics, and a normative disagreement about whether legal or moral responsibility requires genuine causation or only counterfactual dependence on what someone failed to do. I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict on omission-causation itself. What kind of disagreement is this, actually—verbal, substantive, or a difference over what a satisfactory theory of causation is supposed to do? And given the diagnosis, what would count as progress: a sharper semantics, a better account of contrastive or difference-making causation, a formal result, or simply an agreement to separate the metaphysical question from the moral and legal one—and would that last option dissolve the debate rather than resolve it?
