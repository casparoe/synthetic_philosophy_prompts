I would like a poem to close a unit in a seminar on theories of ideology: a Petrarchan sonnet, fourteen lines of iambic pentameter in an octave and sestet with the volta at line nine, that stages the historical-materialist claim about economic base and superstructure rather than merely illustrating it. The octave should carry the strong, non-vulgar version of the view: not that wealth is the only human motive or that everything is economics, but that the concrete historical shape of law, state, religion, and art is conditioned and made intelligible, determined in the last instance, by the organization of production, property relations, and class position. A hand-mill economy is not merely accompanied by feudal lords and feudal piety; it is part of why lords, piety, and law take the shape they do, and the ruling ideas of an age stand in a non-accidental relation to the ideas of its ruling class.

At the volta, the poem should turn to the real difficulty, the superstructure answering back: legal forms, religious idioms, and aesthetic conventions that outlive their original economic basis, that act back upon production, and that have an internal history of their own; the suspicion that one-way causation is too mechanical, and that the base itself may be unreadable without the legal and normative concepts that were supposed to be mere superstructure.

The closing lines should not resolve this quarrel, neither by picking a side nor by a dialectical move that dissolves it, but should hold the explanatory force of the base together with the resistance of the rest. A reader who knows the literature should recognize the poem as being about relative autonomy, reciprocal action, and whether the economic base explains everything; a reader who has never heard those phrases should still be left with a good poem.
