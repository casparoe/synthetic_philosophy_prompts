I recently read a popular survey of metaphysics that summarized the history of monism in a way that struck me as almost too tidy. It said:

"Monism is the doctrine, defended by Parmenides, Spinoza, and today's priority monists, that there is really only one thing. The details change — for Parmenides the one thing was Being, for Spinoza it was God-or-Nature, and for contemporary priority monists it is the entire cosmos — but the core commitment is the same. Priority monism is simply the old view updated with the vocabulary of fundamentality: the cosmos is the single basic entity, and everything else — desks, electrons, people — is a derivative appearance rather than something ultimately real, just as a wave is not really a distinct thing over and above the ocean. Consequently, anyone who grants that the cosmos is a single whole composed of parts has already conceded monism's central claim; the only remaining question is how to describe the appearances."

As a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, I cannot tell whether the article is being incisive or running distinct claims together, but two things bother me. First, "there is really only one thing" sounds very different from "there is really only one fundamental thing," much as "this module does not exist" differs from "this module exists but imports from a root module." Second, I had understood Spinoza's finite modes to be real rather than illusory, which makes the wave simile feel off.

I would like an audit of that quoted summary rather than a verdict on which position I should adopt. Specifically: which parts are accurate, which are common simplifications, what a careful defender of priority monism would object to, and whether the final move — that accepting a cosmos composed of parts already commits one to monism — survives the corrections.
