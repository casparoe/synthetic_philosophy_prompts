I manage research operations at a lab that trains reinforcement-learning agents, and I chair the committee that decides before each new training run whether a system receives any welfare-related safeguards: limits on run length, behavioral monitoring, inspection of the learned reward function, mandatory checkpointing, that sort of thing. Right now we decide case by case and the reasoning is not consistent. I want a reusable decision procedure my committee, mostly engineers with no philosophy background, could run in about twenty minutes per proposal. The central question should be: when should we treat a reinforcement-learning agent's reward signal as valenced, that is, as evidence that the system can be benefited or harmed by its training, rather than as a mere optimization target? The procedure should list the questions in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, identify where it should stop with a no, where it should escalate to someone with more time or expertise, and where the remaining question is a values call rather than a technical finding. Keep the underlying theory in the background, but be explicit about which distinctions you had to preserve to keep the procedure faithful and which you dropped for usability, and flag the cases where the procedure will misfire. Then run the procedure on three proposals we have seen: (1) a simulated legged robot trained to walk with a shaped reward that includes a small energy penalty; (2) an assistant-style model fine-tuned on human preference judgments about its outputs; and (3) a multi-agent system whose agents are rewarded for outcompeting rivals and can learn sabotage. I suspect at least one of these will get an answer my team will not like, and I want the procedure to be honest about that rather than back-loaded toward our convenience.
