For the past two years I have been running an undergraduate philosophy discussion group of roughly fifteen students; we meet weekly for seventy-five minutes, and most members have uneven preparation. I would like one meeting to be built around a problem that has recently stopped being abstract for me. Several months ago, a close friend defended a decision that cost me a great deal by explaining that he had treated everyone's interests equally, weighed the expected outcomes, and gone where the calculation pointed. What unsettled me was not the decision itself but the form of the justification: it stood outside our friendship, handed the relationship back to me as an item in an impersonal reckoning, and thereby misjudged what kind of response the situation called for. I have since come to think that I was experiencing, in an ordinary social register, the force of Bernard Williams's integrity objection to utilitarianism, together with his complaint that an agent who must consult a general moral theory before responding to someone to whom he stands in a deep personal relation has interposed a thought where no further thought should be needed.

Please design a single seventy-five-minute session, in the form of a structured role-play or staged deliberation, that gives the group that felt recognition rather than a lecture. It should include concrete scenarios, roles, rules, a timeline, and a debrief. Do not use Williams's own canonical examples, and assign no prior reading; the problem should emerge from what participants choose and justify under the constraints of the exercise. The terms Williams, integrity, utilitarianism, and the phrase used to name the objection should be withheld until the debrief.

In your design, at least one role should be required to offer an impartial, outcome-maximizing justification for a choice that another participant experiences as a violation of a commitment central to who they take themselves to be; the exercise should be arranged so that the justification itself comes to be felt as part of the difficulty rather than as its resolution. The debrief should map specific moments of the exercise onto the distinction between reasons that belong to a person's own projects and commitments and reasons supplied by an impartial theory, and onto Williams's claim that a moral outlook built entirely around maximizing the good abstracts away from the standpoint of the agent whose life is being organized, and so threatens that agent's integrity. Please also be candid about what a classroom activity cannot reproduce: it can simulate pressure, discomfort, and self-justification, but it cannot reproduce the weight of an actual life organized around such commitments, so the facilitator will need to supply that point explicitly. Finally, give me two or three predicted failure modes and how to handle them, especially participants who conclude that the maximizing choice was simply correct and that any resistance to it is mere sentimentality.
