I retired from a long career in experimental physics and have lately been reading philosophy of physics, especially the debate about gauge freedom and surplus structure: the worry that a gauge theory represents one physical situation with excess mathematical machinery, so that gauge-equivalent descriptions are merely re-descriptions of the same reality and only the gauge-invariant content can be physically real. I would like to test this debate against a case I knew well from the laboratory, the Aharonov–Bohm effect.

As I understand the setup, a long solenoid confines a magnetic field to its interior; outside the solenoid the magnetic field vanishes but the vector potential does not. A coherent electron beam is split so that the two branches pass around opposite sides of the solenoid and recombine, and the interference pattern shifts even though the electrons travel only through a region where the electric and magnetic fields are zero. The size of the shift is fixed by the magnetic flux enclosed by the two paths, equivalently by the line integral of the vector potential around the closed loop the paths form. The effect has been confirmed experimentally many times.

The philosophical puzzle is what this does to the surplus-structure view. Some take the effect to show that the vector potential is physically real, since the electrons respond to something in a region where the field strengths vanish and the shift is most naturally expressed through the potential. Others reply that only the gauge-invariant loop integral matters, that this integral is simply the enclosed magnetic flux, and that the local values of the potential remain unphysical redundancy. I can feel the pull of both readings. Please adopt the role of a careful philosopher of physics and think the case through slowly. Begin by laying out the main positions in the debate over what is real in a gauge theory, being precise about what physicality would amount to under each. Then analyze the Aharonov–Bohm effect in those terms: what it does and does not establish about the potential, which formulations of the surplus-structure view survive it intact, and which must be revised or given up. Finally, reverse direction and say what the episode shows about the philosophical debate—whether it acts as a decisive experiment, an anomaly, or a situation the standard positions never fully anticipated. I am less interested in a survey than in a disciplined mapping of what is gauge-invariant, what is localized where, and where the framework simply falls silent.
