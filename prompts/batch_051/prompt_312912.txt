Here's a counterfactual I've been turning over. Starting tomorrow, we can specify the initial state of the atmosphere perfectly for every weather model run: temperature, pressure, humidity, and wind at every grid point, exactly, with zero measurement or interpolation error. The only initial-condition imperfection left is the tiny rounding the computers introduce when they read in the state. Nothing else changes: the model equations are still the same finite-resolution approximations of the fluid dynamics, and the atmosphere still obeys the same chaotic dynamics. Give me a reasoned prediction of what happens to forecast skill in this world. How much better do 3-day, 10-day, and 30-day forecasts get, and where does the practical predictability horizon end up? I want the chain of reasoning: which effects are forced by error growth in the physics, and which guesses depend on how far model error itself can ever be reduced. And the part I actually care about: does this scenario show that 'unpredictable' is relative to our finite knowledge rather than a fact about the system itself? Or does the butterfly effect still bite even when initial conditions are perfect?
