For my job at a state education agency I evaluate claims that lean on a heritability number, and I keep seeing people on both sides get tripped by the same thing. A district will say reading disability is 60% heritable so extra reading support won't help; another will say that because reading ability is only partly genetic, a costly tutoring program will obviously work. I need a reusable procedure for deciding whether a cited heritability number actually supports the conclusion being drawn, not just a lecture on the concept. It should be a set of questions I can run in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure should stop with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because its become a values question. I'm not a geneticist and get maybe a afternoon per claim, and whatever I conclude has to be defensible in front of a superintendent. What I most need the procedure to enforce is what heritability does and does not mean: it's a statement about variance in a population in a particular range of environments, not about how fixed a trait is in an individual, and a high figure by itself says nothing about how much an intervention could help or about differences between groups. I also keep seeing twin-study numbers and DNA-based numbers treated as interchangeable, so say when that difference matters. Then run the procedure on three cases: a district arguing that reading disability is 60% heritable so extra reading support won't move the needle; a parent group arguing that ADHD is 70% heritable so classroom behavior plans are a waste; and someone in our own office arguing that since reading ability is only 40% heritable, the other 60% must be environment we can change, so an expensive tutoring program ought to work. Be straight about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and make sure at least one of the cases comes out in a way I won't like. Write it as continuous prose, like an email: no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables.
