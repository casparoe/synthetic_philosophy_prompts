I am a PhD student working on interpretability for large neural networks, and I have been turning over a worry about the original case for building the field. As I understand it, that case was fundamentally epistemic: behavioral testing licenses a certain kind of confidence about past performance, but it does not tell you whether a system will fail in a novel regime, because distinct internal mechanisms can produce indistinguishable behavior on your test distribution. The proposed remedy was that a human researcher would inspect the network's parts, identify the features or circuits that drive a class of decisions, and thereby come to understand what the system is doing before trusting it in new circumstances. The assumption I want to examine is that the epistemically valuable position is one in which a human holds the explanation: understanding is something the human achieves by examining the target system directly.

The field has changed in a way that puts this assumption under strain. For current systems the internals are too numerous and too high-dimensional for direct human inspection at the relevant scale, so a large part of the field now delegates the inspection to a second model. The interpreter attends to the target's activations and emits a human-readable gloss, and the researcher is now downstream of a machine-generated report. But the interpreter is itself a trained model whose internal operation is no more transparent than the target's. I keep going back and forth on whether this merely relocates the opacity one level up, or whether an interpreter can transmit the kind of understanding the original argument promised even when no human grasps the interpreter's own basis for the report.

My question is whether the original epistemic argument for interpretability survives this shift from human inspection to machine interpretation. If it survives, what exactly must be true of the interpreter—reliability on cases with known ground truth, behavioral validation, something about the form of explanations it produces—for the argument to go through? If it does not survive, is there a weakened version that remains defensible, or does the argument now turn out to have been an argument for something else, such as auditing the interpreter–target pair together? I want an account of which step of the original argument carries the load and, in particular, whether the recursion is vicious or benign, not a policy conclusion.
