I have been working through Joseph Butler’s sermon on the forgiveness of injuries alongside the contemporary debate about whether forgiveness can be unconditional. Butler’s key move, as I read him, is to distinguish forgiving from forgetting and from excusing. I think that distinction, pressed hard, yields the following argument, and I want to know whether it is stronger than I suspect.

(1) If forgiveness can ever be morally obligatory, then there must be cases in which it is required even when the offender has not repented, apologized, or repaired the wrong.
(2) Forgiveness cannot require the victim to forget the wrong, because memory is not directly under voluntary control; at most it can require the victim to forswear or substantially temper resentment.
(3) Resentment is a backward-looking negative attitude that treats the offender’s past action as a standing wrong done to the victim.
(4) To forswear or substantially temper resentment toward an unrepentant offender is to cease treating the wrong as a reason for hard feelings, blame, or reduced trust.
(5) But if the offender has neither repented nor repaired the wrong, the wrong is still a standing reason for hard feelings, blame, or reduced trust.
(6) Therefore, forgiving an unrepentant offender requires treating something that is still a standing reason as if it were not a reason.
(7) No one can be obligated to treat a standing reason as if it were not a reason; at best that is a supererogatory act of grace.
(8) Therefore, unconditional forgiveness—forgiveness without repentance—can at most be permissible and admirable, but cannot be morally required.

I half-suspect the argument slips somewhere around steps (5) and (6), perhaps by moving illicitly from “there is a reason” to “the reason must be acted on,” or by assuming resentment and blame stand or fall together. Am I right that it is invalid, and if so, exactly which step fails, and on what reading of that step? If the argument is valid, how should someone resist the conclusion that unconditional forgiveness cannot be obligatory? When you answer, please say how confident you are in your assessment and single out the point you are least sure about.
