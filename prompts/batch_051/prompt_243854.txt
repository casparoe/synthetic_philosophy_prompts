I'm a novelist, not an anthropologist, and my work-in-progress has a problem I've only just realised it has. It's set partly in a rural Pashtun family in Pakistan and partly in a British family in Birmingham, and every character's moral machinery is secretly identical: they all feel guilt rather than shame, moralise harm but not disrespect, treat fairness as impartiality, and act as if the individual is the unit of moral responsibility. I know enough about the WEIRD-psychology literature to know thats wrong, but not enough to fix it scene by scene, and the book lives or dies on whether the two families actually think differently.

Could you write me a reusable proceedure for checking a drafted scene's moral psychology against what's known about cross-cultural variation in folk morality? I need a checklist I can run in about twenty minutes per scene with no academic access. It should cover the axes where folk morality is known to vary — guilt vs shame, the size and shape of the moral circle, impartiality vs relational fairness, which of the moral foundations get moralised, whether responsibility sits in the person or the family, how much intentions matter relative to outcomes. For each axis, give me the question to ask, what a WEIRD-default answer looks like, what a plausible non-WEIRD answer looks like, and the traps where flipping it just produces a mirror-image caricature. And I especially want it to flag the moments where my narration treats one side's reaction as simply right — the objectivity slip, basically — when it's actually a WEIRD default wearing a universal costume.

Then run the procedure on three scenes: (1) a daughter in the Pakistani family refuses to go through with an arranged marriage, and the scene is currently written so the reader sides with her automatically; (2) two brothers in that family argue over whether to report a cousin to the police after a fight; (3) a British character feels guilt about lying to her mother, while her Pakistani counterpart feels something closer to shame about the family losing face. Be honest about where the procedure itself is smuggling in assumptions — including the possibility that my WEIRD-vs-non-WEIRD framing is too coarse and is itself a bit, well, WEIRD. I don't need it to settle which morality is objectively right; I need it to stop me writing Londoners in different clothes.
