I spent my working life in experimental physics, retired a few years ago, and have lately been making my way through the philosophy of science with a reading group. We are stuck on Feyerabend's dictum that anything goes: some of us read it as a serious thesis about how scientific change actually happens, others as a polemical joke at the expense of methodologists, and the discussion keeps circling without landing. I would like a poem to close our next session. Please write a sestina on this. Use six recurring end-words drawn from the actual vocabulary of scientific practice, perhaps rule, data, anomaly, ad hoc, progress, and persuasion, and let the form carry the argument: the same words returning in a fixed but shifting order across the stanzas should enact the claim that science exhibits no single unchanging method, only recurring elements recombined each time a successful practice breaks a stated rule. The three-line envoi should leave unsettled whether saying that anything goes is itself just one more rule. The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of science would not wince, and good enough as a poem that someone who has never read Feyerabend would still grasp the dilemma.
