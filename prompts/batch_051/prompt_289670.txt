I sit on the Committee on Doctrinal Standards at a confessional seminary. We are adjudicating the case of Dr. Miriam Hale, our professor of systematic theology, who recently published an essay arguing that penal substitution, by making forgiveness conditional on the prior infliction of punishment, "presents the Father as unable to love until blood has been shed," and that the church should instead recover the moral-influence theory, on which the cross chiefly reveals the depth of divine love and thereby transforms sinners. Our denomination's confessional standard, to which all faculty subscribe annually, states in Article IV, Of the Atonement: "Christ, by His obedience and death, fully satisfied divine justice and bore in our place the penalty due for our sins, thereby reconciling us to God." In her statement to us, Dr. Hale says she can affirm the first clause if "satisfied divine justice" is read in an Anselmian sense—Christ's self-offering repairs the moral order that sin disorders—but she cannot affirm the second clause, which she regards as a later forensic development that imports retributive logic into the gospel. Our bylaws allow a faculty member to declare a scruple against a clause of the confession, provided the committee judges that the scruple does not "defeat the substance" of the article, and she has asked us to receive her scruple on that basis.

Half the committee reads her statement as an outright repudiation and wants to withhold her subscription, which under our rules would bar her from teaching the required course in the doctrine of reconciliation. The dean and others think the article's "satisfaction" language deliberately leaves room for a non-penal account, and they warn that a heresy inquiry over a live theological difference would damage the seminary's academic freedom and its ability to recruit faculty. I have been asked to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's meeting. Please draft the decision memo I will present—around 800 words—recommending one concrete course of action (for instance: receive the scruple unconditionally, receive it with conditions on how the atonement is taught, reject it while permitting a revised statement, or withhold subscription). The memo should engage seriously with the strongest argument on the losing side, distinguish the penal substitution, satisfaction, and moral-influence theories wherever that distinction bears on the recommendation, and close with a rationale the seminary could defend publicly.
