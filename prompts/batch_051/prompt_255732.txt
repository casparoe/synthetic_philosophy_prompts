I work as a program officer at a foundation that funds "new governance" projects — charter cities, special economic zones, seasteading outfits, digital jurisdictions, that kind of thing. I do the first screen on incoming proposals, maybe three or four a month, and I have about half a day per proposal before I have to write a memo for our investment committee. The part I keep getting stuck on is the voluntariness question. Every project says the same thing: it's a private city, people choose to come, they can leave whenever they want, so it's consensual in a way a normal state isn't. But I've read enough to know "you can leave" does a lot of work — there's exit costs, what you can take with you, whether the place is the only realistic option for the jobs it offers, whether future residents and kids get a say, and whether the "city" is really just a smaller state with better marketing. I'm not a political philosopher, and I can't turn every proposal into a seminar.

What I want is a reusable screening procedure I can actually run: a checklist of questions in order, grounded in whatever the consent/exit/legitimacy literature actually establishes about opting out of a state, with each question phrased so I can answer it from the proposal documents and a site visit. For each question, say what counts as a pass, what counts as a fail, and where the procedure should stop with a no rather than continuing. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and where the committee has to make a values decision rather than a factual one.

Then run the procedure on three proposals: (1) a charter city with excellent infrastructure and a credible promise of prosperity, but the only realistic way for most residents to leave is to forfeit most of what they've built and return to a country they fled; (2) a seastead where all current residents are enthusiastic founders who signed on voluntarily, but the platform has no port access rights, no clear legal status for children born there, and no mechanism for a future resident who wants out to be bought out; (3) something unglamorous, like a land trust or a small special district that looks boring but scores well on the procedure. I want the procedure to catch the first two if their actually non-consensual, and to say the third one passes if that's what the procedure implies, even if the committee finds it unexciting.

Please start with the bottom line: the one-sentence rule the procedure reduces to, then the procedure itself, then the worked applications and the reasoning about where the theory forced the choices.
