Peirce argued in The Fixation of Belief that the scientific method is the only stable way of fixing belief, because it alone submits belief to an external permanency—something whose character does not depend on what anyone thinks about it. On his account, the method of tenacity fails because other people eventually shake the believer's confidence; the method of authority fails because no institution can regulate opinion on every subject; and the a priori method merely freezes whatever a like-minded circle finds agreeable to reason. Science alone turns disagreement into a tractable problem, since two inquirers can in principle be brought into conflict by the same independent object and can resolve that conflict by further inquiry. Now consider a later development: for most people, much of the experience that could prove recalcitrant to their beliefs now arrives through algorithmically personalized feeds and recommender systems optimized for engagement. These systems select, order, and filter observations in a way that can keep disconfirming material out of view while still presenting each user with a world that feels externally given. Does Peirce's argument for the unique self-correcting character of the scientific method survive this development? Identify the precise premise that algorithmic mediation of experience puts under pressure, and say whether the argument can be repaired in some weaker form, or whether the development reveals that the argument was doing different work than it first appeared to do. Do not settle for the observation that professional science still works; address the role the argument assigned to ordinary experience in the fixation of belief.
