I have been trying to make precise a claim I keep encountering in philosophy of mathematics—the claim, associated with Lakatos's work on proofs and refutations, that mathematics is quasi-empirical. I have reconstructed the position as the following argument. It strikes me as almost right, but I have a nagging sense that the word "counterexample" is doing two different jobs, and I cannot locate the slide precisely.

(1) In mathematical practice, general conjectures are tested against particular cases. The conjecture that every polyhedron satisfies V − E + F = 2 was confronted with particular polyhedra, and a single counterexample—say, a polyhedron shaped like a picture frame, for which the formula yields 0 rather than 2—refutes the conjecture as it stands.

(2) In empirical science, a general hypothesis is likewise tested against particular observations, and a single disconfirming observation refutes the hypothesis as it stands.

(3) The only difference between a mathematical counterexample and a disconfirming observation is how the particular case is produced: the one is constructed or proved, the other is observed or measured.

(4) How a particular case is produced is inessential to its refuting role. What matters is only that a general claim is confronted with a particular case it cannot accommodate.

(5) Therefore, if empirical hypotheses count as empirical in virtue of being refutable by particular cases, then mathematical conjectures count as empirical in that same respect.

(6) But mathematical claims are not settled by observation; they are settled by proof. So mathematics is quasi-empirical: empirical in its method of conjecture and refutation, yet non-empirical in its subject matter.

Is this argument valid? If it is valid, are all the premises true? If a step fails, identify exactly which step fails and why, then give the strongest repair of that step you can, and say whether the argument goes through once the repair is made.
