I've been reading around in Haidt's moral foundations work for a term paper, and I'm trying to make sure the "objections" section isn't missing anything standard. The objections I've already got, in my own words, are roughly these. First, the taxonomy might be an artifact of how the Moral Foundations Questionnaire is built: the items were designed to capture the exact intuitions the theory posits, and different item pools yield different numbers of factors, so the questionnaire can't really serve as independent evidence for the six-foundation structure. Second, the list of foundations is post hoc and has already shifted—there were five until liberty/oppression was added later—and there's no principled account of how many foundations there are or what would count as finding a new one rather than splitting an old one. Third, Haidt and others sometimes slide from the descriptive claim that conservatives draw on six "taste buds" while liberals mostly draw on two, to the evaluative suggestion that all six are legitimate moral registers and that liberals are missing something; that's a naturalistic-fallacy sort of move and doesn't follow from the psychology. Fourth, the social intuitionist part—the idea that judgment is fast and intuitive and reasoning is mostly post-hoc justification—is overstated by the theory's defenders, since there's decent evidence that conscious reasoning can change people's moral judgments. Fifth, the evolutionary backstories attached to each foundation (purity from pathogen disgust, authority from dominance hierarchies, and the like) are largely post hoc just-so stories, with little direct comparative or historical evidence for the specific mechanisms claimed.

What I want is one more serious objection that is not on that list and not just a rephrasing, special case, or combination of any of them. It should be the sort of thing a researcher in moral psychology would actually take seriously, not a laypersons gut reaction. And I'd like you to tell me honestly whether the objection you give is any good: would it do real damage, would it only narrow the theory's scope, or is it something a defender of moral foundations theory could dismiss pretty easily?
