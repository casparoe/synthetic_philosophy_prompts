I am a novelist, and a question that began for me as a matter of craft has gradually come to seem like a genuine moral problem. I keep writing characters who are scrupulously honest—they do not lie, and they try hard not to mislead—but who are also guarded: they decline to explain themselves, offer their reasons only in fragments, and treat their inner lives as though those lives were not quite anyone else's business. A reader whose judgment I trust recently told me this is not an aesthetic quirk but a moral defect. Her claim was that someone who refuses to be legible to others is withholding something he owes them, in much the same way he would owe them the truth. Honesty without transparency, she argued, is a form of deception by omission: a person who gives no account of himself makes it impossible for others to know whom they are dealing with, and thereby manipulates the terms on which they relate to him.

I find this argument troubling because it seems to turn ordinary reticence into something as blameworthy as lying, and yet I cannot say exactly where it fails. Perhaps sincerity and transparency are distinct virtues, but I am no longer sure the distinction can be maintained in practice. If I care about not deceiving people, do I not bear some responsibility for the false impressions my silence allows to harden? Is there a duty to be legible to others—to explain one's motives, feelings, and reservations so that they can understand where one stands—and does sincerity, properly understood, require exactly that kind of transparency?
