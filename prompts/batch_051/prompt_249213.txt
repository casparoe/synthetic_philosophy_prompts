I spent my career in the natural sciences, and I have recently come to philosophy through the problem of classification. The idea that has most unsettled my laboratory habits is Ian Hacking's concept of a looping effect. As I understand it, when a human kind is identified, the people placed in it become aware of the classification and adjust their conduct and self-understanding in response; the classifiers in turn revise the classification to accommodate those changes; and the kind keeps shifting in a feedback loop rather than sitting still the way a physical kind does.

Consider, as a test case, the rise and decline of multiple personality disorder in North America between roughly 1980 and 2000. I will supply the salient facts so that the analysis does not depend on obscure history: before the 1970s the condition was considered vanishingly rare; within about a decade, diagnoses reached the thousands; patients, mostly women reporting histories of severe childhood abuse, presented with ever-larger numbers of alter personalities; the diagnosis was amplified by popular accounts and by therapists who used hypnosis and actively elicited alters; and by the 1990s professional opinion had turned sharply skeptical, the criteria were tightened, the condition was renamed dissociative identity disorder, and the epidemic subsided.

Use the theory to illuminate the case and the case to test the theory. (1) Map the loop with precision: who is classifying, who is classified, how the classified respond, and how the classification then bends. (2) Say what the looping-effect analysis explains and what it leaves unexplained — in particular, whether it accounts for the runaway multiplication of alters, the spread of diagnoses across clinicians, and the eventual collapse, or whether those call for such mechanisms as suggestion, therapist-induced effects, shifting cultural scripts about trauma, and professional incentives. (3) Distinguish the looping effect from at least two less philosophically loaded alternatives, such as a self-fulfilling prophecy and an ordinary diagnostic fad, and argue whether the episode shows Hacking's distinctive mechanism doing explanatory work beyond those alternatives. (4) Render a verdict: does the episode confirm the theory, expose a gap or an ambiguity in it, or show that it is too permissive to earn its keep? Finally, tell me what would change your mind about that verdict — which consideration about this episode, or which general argument about human kinds, would most likely lead you to reverse or substantially revise your assessment, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
