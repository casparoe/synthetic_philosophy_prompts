For an upper-level seminar in social choice theory (about 15 students, one 80-minute session), design a classroom activity that makes participants feel the force of the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem rather than simply hearing it stated. The activity should have students attempt to design a voting rule for a student-government election with at least three candidates, under two apparent requirements given at the outset: truthful preference-reporting should never be penalized (no voter can get a better outcome by misreporting their rankings), and every candidate should be able to win under some distribution of votes. The rules and incentives should be set up so that the group keeps building rules that pass their own test cases, only to be shown a profile where someone has a profitable misreport. Include the exact procedure, roles (designers, voters, a saboteur who hunts for manipulation), a small no-real-money payoff scheme, a timeline, and a debrief that maps the breakdown onto the theorem's three conditions. The debrief should also say what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run and how to handle it, and should be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce—in particular, the theorem's universal quantification over all possible preference profiles, which the activity samples only sparsely.
