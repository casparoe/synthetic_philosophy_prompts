In Federalist No. 39, Madison writes:

"If we resort, for a criterion, to the different principles on which different forms of Government are established, we may define a republic to be, or at least may bestow that name on, a Government which derives all its powers directly or indirectly from the great body of the People, and is administered by persons holding their offices during pleasure, for a limited period, or during good behavior."

Now consider a concrete feature of the U.S. constitutional order that Madison himself goes on to analyze in that paper. The House of Representatives is apportioned by population; the Senate originally represented the states as states, with equal suffrage regardless of population and senators chosen by state legislatures; the Constitution was ratified by conventions in the several states rather than by one aggregate national vote; and amendments require a supermajority of states. In light of the passage above, what view of the "great body of the People" does this institutional arrangement presuppose? That is, does the Constitution treat the relevant political community as one national people whose will is aggregated at the level of individual citizens, as a plurality of state peoples whose will must be aggregated at the corporate level, or as some third thing? Where does Madison's definition of "republic" commit him on this question, and where is it deliberately underdetermined? And what would have to change in the definition if one held that the Constitution's mixed mode of popular agency was not merely a compromise among factions but the correct account of democratic legitimacy under a federal system?
