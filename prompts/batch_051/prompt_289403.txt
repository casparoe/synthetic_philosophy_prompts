I've been arguing with a friend about Avicenna's flying man — the thought experiment where a person is created out of nowhere as a fully formed adult, floating in empty air, eyes covered, limbs held apart so nothing touches anything else, with no functioning senses at all. Avicenna's point is that this person would still affirm that he exists, and that this is supposed to show the soul's awareness of itself doesn't run through the senses or the body. My friend keeps saying that's just a medieval intuition pump, and that the embodied-cognition crowd treats the reverse as obvious: the felt sense of being a particular "I" is assembled out of interoception, motor predictions, and body maps, and if you genuinely removed all of that, there would be no self left to notice itself.

I want to see whether this disagreement has any empirical bite, because right now it just feels like two metaphors glaring at each other. So here's the claim I'd like help testing: there is a core self-awareness that is not constructed out of bodily or sensory signals. Design the best test you can for that claim — who do you study, what do you manipulate or measure, and what result would count for and against it? Then say what a clean positive result would actually establish, what a clean negative result would actually establish, and what the test could never settle even in principle. It's ok if the test only partially operationalizes the claim; just be explicit about which part is left on the table.
