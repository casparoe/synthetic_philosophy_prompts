Organizations of many kinds — firms, governments, universities — routinely pour additional money into a project that is over budget, behind schedule, and failing by its own early indicators, and one of the reasons they give is that too much has already been spent to stop now. Individuals do something similar: they finish a book they dislike, complete a degree they have stopped valuing, or renew a membership they no longer use because they have already invested in it. Standard decision theory calls this the sunk cost fallacy and says past costs should be ignored; only prospective costs and benefits count. What theory of practical rationality do these honoring practices presuppose? In particular, do they strictly presuppose that past expenditure itself is a reason for future action, or are they compatible with forward-looking rationality through other mechanisms — reputation and credibility, switching costs, the information that sunk costs provide, the independent value of keeping commitments, or the value of maintaining a coherent self-narrative? Is the practice internally consistent given that the same institutions also praise cutting losses? And what would have to change in institutional budgeting and in ordinary practical guidance if the implicit theory were replaced by a strictly forward-looking, expected-value view?
