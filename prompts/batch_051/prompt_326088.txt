Here's something I keep going back and forth on. Dennett's heterophenomenology is the method where consciousness science collects subjects' first-person reports and treats them as descriptions of a private 'heterophenomenological world' (the world as it seems to the subject), while staying strictly neutral about whether the reported items — qualia, mental images, a stream of experience — exist. The neutrality is the whole point; it's supposed to let you study consciousness without smuggling in a Cartesian theater.

Now LLMs are producing fluent, introspective reports of what it's like to be them (or confidently denying any inner life), and people have started taking those as data about machine consciousness. At first glance the method should just apply: any system that emits reports gets a heterophenomenological world reconstructed from them, and we bracket whether that world is real. But the method was built for subjects with a unified first-person point of view, even if that point of view is officially just a theorist's fiction. An LLM's 'report' isn't emitted from a point of view at all; its a next-token prediction over what a textually plausible subject would say.

So: does heterophenomenology survive this development? Which premise is actually under pressure — the subject assumption, the fiction move, or the neutrality itself — and can the method be restated so it still does useful work for the science of consciousness when the 'subject' is an LLM? Or does applying it here quietly start positing a subject the way the method was designed not to posit a Cartesian theater?
