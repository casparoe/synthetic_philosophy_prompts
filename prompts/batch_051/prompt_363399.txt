I'm doing a module on social choice this term and we've just done judgement aggregation, which has leaked into my life in a way that might be over-applied. I'm secretary of my college's student society, and we vote on lots of linked motions, some of which are premises for later conclusions. Since learning about the discursive dilemma (three judges each with fully consistent views, yet a majority accepts premise one, a majority accepts premise two, and a majority rejects the conclusion that follows from both), I've made a rule for myself at meetings: whenever the majority accepts reasons that jointly imply a conclusion they then vote against, I treat the society as having no coherent position and I write the minutes that way. My flatmate says the bottom-line vote is the decision and the reasons are just window dressing. I think a decision whose supporting reasons can't be aggregated cleanly is one we didn't actually make, or at least can't claim to hold as a view. Is my rule defensible, or am I smuggling a textbook paradox into meetings to undermine outcomes I would've voted against anyway? If it's partly right, what's the defensible version of it, and when should a group be allowed to have messy or inconsistent reasons without losing the right to a clear decision? Could you answer in American English?
