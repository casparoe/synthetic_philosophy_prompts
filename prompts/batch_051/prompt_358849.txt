I am a PhD student in the history and philosophy of science, working on the reception and revision of long-run predictions about resource limits, and I keep noticing a pattern for which I have no precise vocabulary. The pattern runs as follows. A theorist identifies a genuine causal mechanism—say, the pressure of a human population that tends to expand geometrically against a food supply that improves only arithmetically—and draws a prediction with a determinate near-term horizon: within a generation or two, scarcity will become acute, prices will rise, or population will be checked. The deadline arrives and the predicted crisis either does not occur or appears in such an attenuated form as to be unrecognizable, usually because some real and often unanticipated development—new crops, trade, fertilizer, a falling birth rate—relaxes the constraint. Defenders of the theory do not then abandon the mechanism. Instead, they treat the failed timing as a discovery about the timescale: the theory was not wrong but early, they say; the mechanism remains in force, the model's clock simply ran too fast, and the reckoning has only been postponed. They then issue a revised horizon, and when that one passes, the same move is made again, sometimes several times across two centuries.

I want to be clear about what I am asking, because I am not after the familiar charge that such theories are unfalsifiable or that their proponents simply move the goalposts. The delaying factors are usually real and causal rather than imaginary epicycles; the underlying mechanism does not seem to be disconfirmed so much as shown to operate over a longer or more elastic timescale than the original formulation allowed. Nor is the prediction vague, since each version comes with a concrete mechanism and a concrete date—only the date is revised, while the mechanism is declared to have been vindicated by the very delay. Is there an established name in philosophy of science for this recurring configuration, in which a determinate prediction survives repeated failure by temporal rescaling rather than by modifying its causal core? I would welcome either answer: a precise term if one exists, or a coined term if it does not. But I am less interested in the label than in the carving—what exactly distinguishes this pattern from an ad hoc auxiliary hypothesis, from ordinary ceteris paribus protection, and from a degenerating research programme, and under what conditions, if any, the move from wrong to early is epistemically legitimate.
