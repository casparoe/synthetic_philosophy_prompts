I am a PhD student in philosophical logic, and I would like you to assess an evaluative policy I have adopted in my work on the Liar paradox and theories of truth. I have stopped counting any proposed solution as an actual solution unless it comes with an explicit story about why its own machinery does not simply generate a revenge paradox. A gap theory that says the Liar sentence is neither true nor false, for example, seems to me to have solved nothing until it explains why the sentence 'this sentence is not true' does not recreate the problem one level up, and I apply the same demand to hierarchical and contextualist accounts. My working rule is: no revenge story, no solution. In practice this leads me to dismiss nearly everything in the literature, and I have begun to suspect that the rule may be less a method than a way of protecting a prior conviction that the paradox is still unsolved. Is this a defensible epistemic habit for appraising theories of truth, or is it too strong? Is there a version of the policy that keeps its filtering value without the blanket dismissiveness, for example treating a revenge-free treatment as one desideratum rather than a precondition for serious consideration? And how could I tell from the inside whether I am applying the right standard or merely rationalizing a bias? Please answer in American English.
