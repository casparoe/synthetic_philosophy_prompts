Kardashev's type I/II/III scale sorts civilizations by how much energy they can command, and the usual rationale is that everything a civilization might want to do—build, compute, move matter—costs energy, so energy use tracks how advanced it is. Barrow's alternative is that advancement could mean manipulating smaller and smaller scales rather than harvesting ever more energy. But in both stories energy use still tends to climb along with capability. Here's the thing I keep going back and forth on. Suppose a civilization is far along in Barrow's sense (it engineers at the molecular or quantum scale) but its total energy use is flat or even dropping, because it runs on reversible computing and near-lossless manufacturing that hand most of the energy back instead of dumping it as waste heat. Does the argument behind Kardashev's scale survive that, does it need a repair, or is it dead as a measure of advancement? I'm asking about the underlying claim that advancement tracks energy, not the specific I/II/III cutoffs. Also, tell me which premise is actually taking the hit, and what would change your mind—where in your answer are you least sure you haven't just replaced one flawed proxy with another.
