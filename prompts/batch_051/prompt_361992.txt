I recently had a long argument with a friend about Bas van Fraassen's Reflection principle, and I came away unsure how much of his version of the idea I should accept. He summarized it roughly as follows: the principle says that your current degree of belief in any proposition must equal the degree of belief you expect yourself to have in the future. So if you think you will be more confident in a claim once you have seen some evidence, you are being incoherent now and should raise your confidence immediately. The principle therefore makes your future self an authority: it requires you to trust what you will later believe, because your future self knows at least as much as you do. He added that the principle is obviously refuted by cases in which you know you will later be drunk, manipulated, or suffering from memory loss, since in those cases it would plainly be foolish to adopt the belief you foresee having. I suspect this summary is partly right and partly unfair, but I am not confident about where the line falls. Which parts of his account are accurate, which are overstatements or conflations, and does the objection from anticipated irrationality refute Reflection, or does it only force a restriction in the principle's scope?
