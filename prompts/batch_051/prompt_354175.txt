In my seminar on judgment and decision-making, I sat next to two classmates who began arguing before the lecture and asked me to referee; I put them off, but I have been turning the exchange over since. They were arguing about a mutual friend, Ravi, a first-year master's student in our department whose paper is under review at the most selective conference in our field. Elena argued that Ravi should be braced for rejection: among first-year master's students from our program who have submitted to that venue, only about one in twelve has ever been accepted. Marcus replied that this was a crude base rate that lumps together weak and strong drafts, and that it ignores everything Ravi actually knows about his own case.

Elena said the prediction has to be dominated by the base rate unless Ravi has strong, validated evidence that he is not a typical member of the reference class. Most students whose papers are rejected also believed, with reasons, that their work was the exception; this is the planning fallacy. Marcus answered that the reference class is exactly what is in dispute: two senior faculty members read Ravi's draft closely and independently told him it was stronger than most first-year submissions they have seen. So the relevant class should not be "all first-year submissions from this program," but something like "submissions that two faculty members have vetted and rated unusually strong."

Elena replied that this is how the planning fallacy defends itself: a person builds a causal narrative about why his case differs, and undervalues the distribution of outcomes in comparable cases. The modest move is to start from a broad, easy-to-agree-on class and move away only for cues whose predictive validity is itself established from experience. Unsystematic faculty encouragement, she said, is an unreliable predictor and should not buy a large update.

Marcus said she had improved her position, but it still leads to trouble. Once the update is governed by how predictive each cue actually is, the question is no longer whether Ravi should be at 8 percent or 40 percent out of deference to some rule; it is an evidence-weighting problem that has to be done case by case. Bayes does not command modesty, he said; it commands weighting each piece of evidence by its known reliability, and that can still leave an honest person at a moderately optimistic number.

Elena answered that the forecasting literature is precisely evidence that humans cannot perform that weighting reliably without an external anchor. Whether the cue is faculty praise or a sense of having worked hard, people systematically over-weight inside information, so Ravi's honest number is exactly the thing we should distrust. Epistemic modesty is a reason to stick closer to the base rate, not to float away from it on the strength of an introspected update.

Marcus replied that modesty and accuracy come apart there. If the case-specific cues really are diagnostic, then correcting for an average human bias by forcing Ravi toward a base rate he may not be a random member of will make him less accurate. The planning fallacy is a population-level finding, he said; applying it to an individual requires the further judgment that this individual resembles the overconfident cases, and that is the very sort of case-specific judgment Elena says no one should trust.

Elena closed by asking whether Marcus's advice is for Ravi to do exactly the thing that in the aggregate produces overoptimism while believing himself the exception to the bias because his case is special. Marcus said no: his advice is to gather whatever data exist about how well similar cues have predicted acceptance among similar students, and condition on all of it; if the cue turns out not to move the acceptance rate, abandon it. The outside view, he said, is a debiasing technique, not a verdict about which reference class Ravi belongs to.

Please adjudicate this exchange turn by turn: say who is right about which specific point, identify any steps where one of them is missing something important, and then say which of the two, on the whole, has the better overall position.
