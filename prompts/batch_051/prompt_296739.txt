As part of my dissertation on trust, deception, and early moral education, I keep returning to a passage in Locke's *Some Thoughts Concerning Education* in which he argues that children should be kept as far as possible from falsehood, and that a lie, especially a deliberate one, is a kind of corruption that parents ought to correct with particular care. Locke seems to treat children's truthfulness as foundational: the child who becomes used to disregarding truth in small matters will later lack the internal restraint that honesty requires. Yet contemporary Western parenting often includes organized falsehoods—Santa, the tooth fairy, the Easter bunny—told by the very adults who also say that lying is wrong. I am trying to pin down the most interesting conceptual issue here, not merely an empirical question about whether these fictions cause measurable harm. Specifically: Is the Santa lie best understood as a lie in the ordinary sense, a fiction, a game, or a kind of make-believe that the child is eventually invited to co-author? And does Locke's principle, if taken seriously, require giving up such practices, or can it absorb the Santa case by treating it as something other than moral deception?

Please act as a moral philosopher who works on the ethics of deception and the philosophy of childhood. Begin by brainstorming three or four candidate ways of framing the tension, then settle on the one you find most illuminating. Give an example of the conceptual issue in concrete terms—perhaps a vignette of a child discovering the truth about Santa and asking why she was deceived—and show what that example reveals about the normative structure of white lies to children and the role of trust in parental authority.
