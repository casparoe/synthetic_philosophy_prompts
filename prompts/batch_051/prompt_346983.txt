I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy, and I'm building a taxonomy of the arguments that have been offered for the view that partiality toward particular others — one's child, one's partner, one's friends — is not simply an ineliminable bias that a sound moral theory should tolerate and then contain, but a legitimate, perhaps even required, feature of moral life. As I read the literature, the standard arguments are as follows:

1. The integrity argument. A life considered from the outside, in which the agent's deepest commitments count only as so much data for an impartial calculation, is alien to that agent. Since a morality that demanded such a standpoint would alienate agents from the ground projects around which their lives are organized, impartialism cannot be the whole of morality.

2. The constitutive-attitude argument. Partial concern is not an extraneous pressure that love and friendship place on an otherwise impartial reasoner; it is internal to what it is to stand in those relationships. A parent who felt the need to establish by impartial reasoning that saving their own child rather than a stranger was permissible would be holding the relationship at the wrong distance, and the need itself would show not that the parent is too partial but that something had gone wrong.

3. The special-obligations argument. Parenthood, friendship, and promising generate deontic reasons of partiality that need not be derived from impartial considerations. These are as basic as the reasons of beneficence, and a theory that declined to register them would not be more impartial but simply incomplete.

4. The care-ethics argument. Impartial deliberation abstracts away from the situated, responsive attention toward a particular other that is a distinct form of moral thought. Treating impartial justice as the only fundamental moral orientation therefore demotes care — and the partiality internal to it — without having shown it to be defective.

5. The epistemic division-of-labor argument. A person is far better informed about the needs of those near them than about distant strangers. A scheme in which each person directs finite attention and resources primarily to a small circle therefore serves even the impartial good better than a scheme in which each tries, badly, to act as a microcosm of the impartial standpoint.

6. The prerogative argument. Even strongly impartial theories typically grant each person a protected space in which their own projects may count for more than the impersonal good. Once that space is granted, an analogous protected space for concern for one's intimates can hardly be refused; keeping the one while denying the other looks arbitrary.

7. The psychological-impossibility argument. No human being can sustain equal concern for everyone, and a morality that demanded the abolition of partiality would demand a psychology we do not have. A plausible morality must take the actual fabric of human motivation as a constraint rather than a defect to be engineered away.

I believe this captures the standard kinds of argument, at least at the level of their basic structure. What I need is a further argument for the legitimacy or value of partiality that is not on this list and is not a restatement, special case, or combination of items on it. In particular, it should not appeal to integrity or the personal point of view, to the constitutive role of partiality in intimate relationships, to irreducible special obligations, to care or situated responsiveness, to superior local knowledge, to a general agent-relative prerogative, or to the psychological impossibility of impartiality. Take the task as a construction, not a survey: if you think the only available arguments draw on one of those families, say so explicitly and explain why. Otherwise, present the new argument with its premises made explicit, state what distinguishes its structure from each of the seven above, and say how an impartialist critic would most plausibly attack it.
