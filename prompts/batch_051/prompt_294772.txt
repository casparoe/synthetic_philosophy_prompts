Two philosophers have spent a term arguing past each other about causation. The first holds a regularity view: a causal relation is exhausted by regular succession — events of type c are constantly conjoined with events of type e, with c contiguous to e and prior to it — and what gets called necessary connection is a habit or projection of the mind rather than something in the objects. The second holds a skeptical-realist view, often called the New Hume in the interpretative literature: necessity is indeed never observed, but that is a point about our cognitive access rather than about the world; there may be hidden powers or necessary connections whose observable signature is the very regularity the first side treats as the whole story. Treat these as living philosophical positions rather than as a dispute about which reading better captures Hume's text, and let nothing in the deliverable rest on a claim about which side has Hume's authority.

Draft the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between these two parties would produce. It must include: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims both sides can sign, written so that neither would charge the other with smuggling in a conclusion — covering at least the observed data, the predictive and technological success of causal reasoning, what is and is not observed when one event is called the cause of another, and what any theory of causation must explain; (2) a disagreements table in which each side's position is formulated in wording that side itself would accept, with the other side on record that the wording is not a strawman; (3) the cruxes — the most finely-grained sub-questions such that settling one would force at least one side to revise a substantive commitment; and (4) for each crux, at least one concrete case, intervention, or argument that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction registered in advance; the cases may be invented but must be described precisely enough that both sides accept the case description as possible. Throughout, keep the semantic project of analyzing causal statements, the metaphysical project of saying what causation is, and the epistemic project of saying what we can know distinct, and say explicitly which disagreements survive once these projects are separated. Conclude by identifying any part of the dispute that is merely verbal — wherever 'necessity', 'power', or 'cause' is being used differently by the two sides — and state what would dissolve and what would remain after the verbal issue is removed.
