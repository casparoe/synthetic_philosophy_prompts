Last night a friend and I argued far too long about a vote I have to cast next week, and neither of us persuaded the other. I want it set down more carefully than we managed.

I am a trustee of a small family foundation whose trust instrument requires that it be administered "in a spirit of frugality." For decades the board has taken this to mean minimal overhead: shared office space, used furniture, no paid fundraisers. Our grantmaking, meanwhile, promotes frugality as a way of life — savings clubs, minor-repair and reuse workshops, debt counselling. At next week's meeting we will decide whether to spend about one-sixth of next year's spending on a development director and one expensive donor event, on the projection that this would roughly double our distributable funds within three years. Half the board says the proposal simply violates the duty to be frugal: you cannot spend lavishly, even once, in the service of a virtue whose content is not spending lavishly. The other half says this confuses practising the virtue with causing it; the foundation's purpose is to promote frugality, and a board that leaves extra money on the table for the sake of symbolic asceticism is serving its own self-image, not its mission.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the meeting. It should recommend approval, rejection, or approval with conditions; give reasons a skeptical trustee could act on; take the strongest point on the losing side seriously; and say how much work the trust language actually does. Throughout, separate what is settled about our institutional practice from what is genuinely contested between a deontological reading of frugality (be frugal) and a consequentialist reading (promote it), and do not present either reading as if it were the consensus.
