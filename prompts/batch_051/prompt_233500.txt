After a career in behavioral ecology, much of it spent building models of how animals decide when to keep searching and when to settle, I have been reading the philosophical literature on bounded rationality. I keep returning to an argument I have seen hinted at but rarely laid out step by step, and I suspect an equivocation in the middle — the word satisficing seems to be doing two different jobs — but I cannot make the distinction precise enough to say whether it matters.

(1) A computationally bounded agent cannot literally maximize over all feasible options; it must use some procedure that tells it when to stop searching and choose.
(2) The rational way to choose that procedure is to treat deliberation itself as a decision problem: each additional step of search has a cost, and its expected benefit is the chance of finding a better option times the expected size of the improvement; one should search one more step only while the expected benefit exceeds the cost.
(3) In the standard model of such a problem, the optimal policy is a reservation rule: keep searching until an option meets or exceeds a threshold, then take it.
(4) But a rule of that shape — set an acceptable level, take the first option that meets it, then stop — is what satisficing is.
(5) Therefore satisficing is not a deviation from expected-utility rationality; once deliberation itself is costed as an action, satisficing is the optimal policy for a resource-bounded expected-utility maximizer.

Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, on what reading of the terms, and what is the strongest repaired version that still delivers the conclusion? In particular, I would ask that you separate what is settled about optimal stopping and search theory from what is genuinely contested in interpreting satisficing, and not present a contested view as the consensus.
