While reporting on hinge epistemology — the view, associated with Wittgenstein's later work, that some certainties function not as empirical beliefs that evidence could overturn but as the fixed hinges on which any practice of asking for and giving evidence turns — I keep running into an asymmetry in how we treat radical doubt. In a lecture hall, a speaker who doubts that the floor will hold, because the whole scene could be a simulation or a deceiver's doing, is treated as doing serious philosophical work. In a hospital, a cockpit, or a jury room, a person who let the same doubt gain any purchase over an instrument reading, a diagnosis, or an eyewitness account would be treated as unfit. The seminar doubt is not merely unanswered; it is deliberately made immune to evidence, since any engineer's report could itself be part of the simulation, whereas the practical doubt is exactly the kind that a cross-check settles. Is there a principle that actually justifies treating these two as different, or is our respect for radical skepticism a ceremonial exemption we grant only while nothing is at stake? I want the strongest defense of the asymmetry you can build: identify the feature that does the work, state the rule it supports, test the rule against the hardest nearby case you can construct, and then say whether the asymmetry should be kept, narrowed, or abandoned.
