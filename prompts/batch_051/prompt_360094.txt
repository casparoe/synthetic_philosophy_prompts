I am drafting a short essay for a workshop on philosophy of science and machine learning, and I would like detailed comments on the argument before I expand it. The essay's working question is: in anomaly detection, when should two anomalies be counted as produced by the same underlying mechanism rather than as two distinct failure modes? Below are my notes. Please give me a point-by-point critique covering: (1) whether the proposed criterion is sufficient and necessary, given the counterexamples I flag; (2) whether the addition of a generative-path condition solves the problem or merely pushes it back a step; and (3) whether the overall idea is interesting and correctly framed, or whether it collapses into a familiar debate about variable choice or model granularity. Be concrete: if any step does not follow, say which one and why, and do not simply summarize the notes.

My starting intuition is that anomaly detection is a covert taxonomy of mechanisms. When a model flags two observations as anomalous, the downstream question is usually whether they should be handled by one response or two. I want to defend the view that sameness of mechanism among anomalies is not settled by the similarity of the anomalous observations themselves, nor by whether they are downstream of the same model component; it is settled by whether they share a counterfactual signature. Specifically, two anomalies A and B count as produced by the same mechanism just in case (i) there is a class of interventions that, when applied to the system's normal operating conditions, systematically removes or attenuates both A and B together, and (ii) the interventions that remove A without touching B are, across the relevant range of contexts, rare or unstable. The motivating picture is that a mechanism is individuated by what it would take to switch it off, not by what it outputs.

First counterexample, which I think the criterion handles well: two anomalies can be observationally confounded through a common buffer. Suppose a factory's anomaly rate shifts because a single sensor ages; the sensor makes both temperature and vibration readings look anomalous. Here the interventions that remove the anomalies are not two but one, namely replacing the sensor, so the criterion correctly lumps them. This is a feature of the view.

Second counterexample, which worries me: two genuinely distinct mechanisms can be yoked together by a common regulating variable so that they toggle in tandem. Consider two failure modes that the system actively compensates for. As long as the compensation budget is unspent, neither manifests; once the shared budget is exhausted, both manifest. In that case, interventions aimed at one mechanism will often fail to remove it alone, because the regulator reallocates or unmasks the other. The criterion would then count them as the same mechanism, when arguably they are distinct mechanisms sharing a resource.

My intended fix is to add a generative-path condition: A and B are the same mechanism only if the causal chain from the target of the intervention to A and the causal chain from the target of the intervention to B pass through the same first branch point in the system's normal causal graph. If the paths diverge before converging on a shared budget or buffer, the mechanisms are distinct even when they toggle together. But this seems to invite a circularity objection: the notions of a branch point and of the same path already appear to presuppose a prior individuation of mechanisms. I want to answer that objection by saying that the causal graph can be fixed at a level of description given by the system's maintenance and control practices rather than by the mechanisms under investigation: a branch point is wherever a repair intervention or a control policy can act locally without affecting upstream variables. Mechanistic sameness would then be relative to a practice, not absolute. But I worry that this gives up too much and makes the criterion unpalatably conventionalist.

Do the notes as they stand make this dilemma forced, or is there a way to avoid conventionalism without circularity? And are there further counterexamples that would push the view in a more productive direction?
