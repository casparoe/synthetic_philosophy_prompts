Ok so in my political theory reading group we keep circling the boundary problem: who should count as part of the demos, the people who's interests and voices a democracy has to answer to? One of the guys keeps shutting the conversation down with the same analogy. He says a country is basically a private club. The people already in it built something together — institutions, trust, a way of doing things — and nobody has a right to join a club they didn't help build. A book club gets to set its own admission rules, reject whoever it wants, and doesn't owe a seat to everyone who would benefit from showing up. So, he says, existing citizens get to decide who crosses the border and who eventually gets a vote, and restrictive immigration and naturalization policies are just the club exercising the same discretion any club has. Outsiders who complain are like people who weren't invited to a poker night but still feel entitled to a chair.

I can feel that this is doing something slippery, but I can't pin down exactly where it runs out, because he's not obviously wrong that groups get to have membership rules. I want a careful audit of how far the club analogy actually carries for democratic boundaries. Give me the explicit correspondence table — what fills the roles of the clubhouse or territory, the membership committee, the dues, the expulsion rules, the shared purpose, and the fact that new members change the character of the group. Then walk me through the places where the mapping breaks.

I especially want to know which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion he's drawing (that existing citizens may be highly restrictive without having to justify themselves to outsiders) and which are just differences that don't threaten the argument. Does the restrictive conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or is the analogy carrying the whole weight? If a club is just the wrong source image, is there a better analogy for democratic boundaries, or is this a spot where analogies mislead more than they help?
