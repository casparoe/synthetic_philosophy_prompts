I got called for jury duty a few weeks back and I cant stop turning over one case in my head, even though we never reached a verdict. It was a civil suit, not a criminal one. A cyclist got sideswiped by a plain white delivery van that took off, and he sued the company that operates most of the vans around there. Nobody got the plate, and the cyclist couldnt say whether there was a logo. The key evidence was that the city's traffic sensors had logged every commercial van that went through that intersection in the half-hour around the crash, and 85 percent of the plain white ones belonged to this one company. The judge told us the plaintiff only had to prove it was more likely than not that the van belonged to the company.

The company's lawyer kept saying that a percentage is not proof about this particular van, and it would be wrong to write the company's name on the verdict form just because most vans in the area happen to be theirs. The plaintiff's lawyer kept saying the law literally asks for more-likely-than-not, which is just a probability, and 85 percent clears that by a lot. One juror asked whether that number would also be enough for proof beyond a reasonable doubt, which nobody could answer. What bugged me even more is that the exact number swung around a lot depending on whether you counted vans citywide, in that neighborhood, or just on that stretch of road during that window, so it felt like the odds were doing an awful lot of work without anyone being able to say which odds were the right ones. I ended up flip-flopping the whole time.

Who has the better argument here? Is there something in principle wrong with letting evidence like this decide a case even when the number is high, or would a high enough number have to count as proof? And does it change things if the standard is proof beyond a reasonable doubt instead of more likely than not? Oh, and could you write the answer in British English? A friend of mine in London wants to read it too.
