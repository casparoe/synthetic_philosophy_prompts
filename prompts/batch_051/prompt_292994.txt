In my biology study group, two friends got into an argument about the handicap principle, and when they asked me to referee I found I couldn't tell where the real disagreement was. Could you go through their exchange and judge each move?

Alex began with the standard peacock case. She said the tail is an honest signal of quality because it is costly: only a genuinely healthy male can afford to grow it and carry it, so females can trust what it seems to advertise. Jordan pushed back that mere cost cannot be doing the work. If a low-quality male could grow and carry the same tail at the same cost as a high-quality male, he would do so whenever the mating benefit exceeded that cost, and the tail would stop carrying information. What makes the tail honest, Jordan said, is not that it is expensive in general, but that it is a worse deal for low-quality males—the cost is differential.

Alex replied that Jordan had simply restated her point: saying the cost is differential is just a more formal way of saying only high-quality males can afford the tail. Jordan answered that the two are not equivalent. A signal could be expensive for everyone and still be produced by everyone if the benefit were large enough; conversely, a signal could be cheap and still be honest if high-quality males gained more from sending it than low-quality males did. What matters, he said, is a difference in costs or benefits between honest and dishonest signalers, not cost as such.

Then Alex shifted to a different case. In many frogs, she said, the pitch of the call is tied to body size, so a small male cannot fake the low call of a large male no matter how much it would gain; the signal is an unfakeable index, not a cost-driven handicap. Jordan said that was exactly his point—honesty can come from several sources, so the loose slogan that costly signals are honest is misleading. But Alex said this makes things worse, not better: if honesty can be achieved without cost, then the sheer costliness of the peacock's tail does not by itself tell us what information it carries; at most it shows the trait is hard to grow.

They then both claimed the other had conceded. Please go through the exchange and say who is right about which specific claims, where—if anywhere—the genuine disagreement lies, and what both of them are missing.
