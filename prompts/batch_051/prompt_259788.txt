I'm a philosophy professor, and two of my colleagues have been going at each other for the better part of a year about whether it's ever literally true to say that a large language model believes something. They've finally agreed to do an adversarial collaboration, and I got roped into helping draft the document. One of them — call her the stance-realist — holds that if treating the model as having beliefs and desires predicts and explains its behavior better than any rival strategy, and the stance keeps paying rent in new cases, then the model has beliefs in the only sense that matters, whatever is going on inside. The other — call him the fictionalist — thinks this is a category mistake: a next-token predictor has no beliefs at all, because its outputs aren't about anything for the system itself, and saying it "believes" something is at best a useful fiction and at worst a dangerous anthropomorphism. To be clear, they're not arguing about whether the system is conscious; both are happy to leave that open. The fight is strictly about whether belief attribution to such systems is literally correct or merely useful.

Write the joint statement these two could sign. Start with a numbered "agreed claims" section that is substantive, not just terminological. Then give a disagreements table: for each residual disagreement, a neutral statement of the exact point at issue, a phrasing each side would accept for its own position, and a crux — a sub-question such that answering it would move at least one of them. For each crux, give a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides agree bears on it, and say what each would predict. I suspect one crux will be whether the stance has to earn its keep on genuinely novel predictions rather than just offering a post hoc redescription, and another will be whether training a model to predict the next token at sufficient scale forces it to build internal representations with genuine aboutness — but you're welcome to find better ones. After the statement, add a short note saying how confident you are in the formulations you've offered and the single point your least sure of.
