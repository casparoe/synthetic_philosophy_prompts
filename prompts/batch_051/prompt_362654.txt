I'm a PhD student in political theory and I keep going back and forth on something in the dirty hands literature. Treat this as your proper area of expertise: you're a professional moral and political philosopher. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming candidate principles before settling on one.

Here's the asymmetry that's bothering me. A PM authorises an extrajudicial killing (or lets a suspect be tortured) to stop a genuinely imminent attack. Most people accept the decision, but they also insist the PM has done something wrong, has blood on their hands, and shouldn't just carry on as if nothing happened. Yet if a surgeon performs a non-consensual amputation to save an unconscious patient, we may call it tragic or a morally difficult choice, but we don't normally say the surgeon's hands are dirty in anything like the same way. And if a private citizen breaks someone's arm to stop them setting off a bomb, we don't usually moralise it in the dirty-hands register either.

So my question is: is there a principled reason why occupying political office, or wielding political power, creates or deepens the moral stain? Or is that just a psychological reaction to public power that we'd be better off dropping? Give me the strongest justification you can find for treating the politician's stain as morally different, then test it against the surgeon and the private citizen cases. If the justification fails, say whether you'd keep the asymmetry anyway or reject the whole idea that the politician's case is special.
