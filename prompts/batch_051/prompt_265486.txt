My stats reading group has been going in circles for a month on the replication crisis, and I've been tasked with forcing a truce document out of the two camps who actually disagree. One is a hardline frequentist: their view is that null hypothesis significance testing is basically sound, that the replication crisis mostly came from researcher degrees of freedom, p-hacking, HARKing, small samples, and publication bias, and that the fix is preregistration, larger samples, stricter (or multiplicity-adjusted) thresholds, and better corrections for multiple comparisons. The other is a hardline Bayesian: their view is that the crisis is what you get when a field compresses continuous evidence into a dichotomous p < .05 decision, that many "significant" findings were never strong evidence for an alternative once you put a realistic prior over tiny effects, and that the real fix is reporting Bayes factors or posterior model probabilities instead of p-values, plus respecting the likelihood principle (including not treating optional stopping as a sin).

Draft the adversarial-collaboration statement these two should have written. I want it structured like this:

1. A numbered list of claims both camps can sign without watering them down into mush. Include at least one non-obvious agreement — something each side would have assumed the other wouldn't grant.

2. A section or table of the exact remaining disagreements, phrased so that each side would accept the wording of its own position but neither would call the other's wording a strawman. Be explicit about where they part ways on: what a p-value is evidence about, whether optional stopping is a problem, what counts as a successful replication, and whether the fix is mostly procedural or mostly a change in the model of evidence.

3. A list of cruxes — the sub-questions such that, if one got settled empirically or conceptually, at least one side would actually move. I suspect one crux is something like "does a prior that puts a lump of mass on zero effect and spreads the rest over small effects predict the observed replication rates better than the frequentist's selective-reporting story alone, holding the same data fixed?" and another might be about whether a p < .05 finding from an underpowered design carries much diagnostic information at all — but find your own and word them precisely.

4. For each crux, a concrete analysis, simulation, or dataset both sides would agree bears on it, plus each side's prediction written in advance (e.g., the frequentist predicts replication failures will track p-hacking indicators more than they track posterior odds; the Bayesian predicts the posterior odds of the original studies will predict replication even after conditioning on p-hacking indicators). Make the predictions sharp enough that the two sides would actually disagree about what the data would show.

Also flag any part of the fight you think is substantive versus mostly verbal (for instance, if you think "evidence" or "what a replication is" is doing different work on the two sides, say so), and say whether the two are closer than they think or further apart once its written down.

The last line of your answer should be a one-sentence summary of the deepest residual disagreement after the two sides have signed everything they can; start that line with "Summary:".
