I've been spending too much time on Bongard problems lately — those puzzles where you're shown six boxes on the left and six on the right, and you have to find the rule that separates the two sets: the lefts all satisfy some rule, the rights all don't. What's striking is how often you can get the rule from just twelve boxes, with no labels and no one telling you what kind of property to look for. And it's the same with kids and words: one or two examples of "dog" and they're basically off and running. Yet if a friend told me "my cousin started taking some herbal supplement and his migraines went away, and two people in his office had the same response," I'd shrug that of as a handful of anecdotes, basically worthless as evidence that the supplement works. So here's the thing that bugs me: what exactly is the asymmetry? Why is it fine — obviously fine — to induce a concept or rule from twelve carefully chosen examples, but wrong to infer a causal or frequency claim from a dozen cases of "took X, then got better"? Is it that concept learning happens inside a bounded hypothesis space, so the examples only have to pick out a rule rather than estimate a rate? Is it selection effects — the Bongard panels are guaranteed to be generated by a single clean rule, while reports of cures arrive already filtered? Or is it just that we trust few examples when our priors are strong and the cost of being wrong is low, and we call the same move "anecdotal" when the stakes are high? Some nearby cases seem to blur it: one taste of a mushroom that made you sick feels like plenty of evidence, and a doctor can sometimes identify a syndrome from a handful of patients. I want the answer to say whether this asymmetry is principled, or a useful inconsistency we live with — and to test whatever justification it gives against the mushroom case and the syndrome case, not just the easy examples.

When you answer: lead with your actual bottom line in the first sentence, no preamble, no restating the question. And please separate what's genuinely settled from what's contested among people who study induction and concept learning — don't present a contested position as the consensus.
