I've been reading scattered stuff on how archives are never neutral, and I want a dialogue that actually works through the disagreement instead of just gesturing at it. So: write a dialogue between two archivists at a county historical society who are wrangling over wether to actively collect the stories of people missing from their holdings. One of them is an old-school custodian: she thinks the archive's job is to keep the records people actually gave us, in their original order, and not to manufacture a record that suits present-day politics; the absences are themselves something a careful historian can study, and if we start adding oral histories and community documents we're becoming authors, not custodians. The other is more of an activist: he thinks appraisal and description always encoded somebody's power, the quiet in the collection isn't neutral but was produced by decisions about whose papers mattered, and a responsible archive has to go out and collect the voices that never got to leave records — even if that means the "collection" is less a neat original fonds and more of a constructed thing. Make them both smart, neither a strawman, and have them actually respond to each other's points rather than trading talking points. Ground it in a specific fight: the society holds a big run of records from a local cannery — ledgers, board minutes, a company photographer's prints — but the workers, mostly seasonal migrants, appear only as payroll entries. One suggests doing oral histories with descendants and putting them in the same collection; the other says that's mistaking a source for a story. Let the dialogue end somewhere uneasy rather than resolving neatly.
