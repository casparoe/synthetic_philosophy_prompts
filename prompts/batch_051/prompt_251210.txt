Write a dialogue between two philosophers of mind who genuinely disagree about whether an ant colony such as Aunt Hillary — the colony in Hofstadter's "Ant Fugue," whose individual ants follow simple local rules while the colony as a whole does strikingly intelligent-looking things — can literally be said to have a mind. Give each character a working label, such as the Colony Functionalist and the Unitary-Perspective Skeptic, and make them equally competent: neither should be a strawman, and each should respond to the other's actual arguments rather than delivering set speeches.

The Colony Functionalist should defend the claim that the colony's coordinated behavior — allocating foragers, choosing nest sites, regulating internal conditions, adjusting when circumstances change — displays enough information integration and goal-directedness to make mental ascription appropriate at the colony level, even though no individual ant represents the colony's aims. She should press the Skeptic on why the absence of a single internal "control room" is disqualifying, given that no individual neuron represents a person's intentions either. The Skeptic should defend the claim that the ant colony is at best a useful metaphor: he should argue that the colony lacks the right kind of unitary perspective, global workspace, and self-model, and that what looks like a decision is only stigmergy — pheromone trails and local interactions that reshape the environment so that subsequent local interactions happen to coordinate, with no single thing that remembers, anticipates, or decides.

The dialogue should test the hard cases, not the easy ones. The Skeptic should ask what the word "mind" is doing beyond "organized feedback system" on the Functionalist's criteria, and whether a whirlpool or a stock market would then count as minded. The Functionalist should ask what principled feature the colony is missing that a brain possesses, and whether the Skeptic's criterion simply presumes that only tightly coupled animal nervous systems can be minds. Midway, the Skeptic should introduce a new pressure test: suppose the ants are replaced one by one with tiny robots implementing the very same local rules, pheromone analogues, and environmental modifications. He should challenge the Functionalist either to accept that the resulting robot colony has a mind or to explain the relevant difference. The Functionalist should meet this challenge head-on, not dismiss it.

Do not force a resolution. The value of the dialogue lies in the distinctions, concessions, and arguments the two characters develop.
