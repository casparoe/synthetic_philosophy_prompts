I'm working through the foundations of a view about economic decision-making under uncertainty, and I want to know whether a classic argument survives a specific recent development. The classic argument, usually associated with Keynes, is roughly this: some of the most important economic judgments — the path of interest rates over decades, the likelihood of war or technological upheaval, the prospects of genuinely novel financial arrangements — concern matters for which there is no calculable numerical probability. Under this kind of true uncertainty, expected-utility reasoning breaks down, and agents fall back on convention, precaution, and animal spirits rather than optimizing over a probability distribution. The development I have in mind is that over the past few decades prediction markets, forecasting tournaments, and structured subjective-probability elicitation have produced apparently well-calibrated numerical probabilities for exactly this sort of long-range, non-repeatable event. I want an audit of the argument, not a survey of forecasting. Specifically: which premise does this development actually bear on; does it refute the Keynesian claim, or does it change the subject by supplying something weaker than the objective probabilities the argument says are unavailable; and is there a repaired version of the risk-versus-true-uncertainty distinction that remains defensible today — for example, one grounded in non-stationarity, ambiguity, or the difference between known probability models and open-ended novelty? Please answer as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
