I'm grading finals in my 20th-century philosophy course and I'd like a second opinion on one paper. The question I set was:

Karl Popper argues in The Poverty of Historicism that there cannot be laws of historical development in the sense the historicist wanted. (1) Explain what a law of historical development would have to be, and why Popper insists it must be distinguished from a mere trend. (2) Reconstruct Popper's argument from the growth of knowledge against the possibility of predicting the future course of history. (3) Explain how Popper would respond to a Marxist who claims that historical materialism has already discovered such laws, and assess whether Popper's argument succeeds.

One student wrote this (I havent cleaned it up):

Popper's target is historicism, the view that history is governed by laws of development, so that the main job of the social sciences is to discover these laws and use them to make long-range predictions about the future of society—predictions of the sort astronomers make about eclipses. He reads Marx, and Hegel before him, as historicists in this sense, holding that societies pass through a fixed sequence of stages. Popper's aim is to show that this project is impossible in principle.

The crucial distinction is between laws and trends. A law is a universal statement that holds without exception. A trend is only a report that something has moved in a certain direction up to now, and whether it continues depends on conditions that may change. So you cannot infer a law from a trend. A historicist who notices that industrial societies have become more bureaucratized and then extrapolates that tendency indefinitely is committing a straightforward inductive fallacy.

Popper's deeper argument concerns the growth of knowledge. Any prediction about future history would have to predict what people will know in the future, because knowledge changes how societies act. But we cannot predict future knowledge: if we could predict today what we will discover tomorrow, we would already possess that knowledge today. For Popper this is a logical impossibility, not a mere shortage of data. So no law of historical development can do what the historicist wants.

In fact, he draws the stronger conclusion that the social sciences cannot produce laws at all, and must confine themselves to recording trends. This is also why he thinks Marx fails: the very act of predicting the revolution alters the course of events—Popper calls this the Oedipus effect—so historical prophecy is always self-defeating.

I think Popper goes too far. There plainly are social regularities: a society without food production will face famine, and raising marginal tax rates tends to reduce reported income. So he must be wrong that no social laws or predictions are possible. Still, I accept his narrower conclusion that the far future of history cannot be predicted, because the growth of knowledge is bound to surprise us.
