I spent thirty years in a research lab before I retired, and since then I've been running a small philosophy discussion group — mostly former engineers and a couple of classicists — where the same argument keeps coming back: is doing something the hard way ever valuable in itself, or is effort just a cost you pay for the outcome? We've gotten nowhere with abstractions, so I want to build a 'which view of effort do you actually hold?' quiz for the group, the way internet personality quizzes are put together but actually diagnostic. Eight to ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, and a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto at least five positions: an intrinsic process-value view, a purely instrumental view, a view on which effort matters as evidence of character or commitment, a view on which effort is disvaluable in itself and should be minimized, and a view on which effort only matters when it's freely chosen or meaningful to the agent. The distinctions I'm trying to keep apart are process versus product, effort as a sign of something else versus effort as an ingredient in the achievement, and chosen versus imposed effort — I don't want the quiz to collapse those into each other.

Some seed cases you can use or replace: a mathematician who grinds for a decade to prove something versus a colleague who finds a two-page shortcut; a hiker who takes the steep ridge to the same summit a cable car reaches; a pianist who learns a piece through months of monotonous practice versus one who uses an app that makes it almost effortless; a scientist whose twenty-year project fails versus one who gets a lucky result in week one; an artist who hand-copies photographs painstakingly versus one who prints them. The questions should sort people by what they choose in cases, not by which slogan they endorse, so none of them should just ask 'do you think effort is valuable in itself?' I'd also like the scoring key to flag any two positions the quiz can't actually seperate, and if some answer combinations come out empty, say briefly whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just under-explored. Keep the questions answerable by someone who has never read a word of philosophy.
