I got into a long argument on a forum a while back about whether broad-spectrum insecticide use in row-crop agriculture deserves the same neglected-huge-welfare-problem treatment that people give to factory farming, and one thing that came out of it is that I started cataloging the distinct arguments for the claim that the direct effects of agricultural insecticides on the insects themselves are a serious moral problem. I'm deliberately bracketing the stuff about farmworker exposure, consumer residues, and knock-on harms to birds and aquatic life, because those are separate debates and they tend to swamp the question I actually care about. So far I've got five buckets:

1. The capacity or evidence argument: insects have nociceptor-like structures, show learned avoidance of noxious stimuli, adjust behavior after injury in ways that look like tradeoffs rather than fixed reflexes, and in some species the responses are dampened by analgesics. The best explanation of all this is an internal negative state at least functionally like pain, not robotic reflex.
2. The moral-uncertainty argument: you don't need to prove insect sentience. Even a modest credence (10%, maybe less) that insects have valenced experience, multiplied by the number of individuals a single spray season effects, gives an expected welfare cost large enough that continued spraying can't be the casual default.
3. The sheer-numbers argument, which I count as distinct from the uncertainty argument because it grants sentience and grants that each insect might matter almost nothing: even then the total welfare loss per year is plausibly comparable to or greater than all vertebrate welfare, so the per-insect-triviality move fails.
4. The anti-speciesism argument: the usual grounds for moral considerability are sentience or valenced experience, not size, fur, facial expressions, or genetic distance from humans. Excluding insects on the basis of the latter is exactly the kind of motivated bias we wouldn't accept if it were used against distant human groups or weird vertebrates.
5. The mode-of-death argument: many common insecticides don't kill quickly; they cause paralysis, desiccation, starvation, or disrupted molting that can go on for days. If pest control is justified at all, the harm is not just the fact of death but the prolonged dying, and broad-spectrum neurotoxins are among the worse ways to do it.

I've got the feeling I've saturated the space, but I want to be wrong. What's a sixth argument for treating the direct effects of insecticides on insects as a serious moral problem, that is not a restatement or special case of any of the five above? In particular, something that doesn't lean on their internal capacities, the uncertainty, the arithmetic, the speciesism charge, or the badness of the particular death. Then be honest: if I pushed on it, does the argument collapse into one of the five, or is it actually doing independent work?
