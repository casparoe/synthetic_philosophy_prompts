You are a professional philosopher with expertise in early modern ethics and political philosophy and a sharp eye for latent inconsistencies in sets of commitments. I have been trying to reconstruct Pascal's scattered remarks on the relation between justice and force into a position I can actually defend, and I need a consistency audit before I present it. Think carefully before you classify anything as a conflict; some of these commitments look opposed but are not. Start by mapping how the commitments bear on one another, then identify the genuine tensions, rank them by severity, and for each one say precisely what would have to be revised and what the least costly repair would be. Finish by telling me which single revision would restore the most coherence overall and what that revision would force me to rework elsewhere.

Here are the commitments:

(1) Unenforced justice is powerless. A standard of justice without organized coercive force behind it is not a social order but a private aspiration.

(2) Force is never self-certifying. Coercion is legitimate only if the arrangement it enforces is at least substantially just. Longevity, settled custom, majority endorsement, or sheer power cannot by themselves make force legitimate.

(3) The content of justice cannot be fixed by any neutral procedure. Every proposed basis for specifying what justice requires can be reasonably disputed by someone who does not already share it, so public appeals to true justice tend in practice to reduce to whatever interpretation of justice prevails among those with the influence to entrench it.

(4) Settled law coats itself in normativity. In a stable legal order that is not clearly monstrous, the fact that laws have long been accepted and relied upon gives me a standing reason to comply with them, even when I cannot derive their content from any independent account of justice.

(5) There is a floor below which deference stops. If an order engaged in mass enslavement, systematic torture, or massacre, its stability and legality would give me no reason whatsoever to defer. I would support resistance, and I would describe that resistance as resistance to injustice rather than as mere dislike of a custom.

(6) The effectiveness of an order exhausts the comparisons we can make. As between two stable legal orders, I can find no independent criterion of justice that could vindicate the less effective one as the more just. The only workable thing I mean when I call an order just is that it reliably wins compliance and stabilizes expectations.

I am genuinely uncertain which of these, if any, are driving the others into contradiction; if something has to go, I do not yet know which it is.
