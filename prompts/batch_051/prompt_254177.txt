I'm leading a discussion of Sense and Sensibility for a book club next month, and I keep swinging between two ways of framing the Dashwood sisters. The lazy reading is that Elinor is a kind of Stoic and Marianne a romantic, and that the novel is an argument that strong feeling is dangerous while emotional self-government wins. But I suspect that flattens both the novel and the actual philosophical positions. A careful Stoicism doesn't say the good life has no feelings; it says the passions are judgments that go wrong, and the goal is to root out false judgments while keeping the "good feelings" like joy or caution. And romanticism isn't just "more feeling is better"; it has a real thesis that intense emotion is a form of perception or honesty, and that a life without vivid feeling is thin. I want a reading of the novel through this lens, but one that goes both ways: where the novel really does track a Stoic-vs-romantic argument, where it resists the pairing (isn't Marianne's "sensibility" a period-specific cult of refined feeling, not romanticism exactly?), and whether the ending actually vindicates one side or just compromises.
