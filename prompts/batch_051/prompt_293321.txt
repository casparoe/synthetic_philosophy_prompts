I've been reading applied causal-inference papers (epidemiology, some economics), and there's a pattern I keep tripping over but don't have a word for. Two papers start from the same dataset and the same variables, but they draw the arrows between the variables differently. One has X causing Y. Another has Y causing X, or has X and Y both driven by an unmeasured common cause. The two diagrams sit equally well with the data — same variables, same pattern of which things are independent of which other things once you hold others fixed — so nothing in the data itself seperates them. Yet as soon as each paper gets to the 'if we were to set X to a fixed value' step, they make completely different predictions: one says Y would move, the other says Y would stay put; add a third variable and one paper can say the net effect of X on Y is positive while the other says there is no effect at all. And neither paper flags that the data didn't pick its arrows; each treats its own diagram as though it were just read off the facts.

I know this sits somewhere near 'underdetermination' in philosophy of science and 'observational equivalence' in econometrics, but I suspect the structural-causal-model people have a more specific idea for the class of graphs that the data can't distinguish even though they disagree about interventions. What is that idea called, if it has a settled name? How exactly does it differ from old-school observational equivalence in simultaneous-equation models and from philosophical underdetermination — where do they overlap and where do they come apart? And what does the existence of such a class tell me about what a structural causal model is actually assuming when it draws one set of arrows rather than another: what evidence or further assumptions are supposed to break the tie? If there isn't one clean name and people use several overlapping terms, don't just give me a term — carve the concepts, tell me which label goes with which edge, and flag the boundary cases where the labels start leaking.
