I've been trying to pin down what "coming to terms with the past" actually means, because people keep using it as if there's one real process a country either does or doesn't go through. It's clearly doing descriptive and evaluative work at the same time, and I want a working definition I can apply rather than a vibe. Here are the cases I keep going back and forth on. West Germany after the war: school visits to camps, the big Berlin Holocaust memorial, criminal trials, reparations, a broad consensus that the past is a warning. That's obviously a yes, but I can't tell which of those features are doing the real work. Japan: official apologies for wartime atrocities, but prime ministers still visiting Yasukuni Shrine and textbooks soft-pedalling the worst of it; the country has done a lot of the acknowledge-and-apologise moves without obviously producing the same thing Germany has. Turkey: near-total official denial of the Armenian genocide and a domestic taboo around the topic. That one feels like a clear no. The American South: a hundred-odd years of building Confederate monuments and teaching the Lost Cause, then a wave of removals and renaming, while a lot of the underlying historical self-image churns on. I'd want a definition that doesn't make the South just "Germany but earlier in the process" by stipulation. Give me a working definition that gets the clear cases right and gives a definite answer either way for the messy ones. I half-suspect the term tracks something like a society actually updating on its own history, rather than performing any particular ritual, but I don't want to bake that in unless the cases force it. Run the definition through all four and tell me where it surprises you or where you'd have to revise it.
