I have just inherited the casebook of a retiring arbitrator whose job was to divide windfalls between two branch offices of a firm, and my first task is to rule on a pending case in a way that is consistent with her precedents. In each case a payoff pair (x, y) lists the utility achieved by office 1 and office 2; the disagreement point is (0, 0); and the feasible payoff set is the convex hull of the corner points listed. Her three recorded rulings are:

1. Corners (0,0), (1,0), (0,1): she chose (0.5, 0.5).
2. Corners (0,0), (2,0), (0,4): she chose (1, 2).
3. Corners (0,0), (3,0), (0,2): she chose (1.5, 1).

The pending case has corners (0,0), (1,0), (1,1), (0,2). What should I rule if I want to be consistent with her precedents? I care less about the bare split than about the reasoning: which candidate principle is she most plausibly running — the Nash bargaining solution, the Kalai–Smorodinsky (equal proportional concessions) solution, the midpoint of the Pareto frontier, egalitarian equal payoffs, or utilitarian maximization of total payoff — and which of the earlier cases is the closest analog to the pending one? If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the ruling, say what principle I would be adopting with each live option, and specify the single further case that would most cleanly separate whichever candidate principles survive her record.
