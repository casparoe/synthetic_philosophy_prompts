I am a graduate student in philosophy of biology, and I have been relying on the etiological account of function in my own work because it seems to me to be the only account that legitimizes talk of malfunction. On that account, a heart is supposed to pump blood because hearts were selected for pumping blood, and that selection history is what makes it true that a heart that fails to pump blood is broken rather than merely different. But last semester I sat in on a molecular evolution seminar, and two kinds of cases unsettled me. The first is de novo gene birth: a stretch of previously noncoding sequence begins to be transcribed, its product happens to do something useful for the organism, and the sequence is fixed by selection. At its origin the gene has no history of having been selected for that effect, yet working biologists apparently have no trouble saying what it is for and would regard a mutant copy that no longer does that thing as malfunctioning. The second is synthetic biology: an engineered regulatory circuit has a function specified by its designers before it has ever been under selection. These cases make me worry that the etiological account, for all its advantages, is committed to saying they have no functions in the strict sense, or only proto-functions, and that repairing the account to accommodate them would quietly collapse it into the causal-role view it was meant to beat.

I would like a careful assessment of whether the etiological account survives this development. Please structure your response as follows. "The argument to update" (about 150 words): state the account's core claim and identify the specific premise that does the work in explaining malfunction. "Where the cases bite" (about 250 words): say whether de novo genes and designed-but-unselected synthetic systems pressure that premise, some other premise, or none, and distinguish what would be decisive from what would be merely awkward. "Repairs" (about 250 words): describe the most plausible repairs, such as forward-looking selection, treating design as a limiting case, or adding a notion of recent selection, and say for each whether it preserves the spirit of the account or turns it into one of its rivals. "My verdict" (about 150 words): tell me how much of the etiological account survives and whether the surviving version still explains malfunction.
