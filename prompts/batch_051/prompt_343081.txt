I am organizing a working group on invertebrate welfare and food policy, and we have reached an impasse over the scale argument as it applies to farmed insects. The practical question is whether the rapid expansion of insect farming for protein—black soldier fly larvae, yellow mealworms, and crickets, farmed in numbers on the order of trillions each year—should be governed by expected-value reasoning. The scale argument runs roughly as follows: even if the probability that any individual insect is sentient is fairly low, say between 1% and 10%, the enormous number of animals involved can make the expected quantity of morally relevant sentient life very large, so the practice deserves serious scrutiny even under deep uncertainty. One member of the group thinks the probability is low enough that the whole question is premature; I suspect it is not nearly that low, and that our disagreement is really about which sub-question is doing the work.

I would like you to give me your own credence, as a number with a decomposed derivation. The target proposition is this: at least one insect species farmed at industrial scale has valenced phenomenal states—states that feel like something to the insect, with a positive or negative quality—as opposed to mere nociceptive reflexes or unconscious information processing. Please decompose your credence into at least four sub-questions, for instance: the probability that some theory of consciousness under which insects could be sentient is correct (such as an integrated-information view with a permissive threshold, or a global-workspace or midbrain-homology account); the probability that at least one farmed species meets the theory's structural and functional conditions; the probability that the relevant states are valenced rather than affectless; and the probability that valence of this kind is the sort of thing that can legitimately be aggregated across trillions of individuals in the way the scale argument requires. For each term, give a number and one line of justification; if the terms should be conditional on one another rather than independent, state the conditionals explicitly. Then show how the terms combine into your overall number. After that, tell me how the scale argument itself should be handled: how one should combine a low per-individual probability of sentience with vast numbers, whether there is some probability low enough that the argument loses its force even at these populations, and which single term in your decomposition you are least confident about. Finally, identify the item of evidence or argument that would most move your overall credence, and say which term you think most plausibly separates the skeptical and non-skeptical positions in my working group.
