Hi, I'm a software person and I've been trying to figure out if a pattern I keep noticing has a name. It goes like this. A company is deciding whether to spend three extra months on safety testing before it ships a large AI system. The engineers mostly agree the testing would catch real failures, and pretty much everyone in the field would be better off if every lab did the same. But if this one company actually does it, a competitor ships sooner, grabs the users and revenue, and the careful company gets punished for doing the thing everyone said they wanted. I see the same shape with content moderation: a platform that spends more on keeping things civil loses people to a platform that doesn't bother, and then the careful platform either caves or slowly dies. The weird part is that nobody in these stories is unusually greedy or anti-safety. Each actor is responding sensibly to what the others will do. Caution is a cost you pay yourself, now, while the benefit is spread over everyone, later, so the structure selects against precisely the behaviour everyone claims to prefer. It's less like a tragedy of selfishness and more like a filter.

I know about race-to-the-bottom thinking and collective action problems, and this is clearly adjacent. But neither captures the thing that interests me: the bad outcome doesn't come from anyone's revealed preference for the bad outcome, and adding more well-meaning actors doesn't fix it; it just makes the one who holds out even more likely to lose. Does this specific configuration have a name of its own? If it does, how exactly does it differ from a plain race to the bottom — is it a special case, a cause of it, or something else? And if their isn't an established term, what would you call it, and where are the edges (does it still apply with only two actors, or when the cautious behaviour also has some private benefit)?
