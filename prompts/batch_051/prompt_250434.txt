In a seminar on the epistemology of markets we read this passage from F. A. Hayek's "The Use of Knowledge in Society":

The peculiar character of the problem of a rational economic order is determined precisely by the fact that the knowledge of the circumstances of which we must make use never exists in concentrated or integrated form but solely as the dispersed bits of incomplete and frequently contradictory knowledge which all the separate individuals possess.

A friend and I have been trying to make that passage precise, and we produced an argument that seems to prove too much. Here it is, with each step made explicit:

(1) If prices are to coordinate agents who each possess only dispersed, local knowledge, then prices must somehow transmit that dispersed knowledge to other agents.

(2) If prices transmit agents' dispersed knowledge, then when one agent is willing to trade at a given price, the other agent can infer something about the first agent's private information from that very willingness, and revise her own valuation accordingly.

(3) In purely informational trade—where agents share the same preferences and initial holdings and differ only in their private information—any offer to trade reveals the information behind the offer.

(4) A rational counterparty will use that revealed information, so the informational difference that made the proposed trade mutually attractive disappears before the trade is completed.

(5) Therefore, in a Hayekian market with only dispersed information and no differences in taste or risk-bearing need, no mutually beneficial informational trade should ever occur.

(6) But real markets—financial and commodity markets especially—are full of trades driven precisely by differential information.

So the Hayekian picture of prices as transmitters of dispersed knowledge seems to lead to the absurd conclusion that such markets should be silent, when in fact they are noisy. I would prefer to keep (1), because it captures what I took from the Hayek passage, but I do not see how to block the inference to (5). Which step or premise is the weak point, and what do the standard ways of rejecting it cost?
