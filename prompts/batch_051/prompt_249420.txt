I'm putting together a movie club with some friends and we're doing Groundhog Day next month. I've been going down a rabbit hole about play and leisure as things that are their own reason for existing, not just ways to recharge for work or improve yourself. Specifically I mean the idea, which I gather comes from Huizinga and Pieper, that play has a kind of sealed-off space with its own rules and stakes, and that real leisure is less about having free time and more about an attitude of being receptive or even celebrating for no productive payoff. I only know the ideas secondhand, so I might be mangling them.

What I can't sort out is whether Groundhog Day actually illustrates that, or whether it pretends to. On one hand Phil after the loop gets going learns the piano, makes ice sculptures, learns French, fixes people's flats—that looks like doing things for their own sake. On the other hand, the movie tends to frame all of it as self-improvement, as if the point is to become someone worth loving, win Rita, and finally wake up to February 3. So maybe it turns play and leisure into just another productivity scheme. Could you read the film through that play/leisure lens? I'm particularly interested in which scenes genuinely show play or leisure as ends in themselves, which scenes undercut or complicate that, and whether the movie as a whole takes a side or stays ambiguous. If you were leading a discussion, which few moments would you point people to?
