I've been going back and forth on the absent-minded driver problem, and I've landed pretty firmly on the action-level side of the debate. Setup for the unfamiliar: you're on a highway with two indistinguishable exits, you get 0 if you exit at the first, 4 if you exit at the second, and 1 if you miss both. The standard ex ante calculation says you should exit with probability 1/3, because that maximizes your expected payoff before you've seen any exit. But I don't buy that the ex ante answer has any normativity for the driver once he's actually at an exit. My position is something like: the ex ante calculation assumes the driver can bind himself to a randomization he will not remember, and then updates his probability of being at the first exit using the very strategy he's supposed to be questioning. So the action-level perspective is the correct one, and the 1/3 result is the artifact. What would count against this position? I don't just want the standard ex ante vs ex interim expected utility line. I want the strongest formal results or cases that would push me back toward ex ante primacy, plus the ones that would only embarrass me. Are there variants of the driver problem where the action-level view gives obviously stupid advice? Does the action-level view collapse into the ex ante view if we add a pre-commitment device, and would that show I was wrong to make the distinction so central? Which of these should a person on my side concede as fatal, and which can they reasonably resist?
