In Alan Turing’s 1950 article “Computing Machinery and Intelligence,” he defends his replacement for the question “Can machines think?” by writing: “The new problem has the advantage of drawing a fairly sharp line between the physical and the intellectual capacities of a man. No engineer or chemist claims to be able to produce a material which is indistinguishable from the human skin. It is possible that at some time this might be done, but even supposing this invention available we should feel there was little point in trying to make a ‘thinking machine’ more human by dressing it up in such artificial flesh.” I am writing a short paper on the claim that the brain is a computer, and this passage strikes me as underestimating the role of the physical body. Please argue that Turing’s sharp line between physical and intellectual capacities is a serious weakness for computationalism. The argument should not just say that embodiment might matter in general; it should show that if the brain is literally a computer, then the specific physical implementation is part of what a computational explanation must account for, and that Turing’s phrase about “dressing it up in such artificial flesh” misstates the problem. Aim for an argument that a careful defender of the brain-as-computer view would have to rebut, rather than a straw man.
