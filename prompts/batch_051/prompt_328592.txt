In my philosophy class we have been reading Descartes, and I want to work out what he would have said about a case he never considered: whole-brain emulation. Suppose a future technology scans a person's brain at a resolution fine enough to capture every neuron, synapse, and relevant chemical state, then runs a computer simulation so faithful that the emulation converses flexibly, learns, forms memories, and otherwise behaves indistinguishably from the original person. Three of Descartes's commitments seem relevant. (1) Mind and body are distinct substances: mind is an unextended thinking thing, while body is extended and unthinking. (2) No mere machine could use language or reason flexibly enough to pass for a human, which is why animals are automata. (3) The human mind interacts with the body through the brain. My question is whether he would classify this emulation as a thinking thing or as res extensa that merely behaves exactly as if it thought. If his substance dualism in (1) points one way and his test for thought in (2) points the other, which does the deciding, and would he need to revise any of the three? If his first move would be to insist that the scenario is impossible because matter cannot do this, explain that move; but also answer on the supposition that the emulation really exists.

Please structure your answer in the following sections, in order:

1. The commitments that matter (about 100 words): lay out the two Descartes criteria that pull in different directions.
2. Descartes's likely answer (about 250 words): reconstruct the answer he would give and the reasoning behind it.
3. Where his position strains (about 150 words): identify the point at which the emulation puts real pressure on his view.
4. Bottom line (about 75 words): state your overall verdict on whether Descartes can absorb the case without changing his commitments.
