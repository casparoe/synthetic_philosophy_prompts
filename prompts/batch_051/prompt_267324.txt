I'm running a seminar on visual communication for international public information, and I want the first session to produce a specification rather than a history lesson. Write the desiderata that a picture language must meet to be adequate to the project Otto Neurath was pursuing with the Vienna Method, later called Isotype: a conventional, teachable system of pictographic signs for presenting social and economic facts to people who share no natural language, without naturalistic illustration and without claiming to mirror reality.

Begin by stating the purpose as you understand it, and what following from Neurath's commitments rules out from the start—treating an image as a copy of the world, allowing one-off artistic illustration to do the work of signs, letting decorative effects override comparative reading, and so on. Then produce the requirement list, with the case or consideration behind each. Go beyond generic good-design advice; a requirement earns its place because Neurath's program or an actual Isotype practice forces it. Cover at least the following pressure points, though you may add others or argue against any of them:

- repeatability and combinability of signs, versus unique illustrations;
- the ban on perspective and naturalism, and which kinds of simplification it demands;
- how quantities are represented—counting repeated signs versus scaling size or changing color—and what each alternative loses;
- how the system represents social kinds (worker, unemployed, migrant, refugee) without leaving the impression that pictograms are a neutral photograph of categories;
- whether the language must display its own limits, and how a deliberately flat sign-system can mark what it cannot say;
- revisability: given that any representation is fallible and replaceable, what a visual standard must be like to remain correctable.

For each requirement, mark it non-negotiable, a preference specific to one reading of Neurath, or contingent on the purpose you fixed. Then name the pair of requirements that pull hardest against each other—say, combinatorial consistency versus cross-cultural legibility, or accuracy to quantitative data versus learnability—and defend that pair as the deepest tension. Finally, identify the single requirement that, if designers actually enforced it, would most change current practice in transit signage, public-health infographics, or data visualization. A compact table is welcome; aim for 900–1,200 words; no external citations, the reasoning must stand on its own.
