I have been working through the debate over gauge freedom and surplus structure in electromagnetism — the question of what, if anything, is physically real in a gauge theory beyond the quantities that come out the same in every gauge. After going back and forth, I have settled on a criterion I have been calling the Residual Content Principle. It says: the real content of a quantity in a gauge theory is exactly what remains invariant under the widest set of gauge transformations that preserve all empirical predictions, and nothing beyond that. The electric and magnetic fields come out fully real, which seems right. The local values of the vector potential come out only partly real, which also seems right, since different legitimate gauges disagree about those local values. And the phase a charged particle picks up in the Aharonov-Bohm setup comes out real, since it is invariant under the relevant gauge transformations even though the potential itself varies from gauge to gauge. So far this has felt like real progress.

But I have started to worry that the principle breaks down exactly where it needs to be sharpest. The phrase "the widest set of gauge transformations that preserve all empirical predictions" seems to depend on how the theory is formulated. If I reformulate electromagnetism entirely in terms of field strengths and the gauge-invariant phases assigned to loops, there is no leftover vector potential for the gauge symmetry to act on, so the criterion's verdicts seem to shift — or at least the question stops being statable. Conversely, I can always add redundant fields to the theory as a bookkeeping device, and those fields will then fail invariance and be judged unreal, which makes the inventory of what is real depend on my choice of variables. I still think the principle is tracking something, but I cannot tell whether it is a genuine criterion or just a wordy restatement of "only the gauge-invariant quantities matter," and I do not see how to fix the formulation-dependence.

I would welcome an honest assessment. Is the Residual Content Principle coherent enough to be worth developing, or does it collapse into something trivial? If it has already been tried in the philosophy of physics, what is it usually called, and what was the standard objection? And what would you say is the most serious difficulty with it — one that forces a revision of the principle, or one that shows the whole approach is misguided?
