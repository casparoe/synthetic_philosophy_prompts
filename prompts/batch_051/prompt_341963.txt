I'm a retired chemist drifting into philosophy, and I've been reading Mill's *Principles of Political Economy*, specifically the chapter on the stationary state in Book IV. There's a passage there—I don't have it in front of me so I'll paraphrase—where he argues that a stationary condition of capital and population doesn't mean a stationary condition of human improvement, and that the endless struggle to get on is not the kind of life worth organizing an economy around. This has pushed me toward a degrowth-ish view: once a society has secured basic material needs and a decent level of security, further aggregate growth should not be treated as a default good, and climate policy—carbon taxes, regulation, all that—should be aimed at deliberately scaling down material throughput rather than at greening growth. I realize that's a position with a lot of load-bearing parts. So tell me: what would count against it? Which of them would a committed Mill-style degrowther have to concede actually count against the position, and which would they think miss the point? Rank from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about whether the view can be insulated by qualifications—and whether that's a problem or a feature.
