I've been working through Hume's economic essays in the Political Discourses and keep getting stuck on how to square two things he says. In Of Money he seems to deny that a country's supply of gold and silver matters for its real wealth: if all the money in Britain were doubled in a night, prices and wages would eventually double too and nobody would be better off, because money is only a device for circulating goods and prices track the amount of circulating medium. But he immediately qualifies that by saying the period before prices catch up is exactly when the extra money does real work — as it flows in gradually, it lubricates commerce, wakes up industry, and makes merchants and manufacturers more enterprising. He even says a wise government should keep money gently increasing, because that keeps alive a spirit of industry in the nation.

The thing that confuses me is that in Of the Balance of Trade he uses very similar machinery to argue that nations shouldn't fret about losing specie to foreigners. If four-fifths of Britain's money suddenly vanished, he says, prices would fall, British goods would become cheap abroad, and the money would flow back until things roughly balanced out. But if the short-run effects in Of Money are as important as he makes them sound, why is it fine to shrug off a big loss of money as self-correcting? During the adjustment the country could be poorer, less industrious, and worse off in exactly the way he warns about. Are these two discussions genuinely consistent? Give the most plausible reconciliations — short-run versus long-run, equilibrium versus transition, different senses of what a nation's treasure consists in — and then say which one is honest and which is special pleading. Is there a reading on which Hume actualy has a deeper unresolved problem here that he never fully clears up?
