I've been trying to work out what I actually mean when I call a belief true, rather than what I was taught to say about truth in lectures. I keep returning to a passage in William James's *Pragmatism: A New Name for Some Old Ways of Thinking* (1907), from Lecture VI, 'Pragmatism's Conception of Truth':

'They lead us, namely, through the acts and other ideas which they instigate, into or up to, or towards, other parts of experience with which we feel all the while—such feeling being among our potentialities—that the original ideas remain in agreement. The connexions and transitions come to us from point to point as being progressive, harmonious, satisfactory. This function of agreeable leading is what we mean by an idea's verification.'

The passage pulls me strongly, but I'm equally drawn to the rival pragmatist view of Peirce's 'How to Make Our Ideas Clear' (1878), on which truth is not whatever happens to satisfy you or me, but the opinion that would be settled by anyone who pursued inquiry far enough. My difficulty is that my snap verdicts on particular cases divide between the two. Here they are.

About Fermat's Last Theorem, I feel no hesitation: in 1900, long before Wiles, the theorem was already true; the proof was there to be found, whether or not anyone had found it. Truth seems to outrun verification. Yet about Polynesian navigators who hold that the stars are ancestors steering their canoes, I want to say something different: the ancestor story is false, but the concrete navigational lore is true because it has led them reliably from island to island for centuries, whatever false cosmology surrounds it. That feels exactly like James's 'agreeable leading'. But then a lucky paranoid: someone with no evidence believes a colleague is embezzling, and the colleague genuinely is. I call that belief true in a bare, correspondence sort of way, but epistemically worthless—it did not lead the person through experience, it just happened to hit. If truth just is agreeable leading, I should presumably not call it true at all, and that bothers me. Newtonian mechanics consoles me a little: I still call it true within its range, even after relativity, because it continues to lead correctly for bridges and artillery. But here is the case that keeps me from simply signing up with James. A dying woman believes her estranged son still loves her; it comforts her and cannot be checked now. I find myself unwilling to call that true, however much it pays in her remaining experience. And I have one case I cannot settle: my sister's belief that her daughter 'will turn out well', which seems partly to bring about its own fulfilment. Is that true now, does it become true, or is it neither?

Please act as an expert on pragmatist theories of truth, especially the competing accounts of Peirce and James. Begin by reconstructing, as precisely as you can, the Jamesian principle in the quoted passage and the Peircean alternative it stands against. Then run candidate principles through my cases one by one, deliberately checking which of my verdicts each principle can keep. I want to know whether there is a single coherent account—Jamesian, Peircean, a hybrid, or something else—that captures most of what I've said, which of my verdicts it would force me to abandon, and why it should be those rather than others. Say explicitly what your preferred principle predicts about the sister's belief, and which of my own verdicts it cannot accommodate.
