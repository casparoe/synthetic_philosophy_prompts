From the rulebook of a competitive argumentation league founded in the early 1990s, before large language models existed:

Rule 14 — Outside Assistance: "During a round, no competitor may receive assistance of any kind from any person other than their registered teammates."

At this year's championship, a finalist competes while a large language model, fine-tuned on her own past debates and the tournament's published readings, streams suggested rebuttals and a running map of her opponent's argumentative moves to a smartwatch on her wrist. The model runs locally on the device, offline for the duration of the round. She is in communication with no human: no coach, teammate, or spectator is sending anything.

The drafters of Rule 14 did not anticipate this. What does the rule require in this case if read according to the plain meaning of its words? What does it require if read in light of its purpose? What would the drafters have said if asked, and what would a reasonable competitor reading the rule today take it to mean? Which of these readings should govern, and why? Whatever your answer, what does this case show about what Rule 14 was ever able to protect, and about whether turning argumentation into a scored sport protects or distorts good reasoning?

After your analysis, provide a table with four rows (plain text, purpose, drafters' intent, reasonable reader) and these columns: Approach; Verdict on the rule; Key consideration; Residual difficulty.
