In Augustine's *On Lying* he first brackets jests, writing: "Setting aside, therefore, jokes, which have never been accounted lies, seeing they bear with them in the tone of voice, and in the very mood of the joker a most evident indication that he means no deceit, although the thing he utters be not true." He then defines the liar: "that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind," and later adds that "the fault of him who lies, is, the desire of deceiving in the uttering of his mind." These passages seem to pull in opposite directions: the definition appears to locate the lie in a mismatch between the mind and the outward sign, while the joke passage appears to locate it partly in whether the manner of utterance conveys an intention to deceive. Are they genuinely in tension, or can they be reconciled without special pleading? If they can be reconciled, does the work get done by the "desire of deceiving" clause, by treating jests as non-assertoric because their signs carry their own cancellation, or by some other distinction? If they cannot be reconciled, which passage should be taken as the better expression of Augustine's position, and what follows for the broader absolutist argument he wants to make about lying? I work in late antique moral psychology and would prefer your sharpest reconstruction and a definite verdict, not a survey of options.
