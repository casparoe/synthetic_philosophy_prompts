I recently had a long argument with a friend about what it means to call something a grammatical rule rather than a fact about individual words, and we kept talking past each other. My friend wanted to say that anything speakers reliably know about their language counts as a rule; I was not so sure. I proposed the following criteria: a grammatical rule is (a) productive — it extends to novel items, as when an English speaker gives the past tense of the invented verb wug as wugged; (b) general — it is stated over a class of expressions rather than as a list of stored forms; (c) a default — speakers apply it unless a more specific form overrides it; (d) exceptionless within its domain; and (e) internally represented — speakers reliably conform to it even if they cannot state it. Now take two cases that seem to pull against different parts of that list. The first is suppletion: the past tense of go is went, not goed, although children routinely pass through a stage of saying goed and every adult knows went; historically went belonged to a different verb, and the go–went pairing is confined to a single lexical item. The second is a sound pattern with lexical exceptions, of the sort found in English derivational morphology: a stressed tense vowel is laxed in certain Latinate derivatives (divine–divinity, serene–serenity), yet the pattern is not exceptionless and speakers do not reliably extend it to new derivatives. Work through my five criteria for each case and say whether the case counts as a grammatical rule. Then address the harder question: do the two cases force different revisions? In particular, does suppletion force us to give up productivity or exceptionlessness, or should we instead admit that an item-specific stored mapping can be rule-governed; and does the sound pattern force us to give up exceptionlessness, or should we distinguish the rule itself from the conditions on its application? I would like your answer in British English.
