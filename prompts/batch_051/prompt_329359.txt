I serve as Secretary of the Faculty Senate at a private university whose Charter functions as its constitution. The Charter assigns the appointment of the Provost to the Board of Visitors, and it states that the Provost chairs the Faculty Senate and that the Senate may act only by a two-thirds vote of its members. It contains no provision for an acting Provost and no general emergency or necessity clause.

The circumstance: eight months ago the terms of a majority of Board members expired, and the body designated by the Charter to appoint replacements—a council of the university's founding religious order—has split and refused to send new members. The Board therefore lacks a quorum and can take no valid action. Two weeks ago the Provost resigned. The Senate now confronts a reaccreditation deadline, an expiring collective agreement, and forty pending tenure decisions, all matters that the Charter says require the Provost's signature or the Board's approval. One faction of the Senate argues that the Senate is the only functioning constitutional organ and may, under an implied doctrine of necessity, appoint one of its members as Acting Provost by the same two-thirds vote the Charter would require. The opposing faction argues that the Senate's powers exist only as the Charter provides, that the text gives appointment power solely to the Board, and that the Senate's only lawful course is to wait, even if the university's operations lapse.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before next week's Senate meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame; argue for it from first principles about where legitimate authority comes from when written rules run out, not merely from institutional politics; engage the strongest objection from the losing side and explain exactly where you disagree with it; and flag the one or two places where your recommendation depends on an assumption a different member might reasonably reject.
