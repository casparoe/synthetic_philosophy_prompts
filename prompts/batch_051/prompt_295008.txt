I'm a philosophy professor, but for my sins I also run a monthly "deep canvassing"-style discussion group through the public library, where we pair people with sharply opposed politics and have volunteer moderators keep the thing from exploding. The one call our moderators consistently blow is when to stop treating a participant as arguing in good faith but wrong, and start treating them as acting in bad faith -- sealioning, moving goalposts, concern-trolling, "just asking questions," the works. Stakes matter in both directions: mislabel a person as bad faith and the program loses trust; fail to catch a bad-faith actor and they hold the floor and drive everyone else away. Write the moderators a decision procedure they can actually run in real time, with no philosophy background. It should be a sequence of questions, with what counts as a sufficent answer to each, where the procedure stops with "no," and where it must hand the call to a human because the question is one of values or mind-reading rather than facts. Please force apart three things we currently collapse: the argument being bad, the person being wrong, and the person engaging in bad faith -- and say which observable behaviors count as evidence for the third. Then run the procedure on three cases: (a) a participant who redefines "rigged election" every time the moderator pins it down; (b) someone who only ever asks questions and never states a position; (c) a regular the moderators agree with, who strawmans and interrupts but is plainly doing it from frustration, not strategy. Be honest about where the procedure will misfire.
