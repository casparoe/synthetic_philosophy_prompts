I've been reading about monetary history for a class and I can't shake what feels like a real parallel. Gresham's law, as it's usually glossed, says that when two forms of money circulate at the same face value but one of them has more intrinsic value, people spend the bad money and hoard the good, and the bad ends up dominating circulation. The good money doesn't disappear from the world, it just stops circulating. And this struck me as almost exactly the same shape as the multipolar trap / Moloch idea from AI-safety-ish discourse: there's some metric everyone is forced to optimize against (face value, engagement, benchmark score), that metric only loosely tracks a real but costlier-to-produce quality (intrinsic value, honesty, actual competence), and under competition the cheap substitute outcompetes the real thing, so the real thing effectively disappears from the space where anyone is looking for it. Individual actors can't unilaterally prefer the good version without losing, so they all keep producing or spending the bad version.

But I can't tell if this is a genuine identity of structure — Moloch is just Gresham's law generalized to any situation with a common metric — or if I'm just pattern-matching on the phrase "drives out." Where does the analogy actually snap if you push on it? I already noticed one place: in Gresham's law the good money isn't destroyed, it's hoarded, whereas in a race to the bottom it seems like the good thing actually stops being produced. Is that a real difference or just a difference in where you draw the system boundary? And does anything else transfer from monetary economics, like the idea that under floating exchange rates the direction can reverse and good money drives out bad? That would suggest the fix for a multipolar trap is to let the exchange rate between good and bad behavior float, instead of having it pegged by one metric that can't tell them apart. Is there a theorem-like core here, or is it just a vibe?
