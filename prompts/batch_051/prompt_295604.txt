I’ve been preparing a short presentation for a reading group on the principle of indifference, and I want a concrete case that moves the discussion away from the familiar geometric examples. In particular, I want you to construct a thought experiment to specification.

Specifications. The scenario should be realistic, with no exotic physics, no perfect predictors, no demons, and no longer than a paragraph. It should involve an ordinary practical choice with modest stakes, such as a household or workplace decision. The scenario must be engineered so that two versions of the principle of indifference lead to different probabilities, and hence to different betting odds. Version A applies the principle directly to the finite observable outcomes the agent cares about. Version B treats those observable outcomes as generated by an underlying continuous quantity, applies the principle to that quantity, and derives the probabilities for the observable outcomes from the resulting distribution. Both versions should look like natural ways for a reasonable person to be indifferent, and neither should rely on an obviously bad partition.

After the scenario, walk through what each version says and why, and show exactly where the divergence comes from. Then identify the structural feature that does the work: what property of the case makes the two ways of applying indifference come apart? Say what would happen if that feature were removed, and whether the case collapses into a standard Bertrand-style lesson or something else. Finally, tell me how a defender of whichever version ends up looking less intuitive would attack the case, and whether the attack is ultimately about the partition, the continuous parameter, or something else.

Also, please say how confident you are in the claims you make, and single out the point you are least sure about.
