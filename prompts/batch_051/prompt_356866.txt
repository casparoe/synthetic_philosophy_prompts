You are a historian of ideas who works on both premodern Islamic philosophy of history and contemporary quantitative macrohistory. Take your time, and before you commit to a verdict, spend a few paragraphs working through the relevant commitments on each side of the question.

Ibn Khaldun, writing in the Muqaddimah in the late fourteenth century, argued that settled civilization follows recurring patterns: dynasties are founded by groups with strong asabiyyah (roughly, group solidarity), conquer and rule, then over a few generations become accustomed to luxury, lose their cohesion, and are replaced by rougher groups from the periphery. He also claimed to have founded a new science of human society, a science of 'umran (civilization), which would uncover the hidden causes behind the rise and fall of states.

Centuries later, cliodynamics—developed especially by Peter Turchin—attempts to turn this kind of macrohistorical generalization into a quantitative, predictive research program. It codes historical records into variables, formalizes mechanisms such as elite overproduction, popular immiseration, and state fiscal crisis into dynamical models, fits those models to data, simulates cycles, and issues probabilistic forecasts of political instability.

Now suppose Ibn Khaldun could examine the cliodynamic program in detail—not only its conclusions but its methods: constructing datasets, estimating parameters, simulating secular cycles, and making forecasts. Would he have recognized this as the realization or legitimate descendant of the science he claimed to have founded, or would he have judged that something essential was lost in the mathematization? In particular, weigh whether his own method carried commitments—about hidden divine causation, the need for the historian's lived judgment and acquaintance with many societies, the repeated patterns he called cycles, or the very possibility of a general science of 'umran—that quantification either fulfills or violates. Give the strongest version of each side, then say which reading, on balance, requires less special pleading.
