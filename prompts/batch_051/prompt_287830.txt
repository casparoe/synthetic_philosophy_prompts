During my career I worked as an evolutionary biologist, and I was trained to regard biological evolution as a process without foresight, an editor rather than an author. Since retiring, I have been reading some philosophy of history and of technology, and I keep meeting the thought that the emergence of increasingly capable artificial systems might give the human story a directional, even teleological, shape after all: human beings as a stepping stone, a transitional stage by which intelligence builds its own successor. I suspect this is often little more than the old nineteenth-century faith in progress dressed up in new vocabulary, but I want to be fair to the opposing view rather than dismiss it.

I would therefore like you to write a dialogue between an articulate proponent of the view that AI succession lends a kind of teleology to human history and an articulate critic who denies that anything directional or normative follows from the fact that we are constructing capable machines. Both characters should be equally competent; neither should be a strawman. The proponent might argue that intention can introduce genuine direction into a process that began without it, or that a sequence can be retroactively recognised as having a goal; the critic might respond that a collection of local intentions is not one overarching intention, and that reading a direction into the whole is exactly the retrospective storytelling a scientist should distrust. The two should address each other's actual points and be willing to concede where the other is strong.

Please write the dialogue in British English. There is no need for citations, footnotes, or a reading list; I am interested in the arguments rather than in authorities.
