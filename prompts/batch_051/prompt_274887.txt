I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about word meaning, by going off my gut on concrete cases. Here are my verdicts; I'd like a principle that gets them, if there is one.

- "The chicken pecked the ground" vs "the chicken was undercooked." One word with two related senses (animal, meat), not two words.
- "He burned the book" vs "he understood the book." I want to say this is somehow the same meaning, just picking out different aspects of a book (object vs content), not two senses.
- "The school is being repainted" vs "the school is hiring teachers." Definitely two senses, building vs institution, but related.
- "River bank" vs "savings bank." Two different words that happen to sound the same. No shared meaning at all.
- "Fast car" vs "fast friend." Same pattern as bank for me: "fast" in "fast friend" has nothing to do with speed, it means loyal/steadfast. Different word.
- "Open the window" and "open the meeting." I keep waffling on this one. It feels like there's one abstract "open" meaning something like "remove a barrier to access or operation," stretched across physical and institutional uses. But the uses also feel distinct enough that "he opened the window and then the meeting" works as a pun. Can't settle it.
- "She's a good surgeon" vs "that's a good knife" vs "that's a good excuse." I think "good" has one meaning (roughly: serves the relevant purpose well), with the standard supplied by the noun each time. Not three senses.
- "The ham sandwich at table six is getting impatient." I do not think "ham sandwich" has gained a sense meaning person. That's just metonymy, one-off, not part of the word.

But here's the pair that bothers me: "newspaper" — "the newspaper fell off the porch" vs "the newspaper endorsed the candidate" — feels like a settled ambiguity: physical copy vs the organization. I want it to be like school. Yet I want "the ham sandwich is getting impatient" to be no new sense. What's the difference, really? Both are thing-for-associated-thing shifts.

I've been going by a rough test: if you can coordinate two uses under one occurrence of the word and it sounds fine (I opened the door and the window), they're the same sense; if it sounds forced or punny (I opened the window and the meeting), they're seperate. But that test would split "open" into separate senses, which conflicts with my feeling that "open" is one abstract meaning. And it would say "burned the book" vs "understood the book" are separate senses, which I don't accept, while I'd accept it for "chicken."

So: is there a principle that captures all or most of this, stated precisely — something that says when two uses are one sense, when they're two related senses, and when they're two different words? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop (I can already tell "open" and "book" might have to go)? And what does it say about cases I haven't listed, like "the author began the book," where "began" seems to pull an activity (reading? writing?) out of the noun that wasn't explicitly there?

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the principle, or the statement that no single principle captures these — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
