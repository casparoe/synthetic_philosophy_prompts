Before I read any further in the history of logic, I want to know what an adequate formal logic is supposed to be adequate for. Take the purpose to be a notation and proof system that can express and validate mathematical inference, with ordinary-language reasoning as a secondary target. Write the desiderata any such logic must meet: the inference forms it must validate, the superficially plausible forms it must refuse to validate, the distinctions it must respect, and the expressive capacities it cannot do without. For each requirement, give the motivating cases behind it, and wherever you can, include cases that the traditional syllogistic handles badly or not at all — inferences about relations, for example (if a circle contains a square and the square contains a triangle, then the circle contains the triangle), and nested or multiple generality (the difference between 'every man loves some woman' and the claim that one and the same woman is loved by every man). Cover at least the following: singular terms and identity; why a logic needs relations rather than only one-place predications; why it needs bound variables and quantifiers rather than just two terms joined by a copula; and how propositional connectives fit into the account. Then flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — in particular, whether the expressiveness required for mathematics makes validity harder to mechanize than it was for syllogistic — and say which requirement should give way if that conflict is real. Finally, score the traditional syllogistic and modern quantifier-variable first-order logic briefly against your list, and state which requirement does the most work in explaining what Frege changed.
