I'm in a philosophy of cognitive science seminar and at the end of class we got into a fight about whether the free energy principle is actually a scientific theory. The criteria we were given for a theory are: (1) it has to make predictions that could in principle fail; (2) it's central terms have to hook onto something measurable; (3) it has to explain something the data don't already force on you; and (4) there has to be a genuine rival account that could have been true instead.

The way a classmate explained it, the free energy principle says any self-organizing system that persists in a changing environment has to minimize a quantity called variational free energy, which is roughly the gap between the system's model of its environment and the sensory input it actually receives — he said it was an upper bound on surprise, and he kept mentioning Markov blankets (supposedly the formal boundary between a system and everything else), though I couldn't tell what those add. On his telling, the principle explains perception, action, attention, even the structure of the brain. He tied it to predictive processing: the brain constantly makes top-down predictions and updates them by prediction error, and minimizing free energy is the deeper reason that works. But then he also said the principle applies to anything that keeps itself around — a bacterium, a brain, maybe even a hurricane — and that's when I started worrying that it can't be falsified at all.

I don't want a reading list or citations. I want you to go through each of the four criteria and say honestly whether the free energy principle meets it, where the judgment could go either way, and then say whether the problem is the principle or our criteria. Is the free energy principle a scientific theory, or is asking that question itself a category mistake?
