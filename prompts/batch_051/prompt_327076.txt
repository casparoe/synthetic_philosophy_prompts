I am a social scientist on the research staff of a regional land trust in the American West, and part of my job is to understand why our members and the broader public support protecting roadless landscapes. The same pattern keeps surfacing at public hearings and in open-ended survey comments: people defend a particular place by saying it should be preserved for its own sake, not because of anything it provides. Yet the places they defend in these terms are almost always places where they have deep personal history—the canyon where they learned to hunt, the river their late father fished, the alpine basin where they scattered a parent's ashes. I have come to suspect that much of what presents as intrinsic value is actually relational value wearing the language of intrinsic value: saying "it would matter even if no human ever saw it" is more public-spirited and harder to argue with than saying "it matters because of what this place means to me and my family."

I want to test that suspicion as a claim rather than keep trading anecdotes with my colleagues. Design the best feasible study, or a small sequence of studies, that could separate genuinely intrinsic valuation of a wild place from relational valuation and from straightforward instrumental valuation (ecosystem services, recreation, property values, and the like). I can imagine, for instance, giving people forced choices between a place they know intimately and a comparable place they have never visited, but I worry that only reveals attachment to a particular place rather than showing which kind of value is doing the work. Be concrete about the population you would sample, the scenarios or measures you would use, the comparisons that actually discriminate among the three kinds of value, and what pattern of results would count for my rationalization thesis and what would count against it. Then say what the empirical tests could never settle: which parts of the intrinsic/relational distinction remain philosophical judgments that no amount of data could adjudicate.
