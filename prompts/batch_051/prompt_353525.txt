You are a professional philosopher who works on the epistemology of skill and the distinction between knowing how and knowing that; I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, so please define terms as you go but do not oversimplify the argument. I have been trying to reconcile Ryle's anti-intellectualist regress argument with what I see in modern machine learning. As I understand Ryle, if intelligent performance required first consulting some true proposition about how to perform it, then applying that proposition correctly would itself require know-how, plus a further proposition about how to apply it, and so on; therefore knowing how cannot simply be a species of knowing that, and practical skill cannot be reduced to the possession or consultation of truths. This distinction always matched my engineering experience. A rules engine can hold thousands of true propositions about a domain and still fail at the task, while a model trained from examples can succeed without any readable declarative knowledge.

However, large models are now eroding the line I used to draw. A single network can be trained end-to-end and then used both to answer questions about a task in propositional form and to produce the sequence of actions that performs the task; agent models with one shared set of weights increasingly alternate between fluent description and skilled execution. At the same time, systems such as AlphaZero became extremely strong at chess and Go, games that seem full of knowing-that (rules, opening theory, tactical principles), mostly through something that looks like practice rather than being told truths. Does Ryle's argument survive this development? I would like you to identify which premise of the regress argument, if any, is affected by the fact that one optimization process produces both proposition-like output and skill-like output. Is the knowing-how/knowing-that distinction now only a difference between two ways of querying the same underlying weights, and would that weaken Ryle's claim or actually reinforce it? If you think the argument survives, say whether it survives unchanged or needs repair; if it does not, say what a modern replacement should claim. Please engage carefully with the strongest version of the machine-learning-based objection before giving your final verdict.
