I chair the disciplinary committee of a secular professional association of mediators. On admission, every member takes a one-sentence oath: "I swear to perform my role honestly, impartially, and without regard to my own interest." Our bylaws make "violation of the oath" a ground for discipline, but they do not define violation, and we are not a court: we cannot lean on contract law or on the text of a statute. I need a reusable decision procedure that my committee — none of them trained in philosophy — can run in under an hour per case to answer two questions: (a) has this member's conduct violated the oath, and (b) is the violation of the kind that warrants discipline?

The procedure should be grounded in a definite view of what gives an oath its binding force. Take a position and build the procedure on it: is the binding force in the words, the speaker's intention, the public act, the reliance of others, the invoked witness, or something else — and what follows for cases where one of those elements is missing? Then use the ancient case of the gods swearing on the river Styx as your hardest test case: if a Styx oath binds even the gods, what feature of it does the binding, and which of those features can a secular professional body actually reproduce, and which can it not? Be explicit about what your procedure deliberately gives up.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a mediator who opens a session by telling the parties she will keep the stronger party from steamrolling the weaker one — conduct that arguably promises a departure from neutrality; (2) a mediator who admits she took the oath only to get on a court roster and never regarded it as binding; (3) a mediator who, years later, announces that the association's discriminatory conduct in its committee appointments has voided her oath.

I expect at least one of these to come out in a way my committee will dislike; tell me which one and why the procedure requires it. At every step, mark where a human being has to exercise judgment rather than apply a rule, and say what a conscientious panelist should do at that point. Do not give me a menu of frameworks: commit to one and defend it.
