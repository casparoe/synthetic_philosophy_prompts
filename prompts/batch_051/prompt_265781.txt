Start from Searle's Chinese Room and swap out the rulebook for a large array of random numbers. The operator doesn't look up rules in anything a human wrote; after each exchange he nudges the numbers in whatever direction made last month's native-speaker judges rate the rooms answers slightly higher. No one knows what any individual number does, and after a few hundred million scored exchanges the room passes for a native speaker. A lot of the original argument's pull comes from the fact that the man is executing rules that an understanding programmer wrote down. In this version nobody wrote the rules down, and nobody understood anything at any point. Does Searle's conclusion survive? Does the systems reply get stronger or weaker once the symbol-manipulating machinery is found by hill-climbing against a score rather than designed by someone? Which of the standard positions, if any, were secretly depending on the rulebook having an author? Work the thought experiment first, and then say whether the analogy to a language model trained on human feedback changes anything.
