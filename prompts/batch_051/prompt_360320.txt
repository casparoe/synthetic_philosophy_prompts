In a seminar on the philosophy of criminal law, the blackmail paradox was presented in its usual form: threatening to reveal a piece of true and reputation-damaging information about a person is, taken on its own, generally lawful; revealing it is generally lawful; but threatening to reveal it unless the person pays you is the crime of blackmail. A student kept invoking the one-liner "two rights make a wrong" to sum this up — first merely to state the structure of the paradox, and then increasingly as though it disposed of the question of why the law ought to treat the combination as criminal. I suspect the slogan is being asked to do more work than it can, but I want to be precise about why. Please distinguish the different claims "two rights make a wrong" could be asserting in this context. For each reading, indicate whether it is a trivial restatement of the legal facts, a substantive and defensible claim, or a false claim, and say which of the readings would actually support the conclusion that blackmail is rightly criminalized rather than merely restate the puzzle. Present this as a numbered menu I can take back to the seminar.
