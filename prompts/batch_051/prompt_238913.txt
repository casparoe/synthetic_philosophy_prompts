My historical linguistics professor always reaches for the same picture: sound change is like a law of nature. Once a change kicks in, it applies mechanically and exceptionlessly across the whole vocabulary, and if a word doesn't show it, the word must have been borrowed or reshaped by analogy afterwards. That's what lets him wave off suppletion cases like go/went—not as exceptions, but as junk left over from a different process. I keep wondering how far the analogy actually holds. Can you lay out what corresponds to what (the law, the domain, a genuine exception, a counterexample), where the mapping breaks, and which features of "law of nature" get quietly imported into language change without earning their keep? And the real question: does the conclusion that suppletion can't count against regular sound change survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy doing the work the whole time?
