The question was:

Explain what's sometimes called the paradox of wu-wei. What exactly is the difficulty, and how do the Daodejing and the Zhuangzi each try to defuse it? Address the role, if any, of practices or techniques in their response.

The student wrote:

Wu-wei is usually glossed as effortless action or non-action, and the paradox is that if you make wu-wei into a goal, the attempt to be effortless seems to reintroduce the very self-conscious effort wu-wei is meant to exclude. You end up trying to become the person who does not try. Both texts see this difficulty. The Daodejing responds with a practice of emptying: Laozi's fasting of the mind, where you strip away your own aims and let the dao move through you, and once the mind is emptied, action becomes wu-wei on its own without any further management. The Zhuangzi's response is more concrete: stories like Cook Ding show that wu-wei is what happens when a skill has been drilled to the point of automaticity. You practice hard, the effort drops away, and from then on the activity carries you. On this reading the paradox is only a stage you pass through — effort is the ladder you kick away once it has done its work. So the two texts differ in emphasis but converge: wu-wei is achieved either by emptying the mind or by training, and in neither case does the original paradox remain.
