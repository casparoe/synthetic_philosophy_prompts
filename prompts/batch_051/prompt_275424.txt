I'm a PhD student in philosophy, mostly working on well-being and the ethics of consumption, and I keep going back and forth on a view I've sorta adopted. Here it is: voluntarily choosing a materially simpler life — deliberately keeping your consumption well below what you can afford, not as a one-month detox but as a standing orientation — is good in a way that isn't reducible to its downstream effects. Not mainly because it makes you happier, and not mainly because it's greener, but because the simplicity itself is worth something: self-command, clarity, freedom from the cycle of wanting and getting. I don't mean full-blown asceticism, more the voluntary-simplicity family.

What would count against this? I want the evidence, arguments, possible cases, and results from adjacent areas that should move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Some things already nag at me. There's the subjective well-being research, once you get past the old "money stops mattering past some threshold" line: if more material resources really do keep buying well-being, or if chosen deprivation still carries real costs, does that cut against the ideal? There's the adaptive-preference worry: a taste for simplicity might be formed by privation, or might function as a status marker (the luxury of renunciation), in which case the simplicity isn't obviously doing the work. And there's the historical-political objection: ascetic and simple-living ideals have been used to make other people's poverty look noble, which would be a consequence the view is at least complicit in, and that looks unacceptable.

But I don't want a list of objections; I want a map of the position's exposure. Which of these would a defender concede count against the view, and which would they dismiss, and on what grounds? Where does the view risk getting qualified into unfalsifiability (e.g., "simplicity is good for those who are called to it"), and is that a real problem for a normative ideal of this kind, or is it fine for an ethical ideal to not be much of an empirical bet? Concretely: if someone showed me solid longitudinal work that people who voluntarily downshift aren't better off on any measure that survives scrutiny, would that damage the view, or would a defender just say the view was never a prediction about welfare outcomes? I can't tell wether that's a feature or a bug.

Also, please seperate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people working on this stuff, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus. I'd rather have your honest sense of where the fault lines are than a tidy one-sided story.
