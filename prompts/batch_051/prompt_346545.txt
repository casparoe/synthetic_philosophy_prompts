For my seminar on the history and philosophy of moral reform, I am writing a paper on American Prohibition, and I want to think through it with the framework we have been developing around temperance and self-control: temperance as virtue, temperance as prudence, and moral reform as repression or social control. I am treating the basic facts of the episode as given. Between 1920 and 1933 the United States banned the manufacture, sale, and transportation of intoxicating liquors under the Eighteenth Amendment and the Volstead Act; the policy was widely evaded through bootlegging and speakeasies, produced a large black market and strengthened organized crime, caused many poisonings from adulterated and industrial alcohol, and was repealed by the Twenty-First Amendment. The temperance movement that pressed for it spoke of alcohol as a threat to individual self-mastery, family order, and democratic citizenship, and it framed Prohibition as a collective act of self-restraint.

The theoretical problem is that these three frameworks do not merely describe Prohibition differently; they assign it different objects and different standards of success or failure. If temperance is an Aristotelian virtue, a settled disposition to desire and be pained at the right times and a mean between insensibility and self-indulgence, then the immediate question is whether a nation can possess a virtue at all, or only its citizens individually. Does the Eighteenth Amendment look like the state legislating a virtue, that is, trying to require by law a disposition that cannot be coerced into existence? If temperance is prudence, a form of self-binding in the face of one's own known weakness, then the analysis must specify who the binding self was, whose weakness was being corrected, and whether Prohibition was prudent collective precommitment or paternalism exercised on people who did not regard themselves as the weaker party. If the appeal to temperance is best read as a mask for social control and for the policing of certain populations, then that reading owes an account of why so many temperance advocates were themselves abstainers genuinely concerned with self-mastery rather than with coercion, and why so many ordinary drinkers experienced the law as an external imposition rather than as self-rule.

I would like the analysis to run in both directions. First, say what each of the three frameworks implies Prohibition was, what the relevant actors faced, and what they should have done by the lights of each framework. Second, use the episode, including its evasion and repeal, to test the frameworks: is Prohibition's collapse best read as evidence that the state cannot legislate virtue, that the precommitment was badly designed because the binding majority was not the group whose drinking it targeted, that the repression involved in enforcement was the real content of the policy, or some messier combination? And I would like a clear statement of where the analogy between individual self-control and a collective prohibition breaks down, and whether the idea of a collective intemperance is coherent or only a metaphor.
