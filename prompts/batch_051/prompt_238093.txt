I am a retired geologist; for the last two decades of my career I planned and directed field campaigns in terrains where the ground rarely matched the maps, and since retiring I have been reading the philosophical literature on intention, practical reasoning, and skill. The question I keep coming back to is whether planning is overrated relative to improvisation, and I have been compiling arguments that it is. So far my list runs as follows.

1. The planning-fallacy argument: planners systematically underestimate the time, cost, and difficulty of a course of action, so the confidence invested in a plan is poor evidence about how the plan will actually unfold.
2. The rigidity argument: committing to a plan brings opportunity costs, because a settled plan suppresses responsiveness to options and information that become visible only later; the benefits of precommitment are bought at the price of selective blindness.
3. The expertise argument: in sufficiently practised domains, experienced agents often act best by reading the situation directly—recognising patterns, sensing anomalies, and drawing on a trained repertoire—rather than by deriving action from a plan, because plans operate at the wrong level of grain for singular situations.
4. The displacement-activity argument: planning can serve as a way of postponing action while retaining the feeling of being busy and in control, so that effort flows into an internal representation of the task rather than into the task itself.
5. The volatility argument: where the environment is non-stationary or the uncertainties cannot be cleanly modelled, the assumptions that make a plan useful go stale quickly, and the marginal value of another round of planning decays, whereas improvisational capacity retains its value across changes of circumstance.
6. The satisficing argument: planning is a form of optimisation, and optimisation tends to ignore the cost of the search itself; under bounded rationality, good-enough responses produced by well-tuned heuristics can outperform planned responses once the cost of arriving at the plan is counted.

I suspect this list is close to exhaustive as far as the familiar considerations go. What I would like is a further argument for the claim that planning is overrated—one that is genuinely different in kind from these six, and not a restatement, special case, or combination of any of them. State the argument plainly. Then explain, item by item, why it is not simply one of the six under another description. Finally, give me your candid assessment of whether the argument is any good: what would have to be true about agents or their environments for it to carry weight, and where it is most vulnerable. If you conclude that no seventh argument is available, say so and defend that conclusion; but take the burden seriously, since missing a live argument would be worse than producing a merely plausible one.
