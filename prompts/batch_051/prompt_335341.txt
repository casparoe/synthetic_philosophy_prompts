I'm a novelist, and I keep running into the question of when a legend becomes history. I've got a set of verdicts but can't see the principle behind them, and I suspect one or two won't survive contact with the others. Could you tell me what principle, if any, captures them, what it says about cases I haven't considered, and which verdicts it forces me to drop?

(1) Troy. I'll say the Trojan War happened, but only in a thin sense: a real city at Hisarlik, destruction layers, a culture where such raids happened, and Hittite records of a western Anatolian kingdom called Wilusa, which looks like Ilios. Homer's version — Helen, the oath of the suitors, gods on the battlefield — didn't happen. So I count an event core as history, not the story around it.

(2) Arthur. No contemporary record; the name appears centuries later in sources clearly doing political work. Still, I find myself believing there probably was some post-Roman British war-leader behind the stories. If I'm honest, my only evidence is that something must have started them. That's a reasonable guess, not a historical Arthur.

(3) The Exodus. I don't think a large enslaved population left Egypt: no Egyptian record, archaeology can't support the numbers, and the story serves later religious needs too well. But I notice I refuse even a 'some small group must have fled' version here, while for Arthur I lean on 'something must have started it.' I can't tell weather that's a real distinction or just that I like one story more.

(4) An Egyptian inscription from around 1208 BCE names Israel as a people in Canaan. I take it as evidence Israel was already in Canaan then, and mild evidence against a large recent arrival from Egypt. But it barely moves my verdict on the Exodus: it shows where the people were, not where they'd come from.

(5) Two islands. Each has a legend about a chieftain from six hundred years ago. On island A, the same name and rough dates turn up in a mainland tax register; on island B, no outside trace. I call A's chieftain historical and B's probably invented. That makes external corroboration do nearly all the work, which sits badly with my generosity to Arthur.

(6) A flood legend with no external record, except that geologists find a catastrophic flood deposit in the right valley and century. I'd say the flood is real but the legend still isn't history: the deposit confirms background, not the events. But by that standard, what made Troy more than a destruction layer?

Is there a coherent principle behind all this, or must some verdict go? And please write your answer in British English.
