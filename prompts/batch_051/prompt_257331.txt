I've been rewatching Designated Survivor with an eye toward using it in my political philosophy course, and I keep going back and forth on whether the show is actually thinking about the ethics of continuity of government or just using legitimacy as a dramatic obstacle that gets dissolved by character. The setup is a nearly perfect thought experiment: a HUD secretary is made designated survivor as a kind of formality, the entire line of succession is killed at the State of the Union, and he becomes president with no electoral mandate at all. The show keeps raising the question of wether he has any right to govern, but then it answers that question with Tom Kirkman being decent and earnest and pointedly not a politician, which makes me suspect the show is quietly changing the subject. I'd like you to read the show through the philosophy of democratic legitimacy and the ethics of the designated survivor. Where does it get the real problem right — that legal succession settles authority but not legitimacy — and where does it fudge it? In particular, the institution is deliberate (someone is always chosen to be saved) but the show treats Kirkman's survival as almost accidental; does it notice that difference, or does it need to hide it to keep him sympathetic? And what, if anything, does the show add to the debate that an article on succession law wouldn't? Write it as continuous prose, like an email — no headings, bullets, bold, or tables.
