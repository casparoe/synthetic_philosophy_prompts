Analyze Adolf Eichmann's defense at his Jerusalem trial through the lens of Sartre's account of bad faith and radical freedom. I know the standard story: Eichmann presented himself as a mid-level functionary who organized transport and claimed he had no real choice in the matter, and Sartre treats the denial of one's own freedom — the pretense of being a thing with no possible course of action — as the core of bad faith. What I want is a careful mapping. What would Sartre say Eichmann was doing when he treated his position, his orders, and the threat of punishment as a complete alibi? Where does bad faith capture his bureaucratic self-presentation exactly, and where does the theory's individualist vocabulary strain against the coercion, ideological saturation, and diffused responsibility of a totalitarian system? Finally, what does the case show about the theory: is Eichmann a confirmation of Sartre's account, an anomaly it cannot assimilate, or a case that forces the account to be reframed?
