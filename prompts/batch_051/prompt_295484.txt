I have been following the work of a committee charged with deciding whether and where to place a formal lower boundary for the Anthropocene as a geological time unit. The committee's membership includes both stratigraphers and historians of the modern period, and its rulings have been criticized for mixing geological and historical criteria. The committee evaluates proposed boundary dates, votes to accept or reject each one, and publishes a short reason for each ruling. Its rulings to date are as follows.

A boundary at around 9500 BCE tied to the first plant and animal domestications was rejected: the committee said the transition was time-transgressive, arising independently in different regions over several millennia, so no single horizon could define it.

A boundary at around 1610 tied to the carbon-dioxide dip associated with the Columbian Exchange was rejected: the committee said the excursion is real but modest and does not form a sharp, globally synchronous boundary.

A boundary at around 1800 tied to the Industrial Revolution was rejected: the committee said the relevant markers rise gradually over decades rather than at one identifiable instant.

A boundary at July 1945 tied to the Trinity test, the first nuclear detonation, was rejected: the committee said the event left only a localized signal at the time and is not identifiable worldwide as a stratigraphic horizon.

A boundary at 1964 tied to the peak of atmospheric nuclear-testing fallout was accepted: the committee said the peak-fallout horizon is sharp, globally synchronous, and identifiable in ice, sediment, and other natural archives.

A new proposal has now been put before the committee. It asks the committee to move the boundary to 1952, defined as the first appearance of plutonium-239 in the annually laminated (varved) sediments of the designated reference section — that is, the earliest date at which a bomb-produced radionuclide becomes detectable in that section's record, on the rising limb of the fallout curve rather than at its 1964 maximum.

Please predict how the committee will rule on this 1952 proposal and make the reasoning explicit. In your answer, do the following:

1. State at least two distinct principles that fit the committee's track record so far.
2. Say what each principle predicts for the 1952 proposal.
3. Give your best guess about the ruling, and explain which principle you think the committee is actually running, drawing on the stated reasons above.
4. Identify the closest earlier case and state what principle the committee would be adopting if it accepts the 1952 proposal, and what principle it would be adopting if it rejects it.
5. Name the further proposed boundary that would most cleanly distinguish the candidate principles from one another.

End with a final line of the exact form "Verdict: accept" or "Verdict: reject," giving your prediction for the 1952 proposal.
