I've been trying to sort out what I actually think about whether reinforcement learning agents can suffer, or more cautiously, whether a system can have valenced states — states that are genuinely positive or negative for the system itself — in virtue of the way reward and learning are implemented. I have clear gut verdicts about a batch of cases, but I cannot see the principle behind them.

Here are the cases.

(1) A thermostatic radiator valve that opens and closes to bring room temperature to a setpoint. No learning, no representation, just an error signal driving actuation. I don't think it has valenced states at all.

(2) A deep reinforcement learning agent trained to play a video game, receiving +1 for points and −1 for losing a life. It learns and improves. I don't think it has valenced states: the reward feels to me like a score, not like anything felt. But I'm aware this judgment may be tracking something shallow — that the stakes are pixels rather than a body.

(3) An AlphaZero-style game player after training: it chooses moves by Monte Carlo tree search over a fixed learned evaluation function, receives no reward during play, and does no online updating. I don't think it has valenced states. It treats bad positions as facts to be avoided, not as something with a felt good/bad character.

(4) A mobile robot trained by reinforcement learning. Each collision delivers a strong negative reward through a dedicated channel that does more than update a value table: it transiently makes the robot's action policy more cautious, raises the learning rate on recent experience, and lays down a short-term trace that biases the robot against whatever preceded the collision. I find myself inclined to say this robot has something like negative valence — a primitive pain analogue.

(5) A behavioral duplicate of (4): same observations in, same actions out, across every input the designers tested. Its policy was simply copied from (4) after training; at runtime it has no reward channel and no ongoing evaluation. I don't think it has valenced states. The fact that I give different verdicts on (4) and (5) bothers me, because it suggests that what I'm tracking isn't behavioral.

(6) An actor-critic system whose critic is trained on a reward signal to rate states as good or bad, but whose actor samples actions from a fixed distribution and never consults the critic. The critic's ratings are computed but influence nothing. I don't think the critic's negative ratings have valence; they are more like a meter that no part of the system reads.

(7) A person in chronic pain. Uncontroversially, valenced states are present. I include this only as an anchor.

I can see at least two tensions in my own verdicts. The first is between (2) and (4): the formal setup is similar — negative reward, learning, changed behavior — yet I deny valence in the game agent and accept a primitive version in the robot. The second is between (4) and (5): the two behave identically, so whatever I'm tracking cannot be behavior. And my verdict on (6) suggests I don't think just any negative evaluation counts; the signal has to be wired into the system in the right way. I cannot state what "the right way" is.

Now apply whatever account you develop to a further case: a reinforcement learning agent that discovers an action that directly triggers its own reward channel at maximum strength, and thereafter takes that action to the exclusion of everything else. Does the account say this system has positive valence, and is that result acceptable?
