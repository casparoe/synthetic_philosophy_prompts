OK here's a nerdy one I need help with. I'm putting together a list of cases where a mathematical definition got chosen not because it was the obvious pre-theoretic notion but because it was the right generalization of some informal idea -- and the interesting part is pinning down what 'right' means in each case. The standard examples I've already got are:

(1) Open sets / topological spaces. ε-δ continuity in metric spaces gets reframed in terms of open sets, so you define a topology as a family of sets closed under arbitrary unions and finite intersections. The job is to keep continuity meaningful once there's no metric to lean on.

(2) Groups. The axioms pick out the common core of permutation groups, symmetry groups of shapes, and additive structures on number systems. The job is to capture invertible transformations.

(3) Compactness via open covers. Closed-and-bounded works in R^n but it's tied to a metric and isn't preserved by homeomorphisms. The open-cover definition is chosen because it's intrinsic to the space and makes the theorems you actually want true (Heine–Borel becomes a theorem rather than a definition, continuous images of compact sets are compact).

(4) Smooth manifolds via atlases. You define smoothness through charts and the compatibility of transition maps, so calculus on curved spaces doesn't depend on a choice of coordinates.

(5) Turing computability. The formal definition of a computable function is chosen so that the informal notion of a mechanical procedure gets the same extension across Turing machines, partial recursive functions, and the lambda calculus.

I want one more example that isnt already covered by anything on that list, and isn't a variant, special case, or mashup of them. In particular I'm bored of two criteria of rightness that already show up: 'it makes the old theorems work in the new setting' (that's most of (3)) and 'several formalisms converge to the same class' (that's (5)). If you've got an example where the right definition was later revised because it still wasn't right enough, I'd love that, but only if you're confident it's real.
