I've been arguing with a friend who loves the gene's-eye view. He keeps saying organisms are basically throwaway vechicles that genes build to get themselves copied, and he leans on this line from George C. Williams, Adaptation and Natural Selection: "[t]he natural selection of phenotypes cannot in itself produce cumulative change, because phenotypes are extremely temporary manifestations." His gloss is: bodies are just the machine part, genes are the persistent thing doing the evolving, so worrying about organisms as units of adaptation is like worrying about whether the car or the driver is really making the trip. I want you to act as an expert in philosophy of biology and evolutionary theory, think carefully, and start by brainstorming a correspondence table for this machine/vehicle analogy: what maps to the vehicle, the engine, the driver, the factory, the route, the product, the waste? Then audit how far it holds. Where does the analogy import features from engineered machines that organisms don't have—planned design, stable parts, a separate builder and built thing? Where do critics of the gene's-eye view say the vehicle metaphor obscures things like multi-level selection, niche construction, or the way a phenotype is less a separate vehicle and more a developmental process? Finally, tell me whether Williams's actual point survives if we drop the vehicle imagery, and whether my friend's larger conclusion—that organism-level selection is just a confused level of descripion—depends on the disanalogy, or on the argument Williams is actually giving.
