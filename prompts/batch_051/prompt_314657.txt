I'm a backend engineer with no formal philosophy background, working on a small feature for an e-reader app: it flags claims in user reviews and forum posts as true, false, or unsuported, using the text of the book where it can. The spoiler-detection side of the feature is its own mess; right now I'm just on the truth-labeling part, and fictional characters keep producing edge cases. So I've been writing down my own gut verdicts to see whether there's one rule underneath them. My verdicts: "Sherlock Holmes is a detective" is true. "Sherlock Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street" is true. "Sherlock Holmes has a sister named Enola" is false — that's from spinoff books, not the originals. "Sherlock Holmes has fewer than five arms" is true, even though no story ever says it. "Sherlock Holmes's favorite color is green" is unsupported, not false. "Holmes is smarter than Hercule Poirot" is also unsupported, since they never show up in the same story. "Sherlock Holmes exists" is false. But "Sherlock Holmes is a fictional character" is true, and "Holmes is more famous than any real detective of that period" is true.

My first attempt at a rule was: true if the text says it, false if the text says the opposite, unsupported otherwise. That rule dies immediately (the arms one, the Enola one), and I can see I'm treating two text-says-nothing cases differently — Enola and favorite-color — without being sure there's a real difference. I don't know the philosophy names for the positions here, and I don't want reading recommendations or citations; I want a rule I can implement. Please give me the principle (or a small set of candidate principles) that captures these verdicts, say which of my examples forced each part of it, apply it to "the present King of France is bald" and "Sherlock Holmes has a birthmark on his left shoulder," and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it can't keep and which one I should drop first.
