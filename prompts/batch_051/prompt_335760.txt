Im writing a paper trying to read Le Guin's 'The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas' as negative-utilitarian or at least suffering-focused fiction, and I keep going back and forth on whether the story actually commits to that or just uses the child as a standard utilitarian counterexample. The easy reading I keep hearing is that the city is one big argument that no amount of general happiness can outweigh one child's misery, and the people who walk away are just drawing that conclusion. But I don't think Le Guin ever says the child's suffering is lexically prior to the happiness, and she spends a lot of the story making Omelas feel genuinely good rather than deluded or hollow. Could you read the story through that lens and be honest about where it fits and where it strains? In particular: would a weak versus a strong negative utilitarian read the ending differently; are the ones who leave acting on suffering-focused grounds or just on a principle against benefiting from injustice; and does the story actually show that happiness can't compensate for suffering, or does it stack the deck by making the suffering hidden and unconsenting? Please keep straight what's settled about the philosophy and what's genuinely contested, and dont present one contested interpretation as the consensus.
