In Immanuel Kant’s Perpetual Peace (1795), two lines of argument seem to pull in different directions, and I want to know whether they can be made consistent. I am working without a reliable English translation in front of me, so I will paraphrase the relevant passages.

In the Second Definitive Article, Kant argues that nations, like individuals in a state of nature, must leave their lawless freedom and submit to public lawful coercion; according to reason there is no way out of their mutual hostilities except a state of peoples, a civitas gentium, that would ultimately include all peoples. But he immediately adds that states are unwilling to adopt this solution, and that in place of the positive idea of a world republic we must settle for the negative substitute of a federation or league of free states that prevents war but cannot compel its members. Later, in the First Supplement, he says that nature itself guarantees perpetual peace through the very self-interest and unsocial sociability that make peace initially difficult, even independently of moral improvement.

Those passages seem to require different things. The world-republic passage makes enforceable public law among states look like the only fully rational solution; the federation passage says we must accept something unenforceable; and the nature passage says the outcome is guaranteed by the same inclinations that produce conflict.

Can these be reconciled without special pleading? In particular: Is the federation of free states Kant’s actual proposal, or a second-best that he accepts only because the world republic is unattainable and whose rational standing he therefore regards as defective? Does nature’s guarantee make the moral demand to establish peace redundant, or does it instead supply the empirical condition under which the moral task becomes feasible? And is Kant entitled to say, as he appears to, that permanent peace is both a duty we must bring about and an end that nature will produce mechanically even for a nation of intelligent devils?

Give the strongest reading on which the passages are consistent, then identify where, if anywhere, that reading strains the text. Begin with a one-sentence bottom-line verdict—consistent, inconsistent, or only partially consistent—and then argue for it. Maximum 800 words for your response, not counting the passage I paraphrased.
