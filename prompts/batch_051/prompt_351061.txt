I'm in a philosophy of religion class, and a grad student I study with made an argument I've been unable to shake. I'll reconstruct it as faithfully as I can:

(1) In everyday life, if someone tells me they saw a dog in the quad, and there's no special reason to doubt them — they're not known liars, they aren't the sort to hallucinate, there's no motive to mislead — then it's rational for me to treat their report as evidence that there was a dog in the quad. That's just how testimony works.
(2) People who report religious experiences describe them as direct, first-hand encounters with something divine; they are mostly not mentally ill or lying, and they report these experiences with the same kind of lived detail and confidence as ordinary perceptual reports.
(3) These reports aren't rare: they show up in huge numbers across otherwise unrelated cultures and historical periods.
(4) So if I'm applying the same standard to both kinds of report, I should treat religious experience as evidence that the object of those experiences — some kind of divine reality — exists.
(5) Therefore religious experience is evidence for God, and someone who dismisses it is holding religious testimony to a higher bar than ordinary testimony.

I half suspect the argument is fine and I just don't like the conclusion because I'm not religious. But something in the move from (1) to (4) feels like a bait-and-switch — maybe the word evidence is doing different work, or maybe the divine object isn't the sort of thing that testimony can reach in the same way. I cant locate the exact step though. Is the argument valid as stated? If not, tell me exactly which step fails, on what reading of the key term, and what the corrected version of that step would have to say. If it is valid, give me the strongest way to resist it short of blanket skepticism about testimony.
