I'm a PhD student TAing for an introductory philosophy of mind course, and next week the instructor wants me to run one fifty-minute section on Hempel's dilemma for physicalism. The students are second-years who have already met the basic statement of physicalism — roughly, that everything that exists is physical or is exhaustively determined by the physical — but they have not yet been asked to scrutinize the word "physical" itself. I am not asking you to explain the dilemma to me; I can state it. I'm asking you to design the sequence in which I should explain it to them. What has to be in place before the dilemma can even grip them, and in what order should the two horns be introduced, and what does each horn depend on in their understanding? I want one carrying example or case that runs through the whole session — I'm tempted to use phlogiston, but I'm not sure whether it does the right work for both horns — and I'd like you to tell me what the ideal example must have and must lack.

Could you also map the misconceptions that second-years predictably bring or form: I expect at least one student to say the dilemma is just a verbal dispute, another to conclude that physicalism is thereby refuted, and another to try to define "physical" as "made of matter" and miss the point entirely. At exactly which point in the sequence should each misconception be headed off, and how do I head it off without simply asserting the right view? Then give me one question I can pose in the final five minutes that would show whether a student has actually grasped the structure of the dilemma, as opposed to repeating the slogan. Finally, tell me what I should deliberately leave out at this level and how to flag that I'm leaving it out.
