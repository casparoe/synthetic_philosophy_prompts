I'm in a metaphysics course and we spent two weeks on endurantism vs. perdurantism. When I tried to explain it to my roommate he waved his hand and said it's obvious: a persisting thing just is a kind of four-dimensional worm made of temporal parts, the way a road is spread across space. The road isn't moving, the road doesn't have a single 'here' that somehow travels along it, and talking about a road 'changing' would be a category mistake — you just see different segments of it as you drive. He said once you see a person as that kind of worm, the whole problem of change dissappears; there's no single thing that is wholly present at different times and has incompatible properties, there are just different temporal bits with different properties, like different stretches of road with different asphalt. His conclusion was that perdurantism is basically forced and endurantists are just in the grip of a bad picture.

I'm not convinced, but I can't put my finger on where the correspondence breaks. I want a proper audit of the analogy. What maps onto what: the road, the spatial segments, the driver, the journey, maybe the fact that the road was built one section at a time? Then where does the mapping fail? I half-remember that the parts of a road all coexist at the same time, but temporal parts by definition don't, so comparing a spatial whole to a temporal whole might be smuggling in exactly the thing at issue. But maybe that disanalogy doesn't matter for the conclusion he wanted. Does the 'change is dissolved' conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or is the analogy carrying it the whole time? And if the road is a bad guide here, is there a better everyday analogy for what a perdurantist actually claims?
