An adversarial collaboration is being drafted between two positions in the law and epistemology of statistical evidence. Let Position A be the view that naked statistical evidence — evidence that establishes only that the defendant belongs to a class within which some proportion is guilty or liable, and that offers nothing tying the defendant to the particular event — cannot by itself justify a legal finding, no matter how high it pushes the probability. Let Position B be the view that naked statistical evidence is evidence like any other, and that if it raises the probability of the material proposition to whatever the governing standard of proof requires (preponderance or beyond a reasonable doubt, as the case may be), it can in principle justify a verdict. Both sides grant that the blue bus case (80 percent market share, no further facts) and the gatecrasher case (a bare proportion among the possible wrongdoers) strike most people as insufficient; they part ways over whether that reaction reveals a genuine epistemic or normative difference, or merely a psychological resistance to acting on unvivid, class-based information.

Write the joint statement this collaboration should produce — the constructive document both sign, with the residual disagreement made exact — not a survey or an adjudication. It should include the following.

(1) A numbered list of substantive claims both camps can sign. Avoid truce language and vacuous formulations. The claims should cover, at minimum: what the two canonical hypotheticals establish about each side's position; what is and is not being claimed about the probability calculus itself; what the law's verdicts do and do not assert, if anything, about the defendant beyond probable truth; and any structural constraints the two sides agree a satisfactory account must respect.

(2) A disagreement table. For each residual disagreement, state the two positions in wording each side would accept as a fair statement of its own view, and separately mark any disagreement you judge to be merely verbal. Where the camps have been talking past one another, say how the point should be rephrased so both recognize the same question.

(3) The cruxes: the sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side. For each crux, specify a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts about it. Among the cruxes to consider are: whether the difference between naked statistical and individualized evidence is best explained by sensitivity, safety, robustness, explanatory or causal connection to the event, or some non-quantitative notion of support that tolerates only non-accidental exceptions; whether the skeptical side's resistance survives as the probability approaches one in a case with a single actual wrongdoer; whether the way agents rely on statistical evidence in prudential, medical, or professional settings has the same justificatory structure as a legal verdict; and whether the asymmetry is best located in the law's commitment to individualized blame and responsibility rather than in a requirement of evidential accuracy. Indicate any cruxes that appear live and any that appear dead, by each side's own lights.

(4) For each camp, a short statement of what it would count as evidence or argument against its own view — what observation, case, or conceptual result it would have to concede as a genuine cost — and an assessment of whether either position has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a defect here.

Throughout, separate what is genuinely settled from what remains contested among people who know this debate, and do not present either side's reconstruction of the cases as the field's consensus view. Where the dispute depends on an empirical claim about intuitive reactions, a normative claim about what the law is for, or an epistemic claim about how beliefs are supported, say so explicitly.
