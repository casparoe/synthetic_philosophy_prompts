I teach moral philosophy, and a question that keeps recurring in my seminars is not simply whether a piece of criticism is true, but whether the critic is in a position to make it — what moral philosophers call standing to blame. I have been collecting my own verdicts on concrete cases, and I cannot yet state the principle behind them, so I want to test whether a clean principle can be extracted from them, and at what cost. Here are the cases, with my honest reactions to each.

Marcus is a lawyer who has quietly padded his billable hours for years. A freelance editor working on one of his cases adds a few hours to an invoice, and Marcus sends her a sharp letter accusing her of overbilling. I think what he says about her is true, but I also think he is in no position to say it. Leila is an alcoholic who has been sober for fifteen years, has been open about her history, and now sponsors others. When her sister hides her drinking from her family, Leila confronts her and insists she stop. I think Leila's past does not weaken her standing; if anything it strengthens it. Tom cheated on his taxes decades ago, was caught, paid everything back with penalties, and has since spent years volunteering in a free tax clinic. When he now condemns a neighbor who boasts about dodging taxes, I think Tom has every right to do so. Rachel has smoked half a pack a day for twenty years and, when her teenage daughter comes home with cigarettes, delivers an earnest lecture about how self-destructive smoking is. I do not think Rachel has no standing at all, but I would not say her standing is fully intact; something in the lecture is off, and I want an account of exactly what has been lost. Gwen habitually drives well over the speed limit and sees nothing wrong with it. When she condemns a neighbor for hiding income from tax authorities, I do not think her speeding bears on her standing at all; faults that are unconnected to the wrong being blamed seem to me to leave standing untouched. Finally, Evan fabricated data in his graduate work decades ago, never admitted it, and now tries not to think about it. When he denounces a local teenager caught plagiarizing for a school essay, I find that I do not think he is in a position to do so, even though his own offense lies far in the past. This last case unsettles me most, because it seems to put pressure on any simple story about current conduct.

My best rough principle is that a person loses standing to blame for a wrong when his blame would be a performative contradiction: he invokes a norm he does not, right now, treat as binding on himself. That would explain Marcus clearly, and it would explain why Gwen's speeding is irrelevant; but it does not explain why Rachel is only partly disqualified rather than wholly disqualified, and it does not distinguish Evan from Tom and Leila, since none of them is currently violating the norm they invoke. I am unsure whether the principle is too simple or one of my verdicts has to go. State the most defensible principle of standing to blame that you can extract from, or impose upon, these responses. Show precisely how it handles each of my six cases. Then tell me what it predicts about at least two further cases I have not listed — for instance, a complete stranger who berates a parent for slapping a child in a supermarket, and a famous anti-corruption campaigner who is secretly taking bribes while denouncing embezzlement. Finish by saying honestly which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether the more rational move is to revise the verdict or to accept a more complex principle.
