I keep running into "neglectedness" as one of the three standard filters for comparing causes — importance, tractability, and neglectedness — but I have never found a statement of it that survives contact with messy cases. I want a definition precise enough that I can apply it to a new cause without falling back on a vague sense of recognition.

Here are the cases I want the definition to handle:

- A rare tropical disease with a few thousand patients, no dedicated research fund, and no advocacy organization. I take this to be a clear case of neglectedness.
- A cause that attracts little money or attention because it is widely and correctly believed to be nearly intractable — say, averting a particular kind of large asteroid impact with current technology. I am genuinely unsure whether this counts: it is ignored, but extra money probably would not help.
- Climate change mitigation. I take this to be a clear case of non-neglectedness on any useful definition.
- An area with abundant funding but a severe shortage of trained people who could absorb more — a field where every grant round struggles to find competent reviewers and labs. Is this neglected for talent but not for money, or just bottlenecked?
- A large field in which nearly all funding and attention flow to one fashionable sub-question while an adjacent, comparably important sub-question receives almost none. The field as a whole is not neglected; the sub-question may be.
- A cause with no obvious neglectedness on paper — money, attention, and talent all exist — but the existing resources are locked into an approach everyone privately suspects is dead, and nobody is funding the alternative. Is that neglect or just misallocation?
- A cause that is obscure and underfunded but has no organizations capable of absorbing a large influx of money; the field does not yet exist as a field. Does a cause need to be able to use resources before it counts as neglected?

I suspect the term is doing at least two jobs: one descriptive, tracking how little attention or funding a cause receives relative to its importance, and one instrumental, tracking how much extra progress an additional dollar or hour would buy. The intractable-asteroid case and the no-absorptive-capacity case pull those apart. Give me the definition or definitions you would actually use for cause prioritization, apply it to each case, and say explicitly where it forces a verdict you did not expect or where you need to revise it.
