I'm on a small commission that over the years has had to rule on whether various entities count as legal persons for limited purposes. Our own precedents are: a closely held corporation (1987) was allowed to sue in its own name for breach of contract, because it has a seperate pool of assets and liabilities from its shareholders; a deceased person's estate (1996) could be sued as a person for the decedent's debts, since the estate is a distinct fund with an executor to act for it; a river (2004) was granted standing as a legal person in a pollution case, with a guardian appointed to represent it; an unincorporated neighborhood association (2011) was denied personhood to own its clubhouse, because it had no registered constitution and no distinct treasury, so its members just owned the property jointly; and a chimpanzee (2018) was denied habeas corpus, because whatever it may feel, it cannot bear legal duties and so cannot be a rights-bearer in our system.

Now we have a petition from a decentralized autonomous organization that operates through a smart contract, with its trading decisions made by an AI agent. It holds a segregated pool of assets, nobody can alter its decisions after deployment, and it has been entering contracts in its own name. The petitioner's brief leans hard on the corporate-person analogy, arguing the thing is just a corporation with code where a board of directors would be. We need to decide whether to recognize it as a legal person for the purpose of being sued on those contracts.

I want a decision that hangs together with our precedents, with the closest analog flagged, and if the precedents underdetermine it, an honest statement of which principle we'd be adopting with each choice.
