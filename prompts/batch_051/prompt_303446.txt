I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about what is actually lost when a language dies in an age of large language models. The standard argument I keep encountering among linguists and language-preservation advocates runs something like this. Each language is a distinct conceptual scheme — a way of dividing up space, time, kinship, color, causation, and number — and when a language disappears, a unique and irreplaceable way of organizing human experience disappears with it. The loss is therefore supposed to fall on humanity as a whole, not only on the last speakers. What interests me is that the technological situation has shifted beneath this argument. We can now assemble large corpora of endangered languages, transcribe them, translate their distinctions into major languages with increasing fidelity, and even train models to produce fluent text in a language after no living community speaks it on a daily basis. If what matters is conceptual diversity — preserving a scheme of distinctions and patterns of thought — then a well-documented archive plus a competent model looks as though it should preserve most of the relevant object, much as a library preserves the ideas of a dead author.

What I am after is a precise account of whether the standard argument survives that development, needs repair, or quietly collapses into a different claim — namely, that what is worth preserving is not the language as a system of concepts but the living community of practice in which it is used. Could you walk me through what the argument has to be true in order to retain its force in this setting, and then assess whether machine translation and large language models genuinely leave those premises untouched or merely make the losses easier to ignore? I am interested in what has happened to the argument itself, not in the policy conclusions one might draw for language revitalization.
