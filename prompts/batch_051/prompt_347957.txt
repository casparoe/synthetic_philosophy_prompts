As a retired scientist I keep returning to an argument that cannot be right, and I would like to know precisely where it goes wrong.

Consider a fair lottery with one thousand tickets and exactly one winner.

(1) For any given ticket, the probability that it will lose is 0.999.
(2) A proposition that is 99.9% likely is one I am justified in believing outright. If 0.999 seems too low, enlarge the lottery until the probability exceeds any threshold short of certainty you care to propose; the structure of the argument is unchanged.
(3) Therefore, for each ticket, I am justified in believing that that ticket will lose.
(4) If I am justified in believing one proposition and justified in believing another, then I am justified in believing their conjunction.
(5) By repeated application of (4), I am justified in believing that ticket 1 will lose and ticket 2 will lose and ... and ticket 1000 will lose.
(6) I know that exactly one ticket will win, so I am justified in believing that it is not the case that all tickets lose.
(7) Therefore I am justified in believing a proposition and its negation.

Each step seems sound to me, yet (7) strikes me as unacceptable. Is the argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails, and why?

My first thought was that (2) is the culprit: high probability is not certainty, and perhaps a probabilistic ground never licenses flat-out belief. But then I considered what I am told is called the preface paradox. An author completes a careful book containing two hundred factual claims. Each claim, checked individually, is justified for her; she believes each one. Yet her long experience tells her that a long book of factual claims almost always contains at least one error, however careful the author. So in the preface she writes that she is confident the book contains at least one mistake. It seems she is justified in each of the two hundred claims and also justified in the second-order claim that at least one of them is false. Applying the same closure step as in (4) and (5), she would be justified in the conjunction of all two hundred claims and in its negation.

Here is what I want to know. Do the lottery argument and the preface argument fail at the same step, or do they require different diagnoses? Does the preface case show that weakening or rejecting (2) is not enough, because it can go through without any threshold for probable belief? And if the right response is to reject closure of justification under conjunction, please say exactly which closure principle remains, and how it permits ordinary reasoning that combines justified beliefs while blocking these cases.
