Hey, I'm a PhD student in atmospheric science and I've gotten weirdly obsessed with the governance side of stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI), so I'd love a short story that actually lives in the details rather than just doing "we geoengineered the sky and fixed the planet."

Set it around 2055 or so. A coalition of large emitters has been running an SAI program for about fifteen years—modified planes lofting sulfate aerosols into the lower stratosphere to hold warming near 1.5C. Emissions never really fell, partly because the program existed as a kind of background insurance, and that's the moral hazard I want you to make felt.

Then put the system in danger of stopping. Maybe a war disrupts the supply chain for the injection fleet, or a UN vote demands it be shut down, or a monitoring station fails at the worst time, whatever feels most alive to you. The key thing is termination shock: if the injections stop abruptly, all that supressed warming comes roaring back in maybe a decade or two—faster than agriculture and ecosystems can adapt. I want the reader to feel how the planet has become, in effect, dependent on a machine that nobody really agreed to in the first place.

Center it on a character who has to decide or defend something: an engineer, a diplomat, a scientist whose early models made the program possible. Show the inequality too—some regions are getting less monsoon rain because of the aerosols, while other regions are quietly benefiting. Who decides what "safe" means, and what counts as consent when the effects are global and uneven? Don't lecture; let the choices and conversations carry it. End on an actual decision, even if its consequences stay unresolved.
