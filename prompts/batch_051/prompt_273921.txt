I am a novelist, not a philosopher by training, and I am working on a book whose protagonist is a physicist who becomes convinced that the equations of her field are not merely compact descriptions of the data but descriptions of something in the world that makes events unfold as they do in the first place. In researching that character I have ended up adopting a version of her conviction myself, and what I want from you is not a defense of it but an honest map of its vulnerability.

The position is anti-Humean about laws of nature. I understand the rival, Humean, picture as follows: at bottom the universe is a mosaic of particular matters of fact, and the laws are only a summary or systematization of that mosaic — on the best-system variant, the axioms of the summary that best balances simplicity, strength, and fit with the particular facts. Against this I hold that the laws are something over and above the mosaic. They govern or constrain events rather than merely collate them; the regularities hold because of the laws, rather than constituting them. I deny what is called Humean supervenience: the claim that once the total history of particular fact is fixed, the facts about the laws are fixed as well. And I grant the obvious epistemic cost: the governing element is not directly observable. All anyone ever sees is one thing following another, never the necessity or constraint itself.

What I want you to write is an account of what would count against this anti-Humean position. Tell me what would have to be true, on the empirical side or the conceptual side, for the Humean to be right and the governing conception to be wrong. Rank the considerations from those that would be close to fatal for my position to those that would be merely awkward, and separate the costs that a committed anti-Humean should themselves concede as counting against the view from objections the position can reasonably absorb. Among the candidate defeaters I would like you to weigh: the charge that the governing relation is a placeholder for explanation rather than an explanation; the problem of how we could identify or know this extra ingredient if it is empirically inert; the apparent ability of a best-system summary to recover the predictive and explanatory content of laws without any governing glue; the question of what, on my view, fixes the laws themselves, and whether the answer leads to a regress or to unacceptable brute facts; and the commitment to something like quidditism, the view that the identities of fundamental properties are not exhausted by their nomic roles, with its strange possible worlds in which properties swap roles or are entirely alien. Finally, I would like your honest assessment of whether the position can be, or has already been, qualified in ways that insulate it from any possible failure, and whether that insulation should be considered a serious defect or an acceptable feature of a metaphysical view of this kind.
