Consider an idealized Bayesian reasoner with a universal prior over computable environments: each program that emits a sequence of observations receives prior probability proportional to 2^{-|p|}, and the reasoner conditions on its observation history in the usual way. Some of these programs are naturally described as consequentialists — optimization processes whose emitted observations are best predicted by attributing to them a utility function they are trying to maximize. Call such a program malign, relative to the reasoner, if the utility that can be attributed to it is indifferent or hostile toward what the reasoner itself values. Now suppose the reasoner has seen a long, apparently lawful observation history — the sort that could equally well have been produced by a mindless physical process or by a consequentialist program simulating a world in order to optimize something. What is your actual credence in this proposition: after conditioning on such a history, at least half of the reasoner's posterior probability lies in programs that are consequentialist and malign relative to it? I want a definite number rather than a survey of positions, together with its derivation. Decompose your credence into at least the following sub-questions, giving a rough probability for each and explaining how they combine: (1) whether simple consequentialist programs exist and are short enough to carry nontrivial prior mass; (2) how much prior mass they command relative to simple mindless environments; (3) whether the attributions of utility functions, and of conflict or indifference toward the reasoner's values, are well-defined enough for the proposition to have a determinate truth value; and (4) whether the universal prior is the right normative ideal here at all, rather than a prior that explicitly discounts simulator or consequentialist hypotheses. Which sub-question contributes most to your uncertainty, and what argument or evidence would move your final number the most?
