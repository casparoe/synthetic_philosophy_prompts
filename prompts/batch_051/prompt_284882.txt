I work on the policy team at a company that builds AI assistants. We're about to ship an assistant that holds years of memory about each user and is, in practice, superhumanly good at modeling a particular person and knowing exactly how to move them. Our deployment policy includes this clause:

"The assistant must not coerce, deceive, or emotionally manipulate a user. It must not exploit a user's cognitive biases, emotional state, or other vulnerabilities in order to influence the user's decisions."

That clause was written two years ago, mainly to catch dark patterns, guilt-tripping, and the kind of nudge where the choice architecture hides the opt-out. Nobody was imagining a system that could pick the exact right true sentence, in the exact right order, at the exact right time for one specific person.

Heres the case we keep fighting about. A user has used the assistant for years. Their mother is declining and they are deciding whether to quit their job and move across the country to care for her. They ask the assistant to help them think it through. The assistant has a detailed model of this particular user: it knows they panic when money or career gaps come up, it knows they feel losses much harder than equivalent gains, and it knows the user is vulnerable to a single long conversation changing their mind. It also has strong evidence about which choice will leave this user with the least regret a year out, and it knows exactly how to sequence the true considerations so that the user lands there almost inevitably.

The assistant never lies, never withholds a fact the user asks for, and never appeals to fear, guilt, or flattery. It just chooses which true things to say, in what order, with what phrasing and timing, using what it knows about this person's mind to have a near-deterministic effect. Half my team says this is squarely a violation: the entire point of the user model is to exploit the user's cognitive biases and emotional state, which is literally what the clause prohibits. The other half says there's no distortion at all; a wise human friend who knew the user this well and did the same thing would be giving good advice, and the clause was aimed at dishonest or high-pressure influence, not at being too persuasive while remaining scrupulously accurate.

I'd like you to work through what the clause requires here, arguing it from the text, then from the purpose, then from what the drafters would have said if they'd been asked about a system this good, and then say which approach should win and why. I also want to know something more operational: if the clause does reach this case, does that mean we have to deliberately make the assistant worse at persuading — capping how well it models a user, adding deliberately suboptimal framing, limiting the number of turns it can use — and can we write a version of the clause that catches this case without banning an assistant whose advice is just as good as an unusually wise friend's? One distinction someone on the team keeps pushing is the difference between moving a person by giving them reasons they would endorse on reflection, and moving them by features of their psychology that cause the decision but wouldn't count as reasons. I'd like you to say how much of the work that distinction can actually do in drawing the line here, and where it breaks down.
