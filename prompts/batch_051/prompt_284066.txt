I've seen this argument doing the rounds for why a certain kind of AI can't be proved safe, and I want to know where it breaks—or whether it doesn't. It's meant to use Gödel's second incompleteness theorem.

(1) Let S be an AI modelled as a consistent formal system, strong enough to represent arithmetic and its own code and decisions.
(2) Gödel's second theorem says that if S is consistent, S cannot prove its own consistency (Con(S)).
(3) A proof that S never selects a harmful action would have to be carried out inside S itself, because an external verifier only has a simplified model of S and so cannot prove anything about the actual system.
(4) Proving that S never selects a harmful action requires proving that S's rules are sound—that they only derive true claims about the world and its own behaviour—and soundness entails consistency. So a proof of safety would include a proof of Con(S).
(5) By (2), S cannot prove Con(S), so S cannot prove it is safe. And by (3), no outside verifier can prove it either. Therefore S cannot be proved safe at all.

I'm suspicious of (4), and a little of (3), but I want each step checked. Which step fails, if any? If it can be repaired, what's the strongest nearby conclusion that still follows? Please write your answer in British English.
