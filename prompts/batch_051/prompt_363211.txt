I'm putting together a handout for an aesthetics reading group on imaginative resistance — the fact that people will happily accept that a story suspends the laws of physics, but balk when the story tells them a character did the right thing by, say, drowning her infant. I want a quiz that sorts readers into the explanation they actually hold, not the explanation they think sounds most sophisticated. The angle I want baked into the questions is that resistance can be located at different stages of engaging with a fiction: (a) importing the proposition into the fictional world at all, (b) going along with the evaluative stance it comes wrapped in, (c) reconstructing what the author meant or endorsed by writing it that way, or (d) generating the relevant imagery or affect. So the questions should be designed so that people who all feel "I wouldn't accept that in the story" come apart according to which stage is doing the work for them.

Make it eight to ten questions, each a short concrete vignette, with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto at least these positions: a strong impossibility view, an unwillingness view, an authorial-misfire view, a no-unified-phenomenon view, and an "I basically resist nothing" position. Add more leaves if the answer combinations force them, and mark any combinations that end up unoccupied, saying briefly whether that's because the view looks incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Include at least one question where the deviance is aesthetic rather than moral (a story insists a tinny jingle is the most beautiful music ever written), one for contradictions or conceptual impossibilities, to test whether those get resisted too, and one with a repulsive narrator in a genre that already signals unreliability, to see whether genre cues loosen the resistance. Phrase every question so someone can answer it without knowing the labels; no questions like "do you think resistance is a matter of ability?" Also flag any two positions your questions can't cleanly separate and suggest a tie-breaker question for each such pair.

After the quiz itself, say how confident you are in the scoring key and the one-sentence descriptions of each position, and single out the point you're least sure about. For format: give the quiz as a normal readable list first, then append a JSON object with these fields: "questions" (array of objects with "q" for the vignette and "options" for the answer choices), "scoring_key" (mapping each leaf position to the answer pattern that points to it), "positions" (array with "name", "summary", and "awkward_commitment"), and "unoccupied_combinations" (array with "pattern" and "diagnosis").
