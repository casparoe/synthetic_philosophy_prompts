I am a novelist writing a series that follows a single character from girlhood to old age, and I have hit a difficulty that feels less like craft and more like philosophy. In the final volume, the old woman clearly remembers the middle-aged woman she was; the middle-aged woman clearly remembers the girl of the first volume; but the old woman no longer remembers the girl. The books treat all three as one person, and I am confident readers will too. Looking at how I produce this effect, I see that I have hung the character's identity on an overlapping chain of remembered episodes, and that this is exactly the structure of Reid's brave officer objection to Locke's memory criterion: the old general remembers the officer, the officer remembers the flogged boy, the general has lost the boy, so the transitivity of identity combined with the non-transitivity of memory forces Locke's account to say both that the general is the boy and that he is not.

What I want to know is whether this parallel is deep or superficial. Map the two structures. On the Lockean side, what carries identity across the gaps in the chain, and where does the view break? On the side of serial fiction, what carries identity across the gaps—the body, the name, the narrator's continuous perspective, other characters' testimony, the reader's own accumulation, the brute facts of the plot—and why does it seem not to break? Does Reid's objection transfer to narrative: would a story that relied on nothing but an overlapping chain of remembered scenes be unable to make the old woman and the girl one person, and do I save the identity only because I quietly supply something outside the chain? If the analogy is only apparent, I want to know precisely where the structures come apart, and what that gap tells me about the difference between a self's persistence and a character's persistence. And I want the comparison tested, not flattered: state what the parallel predicts about serial fiction and point to the prediction that fails.
