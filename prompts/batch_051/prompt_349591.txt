I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who has recently turned to the philosophy of AI, and I have been unable to settle whether an analogy I keep noticing is substantive or merely verbal. The disagreement over AI takeoff speeds — whether a system able to do AI research would bring on a fast, discontinuous increase in capability or a slow, continuous one — looks to me like the distinction between first-order and second-order phase transitions. In a first-order transition, the control parameter varies smoothly while the order parameter jumps; the pre-transition phase can survive metastably past the transition point, and the change proceeds by nucleation, in which a small fluctuation crosses a free-energy barrier and then propagates. In a second-order transition, the order parameter rises continuously from zero, with growing fluctuations and critical slowing down near the threshold instead of a jump. On the fast-takeoff view, as I read it, capability is the control parameter, the share of AI research carried out by machine systems is the order parameter, and the first system capable of contributing to its own improvement is the nucleating droplet. On the slow-takeoff view, the rise is continuous, and hardware, data, and coordination bottlenecks play the role of critical slowing down that rounds the transition. I would like to know whether this is a genuine structural identity, a loose analogy, or a confusion. Please assess the mapping in detail: where the two pictures match, where they come apart, what would have to be true of AI development for the first-order reading to be more than verbal, and what the analogy predicts that observation should show — including any prediction that appears to fail.
