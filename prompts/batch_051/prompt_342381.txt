I've been working through a line of thought in Wittgenstein's On Certainty and it looks like it proves too much, so I need someone to find the leak. Wittgenstein says somewhere around the hinge remarks that some propositions are exempt from doubt, not because they've been checked and confirmed, but because they're what doubt turns on—like hinges, if you doubt them the whole machinery of questioning and verifying loses its grip. So far so good. But then I run this argument:

(1) Knowing that p requires having grounds for p.
(2) Hinge propositions are precisely those for which any ground you could offer would already presuppose the proposition, so they cannot be grounded.
(3) Therefore hinges cannot be known.
(4) But if the hinge view is applied consistently, ordinary Moore-type claims like "I have two hands" or "the earth has existed for more than five minutes" are hinges in their everyday contexts—we don't verify them, they're part of the scaffolding that makes verification possible.
(5) So ordinary empirical claims like "I have two hands" cannot be known.
(6) That's absurd, and it turns Moore's common-sense proof into a self-defeating joke.

So either (1) is false, or (2) overstates what a hinge is, or (4) misidentifies which propositions are hinges. I'd rather keep (4) than reject it, and I'm not attached to (1), but I don't know what giving up (1) would cost. Where does the argument actually go wrong, if it does? And if the answer is that hinges can be known without grounds, what does "knowledge" amount to on that picture?
