In my early modern political thought course, we have been reading Hobbes’s account of covenant and sovereignty alongside Spinoza’s *Theologico-Political Treatise*, and I am trying to sort out a contrast in Chapter XVI that our professor keeps flagging. There Spinoza gives his version of how people leave the state of nature by transferring their natural right to a sovereign, but the mode of transfer seems to change when he reaches democracy. For Hobbes, the covenant institutes an artificial person to whom subjects alienate their right, and the sovereign stands outside the original covenant. Spinoza appears to say that in a democracy nobody transfers his natural right to another person in such a way that he ceases to be consulted; instead, right is transferred to the majority of the whole community, and all remain equal as they were in the state of nature. I want to understand whether Spinoza’s language of “transfer” is doing the same work as Hobbes’s language of “authorization,” or whether he has quietly changed the subject.

Please compare the two accounts with close attention to the following problem. If Spinoza identifies natural right with power, and if no one can really alienate his power to another, then what exactly is transferred in a democracy, and why should a minority be bound by majority decisions? Is Spinoza’s democracy best read as a normative claim about equality and the preservation of freedom, or as a descriptive claim about where power reliably tends to reside and what can be alienated without destroying the person? I am less interested in historical influence than in whether Spinoza’s position is internally coherent and whether it amounts to a genuine alternative to Hobbesian sovereignty rather than a softened restatement of it.
