An adversarial collaboration has been convened between two positions on the question of whether elegance or beauty in a mathematical or scientific theory — and, secondarily, in a proof — is evidence for its truth or correctness. Position A, the elegance-as-evidence view, holds that perceived elegance is a defeasible but genuine reason to raise one's subjective probability that a theory is true or that a line of inquiry is sound, because elegance is a fallible signal of virtues such as simplicity, unification, compression, and structural match. Position B, the elegance-as-bias view, holds that judgments of elegance are aesthetic, shaped by cognitive biases, training, and intellectual fashion, often applied retrospectively to whichever theory already won, and that elegance carries no independent evidential weight; it may be a useful heuristic for allocating attention, but using it as evidence systematically inflates confidence. Draft the joint statement these two sides should produce.

The statement should open with a short preamble delimiting the question and disavowing readings neither side intends. It should then give a numbered list of claims both sides can sign. The agreed claims should cover at least: the phenomenology of beauty in mathematics and science; the variability of beauty judgments across time, fields, and practitioners; the uncontroversial historical fact that some admired elegant proposals failed while some initially unlovely proposals succeeded; the distinction between the elegance of a theory and the elegance of a proof; and the permissibility of elegance as a non-demonstrative heuristic for generating and prioritizing hypotheses. These agreed claims should be substantive rather than vacuous, and should not smuggle in either side's conclusion.

Next, the statement should include a disagreements table. For each residual disagreement, state the issue in neutral terms, then give each side's position in wording that side would accept as its own, without caricature. The table should separate the genuinely epistemic disagreement from any disagreement that dissolves once elegance is decomposed into component notions such as simplicity, symmetry, concision, unification, and explanatory power. If part of the dispute is verbal — for example, about whether evidence must raise posterior probability or may merely justify pursuit — say exactly which part is verbal and why.

The statement should then identify two or three cruxes: sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one side. For each crux, propose a concrete investigation or case that both sides agree bears on it — for instance, a historical base-rate study with coding criteria fixed in advance, asking whether ex ante judgments of elegance predict later empirical success, or a prospective study that rates the elegance of currently unresolved hypotheses and records each side's predictions before the evidence arrives. Where suitable, anchor the cruxes in specific episodes from the history of mathematics and physics, such as the Dirac equation's successful prediction of antimatter, Kepler's early planetary model built from nested Platonic solids, the computer-assisted proof of the Four-Color Theorem, or supersymmetry as an elegant and as-yet-unconfirmed research program. Conclude with a section in which each side states, in its own voice, what it would count as evidence against its own view.
