I work as a software engineer at a small company, and I have been asked to help draft our business continuity policy. The current proposal deliberately borrows from continuity-of-government practice. At any given time, one senior engineer would be designated as the survivor: they would be kept away from company-wide events and offsites, and they would be expected to keep enough working context to restore production systems, hold the relevant credentials, and communicate with customers if a disaster incapacitated everyone else. The proposal also discourages people who jointly hold critical credentials from traveling together, and it leaves open whether the rest of the company should be told who the current survivor is or whether that should be kept quiet.

I have no formal philosophy background and only a rough sense of how governments think about continuity of government and designated survivors. Please describe the most important implications of those practices and the ethics surrounding them for how we should design this policy. I am especially interested in what they imply about how the survivor should be selected and rotated, whether the role is best understood as a burden or a privilege, what the company may legitimately demand of someone in that role without their ongoing consent, and whether the existence of the policy itself might produce a false sense of security rather than making the company more genuinely resilient.
