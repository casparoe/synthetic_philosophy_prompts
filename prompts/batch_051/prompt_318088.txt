I'm a TA for an intro ethics class and I'd like a second opinion on one of the midterm answers before I write comments. The question was:

"In class we discussed the inefficacy objection to the idea that individuals have obligations to do things like eat less meat, fly less, or vote, on the grounds that no single action makes a difference to the aggregate outcome. Explain the objection in both a stronger and a weaker form. Then explain and evaluate the main replies, especially the threshold reply and the expected-utility reply, and be precise about what each reply shows and what it doesn't."

The student wrote:

"The strong version of the inefficacy objection says that since one person's chicken purchase or car trip is neither necessary nor sufficient to bring about the relevant harm—suppliers order in fixed quantities, the atmosphere doesn't notice one more drive—the act has no effect at all and therefore can't be wrong for reasons of harm. The weak version says that even if the act has some small expected effect, the effect is too tiny to override the agent's convenience. The threshold reply targets the strong version. It points out that aggregate demand works in steps: there is some number of purchases at which the supermarket increases its next order, and some point at which the airline adds a flight, even if we don't know where the threshold falls. Because my purchase could be the one that crosses the threshold, it has a nonzero probability of causing the entire marginal unit of harm. The expected-utility reply then says this probability is enough: the expected harm of my purchase equals the probability that I'm the threshold-crossing purchase times the harm of the marginal unit. If the marginal unit is a crate of 30 chickens, and there are 30 shoppers who together trigger the reorder, then my probability of being pivotal is 1/30, so the expected harm of buying a chicken is the harm of one whole chicken. That refutes the strong version, because the act makes an expected difference, and it refutes the weak version too, because one chicken's worth of harm is not negligible. Against the weak version, there's also an aggregation point: even if the expected harm of a single meal is small, over a lifetime the probability that at least one of my purchases crosses a threshold becomes large, so my consumption as a whole clearly makes a difference. The remaining worry is just that the threshold might be very unlikely to be near my purchase, but that's empirical uncertainty rather than an objection of principle. So the inefficiency objection, in both forms, rests on confusing 'my act isn't sufficient for the harm' with 'my act has no expected effect,' and once that confusion is cleared up the objection collapses."
