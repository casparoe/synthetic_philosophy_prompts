I produce a science-and-ideas podcast and I'm working on an episode about historical truth, and I keep noticing that 'Rashomon situation' is the one reference everyone leans on without actually working through it. I'd like a careful reading of Kurosawa's film through the actual debate: Ranke's idea that history should show the past as it actually was, against Carr's line that facts don't become historical facts until a historian selects them, and Hayden White's stronger claim that history is written as plotted narratives and so is closer to fiction than most historians admit. The easy take is that Rashomon just demonstrates there's no single true account. I dont want the surface take. Does the film deny that there was a real event outside the four tellings, or only that the characters are motivated to distort it? How does the woodcutter's account — usually treated as the closest thing to a reliable version — actually function, given the film never confirms it and his motives are visibly grubby? And what should a Rankean do with the ending, with the rain stopping and the baby, because a skeptic could say Kurosawa undermines his own point by letting a little decency resolve the frame? I want criticism that takes both the film and the philosophy seriously: where does Rashomon genuinely complicate Ranke, Carr, and White, and where does the old objectivity debate look at the film and say it is cheating by conflating self-serving witness testimony with the writing of history?
