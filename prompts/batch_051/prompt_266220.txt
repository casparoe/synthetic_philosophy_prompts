I am preparing a guest session in a political-theory graduate seminar on the origins of social norms. The students work on promising, property, and honor codes, but they have not read David Lewis's Convention, and most of them assume that a norm must trace back either to something like a tacit collective agreement or to selection at the level of the group. I want the session to give them a third possibility: a convention as Lewis analyzes it — a self-perpetuating regularity in a recurrent coordination problem, one equilibrium among several, sustained by precedent and common knowledge rather than by anyone's decision or by sanctions. The session's payoff is the thought that the selection of a pattern of behavior and the normativity of that pattern are two distinct problems and need not have the same solution.

Do not explain the theory to me; plan how I should explain it. The session runs seventy-five minutes. I need the following.

First, the order in which the components must be introduced and why each depends on the previous one — coordination games with multiple equilibria, the idea of a regularity, salience, expectations about others' expectations, common knowledge, arbitrariness, and the gap between a convention and a norm — up to the point where the students can see a convention as a distinct source of social order that is neither an agreement nor a sanction-backed rule.

Second, the one example that should carry the session. Argue for it, and say what an example must include and must lack in order to do this work, addressing explicitly whether the familiar case of driving on the right or the left is adequate at the start.

Third, the misconceptions the students will bring or form and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off. I already expect these: that convention just means tacit agreement; that a convention is a norm with mild sanctions; that common knowledge requires literally infinite iterations of information in anyone's mind; and that arbitrariness means pure accident, so nothing explains why one alternative rather than another took hold. Add any others that matter.

Fourth, where to stop with respect to the evolution of norms: what Lewis's account does and does not explain about how a mere convention acquires normative force, what I should openly leave as a further problem, and how the session's ending should set that problem up without pretending to solve it.

Finally, one question I can pose at the end that would distinguish students who now see a convention as a third kind of thing from those who still fold it back into agreement or sanctions.
