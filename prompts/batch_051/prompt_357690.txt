I have come to accept a broadly Zahavian view of animal signalling: where signaler and receiver interests conflict, honest signals are stabilized by differential costs rather than by absolute cost or waste, meaning a signal keeps working because it is more expensive for a low-quality signaler to produce than for a high-quality one, and sender–receiver models recover this as the condition that keeps cheap deception from invading. I take rival accounts, such as unfakeable indices, shared interest, and receiver-sensory bias explanations, to cover special cases or to reduce to the cost picture once stated precisely. I want you to stress-test this view, not strawman it. What would count against it? Which observations, models, or experimental results would be decisive rather than merely awkward, and which would defenders of the strategic-cost view themselves concede as genuine problems? Which putatively honest low-cost or cost-free signals actually bite against the view, and which fail to because they involve common interest or physiological constraints the view already accommodates? Finally, has the position I have just stated been qualified enough that it risks nothing, and if so, is that a defect in a view of this kind? Rank the defeaters from fatal to superficial, and give an honest assessment of what the view predicts that its rivals do not.
