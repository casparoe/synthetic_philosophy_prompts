I keep returning to an argument I sketched for a paper on the doing/allowing distinction, and it seems to prove far too much, so I want a diagnosis before I build anything on it.

(1) In every supposed case of letting someone die, the agent can truly be described as doing something. A doctor who withholds an available dose of a drug does not simply do nothing; she writes the order, withholds the drug, and lets the disease run its course. A bystander at a pond does not do nothing; she stands on the bank rather than wading in.
(2) So the choice between describing someone as killing and describing her as letting die cannot be a choice between an action and the absence of one. It is a choice among true descriptions of behavior: which of the agent's movements, refusals, and refrainings we foreground, and which we treat as background.
(3) Which description we foreground depends on a baseline—a view about what the agent would otherwise have been doing, or what the world would otherwise have done—and nothing in the world fixes a unique baseline. Different interests and conventions yield different baselines, and each baseline redistributes the deaths the agent "does" and the deaths she merely "allows."
(4) If that is right, the action/omission line is an artifact of description rather than a joint in nature.
(5) But the distinction between killing and letting die is standardly treated as just that action/omission line. So, if the line is an artifact of description, the moral significance we attach to killing as opposed to letting die is tracking no real difference between cases.
(6) Therefore killing and letting die are morally on a par: for any case of letting a person die, a corresponding case of killing that person is morally no worse, other things equal.
(7) But (6) has consequences I cannot accept. The person who could prevent a child's death with an easy donation and keeps the money instead is, on this argument, morally no better than the person who kills a child in order to keep the same money; and by iteration, everyone who has ever bought a nonessential while others starved is doing something on a par with killing every day. I assume the argument has gone wrong somewhere, rather than the conclusion being true.

Where does the argument go wrong? I would most like to hold on to (1) and (2), since they seem like careful attention to how we describe behavior rather than substantive moral theory, but I am prepared to pay whatever those premises cost. Please tell me which premise or step to reject, why it looked innocent, and what the main ways of resisting the argument each commit one to.
