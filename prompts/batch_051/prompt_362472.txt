I keep coming back to this argument that longtermism has a totalitarian core, and I can't tell whether it's actually tight or where it comes apart:

(1) If the far future contains astronomically more people than the present, then nearly all expected value lies in the far future.
(2) Whatever carries nearly all expected value should dominate our decisions: when a present-day interest conflicts with it, the present-day interest loses.
(3) Civil liberties and democratic checks are present-day interests; their long-run significance is only instrumental, via how they shape the future trajectory.
(4) So a liberty should be overridden whenever doing so locks in a better far-future trajectory.
(5) A state empowered to override liberties whenever the trajectory improves is a state that rules by its own vision of the far future, not by the consent of the governed.
(6) And a state that rules by its own vision of the far future is totalitarian in tendency.
(7) Therefore longtermism, taken seriously, licenses a kind of totalitarian control.

I half think step (2) is doing the real work, but I also suspect the slide from a one-off override to a standing license happens somewhere around (5), quietly. Is the argument valid? If not, say exactly which step fails and give the strongest version of it a defender could still accept. If it is valid, tell me which premise a longtermist should reject.
