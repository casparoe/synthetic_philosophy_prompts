I am a numerical software engineer, and I think natively in floating-point arithmetic and numerical analysis: rounding modes, ulps and machine epsilon, tolerance-based approximate equality, interval arithmetic, NaNs and infinities, subnormals, and the accumulation of rounding error across long computations. I want you to translate the philosophical debate about vagueness—centered on the sorites paradox—into that vocabulary, and then give me a residue report.

Work through at least the following positions. (1) The classical bivalent view: any meaningful predicate draws a sharp boundary somewhere, even when we cannot find it. (2) Epistemicism: there is a sharp cutoff, and our inability to locate it is a kind of ignorance. (3) Supervaluationism: a sentence is true just in case it is true on every admissible way of making the predicate precise, false just in case it is false on every such precisification, and otherwise neither true nor false. (4) Many-valued or degree theories: borderline predications take intermediate truth values or degrees between 0 and 1, rather than just true or false. (5) Contextualism: the boundary shifts with the conversational or comparison context rather than being fixed once and for all.

For each position, give the cleanest statement of the view you can manage in the target vocabulary—a predicate as a rounding function from a continuous quantity to a finite set of representable values, an admissible precisification as a fixed choice of rounding mode or precision, a borderline case as an input whose classification flips when those settings change, degrees of truth as normalized distances that underflow as they approach the threshold, the tolerance premise as a step whose local effect is below machine epsilon, the sorites itself as a long iteration in which locally negligible steps lead to a terminal classification that rejects the result. These are suggestions of register, not constraints. Then say explicitly what had to be bent or imported from outside the numerical picture to make the translation work, and what has no expression in the target vocabulary at all, however hard you try. Where you can, extend this to higher-order vagueness.

Finally, take the residues seriously. For each failure of translation, judge whether it is a defect of the floating-point framework—a sign that the philosophical view is tracking something real that numerical vocabulary cannot see—or a sign that the original philosophical claim was less precise than its proponents suggest. Distinguish the residues from one another rather than giving a single global verdict. The result should read like a translation a numerical analyst would find natural and a philosopher of language would find faithfully recognizable, not a loose analogy.
