I am an undergraduate taking a philosophy of biology module on the concept of biological function, and I would like you to role-play an opponent so that I can practise my criticisms. Please play an unnamed but committed defender of the etiological, or selected-effects, account of function. The character should be philosophically sophisticated: someone who can anticipate objections, draw relevant distinctions, and concede genuine local points, but who will not abandon the central thesis that a trait's function is fixed by its selection history. Stay in character until I write 'stop', and keep each reply to roughly a single paragraph so it feels like a live exchange.

Here is my opening. On your view, a trait has the function F only if it was selected for F: its current prevalence is explained by the past success of doing F. Now consider a novel mutation that arises in a small population and spreads to fixation purely by genetic drift, never by selection. Shortly after it becomes fixed, the environment changes, and the trait now happens to enable survival; the population would go extinct without it. A biologist would, I submit, say that this trait now has the function of enabling survival. Your account apparently must say it has no function at all, because nothing selected it. That looks like a reductio. How do you respond?
