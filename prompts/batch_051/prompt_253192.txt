I have been reading Zera Yacob's Hatata, the philosophical inquiry written in Ge'ez in the 1660s, and it has sharpened an asymmetry I keep noticing in ordinary religious life. The book, as it presents itself, is the record of a man caught in the violent Catholic–Orthodox disputes of seventeenth-century Ethiopia who withdraws to think from first principles and concludes that the intellect is a gift God has distributed to all human beings equally, so that no one may simply accept a creed on the authority of birth, teachers, or neighbors. On this view, the fact that one was raised in a tradition explains how one came to hold a belief, but it does not itself count toward the belief's being true.

The asymmetry is this. When someone remains in the religion they were raised in, we tend to treat that as unsurprising and often as legitimate; we rarely demand that they produce arguments before taking their adherence seriously. But when we consider someone in a rival tradition doing exactly the same thing — staying because they too were raised in it — we are inclined to treat their upbringing as an accident of birth and to expect something more before crediting their conviction. The very same consideration, that this is the tradition one was taught, seems to carry weight when it is our side's case and to lose its weight when it is theirs. I want to know whether that asymmetry can be defended, or whether Yacob's equal-intellect principle forces us to treat inherited affiliation as a psychological explanation and never as an epistemic credential. Please construct the strongest justification you can for the asymmetry, test it against nearby cases such as political or moral beliefs acquired through upbringing, and then say whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
