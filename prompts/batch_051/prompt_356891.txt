I work at a large company that has recently invested heavily in what it calls "employee experience." We now have game rooms, unscheduled blocks of time that may not be booked with meetings, generous sabbaticals, and an official norm that managers must not ask what a team offsite or a round of play was for. At the same time, the organization justifies these programs internally by pointing to studies about burnout, retention, and creativity, and the programs survive budget reviews only by showing engagement scores and downstream productivity gains. I also notice that some of the supposedly playful activities are quietly compulsory: attendance at the offsite is expected, and skipping the "fun" events reads as not being a team player.

So we are asked to treat play and leisure as things that carry their own point, while the institution treats them as instruments—and sometimes as obligations, which seems to erode the very idea that they are freely chosen. I keep running into the philosophical claim that play and leisure are ends in themselves rather than means, autotelic activities whose point lies within themselves, and this claim makes the practice look even more puzzling to me. What theory of value does this institutional practice presuppose? Does it strictly presuppose that play and leisure are good only as means, or only as ends? Is it simply torn between an instrumental and an autotelic conception of play, and is the tension merely in the justifications, or is it deep enough to make the practice incoherent? Would compulsory "fun" have to go, and could a genuinely autotelic policy survive inside a firm whose reason for being is economic, or would instrumental justification sneak back in?
