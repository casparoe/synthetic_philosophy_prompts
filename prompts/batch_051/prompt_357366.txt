So I started volunteering at a farm sanctuary last month, and there's this one policy I can't stop turning over in my head. The sanctuary has a bunch of rescued battery hens, and those hens lay eggs constantly—they're not fertilized, there's no rooster, and honestly the hens mostly ignore them once they've clucked about it for a minute. The sanctuary feeds some of the eggs back to the hens for the calcium and chucks the rest. They won't let volunteers eat them, and they won't sell them or give them away, because they say a sanctuary isn't supposed to turn the animals back into a resource. Fine. But here's the thing that nags at me: the same sanctuary uses the hens' manure on it's vegetable garden, and nobody blinks, and my flatmate is a freegan who will happily eat a cheese sandwich out of a bin because it would otherwise go to landfill. She'd never rescue a hen with the plan of selling eggs, but she thinks throwing away edible eggs while buying tofu is insane. I don't necessarily think either of them is wrong. I want to know what theory of what makes animal use wrong the no-eggs policy actually presupposes. Is it committed to the idea that using animals is wrong in itself, even when no demand is created and no animal is harmed? Or is it better understood as a consequentialist policy about the message the sanctuary sends, where eating or selling the eggs would shift how visitors treat the animals? And if the sanctuary switched to a harm-and-demand view, would it have to let volunteers scramble the abandoned eggs, or even sell them to fund the rescue—or are there real downstream effects that would still justify throwing them out? I can't tell which parts of the policy are doing the philosophical work and which parts are just, like, brand management.
