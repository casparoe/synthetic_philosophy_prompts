Take the following episode as given and analyze it with the tools of Bayesian convergence theory. In 1980 Luis Alvarez, Walter Alvarez, and their collaborators proposed that the end-Cretaceous mass extinction was caused by a large asteroid impact, on the strength of a worldwide iridium anomaly at the Cretaceous–Paleogene boundary. Over the next decade the evidence accumulated: shocked quartz, microtektites, and, by the early 1990s, a candidate impact crater at Chicxulub. Through most of that period, many geologists and paleontologists favored the rival view that the extinction was caused by the long-lived flood basalt eruptions of the Deccan Traps, and they read the impact evidence as circumstantial. The two camps saw largely the same data arrive while continuing to disagree sharply.

Treat the controversy as a natural experiment in the washing out of priors. Spell out what the Bayesian convergence results actually require: agents with different prior probabilities over a shared hypothesis space, who agree on the likelihood of the observations under each hypothesis and update by conditionalization on a common stream of data, should have posterior probabilities that merge as the data grow. Now do the mapping carefully: what was the relevant hypothesis space (impact only, volcanism only, both, neither), what counted as the prior — including background priors about uniformitarianism versus catastrophism and about whether mass extinctions were rapid or drawn out — and what the likelihoods for the iridium layer, the shocked quartz, the extinction pattern, and the dating of Chicxulub look like under each hypothesis. Pay special attention to whether the two camps actually shared a likelihood, or whether they differed in auxiliary assumptions: how volcanism might produce iridium, whether the fossil record's apparent gradual decline was real or an artifact, and how much a crater counts for when its date is uncertain. Then identify, assumption by assumption, where the convergence results break down or fall silent in this episode, and what they nevertheless get right about the gradual movement of opinion as independent lines of evidence accumulated. End with your verdict: is the eventual near-consensus around the impact hypothesis a delayed Bayesian washing-out, or does it arrive only after the model space itself changed? What does the episode show about the idea that shared evidence tends to wash out divergent priors in real science — an anomaly, a confirmation once the fine print is read, or a case the asymptotic results never anticipated?
