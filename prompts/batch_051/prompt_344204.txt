I keep going back and forth on this, so I want to stress-test the absurdity doctrine rather than just absorb it. The criteria I've gathered from my statutory interpretation seminar are: (1) the text has to be clear, with the weird outcome following straight from the words and not from some arguable construction; (2) the outcome has to be genuinely absurd, not merely harsh, unwise, costly, or bad policy; (3) the absurdity has to be demonstrable from the text, context, or a clear enough legislative purpose, not just from the judge's own sense of fairness; (4) the fix has to be minimal—the court refuses to apply the literal words to the odd case rather than rewriting the provision to be better; and (5) the doctrine shouldn't apply if the legislature might plausibly have wanted the literal rule, even as a blunt or imperfect one.

Now here's the case I can't sort out. A state environmental statute says the commission "may waive" a permit fee for an applicant who shows financial hardship, and then says: "Any decision of the commission under this subsection is final and shall not be reviewed by any court." An applicant submits paperwork showing an annual income of $8,000, which qualifies for the waiver under the agency's own rules. A data-entry clerk transposes the digits as $80,000, and the commission denies the waiver on that basis. The permit costs $20,000, and without the waiver the applicant's small business loses its permit. The commission admits the income was mis-entered but says the no-review clause means no court can touch the decision even when the denial was just a typo.

Would a court taking the five criteria seriously apply the absurdity doctrine here and ignore the no-review clause? Go criterion by criterion, say honestly where the evidence points both ways, and then tell me whether this case shows that one criterion—I suspect the one separating "absurd" from "harsh"—is doing all the real work, or whether the criteria as a set need changing.
