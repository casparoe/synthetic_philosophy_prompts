I'm helping teach a media philosophy course next term and I've been re-watching Rear Window, and I keep thinking it's actually a film-length argument about photographic transparency — the Kendall Walton idea that looking at a photograph is a way of literally seeing the thing photographed, rather than seeing a representation of it in the way you do with a painting. I can't work out which side the film lands on. On the one hand Jeff is a photographer, his telephoto lense is treated as a transparent window onto the courtyard, and for long stretches the audience sees the neighbours' lives exactly as he sees them through the viewfinder, so Hitchcock seems to be treating the camera (and the cinema screen) as transparent. On the other hand the film is constantly drawing attention to framing and selection: each neighbour is a little framed picture, Jeff keeps reconstructing the wrong story from what he sees, and the murder plot is mostly assembled in his head from a few ambiguous glimpses.

Read the film through this debate for me. I'm less interested in whether the transparency thesis is true than in what the film contributes to it: where does the lens-as-eye metaphor hold and where does it cheat, and does the film itself seem to know the difference? Points I'd want the reading to engage: the dog-in-the-courtyard sequence where everyone rushes out except the man Jeff is watching, the sequence where Lisa is in Thorwald's flat and Jeff can see her but not intervene, and the ending, where Jeff is back in his chair with both legs in casts. Does Rear Window dramatise transparency, critique it, or both — and what does it show that the philosophy papers miss?
