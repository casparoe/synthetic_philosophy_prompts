For my upper-level epistemology seminar (about 14 students, 80 minutes; a few of them have already read the contextualist literature and will probably see where this is going, so write it with that in mind), design a classroom game that makes the force of pragmatic encroachment, and the contextualist reading of it, show up in what students do before I name either view. No lecture up front, and I shouldn't need to say the word "knows" in my instructions until the debrief.

Here's the shape I'm picturing. It's a two-jar sampling task. Each round there are two jars: one is 60% red chips and 40% blue, the other is 40% red and 60% blue. Students know it's one of those two, with a 50/50 prior, and I reveal draws with replacement one at a time from pre-printed sequences, so I control when the publicly available evidence is exactly identical across rounds. After each reveal, each student privately writes down (a) which jar they'd bet on, and (b) whether they know which jar is which. Payoffs change by round: some rounds are low-stakes and symmetric, like +5 points for correct and -5 for wrong; other rounds are high-stakes and asymmetric, like +5 for correct but -25 if you're wrong, with maybe a mirror case where a false negative is the expensive error. Crucially, build in at least one pair of rounds where the revealed draw sequence is exactly the same but the payoff matrix differs, so when we compare their know-claims across the pair, the only thing that changed is the stakes.

Work out concrete rules: how many rounds, the exact payoff matrices, how many draws per round, whether betting is optional or forced, how scoring works (keep it light, maybe points toward a small prize for one randomly selected student), and how to stop them from gaming the asymmetric payoffs by always betting the direction that's expensive to get wrong. Also tell me how to record answers so the paired-rounds comparison actually lands for everyone, including the students who guessed the point by round two.

Then write the debrief. Show the paired rounds side by side and ask them to seperate what changed in their evidence from what changed in their willingness to claim knowledge. Map that onto the old bank case: same remembered fact, same strength of evidence, different costs of being wrong. Then walk through at least three explanations of what showed up: (1) classical invariantism, where they knew in both rounds and only their betting, or their willingness to say "know," moved; (2) contextualism, where the content of "knows" shifts with the practical stakes of the attributor; and (3) subject-sensitive invariantism or pragmatic encroachment, where the knowledge facts themselves are interest-relative. Be honest that the game delivers the pattern but can't by itself seperate the semantic story from the metaphysical one. What I want them to walk out with is a clear articulation of why the evidential relation alone doesn't settle which of those explanations is right, plus a felt sense that the stakes effect is real and also very easy to explain away.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run it: a student who flatly refuses to change their know-claims on principle, the "know" question feeling weird or goofy, high-stakes rounds making people cautious but having them describe it as confidence rather than knowledge, someone trying to reduce the whole thing to a probability exercise. Say what I should do for each. And say plainly which part of the real phenomenon the game can't reproduce and has to come from me in the debrief.

End with a short note on how confident you are that the paired-rounds design will actually produce the stakes flip in a room of fourteen, and the single point in this whole design you'd be least willing to bet on.
