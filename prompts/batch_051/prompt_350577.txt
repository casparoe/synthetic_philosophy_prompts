So my friend says Rousseau's real point is that a theater in a small republic like ours would do something to the audience that other kinds of spectatorship don't, because of how the play presents a whole way of being—the witty, easy, slightly false social type—as the one you should want to be or at least not want to be seen despising. And that got me thinking.

We're not Switzerland in 1758, obviously, but our community does trade on being small enough that people expect each other to be reasonably sincere and to value the kind of person who says the true thing over the kind of person who makes the room feel comfortable. The grant we've been offered would let us put on a real season, mostly modern comedies and a few musicals. I'm not asking whether Rousseau is right about all theater, or about Molière. I want to know whether a high-quality community theater is more likely to strengthen or quietly corrode a community ethos like ours, which is the thing we all say we value. If a theater can be a school in certain habits of feeling, as Rousseau seems to think, then what does that mean in practice for how we choose our shows, how we cast, who we cast, and how we talk about the plays before and after? So, here's my request.

Please write me a decision procedure that our arts council could actually run on a proposed show—an ordinary one, not the ones where someone's trying to shock the audience—so that the kind of concern Rousseau raises is part of our process, not just someone's speech before the vote. I don't want a set of content-rating boxes; I want a way of asking, for each show, what it makes attractive, what it makes ridiculous, whose sympathy it enrolls, and what kind of self it gives the audience to try on for about two hours. The procedure should be concrete enough to use in a meeting, but it should also mark the places where our readings will differ and where, if we're honest, we're not applying Rousseau's idea but just our own nerves. Then run it through two shows: a recent social comedy about a wedding where the humor comes from every character hiding something and the one blunt aunt who speaks her mind is played as the problem, and a production of Our Town, which our treasurer keeps calling "the safe alternative." Give me the result even if the procedure tells us the feel-good choice is the worse one, because that's exactly the kind of thing we need to hear spelled out.
