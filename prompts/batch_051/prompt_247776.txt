I am marking an upper-level philosophy of mind take-home exam and would value a careful second read of one submission. The exam question was:

Explain the notion of sphexishness, using the standard digger-wasp case, and assess whether the flexibility of an agent's behavior is a reliable criterion for the presence of free will or genuine understanding, in humans or in artificial systems.

The student's full answer was:

Sphexishness is named after the digger wasp. The wasp follows a fixed routine: it paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the burrow's threshold, goes inside alone to inspect the burrow, comes back out, and only then drags the cricket in. If the experimenter moves the cricket a few inches while the wasp is inside, the wasp will, on emerging, drag the cricket back to the threshold and run the entire inspection routine again; repeated moves produce the same loop. The wasp is sphexish because its behavior is controlled by a closed sub-routine keyed to cues rather than by a representation of the task as a whole. Flexibility is what is missing: a flexible agent holds its goal in view and can vary its sub-routines when circumstances change. So we have a clear criterion. An agent that cannot deviate from its routine at all is a pure mechanism and has neither free will nor understanding; an agent that alters its means while holding its end fixed is not merely mechanical, and this self-directed variability is what free, understanding behavior consists in. A large language model that merely rephrases a prompt without changing its answer is only superficially flexible; but a model that changes its output appropriately across a wide range of paraphrases and novel tasks shows exactly the kind of variability the criterion picks out. Fluency in generalization is therefore direct evidence that the system understands what it is doing, while brittleness is direct evidence that it does not.
