For the past couple of years I have followed a deliberate rule about uncertainty. Whenever I have to assign probabilities — a bet with friends, a project timeline, a range of possible measurements — and I can state only a few things about the quantity in question (its possible range, its long-run average, its variance), I pick the probability distribution with maximum entropy subject to exactly those constraints, and I refuse to add any further assumptions. I treat that distribution as my prior and move off it only by conditioning on new evidence. I do not let intuition thicken the tails or shift the center; if the maximum-entropy distribution looks physically odd, I still use it until data move me. Last month a colleague asked me to estimate how long a server migration would take. All I was willing to claim was that it would finish sometime between two and eight hours, and that similar migrations in my experience had averaged about three and a half hours. So I used the maximum-entropy distribution over that interval subject to that mean, and planned around it. He objected that I had turned brute ignorance into a suspiciously specific curve.

I know this resembles the objective Bayesian idea that there is a uniquely rational prior given one's evidence, and that maximum entropy picks it out. But I have also run into the familiar worries: the distribution changes if I redescribe the same problem in different units or parameters, and "maximum entropy" sometimes strikes me as a dressed-up way of saying "as close to uniform as the constraints allow." What I want to know is whether my standing policy, as opposed to a one-off calculation, is defensible. When does it genuinely extract a rational prior from evidence, and when does it quietly assume flatness, a particular parameterization, or a particular reference class? Is there a restated version that keeps the discipline without pretending that ignorance is a kind of information? And if the request as I have framed it rests on a confusion — for example, if "the prior my evidence determines" is not a well-defined object — please say that plainly rather than polishing my rule.
