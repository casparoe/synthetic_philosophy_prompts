I'm a philosophy professor, and for the last month I've been swapping emails with a collegue in economic history about the Great Divergence. At a workshop we ended up arguing over Adam Smith's opening to The Wealth of Nations, in the Introduction and Plan of the Work. She thinks it gives us a clean test for whether the Needham question — roughly, why did the Industrial Revolution take off in Britain rather than in China, which had been the more advanced economy for centuries — is well posed. The passage she keeps quoting is:

“The annual labour of every nation is the fund which originally supplies it with all the necessaries and conveniencies of life which it annually consumes, and which consist always either in the immediate produce of that labour, or in what is purchased with that produce from other nations.”

Now suppose we treat this not as a bit of stage-setting but as a rule for framing comparative economic history. Smith wrote it in 1776, and he did not have before him the later debate about why China stagnated after being far ahead. My instinct is that the passage smuggles in a labour-centric picture that makes the Britain/China comparison slightly rigged from the start, because it directs us to look at the “skill, dexterity, and judgment with which its labour is generally applied” rather than at institutions, property rights, war, coal, colonisation, or any number of things a modern historian might reach for. But my colleague says I'm reading it too narrowly; it's just a neutral opening definition of national wealth.

Can you work through how that passage applies to the case Smith did not foresee: the Needham question. What would the different approaches to interpretation — the words, the purpose, what Smith would have said if asked about China and Britain in 1800, what a reasonable reader takes him to mean — each give? Does the passage make “why Britain and not China?” well posed or does it bias the answer? And is that the passage's fault or our use of it?

I'd like this in British English, and since this is partly a matter of judgement, I'd like you to say how confident you are in the main claim and single out the point you're least sure of.
