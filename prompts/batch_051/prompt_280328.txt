Something's been bugging me about the way legal systems handle laws everybody ignores, and I want to know what philosophical picture they'd have to be assuming for the practice to make sense. My city has old ordinances still in the code that nobody has enforced in decades: spitting on the sidewalk, swearing in public, some 1800s thing about hitching posts. The city council won't repeal them because they don't want to look soft on crime, but no prosecutor actually charges them. Except occasionally one gets dusted off and charged against somebody, and courts sometimes uphold the conviction on the ground that the law was on the books and the person technically violated it. The same system insists that the rule of law requires law to be public, predictable, and not enforced arbitrarily. So what does this practice presuppose about what makes something a law: that validity floats free of actual enforcement, or that a law everybody ignores stops being law after a while, or something else? Is the setup internally coherent? And what would have to change in how courts and legislatures behave if we actually took dead letters seriously as not-law?
