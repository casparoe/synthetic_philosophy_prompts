Below is a question from a midterm in an introductory moral philosophy course, followed by a student's answer.

Question. Explain the Doctrine of Double Effect (DDE) and use it to explain the moral contrast between the standard trolley case (diverting the trolley onto a side track where one person is killed) and the transplant case (killing one healthy person to use his organs to save five). Then state one objection to the DDE and evaluate whether it succeeds.

Student's answer.

The Doctrine of Double Effect holds that an action with both a good and a bad effect may be permissible only when four conditions are met: the act itself is not intrinsically wrong; the agent does not intend the bad effect, either as an end or as a means; the good effect is not brought about through the bad effect; and the good effect is proportionate to the bad effect. In the trolley case, the bystander who diverts the trolley does not intend the death of the one person on the side track. He foresees that the death will occur, but the death is not the means by which the five are saved; the five are saved by redirecting the trolley, and the one death is a foreseen side effect. The DDE therefore treats the diversion as permissible. In the transplant case, by contrast, the surgeon kills the healthy patient precisely in order to remove his organs and save the five. Here the death is intended as a means: the good effect is produced through the bad effect. So the DDE forbids the transplant, even though it saves more lives than it loses.

The deeper rationale of the doctrine is that what matters morally is whether the agent does something or merely allows it to happen. The bystander merely lets the one person on the side track die as a side effect of redirecting a threat, whereas the surgeon actively brings about the healthy patient's death. This distinction between doing and allowing is what really carries the weight of the DDE in these two cases.

One standard objection to the DDE is the closeness problem. The objection says that the distinction between what an agent intends and what she merely foresees is too easy to draw, because agents can almost always redescribe an intended harm as a foreseen side effect when the bad effect is closely tied to the good one. But this objection does not succeed. Agents have direct and infallible access to their own intentions: they can simply check whether they intended the harm, and once they sincerely report their intention, there is no further question to ask.
