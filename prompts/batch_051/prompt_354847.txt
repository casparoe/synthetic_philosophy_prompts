For an examination in formal epistemology I set the following question: State van Fraassen's Reflection principle, distinguish it from Conditionalization, and explain the structure of the Dutch book argument offered in support of it; how far does that argument show that violating Reflection is irrational? One student answered as follows. Van Fraassen's Reflection principle says that for any proposition A and any later time, my present credence in A should equal my present expectation of my future credence in A; put conditionally, my credence in A given that my future credence in A will be x should itself be x. This is not the same as Conditionalization, which says how credences should move when new evidence arrives, whereas Reflection says how present credences should defer to the credences I expect to have later. A Dutch book can be run against a violator. Suppose I am 0.5 in A now but certain that tomorrow I will be 0.9 in A. Today the bookie makes a bet with me on A at today's fair odds; tomorrow, knowing my future credence, he makes the reverse bet at the odds my future self will accept. Each bet is fair by my own lights at the time I take it, yet the two together guarantee a loss. So any agent who violates Reflection is open to a Dutch book and is irrational in exactly the same sense as an agent whose credences violate the probability axioms. In fact, once it is put this way Reflection is best understood as Conditionalization on a special kind of evidence: I update on the proposition that my future credence in A is x by adopting credence x, which is just what Conditionalization would prescribe.
