I run a reading group on transitional justice, and two members have been arguing for a year without producing anything we could actually use. Call them the accountability camp and the peace-and-reconciliation camp. The accountability camp thinks amnesties for gross human rights violations are almost never justified: they point to Argentina, where the amnesty laws were eventually repealed and trials went ahead, and say South Africa's TRC let torturers walk free in exchange for partial truth. The peace camp thinks that's naive: they point to South Africa as the transition that didn't detonate, and to post-genocide Rwanda's prisons, backlogged trials, and gacaca courts as a workaround, to argue that maximum prosecution has real costs and sometimes a conditional amnesty is the only way to stop the killing or manage the transition. Write the document they should have produced instead: a joint statement, opening with a numbered list of substantive claims about the cases and the trade-offs that both camps can actually sign (not just "transitional justice is hard" — I want claims that are non-vacuous but genuinely shared). Then a table of the exact points where they still disagree, with each disagreement phrased so that the accountability camp would accept the wording of its own side and the peace camp would accept the wording of its own. Then identify the cruxes — the sub-questions where, if one side were convinced, the disagreement would actually move — and for each crux say what kind of evidence or case both sides would agree bears on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance. If some of the fight turns out to be about words (like what "justice" or "reconciliation" is even supposed to mean in a place that's just come out of mass violence), say which part and why. Use common knowledge of South Africa, Argentina, and Rwanda, and don't turn this into a bibliography. And don't adjudicate; the point is to write something both could sign of on and to make the leftover disagreement exact.
