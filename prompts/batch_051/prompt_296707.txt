I am serving on a university committee charged with revising our campus expression policy, and I keep running into a puzzle that strikes me as philosophical rather than merely legalistic. The current policy announces two commitments at once. It says that the university will not restrict speech merely because it is offensive, insulting, or in poor taste; that exposure to ideas one finds repellent is part of education; and that no member of the university has a right not to be offended. It also says that the university prohibits harassment, defined as repeated or severe demeaning, degrading, or intimidating remarks directed at a person or group because of race, sex, religion, disability, sexual orientation, or similar characteristics, when the conduct is severe or pervasive enough to deny someone equal access to education. In committee meetings, colleagues routinely describe these two commitments as a straightforward application of Mill's harm principle: restrict expression only to prevent harm to others, never to spare adults from being offended. But in the cases that actually come before us, nearly every alleged act of harassment arrives as speech that the accused insists was merely offensive, and our decisions look less like the application of a single principle than like a running negotiation over which kinds of offense get reclassified as harm.

I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of free speech and harm that a policy of this kind actually presupposes, as distinct from the theory it advertises. Specifically: what must such a policy assume about the difference between offense and harm for the line to be principled rather than an administrative convenience? Does it quietly treat some offenses as harms because they are repeated, targeted, aimed at protected characteristics, or because they interfere with equal access to education? Which rival theories could honestly endorse the same pair of commitments — a strict Millian who takes harm to mean concrete injury to person or property, someone who thinks dignitary or psychological injury is a real harm, someone who thinks the policy's true foundation is equal participation in the institution rather than the prevention of harm — and which of those theories does the policy silently rule out? Finally, if the committee really committed to Mill's principle as the sole legitimate ground for restricting expression, what would the policy have to change, and where would it most visibly break?
