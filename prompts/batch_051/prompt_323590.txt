I’m in a course on the concept of religion and we hit a wall on William James’s The Varieties of Religious Experience, which I think is from 1902. Early in the book he makes a move that I keep going back and forth about. I don’t have the page in front of me, but the passage goes roughly like this: for the purposes of his phsychology, he is going to treat religion as what happens in the inner life of a single person — their feelings, impulses, and experiences — insofar as that person takes themselves to be related to whatever they call divine. He then says, more or less, that this lets him ignore organized religion, ritual, and theology because those are second-hand products built around the primary personal experience.

What I want help with is not so much what the sentence says, but what it is doing in the larger debate about whether “religion” is a coherent category. On one reading, James is just stipulating a working definition: he wants to study personal religious experience, so he tells you what he is going to attend to and what he is going to set aside, and that’s fine. But on another reading, the definition is doing sneaky normative or Protestant-ish work, because it treats private inward experience as the real core of religion and makes institutional, ritual, and communal traditions look derivative. That would mean he hasn’t shown why Confucianism, say, should count as religion in the full sense rather than as ethics or civics; he’s just defined the problem away. So is this passage a neutral methodological move, a substantive contestable claim about the essence of religion, or both? And if somebody later says “Confucianism isn’t a religion under this definition,” have they learned something or are they just repeating the bias baked into the definition? I’m honestly not sure if I’m making a distinction without a difference.
