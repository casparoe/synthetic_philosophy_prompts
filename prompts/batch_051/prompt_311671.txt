Write a focused dialogue (roughly 900–1,200 words) between two equally sophisticated colleagues, Vera and Marcus, who have just learned that a mutual friend kept each of them waiting for twenty-five minutes on the same afternoon. They disagree about whether what happened was a moral wrong in the strict sense, and their exchange broadens into a disagreement about the moral structure of small commitments: promises to meet, to call back, to send something by a certain day.

Vera defends the strict-obligation view. When a person makes a small promise or sets a definite time, she creates a genuine obligation tied to that particular episode. The wrong of being late lies in the act itself: the other person arranged part of her day around the commitment and is made to spend time she cannot recover. On this view the range of proper defeaters is narrow — genuine emergency or a mutually agreed revision. Fatigue, forgetfulness, or the appearance of a better use for the afternoon do not dissolve the obligation, though they may bear on how culpable the breach is and what repair is owed.

Marcus defends the reliability-as-virtue view. Punctuality and small promises are governed primarily by norms of care, flexibility, and long-run dependability rather than by per-episode obligations. What matters morally is the pattern: whether someone is the kind of person others can count on over time, and how she handles lapses, rather than compliance on any single occasion. Treating each lateness as promise-breaking over-moralizes ordinary life, encourages brittleness and defensiveness, and mislocates the value of keeping commitments, which lies in the relationship and the disposition, not in the isolated act.

The dialogue must be a genuine engagement, not two monologues. Each speaker should answer the other's strongest point before advancing her own, and neither should be a strawman; each should concede a small point or refine her view at least once under pressure. As the exchange unfolds, have them work through the following pressure points in whatever order arises naturally:

1. whether the wrong of lateness attaches to the individual episode or only to the broader pattern of reliability;
2. what defeasibility requires — which considerations (emergency, competing obligations, good-faith miscalculation, forgetfulness, changed mood) each view can absorb and which it struggles to absorb;
3. what repair or apology is owed, and how that compares with what would be owed for breaking a weightier promise;
4. whether someone who is always on time out of anxiety or fear is morally better at keeping commitments than someone who is occasionally late but generally trustworthy.

Close with each character stating, in one or two sentences, something the other has made her see differently about her own view.
