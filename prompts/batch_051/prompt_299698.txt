In 1977, a small neo-Nazi group announced plans to demonstrate in Skokie, Illinois, a suburb that was home to a large Jewish population, including many Holocaust survivors. The village responded with ordinances intended to stop the demonstration; the ACLU, over significant internal dissent, defended the group's right to demonstrate; the courts eventually struck the ordinances down, and in the end the group demonstrated in Chicago rather than Skokie. I am not asking for the legal history; I am asking for the theoretical case analysis. Treat the episode as a stress test of the paradox of tolerance, including its usual corollary that a tolerant society may need to refuse tolerance to the intolerant. Run the analysis in both directions. From the theory to the case: what does the paradox say each actor — the village, the ACLU, the courts, the residents — faced, and what would it have licensed or prohibited each from doing? Be disciplined about the players, the options, and what would count as evidence for the paradox's empirical claim that tolerated intolerance spreads. Then from the case back to the theory: which features of the episode does the paradox explain well, which does it misdescribe, and where is it simply silent — for instance, about the swastika's distinct harm to a particular audience rather than general offense, or about the difference between suppressing a speaker and denying that speaker a particular town's public space? Finish by saying what the episode shows about the paradox: does it confirm it, expose it as a diagnosis rather than a decision rule, or force a revision in what the paradox is even about?
