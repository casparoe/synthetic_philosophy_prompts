I've been working through Fearon's rationalist explanations for war and trying to transplant the argumentative structure onto AI development races, and I've ended up with an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept, even though each step looked obvious when I wrote it down. Let me lay it out. (1) The main actors in a frontier AI race — leading labs, their home governments, and the coalitions around them — are instrumentally rational: they rank outcomes, respond to evidence, and can enter into binding arrangements when it pays to do so. (2) A flat-out race to transformative AI is dangerous for each of them in expectation, including the eventual winner, because the race consumes the very time and slack that safety research, verification institutions, and alignment work would need. (3) Whenever an arrangement is strictly better in expectation for every party than the path they are on, rational parties can bargain their way to that arrangement; in this case there should be some deal — a staged moratorium, a compute cap, a monitored pause — that each prefers to racing. (4) The obvious worry is monitoring, but the inputs to frontier training are unusually legible: chip shipments, datacenter power draws, and large training-run purchases are big, physical, and hard to hide, so compliance is at least in principle verifiable. (5) Therefore, the parties should find and keep some slowdown deal. (6) But the same actors look for all the world like they are racing — treating public safety commitments as moves inside a competitive game, hiding capability progress, and expanding compute as fast as they can finance. Since (5) is empirically false, at least one step has to go wrong. I would most like to hold onto (1) and (2); I do not want the answer to be simply that the actors are irrational or that the race is not actually dangerous. So where exactly does the argument break? Give me a committed verdict: which step or steps fail, why they looked sound, and what I am giving up in accepting the diagnosis.
