I keep noticing a double standard in debates about lethal autonomous weapons, and I can't tell whether it's a real principle or just discomfort with machines making life-and-death calls. Suppose a soldier or pilot, in poor visibility, misidentifies a target and kills civilians. There will be an investigation, and often the person is treated as having made a tragic fog-of-war mistake—maybe reprimanded, maybe not even that. Now suppose an autonomous system with the exact same sensor data and the same uncertainty makes the identical call. Even if the system's overall error rate is lower than the human's, the reaction is completely different: calls to ban the thing outright, and someone—the programmer, the commander, the manufacturer—is expected to take the blame. Same outcome, same evidence, but our judgment flips depending on whether a human being or a machine made the selection.

Write an essay testing whether this asymmetry is justified. Consider the best candidate justifications you can think of: there's a responsibility gap because the machine can't be punished or feel remorse; human judgment has some kind of authority even when it's worse on average; a machine error is a design defect while a human error is a tragedy; meaningful human control is valuable for its own sake. Then test those justifications against harder cases—for example, a negligent or drunk human who clearly should have known better, or a human who just rubber-stamps an AI targeting recommendation with seconds to veto. Reach a conclusion: should the asymmetry be kept, revised, or abandoned?
