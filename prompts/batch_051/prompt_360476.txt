I'm a retired particle physicist (spent most of my career in data analysis, where the look-elsewhere effect was basically part of the job) and I've recently started reading philosophy. I'm especially stuck on the epistemology of anecdote: the person who tells you they dreamed about their aunt the night she died, or bumped into an old friend in a random foreign city, and says "what are the odds?!". I'm writing a short piece for my reading group on why these stories don't impress me, and I want to get my list of deflationary mechanisms straight before the philosophy PhD in the group pokes holes in it. So far my list runs:

1. The birthday paradox: collisions are combinatorially cheap; with 23 people you're more likely than not to have a shared birthday, even though any particular pair is a long shot.
2. The law of truly large numbers: with enough trials, even very rare events will happen somewhere to someone.
3. The look-elsewhere effect (multiple comparisons): if you scan many possible coincidences and then report the one that hit, you've rigged the significance test.
4. Selective memory and reporting: we remember the dream that came true and forget the hundreds that didnt, and only the hits get turned into anecdotes.
5. Hidden common causes: two seemingly unconnected events line up because of some boring shared cause (we were both in Berlin because it's spring break in our field).

I have the feeling I'm forgetting a sixth, well-known mechanism — not a rewording, special case, or combination of the five above, and I suspect it's a psychological one about the pattern-hungry mind rather than about the probability calculus itself. Which one is it, and what work does it do that the other five don't? Please organize the answer under three headings: "The missing mechanism" (2–3 sentences, stated precisely enough to use), "Why it isn't one of the five" (one sentence per listed item), and "What it adds" (1–2 sentences).
