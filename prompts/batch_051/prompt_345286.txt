Since my mother entered the late stages of Alzheimer's, I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about personal identity: I find I cannot treat her as anyone other than the person who raised me, even though she no longer recognizes me and has lost nearly all her memories. I want one account that captures my verdicts on the following cases, with a committed defense of it rather than a survey of the options. In Locke's prince-and-cobbler case, where the prince's consciousness and memories are transferred into a cobbler's body, I do not think the prince survives in the cobbler; the prince is gone and the cobbler is deluded about his past. In Reid's brave-officer case, where a boy is flogged for stealing from an orchard, later takes an enemy standard as an officer, and as an old general remembers the standard but not the flogging, I think the boy, the officer, and the general are all the same person; this tells me memory cannot be the whole of identity, because identity must be transitive. After an accident that causes total amnesia and a lasting change of personality, I still think the person is the same person as before. A person in an irreversible vegetative state is also the same person. But if a perfect duplicate of me were created on Mars while I slept, complete with all my memories, I do not think the duplicate is me. Here is the verdict that may not fit the rest: if my neurons were gradually replaced over a decade by functionally identical artificial neurons while consciousness, memory, and character continued uninterrupted, I think the person at the end is still me. Is there a single principle that delivers all of these, and if not, which verdict would you overturn? Please apply that principle to the case I began with — the late-stage Alzheimer's patient whose episodic memories are gone but whose habits, mannerisms, and emotions remain in a reduced form — and tell me whether my conviction that she is the same person survives it.
