So our school started using this early-warning algorithm that flags kids who are probably going to fail math so they get put into tutoring before the semester ends. In stats class we got the confusion matrix from last year and me and my friend cant agree on whether it was fair. I decided a fair flagging system has to meet three rules: (1) among the kids who actually failed, the same fraction should get flagged in each group, so it isn't letting one group slip through; (2) among the kids who actually passed, the same fraction should get flagged in each group, so it isn't over-flagging one group; and (3) among the kids who got flagged, the same fraction should have actually failed in each group, so a flag means the same thing no matter who you are.

The two groups I care about are transfer students and everyone else. Transfers fail math around 30% of the time, non-transfers around 10%. The current model caught 80% of the kids who ended up failing in both groups, and it flagged 20% of the kids who ended up passing in both groups, so it satisfies rules 1 and 2. But when I do the arithmetic, rule 3 is badly violated: among flagged kids, about 31% of non-transfers actually failed, versus about 63% of transfers. My friend says you could just retrain the model or tune the threshold and eventually make all three hold at once. I don't see how, given that the base rates are stuck being different, and the model will never be perfect (theres always going to be some false positives and some false negatives in both groups) and it has to catch at least some of the kids who end up failing or there's no point.

So my actual question is whether my three-rule definition of fair is even satisfiable in this situation, or whether I accidentally wrote down an impossible set of demands. Go through it criterion by criterion and show me either how all three could hold at once or why they can't, and then tell me which rule I should drop or weaken and what the school should actually be trying to balance.
