Two of my coworkers, Ethan and Rosa, got into an argument after one of them came back from jury duty. I was asked to referee and could not, so I want a careful verdict. The exchange, as I remember it, went like this.

Ethan: Suppose a pedestrian is hit by a bus that leaves the scene. In a civil case the plaintiff only needs to show it is more likely than not that the defendant did it. Now suppose the only substantial evidence is that the Blue Bus Company runs about eighty percent of the buses along that road at that time of day. Then it is about eighty percent likely that a Blue bus was involved, which is comfortably above the civil threshold. So the plaintiff should win.

Rosa: That makes liability follow market share rather than facts about the crash. Blue would pay for some accidents it did not cause, and a small rival would escape paying for accidents it did cause, all because of a statistic that floats free of the particular incident. A verdict is supposed to connect this defendant to this plaintiff.

Ethan: The statistic is not floating free; it is about that road and that time. And the law uses background rates constantly. If there were also a witness who thought the bus was blue, or records placing a Blue driver near the intersection, you would happily add the eighty percent to that evidence and call it a strong case. You cannot consistently treat the same probability as genuine evidence when it has company but worthless when it stands alone.

Rosa: Its weight does change, because the number depends on the denominator you choose. Blue might be eighty percent of the buses on that road at that time, but sixty percent of the buses in the whole city, or ninety percent of the buses travelling in that direction, or forty percent of the buses making that particular turn. Each of those is a true description, and each gives a different probability for this accident. Unless someone justifies selecting one denominator rather than another, the eighty percent is being asked to do more work than it can support.

Ethan: That is a general problem for all evidence, not just statistics. A witness's reliability also depends on the class you put her in — lighting, distance, eyesight, how long she looked. We do not exclude eyewitnesses because their reliability can be sliced different ways; we pick the relevant class and make an estimate. The bus figure deserves the same treatment rather than a special ban on background rates.

Rosa: Then consider the rodeo case. One thousand people attend a ticketed event, and the records establish that four hundred ninety-nine paid while the rest did not. So we know five hundred one people got in without paying. Pick any single attendee, and the odds are slightly better than even that they are a gatecrasher. Would you let the venue sue a randomly chosen attendee and win on those odds alone? If not, then you already accept that clearing the probability threshold is not enough, and you owe an account of what the bus case has that the rodeo case lacks.

Ethan: The bus case has a defined injury and an identified causal channel: a bus hit someone, and the only open question is whose. The rodeo case would be a pure guess pulling a defendant out of a crowd. There is also a difference in what a verdict says about a defendant. A negligence judgment against a company is spreading a cost; an accusation of gatecrashing brands an individual as dishonest. The same probability standard can be sensible in the first setting and not in the second.

Rosa: The first point assumes the very link that is in dispute: the statistic tells us about the fleet, and the step from fleet facts to this crash is what you were supposed to justify. The second point can be removed by restating the rodeo suit as a claim for the price of admission, with no charge of dishonesty. The arithmetic is unchanged, and I doubt you would allow that either.

At that point they had to go back to work, and I was left unsure which of them was right about which point. Please adjudicate the exchange step by step — say whether each move is broadly right, broadly wrong, or mixed, and why. Then give an overall verdict on whose position is stronger, and explain what principle, if anything, genuinely distinguishes the bus case from the rodeo case. Finally, briefly say whether the analysis should differ in a criminal trial, where the standard is proof beyond a reasonable doubt.
