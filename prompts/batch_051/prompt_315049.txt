I serve on the board of a mid-sized public library system, and we have to choose a replacement for the software that runs our catalog, circulation, and patron accounts. The current vendor ends support in fourteen months. We have been looking for eleven months, have sat through demonstrations from nine vendors, and have rejected every one of them. We are seven volunteers with full-time jobs, our single director is already overworked, and we set aside no money for a paid consultant to do an exhaustive comparison; the search itself is consuming the time of the people who would have to manage the migration. No product has been strongest on every dimension, and whenever a candidate comes close, someone on the board finds a reason to set it aside and go looking for a better one. Our own staff members have twice narrowed the field to a system they could live with, and both times a different board member blocked the choice over a shortcoming the rest of us thought was tolerable. Several of us keep arriving at meetings with news of another promising vendor someone read about, which restarts the process. Meanwhile the support deadline is getting closer, and the delay is no longer abstract.

I have no academic background in philosophy or decision theory, so I may be reinventing something that already has a name. But I have been trying to understand the pattern we are in. It seems to me that we are waiting for an option that beats every alternative on every relevant measure, and that even if such an option exists, the time and attention required to find it may cost more than the difference between it and the best system we have already seen. Waiting is not a neutral act. My view is that the board should decide in advance what an acceptable system would have to do, and then commit to taking the first option that clears that bar, even if something better might turn up later.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the next board meeting. It should recommend, concretely, that before any further vendor materials are shared, each board member submit no more than five requirements a system must meet to be acceptable and no more than three conditions under which they would vote no; that the board then argue the floor out and adopt a single list by majority vote; that staff bring forward no more than four vendors who state in writing that they can meet that floor; and that the board commit to selecting the first vendor who verifiably meets every item on the list, reopening the choice only if a vendor has misrepresented something material. If none of the four passes, the board should revise the floor rather than widen the search. The memo should also tie the process to a firm end date, so that within the next two regular meetings we either adopt a system or formally lower the floor.

I need a document the board could actually act on, so the recommendation should be stated plainly at the start rather than buried at the end. The memo must also engage seriously with the strongest objection I expect from my colleagues on the other side: that we are spending public money and therefore have a duty to get the best available system, not merely an adequate one, and that a rule telling us to stop looking is a way of dressing up laziness or low standards as wisdom. I do not want that objection dismissed as perfectionism; I want it answered honestly, with a clear account of when someone in their position should win.

Please write in continuous prose only, the way one would write an email or a letter, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables. Do not include citations, footnotes, or reading recommendations; I want your own reasoning.
