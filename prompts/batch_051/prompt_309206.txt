Question: Distinguish the easy problems of consciousness from the hard problem as Chalmers draws the distinction, and evaluate whether the hard problem could be dissolved rather than solved. Your answer should present at least one deflationary strategy and assess whether it succeeds.

Student's answer:

Chalmers distinguishes the easy problems of consciousness from the hard problem. The easy problems ask how the brain performs certain functions: how it discriminates stimuli, integrates information from different senses, focuses attention, and produces verbal reports about its own states. These are easy only in a relative sense, since they are still enormously difficult scientific tasks, but they can in principle be pursued with the ordinary methods of neuroscience and cognitive science. The hard problem is different. Even if all those functions were explained, we would still not have explained why the processing is accompanied by subjective experience at all, or why there is something it is like for the organism. Nagel's bat example is often read as anticipating this distinction: we could understand how a bat's sonar works without grasping what it is like for the bat to perceive through sonar.

One prominent response is the deflationary one associated with Dennett. Dennett argues that consciousness does not really exist. Qualia, understood as private, ineffable items in an inner theater, are a leftover from Cartesian dualism, and a complete functional account of the brain would leave no further experience to explain. On this view the hard problem is not solved but dissolved, because the question asks after something that was never there.

Critics maintain that this only changes the subject. Levine's explanatory gap makes the same point in a sharper form: no account in purely physical or functional terms explains why a particular neural process feels like something distinctive, such as the taste of lemon, and this shows that physicalism cannot account for consciousness. Chalmers has also argued that a zombie physically identical to an ordinary person but with no experience at all is conceivable, and he takes this to show that physicalism leaves something out. The deflationary strategy therefore denies the data rather than explaining it, and the hard problem remains in place.
