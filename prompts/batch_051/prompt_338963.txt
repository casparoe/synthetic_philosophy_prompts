Here is an argument about the epistemology of testimony that I keep failing to settle. I will write it as numbered steps.

(1) Reductionism about testimony says that a hearer is justified in believing a speaker's assertion only if she has non-testimonial evidence—from her own perception, memory, and inference—that the speaker, or reports of that kind, are reliable.
(2) To have such evidence about a speaker's reliability, the hearer must be able to understand the speaker's assertions and compare them with facts she has observed for herself.
(3) Understanding an assertion requires knowing what its words mean.
(4) A hearer's knowledge of word meanings is itself acquired by trusting users of the language: nobody observes the meaning of "cold" the way she observes cold water; she absorbs it by taking others' word for how the expression is used.
(5) So before a hearer can check any report against the observed facts, she has already acquired a large stock of beliefs that depend on testimony.
(6) Therefore the reductionist's requirement—that testimonial justification be built from non-testimonial materials—cannot be met, because the would-be evidence gatherer always arrives already relying on testimony.
(7) But ordinary hearers plainly are justified in their early, language-acquiring testimonial beliefs: we learn that the bathwater is cold, that the stove is hot, and that the cat is called "the cat" this way.
(8) Hence reductionism is false, and testimony must be a basic source of justification in roughly the anti-reductionist sense: absent defeaters, we are entitled to accept what we are told.

I half-suspect the trouble is in step (4), and that there is a slide between learning a language by living among its speakers and believing something on the strength of a speaker's say-so—but I cannot make the objection stick. Maybe the distinction is merely psychological rather than epistemic, and the argument is simply right that reductionism collapses this quickly. So: is the argument valid?
