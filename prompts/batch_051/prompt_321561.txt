In a jurisprudence seminar I'm taking, the old maxim *ignorantia juris non excusat* — "ignorance of the law is no excuse" — keeps being invoked as if it closed the question. Most recently I raised a fairness worry: modern legal systems are so large and change so quickly that a person can, in good faith and with ordinary diligence, still fail to know the rule they are later punished under. My examples were a technical regulation buried in an administrative code, a traveler subject to a foreign statute they had no realistic way to learn, and conduct criminalized by legislation enacted while someone was abroad and beyond the reach of normal notice. My classmate replied that the maxim already answers the worry: the law may bind and punish you whether or not you knew it.

I want to respond with more care than he did. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that the maxim might be expressing, from the comparatively trivial — say, a procedural rule about which defenses a court will entertain — to the strongly moral, such as a substantive thesis about when it is fair to blame or punish someone? For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a defensible substantive claim, or something likely false, and note how much the reading depends on the kind of law at issue: conduct wrong in itself versus wrong only because prohibited (*malum in se* versus *malum prohibitum*), criminal versus civil liability, and genuine mistake of law versus mistake of fact. Finally, tell me which reading my classmate would need for the maxim to actually answer my fairness objection, and whether that reading survives contact with the examples I gave. I am less interested in a single interpretation than in a menu I can use to show exactly where the maxim reaches and where it does not.
