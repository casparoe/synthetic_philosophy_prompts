I run a historical-interview podcast in which the guest answers in the first person as a past philosopher, defending that thinker's actual arguments rather than supplying biography. The next guest will appear as David Hume, and the episode will focus on his short essay Of Suicide. The audience will get a summary of the essay in the introduction, so the questions must not simply ask him to restate the position. Please draft eight to ten questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give, and make sure all four of the following pressure points are covered. The remaining questions should probe other specific vulnerabilities in the essay rather than asking about influences, legacy, or how he came to the topic.

1. The providence argument. One question should press his claim that suicide cannot violate a duty to God once providence is understood to run through general laws, since a self-inflicted death is no more a breach of the divine order than a falling stone or a river's changing course. The question should ask whether this defense succeeds only by draining divine-command morality of any restrictive content, since on that picture every deliberate human act is equally the working of general laws, and whether he has not defended suicide at the price of showing that no act whatever can be an offense against God. Its follow-up should anticipate his likely reply that the theological objection is dissolved and the real question moves to our duties to society and to ourselves, and should ask what is left for the objector who is convinced God forbids suicide if God's will can never be read out of any particular event.

2. Duty to society. One question should press his claim that obligations to society are reciprocal and bounded, so that a person who has ceased to contribute, or for whom remaining alive offers only a small public good at the cost of great private evil, does society no meaningful wrong by departing. The question should confront him with a case in which leaving would not merely withdraw a contribution but would wound or burden particular others, and should ask whether those harms fit within reciprocal-benefit accounting or are something stronger. Its follow-up should anticipate his likely reply that special obligations to dependents or intimates can render a particular suicide wrongful on his own terms, and should ask how far the view then makes permissibility depend on a self-conducted audit of who owes what to whom, in which the sole auditor is the person with the strongest motive to settle the accounts in his own favor.

3. Duty to oneself. One question should press his claim that prudence need not prolong a life that age, sickness, or hopeless pain has turned into an evil, and that in such circumstances ending one's life can be self-regarding rather than self-destructive. The question should raise the divided-self problem: what licenses the present, suffering self to close the account for a future self whose conditions might be improved, and does the argument survive if the future self would repudiate the verdict? Its follow-up should anticipate his likely reply that the one living the life is the only available judge of whether it remains worth living, and should ask how he distinguishes that calm judgment from the very despair the objector says is doing the judging, without simply declaring the judgment calm because he endorses its conclusion.

4. The charge of superstition. One question should press his diagnosis of the traditional ban on suicide as superstition rather than reason, and should ask whether the deep-seated horror most people feel at suicide could be evidence of a real moral boundary rather than a prejudice to be explained away. Its follow-up should anticipate his likely reply that moral sentiments are partly acquired and vary with custom, and should ask what test he has for sorting a corrigible custom from a well-founded moral sentiment, and whether the fact that various nations have admired, tolerated, or condemned suicide is by itself enough to show that the horror is merely customary.
