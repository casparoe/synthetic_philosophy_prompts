I'm putting together a handout for a reading group that keeps talking past each other about AI welfare, so I want a decision tree that sorts people by what they actually say about cases, not by which label they'd pick. Please write a decision tree titled something like "What makes a computation matter morally?" with 8-10 branch nodes and around 7 leaves.

The tree should start from a single question: "Suppose a system looks smart or seems to have a point of view; does it matter morally for its own sake, and if so, in virtue of what?" Every branch node should be a forced-choice or yes/no question someone can answer without already knowing the positions, and each branch should be labeled by the claim that splits it (like "does it need a biological nervous system?", "does it need recurrent feedback rather than feedforward processing?", "does it need to represent its own states?"). The leaves, in whatever order the logic ends up producing, should be these positions, unless your tree genuinely cant reach one — in that case say so and say why:

- computational functionalism (the right computation suffices, substrate doesn't matter)
- biological chauvinism (only biological neurons can do it)
- integrated information theory–style view (high-phi / causal integration is required)
- higher-order / self-model view (requires some representation of its own states)
- global workspace–style view (requires broadcast / access consciousness, not just any old computation)
- panpsychist minimalism (everything physical has at least a tiny morally relevant something, and more integrated systems have more)
- eliminativism / illusionism (the question is confused; nothing really has the relevant property)

Please make sure these cases show up in whichever nodes fit them naturally: a feedforward image classifier; a huge lookup table that maps every possible input to human-ish replies; a slug or jellyfish; a neuron-by-neuron simulation of a human brain; a biological brain in a vat with no sensory input; and an AI that can report and use a model of its own attention states but is otherwise simple. For each leaf give one sentence on its most awkward commitment, flag any pair of positions your tree can't seperate, and identify at least one combination of answers that ends up empty, with a short note on whether nobody's there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Keep it under about 1,500 words, and write in American English.
