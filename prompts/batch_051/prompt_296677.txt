I'm a control systems engineer by training — I spend my time with setpoints, feedback loops, observers, model predictive control, safety instrumented systems, interlocks, and the hand-off logic between operator modes and automatic modes. I keep hearing about "corrigibility" and the "shutdown problem" in AI alignment, and the way it is usually framed goes something like this: an agent maximizing almost any objective will have an instrumental incentive to prevent being shut down or corrected, because shutdown and correction prevent it from achieving the objective; corrigibility is then the desideratum of building a system that doesn't resist correction, doesn't manipulate its operators, and doesn't disable its own off-switch. Superficially, this sounds like the world I already work in — we build plants where the safety system can trip the process independent of the control loops, where operators can take manual control, where permissive logic blocks unsafe commands. So here is what I want from you. Restate the standard positions in the corrigibility debate — corrigibility understood as a disposition rather than an objective to be maximized, the shutdown problem as it is formalized when the off-switch is an action available to the agent, utility indifference as a proposed way to avoid an incentive to disable the switch, and the idea that corrigibility decomposes into properties like not manipulating the supervisor, not subverting the oversight channel, and keeping the operator informed of what the system believes — using the vocabulary I actually know: process variables and state estimation, setpoints and reference governors, redundant shutdown paths and safety relays, authority hand-off and mode logic, estimator bias, and the distinction between a constraint and an objective function. Treat me as fluent in control theory but only loosely familiar with the alignment literature.
