I have spent the last year reading Thomas Aquinas's Summa Theologiae in translation, without any formal training, and I keep returning to a short passage in his discussion of sedition. In II-II, q. 42, a. 2, ad 3, he writes:

A tyrannical government is not just, because it is directed, not to the common good, but to the private good of the ruler, as the Philosopher states (Polit. iii, 5; Ethic. viii, 10). Consequently there is no sedition in disturbing a government of this kind, unless indeed the tyrant's rule be disturbed so inordinately, that his subjects suffer greater harm from the consequent disturbance than from the tyrant's government.

I have been trying to pin down what positive view about resistance to tyrants this commits Aquinas to, and I have gathered four readings people give of the passage. The readings I have so far are:

1. The appointed-authority reading: because Aquinas says only that there is no sedition, he is not licensing private persons; the only person who may act against a tyrant is one with public authority to act for the community.

2. The lesser-evil reading: the passage's operative condition is the "unless" clause, so resistance is permitted only when it will not produce greater harm than the tyrant's rule.

3. The definitional reading: the sentence is primarily about the definition of sedition as a sin against the unity and common good; since tyranny attacks the common good, opposing it fails the definition of sedition, without yet saying who may act or when.

4. The inversion reading: the remark that tyrants encourage discord to lord over the multitude means Aquinas treats the tyrant, not the resister, as the true seditionist, and the passage's force is rhetorical rather than juridical.

I want one more reading of this passage that is not on the list and not just a combination of items on it. In particular, I would like one that does not treat the passage primarily as a text about the morality of tyrannicide at all, but as something else that follows from Aquinas's use of Aristotle's politics in defining sin. Then say honestly whether the new reading fits the text better than the four I already have.
