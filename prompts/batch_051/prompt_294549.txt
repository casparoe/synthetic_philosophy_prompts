I run a small physiotherapy clinic—five clinicians, two receptionists, about three hundred appointments a week. Last night I had a long argument with a friend who is pathologically punctual; he kept insisting that when I said 'I'll call you at the weekend' and then didn't, I broke a promise, and that treating these things as trivial is exactly what makes people unreliable. I half-agree with him, but I also think it would be mad to run a clinic as if every small commitment were a sacred vow. The argument made me realise I don't actually have a policy for the small promises we make to patients and to each other: calling back by the end of the day, starting appointments on time, holding a slot, sending a promised email, covering a colleague's admin task. Right now it's just whoever is on the desk deciding on vibes.

I'd like a decision proceedure my receptionists and clinicians can actually follow in the moment, grounded in whatever the philosophy of promising and reliability actually knows—not just customer-service instincts. It has to be printable on one page, usable by people with no philosophy background, and it should tell them when a small commitment can be dropped, postponed, broken or renegotiated, and when it cant. It should also force them to distinguish a real commitment from a mere estimate or a polite figure of speech, because that's where most of our complaints start. I'm the one who signs off on it, but the people using it won't have time to deliberate for more than a minute or two.

Then run the procedure through three cases: (1) a clinician is running fifteen minutes late and the receptionist knows the next patient will sit and wait; (2) a patient has been promised a call by Friday about their scan result, but the result won't be back till Monday and the receptionist could just not call; (3) a clinician promised a colleague they'd cover a small admin task by Tuesday, but an urgent family thing comes up on Monday night. Flag where the procedure gives an answer I won't like, and where it just hands the decision back to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Oh, and please write the answer itself in British English—the policy will be read in the UK.
