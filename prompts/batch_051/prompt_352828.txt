I'm trying to think through a statute of limitations case and could use another angle. A statute says: "An action to recover damages for personal injury caused by exposure to a hazardous substance shall be commenced within three years after the cause of action accrues, and not thereafter." A company dumped a chemical into a town's groundwater in 1982—perfectly legal at the time, and no one had any idea it was harmful. In 2022, studies finally link it to a rare kidney disease, and a bunch of residents who grew up drinking that water get diagnosed and want to sue. The company says the cause of action accrued decades ago, back around the exposure, so the claim is dead. The residents say there was no injury anyone could have sued over until the disease actually showed up. The legislature that wrote the statute in the 1950s was picturing spills with immediate, obvious harm—not chemicals that sit quiet for forty years. How should the statute apply? Walk me through the plain text, the purpose of a limitations period, and what the drafters would most likely have said if you'd asked them, then say which approach should win and why. I'm less interested in the practical answer then in whether the passage of time itself does any moral work in these limitations periods, or whether time is just a rough proxy for stale evidence, faded memory, and the defendant's interest in being done with it. If you think the residents should win, does that reading quietly make every hidden long-latency harm sueable forever, and is that a reason to prefer one of the other approaches?
