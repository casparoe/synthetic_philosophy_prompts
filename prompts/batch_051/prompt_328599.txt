Since retiring from a research career in systems ecology, I have joined a reading group for retired scientists and have been working slowly through G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica. The passages I keep returning to are his discussion of what he calls organic unities — the claim that the intrinsic value of a whole need not equal the sum of the values of its parts — and his associated claim that the greatest intrinsic goods are those realized in personal affection and in the appreciation of beauty. To my ear this sounds remarkably close to something I used to tell graduate students in another vocabulary: that an ecosystem is not additive in its components, that a community has properties that cannot be read off a species list, and that measures good for each part can nonetheless degrade the whole. I have learned to distrust that kind of recognition, since a false friend is more dangerous than a discovery.

I would like you to restate Moore's views on beauty and friendship, together with the doctrine of organic unities that supports them, in the working vocabulary of a systems ecologist: emergent properties, nonlinearity, feedbacks, community structure, functional groups, ecosystem services, resilience, and regime shifts. I am fully fluent in that vocabulary and only a novice in the philosophical one. For each element of Moore's position, say what translates cleanly, what must be bent or stretched to fit, and what has no ecological counterpart at all. Then report the residue: judging by what fails to survive the translation, is the problem that Moore's claims are less precise than the prose suggests, that the ecological framework is the wrong instrument for the question, or something else? I want to know whether my intuition that all of this is just non-additivity is correct, and if it is not, exactly what I am missing.
