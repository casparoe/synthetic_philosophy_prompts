i keep going in circles on Moore's paradox — the thing where someone says that it's raining, but also says they dont believe it's raining. The sentence can be true, since whether it's raining and what you believe are independent, but actually coming out with it feels like a program crash. I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background, so I've got two candidate bug reports for the crash and I want you to build me a case that separates them.

Explanation A (the state bug): saying the first half of the sentence in your own voice normally expresses the belief that it's raining. So the whole thing expresses two claims about your own head at once: I believe it's raining, and I don't believe it's raining. Those two can't both be accurate — if the first is, the second is false, and if the second is, the first is false. So the speaker is guaranteed to be wrong about at least one thing in their own head. The weirdness is a self-undermining mental state.

Explanation B (the act bug): the problem isn't the mental state, it's the asserting. Asserting that it's raining carries a precondition, roughly that the person doing the asserting believes it. Following it up by saying that you don't believe it is like calling a function without satisfying its precondition. The weirdness is in the act, not in the state.

Construct an ordinary-life thought experiment where these two explanations come apart: specifically, a case where Explanation A predicts the sentence is still defective and Explanation B predicts it isn't defective in the Moorean way. Constraints: keep it first-person and present-tense, no actors and no one speaking on someone else's behalf, no mental illness or self-deception, and nobody gets cute about what believe means. Then walk through what each explanation says about the case and pin down the exact feature that makes them split. Finish with what a defender of Explanation B would say to argue the case doesn't count.
