I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character undergoes an experience she can only call, with some embarrassment, mystical: a season of intense, wordless conviction that the world is in some way unified and that this matters enormously, followed by a long aftermath in which she tries and fails to tell another character, and herself, what happened. The novel stands or falls on how I render these scenes, and I find that my craft intuitions give out exactly where the interesting difficulties begin. I want to replace them with something I can use at the desk: a repeatable procedure for deciding how to write any scene in which a character is at, or beyond, the edge of what language can carry.

The question I want the procedure to answer is this: given a scene that trades on ineffability, when do I have something worth keeping, and when am I only gesturing at profundity while saying nothing? I keep circling a philosophical view, and I want the procedure either to respect it or to tell me plainly to abandon it. The view is that an experience can be genuinely contentful—can be a way of knowing something—without its content being the kind of thing a declarative sentence can state. If that is right, the writer's task is not to describe the indescribable, which would be self-undermining, but to make the reader grasp what it is for something to resist description, and to feel the weight of a knowledge that will not pass into speech. If it is wrong—if the ineffable is only the intense but empty—then my novel is built on a mistake, and the procedure should say so rather than flatter it.

The procedure has to handle the cases I actually meet in drafting, not only the theory. In particular it must distinguish: (1) narration that merely asserts that something cannot be put into words, which I can attach to any event and which does no work, from narration that enacts that unsayability for the reader; (2) negation that does real work, the not-this and not-that that narrows a space, from negation that merely drapes prose in profundity; (3) a scene in which a character tries to report the experience to another person and fails, from one in which she reports it too easily, and what each failure of language is doing dramatically; (4) whether an ineffable experience can still support definite commitments—a character who returns convinced that she must leave her family, or that nothing is forbidden—and how the rendering can make such an inference legitimate or expose it as self-deception; (5) when silence, a scene break, or a blank space is the honest choice, and when it is the author spending a lack of understanding as if it were a style.

Ground the steps in a defensible account of what it means for something to be unsayable in principle: not merely that no words have been found yet, and not merely that a full account would take a long time, but some kind of principled limit. Mark explicitly where the philosophy stops and my judgment as a writer begins. I do not want false precision that turns craft into an algorithm, and I do not want the procedure to smuggle in a metaphysics I did not agree to. If the procedure must take a stand—for example, on whether the character's conviction counts as knowledge or only as a powerful feeling—it should say which stand it is taking, and which alternative stand would require a different procedure.

Apply the procedure to three scenes from the book, including at least one where it instructs me to do something I will resist: to cut a passage I am proud of, to abandon a framing I have relied on, or to say plainly in narration something I wanted to leave as a resonant fog. Be honest about what the procedure cannot do. I expect it cannot judge the quality of a sentence, guarantee how a reader receives a scene, or decide whether my overall project is worth finishing; say where using it would actually harm the novel, and what in those places remains a matter of ear and nerve rather than principle.
