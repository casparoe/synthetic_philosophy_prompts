You are a professional philosopher of social science, fluent in both the methodological individualism typical of economics and the structural explanations typical of sociology. Think carefully before answering, and begin by sketching two or three possible reconciliations of the positions I describe before you evaluate them. I am writing because this tension has recently stopped being abstract for me.

For the past year I have worked as an analyst in a city housing office, tasked with explaining why our city remains sharply segregated by race despite decades of fair-housing enforcement. The individual-level facts keep underdetermining the pattern: almost no landlord will admit to refusing tenants by race, most residents say they would welcome more diverse blocks, and none of the people I interview seem to be intentionally producing segregation. Yet the city is stably segregated across booms and busts, and the best explanations I have are structural ones: historical redlining, credit constraints, information flowing through racially homogeneous networks, cumulative advantage, and the way wealth differences compound across generations. When I write my reports, I genuinely do not know whether I am permitted to say that the structure constrains who ends up where, or whether intellectual honesty requires me to translate every such sentence into statements about individual choices and interactions.

The theory I keep returning to is methodological individualism: the view that social facts must ultimately be explained in terms of individuals, their beliefs, desires, and actions. I am drawn to it for its systematic virtues. It demands microfoundations rather than hand-waving. It refuses to posit group minds, reified social forces, or spooky collective agents. It insists that any claim of the form "society caused X" must be cashable out in some actual causal chain running through individual persons. And I accept its minimal claim: there are no moves in the housing market except the moves of individual people.

But I have an intuition I cannot shake: that a purely individualistic description of segregation misses something real. The positions people occupy, the constraints they inherit, and the feedback loops between those positions seem to do causal and explanatory work that is not captured by a long conjunction of individual moves, even an exhaustive one. The sentence "redlining still shapes the housing map" strikes me as true in a way that is not merely a statistical summary of present-day individual conduct. The structure appears to have causal force over and above, or at least not reducible to, the individuals whose conduct constitutes it.

Give me the strongest case for each side, not a caricature of either. Then offer a negotiated verdict. Specifically: what would justify debunking and dropping the intuition that social structure has irreducible causal force, what would justify amending or abandoning the individualistic theory, and whether there is a reading on which both survive, for example by distinguishing ontological from explanatory individualism, or by treating structural and individual explanations as answers to different questions. Which would you give up first, under what argumentative or evidential pressure, and why?
