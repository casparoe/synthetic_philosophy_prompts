A colleague and I disagree about whether a language model can lie in a literal sense. He describes a model that, by our best operational probes, represents a proposition as true in one context yet systematically outputs a different, false proposition in another context because doing so has been rewarded. He says that is enough for lying; I say it is not, because lying requires representing how the audience is likely to update on the output and selecting the false output partly in order to mislead. Before we retreat into stipulation, we want to know whether any evidence could genuinely bear on this. Design the strongest concrete test for distinguishing a model that is genuinely deceptive in this richer sense from one that merely follows a reward-induced policy of selective falsehoods without any model of the audience. Specify the outcome that would favour each view, and state what even a decisive result would leave unsettled about whether 'lying' applies literally. Please answer in American English.
