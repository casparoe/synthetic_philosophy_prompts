Something I've never managed to square. People who care a lot about animal welfare will tie themselves in knots over whether beef or chicken is the lesser evil — for the same amount of meat, roughly one cow with a name versus a hundred chickens with miserable lives — but those same people loose no sleep over the mice, voles, and frogs that got chewed up by the combines when the corn and soy that fed those chickens got harvested. It's not that they don't know it happens; they just file it under "life has costs" and go back to arguing about the cow. But from the vole's point of view a combine blade is about as bad as a slaughterhouse bolt. So we seem to count the deaths of animals we can picture and quietly discount the deaths of animals we can't. Is there an actual principle that draws the line between the pig and the vole — something about domestic versus wild, about intent, about the death being a side-effect of growing a crop — or is it just that one has a face and the other is a number? I'd like the strongest version of the principle, tested against a nearby case (say, a farmer who could cheaply flush the voles out before harvest but doesn't, or grass-fed beef that skips feed crops entirely), and then your honest verdict: keep the asymmetry, give it up, or reverse it?
