I run an interpretability review team at an AI lab. We assess dozens of claims each month — 'this attention head represents the indirect object,' 'this direction represents honesty' — and some of them end up in safety documentation, so the stakes in getting the wording right are real. We keep disagreeing about whether such statements are literally true of the model's internals or just useful summaries of our probes. I want a reusable decision procedure for when a specific attribution of representational content to a network is warranted, one an engineer can run in a review meeting without reading the philosophy. Lay out the questions in order; say what counts as a sufficient answer to each; say where the procedure stops with 'not established' and where it hands the call to a person; flag the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as tests; and tell me which distinctions from the realism/instrumentalism debate must survive translation and which can be dropped. Then apply the procedure to three worked cases and take a definite position on each rather than laying out options: (1) a linear probe that decodes a concept well above chance, but intervening on the decoded direction produces no downstream change; (2) a small circuit that causally mediates a behavior and is given a clean natural-language gloss, where the gloss was written only after the circuit was found; (3) a saliency map that highlights input features that look meaningful to a human, though an untrained network on the same inputs yields similar highlights. Make sure at least one verdict goes against what my team would prefer, and close by saying what the procedure cannot settle.
