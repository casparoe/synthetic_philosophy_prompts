I am a teaching assistant for an undergraduate course in the philosophy of language, and I am grading a short-answer exam on theories of reference and meaning. I would like a second opinion on one student's response before I finalize my comments. The exam question and the student's answer are reproduced below.

Question: Explain Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment and what it is supposed to establish. In your answer, describe the scenario and the relevant respects in which the two planets and their speakers are alike or different; reconstruct the argument for the claim that two speakers can mean different things by the same word; state what the argument is meant to rule out; and identify at least one limitation, objection, or further assumption on which the argument depends.

Student's answer: Putnam imagines a planet, Twin Earth, that is exactly like Earth except that the clear, drinkable liquid that fills its lakes and rivers and is called water by its inhabitants has the molecular structure XYZ rather than H2O. Its observable properties are identical, and the scenario is set in 1750, so nobody on either planet could distinguish the two substances by appearance. Oscar on Earth and Twin Oscar on Twin Earth are molecule-for-molecule duplicates and are in exactly the same brain state, with the same narrow psychology. Nevertheless, the word water as used by Oscar refers to H2O, whereas as used by Twin Oscar it refers to XYZ. Since the two speakers share the same internal state but their words pick out different substances, the meaning of water cannot be something inside their heads. This is Putnam's argument for semantic externalism: the claim that meaning is not in the head. It follows that the meaning of a natural kind term is fixed entirely by its extension, so the meaning of water simply is H2O on Earth and XYZ on Twin Earth. The thought experiment therefore refutes internalism about the mind quite generally; because the two Oscars have identical brains yet different meanings, even the phenomenal character of their conscious experience must be constituted by the external environment. Putnam reinforces the case through the division of linguistic labor: since ordinary speakers cannot tell H2O from XYZ, the content of their words is settled by the scientific experts in each community rather than by anything in the individual speaker's head. One limitation of the argument is that it applies only to natural kind terms such as water or gold and says nothing about functional or artifact terms such as chair, whose extension is plausibly fixed by role rather than hidden structure.
