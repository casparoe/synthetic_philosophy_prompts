I am a high-school student completing an internship with the secretariat of my regional health authority's Priorities and Resources Committee, which allocates a small number of scarce treatment places and makes one-off funding decisions. Next month the committee will hear a contested case, and I have been asked to draft the memo stating how it should decide if it wants to remain consistent with its own record.

The committee has no single written rule, so I have been keeping summaries of the decisions that seem relevant. Throughout, health benefit is measured in quality-adjusted life years (QALYs).

1. Last winter, the committee awarded the final intensive-care bed to a 29-year-old expected to gain 31 QALYs over a 72-year-old expected to gain 4 QALYs; both were expected to die without the bed. Costs were not compared, since the bed was already staffed and equipped.

2. With the last course of a scarce antiviral, the committee chose a 58-year-old expected to gain 13 QALYs over another 58-year-old expected to gain 4 QALYs; both would have died without the drug.

3. The committee gave the single opening in a stroke-rehabilitation program to a 35-year-old expected to gain 11 QALYs over a 74-year-old expected to gain 2 QALYs; neither patient was at risk of dying. The minutes note that improving quality of life, not only preventing death, can justify granting a scarce place.

4. In its budget work, the committee approved a lead-abatement grant of $2.8 million expected to avert 0.7 statistical deaths over the program's lifetime, which it calculated as roughly $4 million per statistical death averted. The same grant was expected to gain only 3.2 QALYs, roughly $875,000 per QALY. The committee approved it anyway on the stated ground that death-prevention projects are measured against a standing benchmark of $5 million per statistical life—the figure the committee calls the value of a statistical life, meaning the amount the community is willing to spend to prevent one death among unnamed people—and that a project costing less than that per death averted may be funded even when its cost per QALY is high.

The new case concerns a single open place in a specialized treatment program. Assigning the place commits the budget to paying that patient's treatment costs, so both clinical benefit and cost will be weighed. Patient M is 64; without treatment she will die within a year, and with treatment she is expected to gain 3 QALYs at a cost of $1.2 million, or $400,000 per QALY. Patient N is 31; without treatment she will survive but with a permanent impairment, and with treatment she is expected to gain 9 QALYs at a cost of $900,000, or $100,000 per QALY. Only one place can be assigned.

What should the memo recommend? Identify the candidate principles that fit the committee's four prior decisions; state which patient each principle selects in the new case and which prior decision is the closest analog under each principle. If the record genuinely underdetermines the new case, say which principles survive, give a best recommendation with your reasons, and describe one future case that would settle which principle the committee is actually following.
