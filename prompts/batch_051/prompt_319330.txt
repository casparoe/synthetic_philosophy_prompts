Consider the standard two-path criterion for declaring death: a person is dead when either circulatory and respiratory functions have ceased irreversibly, or every function of the entire brain, brainstem included, has ceased irreversibly. Now take a case that sits uneasily on the second path. A patient is in an unresponsive coma, has lost all brainstem reflexes and all respiratory drive, and therefore meets every standard clinical test for brain death. But the patient's hypothalamus is not entirely silent: antidiuretic hormone is still secreted in amounts sufficient to prevent diabetes insipidus, body temperature is regulated without external support, and the patient continues to menstruate. Does this patient satisfy the brain-death criterion, or does the requirement that every function of the entire brain has ceased fail against these residual neuroendocrine activities?
