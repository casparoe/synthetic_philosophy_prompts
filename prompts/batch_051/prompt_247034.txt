I retired from a physics department a few years ago and only recently started reading philosophy of science properly. I just read Friedman's essay on positive economics, and the thing people call the F-twist — the idea that it doesn't matter whether a model's assumptions are realistic, only whether its predictions pan out — has me thinking about my old colleague Hal. Hal was the person everyone brought models to, and he had strong, consistent-seeming verdicts about which unrealistic assumptions were fine and which ones poisoned the whole thing. I've been trying to reverse-engineer his rule, and I'd like you to predict a verdict for me.

Here's what I remember of Hal's track record. He thought the ideal gas — point particles, no interactions, perfectly elastic collisions — was a great model, not merely acceptable. He was fine with the frictionless plane, as long as you kept it to setups where friction really is small. He accepted the small-angle pendulum for small swings, but said using it for large amplitudes is just a mistake. He thought the classical harmonic crystal was fine for heat capacity at high temperature, but he'd get annoyed if someone used it to ask about thermal expansion, because the harmonic assumption rules out the very thing you're asking about from the start. And he dismissed the caloric fluid outright — heat as a conserved fluid — because it gets friction wrong, among other things.

The case I want to predict him on is the Bohr model of the hydrogen atom: electrons in fixed circular orbits, no radiation, and yet it nails the Balmer series. If I asked Hal whether the Bohr model is acceptable in the way he thought the ideal gas was — as a real model of the system, not just a mnemonic for the spectrum — what would he say? I'm less interested in the one-word answer than in the rule he seems to be running. Friedman's predictive rule is one candidate; another is that a false assumption is fine only when the model is a limiting case of a better theory or the assumption is approximately true in the target regime. Those two come apart on the Bohr model, I think, but maybe I'm missing other rules that fit his earlier calls just as well. So: which candidate rules fit the track record, what does each predict about the Bohr model, which is your best guess and why, and which further case would best seperate the remaining candidates?
