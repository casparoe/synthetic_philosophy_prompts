My professional life was spent in the natural sciences, much of it on animal acoustic communication, and I confess that for years I quietly took language to be, at bottom, a matter of sound. Since retiring I have turned to the philosophy of language, and a friend who works with British Sign Language has persuaded me that any serious account of language must treat signed languages as languages in the fullest sense. That conversation has made me notice how many familiar characterisations of language still take speech as their starting point, and I am trying to sort out which candidate criteria for languagehood fare best once that habit is set aside.

I have in mind the following candidate criteria, and I would like you to rank them, perhaps in tiers, from most defensible to least defensible: dependence on the vocal-auditory channel; arbitrariness of the connection between symbols and their meanings; duality of patterning, by which meaningless elements combine into meaningful units and those units into larger structures; displacement, the ability to communicate about things remote in space and time; productivity, the capacity to produce and understand an open-ended range of novel messages; and cultural transmission through learning within a community. In ranking them, please use as the relevant test their ability to include British Sign Language, American Sign Language, and similar systems as full languages while excluding gesture, mime, traffic signals, animal alarm calls, and the home sign systems created by isolated deaf children without a conventional linguistic model. I am especially interested in the places where signed languages put pressure on these criteria, for instance the iconicity visible in much of the signed lexicon and the visual-manual rather than vocal-auditory medium of sign.

This is an exercise in conceptual clarification rather than a request for a literature review, so I do not need citations. Please write your answer in continuous prose, as though you were writing a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, and express the ranking itself in ordinary sentences.
