I'm working through a chapter on Ibn Rushd's Decisive Treatise, specifically on whether he actually secures the conclusion that falsafa is *obligatory* for anyone, rather than just permitted. There's this passage in the Hourani translation that I keep coming back to, and I think there might be a subtle slide in it, but I can't pin it down. He says:

If the activity of 'philosophy' is nothing more than study of existing beings and reflection on them as indications of the Artisan, i.e. inasmuch as they are products of art (for beings only indicate the Artisan through our knowledge of the art in them, and the more perfect this knowledge is, the more perfect the knowledge of the Artisan becomes), and if the Law has encouraged and urged reflection on beings, then it is clear that what this name signifies is either obligatory or recommended by the Law.

Here's how I've reconstructed it, and I'd like to know whether it goes through and, if not, where exactly it snaps.

1. Falsafa is nothing other than the study of existing beings and reflection on them as indications of their Artisan.
2. The Law commands (or at least urges) reflection on existing beings.
3. If X is nothing other than Y, and the Law commands Y, then the Law commands X.
4. So the Law commands (or at least recommends) falsafa.

Now, the conclusion in (4) is obviously weaker than his later claim that demonstrative study is obligatory for the class of people capable of it. So first question: is the inference from (1) and (2) to (4) valid as stated, or does premise 1 already smuggle in something that the Qur'anic "reflection" doesn't amount to? I'm especially suspicious of a possible equivocation in the phrase "study of existing beings": it could mean the kind of demonstrative inquiry into essences and causes that the Peripatetics practiced, or it could just mean noticing design in the natural world (the camels, the sky, etc.). If it's the latter, then (1) is false, since falsafa is clearly more than that. If it's the former, then premise 1 is true by definition but premise 2 becomes questionable, since the Law's urging to "reflect" doesn't obviously include syllogistic demonstration. So my worry is that the argument only works by sliding between a stipulative and a substantive reading of "philosophy."

Can this be repaired by reading premise 1 as a stipulative definition of what he *means* by 'philosophy' for the purposes of the treatise, in which case the argument establishes only that *something called 'philosophy'* is commanded, but not that the Greek demonstrative sciences in particular are? Or is there another step that fails, like a hidden transfer principle between a commanded end and a specified means? Be specific about the step and the reading of the key term.

Also, am I right that he himself seems to notice the gap, given the "either obligatory or recommended" hedge, and that his next move—that the study must be by demonstration because it's most perfect—is what's supposed to turn the recommendation into an obligation? If so, does *that* move depend on an extra premise about the Law always commanding the most perfect means, and is that premise defensible?

Tell me exactly which step, if any, fails, and what the strongest version of the argument would be.
