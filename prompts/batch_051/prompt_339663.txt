I sit on my university's research ethics committee. We've got a social-psychology proposal where participants, after giving informed consent, are asked to carry out a small act of symbolic desecration: for example, sign a statement denouncing a moral or religious commitment they've told us they hold deeply, or tear up a photograph of something they describe as sacred to them. The statement gets destroyed in front of them afterwards, and there's a full debriefing. The researchers say the only real issue is transient distress, which they claim is mild and measurable. Half the committee think that's naive: they say consent doesn't make it acceptable to have someone violate something sacred, and that the committee shouldn't license acts that are wrong in themselves. The other half say that once you've got informed consent, low distress and a proper debriefing, refusing it on 'sanctity' grounds means smuggling a substantive moral view into what's meant to be a harm-and-autonomy review. Please draft the short decision memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting: recommend approve, reject or approve with conditions, give reasons the researchers could actually respond to, and treat the strongest point on the losing side fairly.
