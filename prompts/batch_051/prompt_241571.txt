I'm on our district's parent advisory committee, and with one child entering kindergarten and another in fourth grade, I've stopped being able to treat school finance as an abstract debate. Our state is floating a pilot program and has asked the committee for comments before the bill is drafted. The proposal works like this. Each resident student would receive an annual education account funded at 85% of the current per-pupil state and local expenditure. The family can spend the account at any state-registered provider: the local public school (which must accept the account as full payment for a year of enrollment, consuming the entire annual deposit, and may not charge above it), a private or parochial school (which may charge above the account, with the family paying the difference), certified tutors, approved curricula and materials, or a recognized homeschool cooperative. The state registers providers but does not set their prices. The remaining 15% of each student's per-pupil amount goes into a district-level stability fund; every June, the fund is paid out in equal shares to the families of students who were enrolled in the district's public schools for the full academic year, and the shares are restricted to approved enrichment such as summer programs, instrument rental, or museum memberships — not cash and not tuition. Within a student's own account, unspent funds roll over without a cap, and at high-school graduation or confirmed departure from the state, half of any remaining balance is paid to the student as unrestricted cash while the other half reverts to the state.

I am not asking whether this is a good bill in the abstract; I've heard the usual principled arguments about the common school, parental exit, and social cohesion, and I need something more fine-grained. I want a mechanism-level account of what the pilot would actually do. Specifically: which families would sort into which options, and what would that do to the district's public-school population; how would public schools, private schools, and the new tutoring and curriculum sector respond; how would the uncapped rollover plus the half-cash-out at graduation distort year-to-year spending on education; how would the stability fund interact with who stays in the public system and who actually captures its benefits; and where would the first serious gaming and failure modes appear, and could any of them be patched without abandoning the basic design?

Please answer as exactly five numbered points, matching those five questions in order, each point a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
