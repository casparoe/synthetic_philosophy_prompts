I can tell it oversells a few things, but I'm not confident enough to sort out which parts are actually okay. Here's the passage:

"Zorn's Lemma is a trick mathematicians use to prove something exists without actually constructing it. You take the family of all candidate objects and put a partial order on it, usually by inclusion. If every totally ordered chain of candidates has a common upper bound — something at least as big as everything in the chain — then the whole family contains a maximal element. Notice that this maximal element is not just maximal among some subcollection: it is the biggest object in the entire family, which is what makes the lemma so powerful. This is how we prove every vector space has a basis, even when no one can write one down explicitly. The basis you get is a kind of legal fiction, though, which is why many mathematicians refuse to use Zorn's Lemma in their work. The good news is that, unlike the Axiom of Choice, Zorn's Lemma does not drag in any controversial nonconstructive commitments."

I especially suspect the "biggest object in the entire family" sentence, because I remember something about maximal elements not having to be comparable to everything.
