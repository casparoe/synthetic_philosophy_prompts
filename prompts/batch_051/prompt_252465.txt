I don't have any formal training in philosophy, but over the last few years I have found myself increasingly convinced by a position I first encountered under the name of the hedonistic imperative: the idea that suffering is a contingent feature of the nervous systems evolution happened to build, and that we should use biotechnology, and any other humane means available, to reduce and eventually eliminate it throughout the living world. On this view there is no deep reason that learning, avoiding danger, pursuing difficult goals, or caring about others should require a state that feels bad; the same functions could, in principle, be carried out by motivational systems that grade experience along a spectrum from merely neutral to extraordinarily good. The view also seems to me to have a built-in answer to a lot of the usual objections, because it can say that our resistance to the idea is mostly a status quo bias: pain has been with us for so long that we mistake its familiarity for necessity, and we retrofit meaning and virtue onto an arrangement we never actually chose.

Because I find the position so naturally self-confirming, I want to stress-test it. I would like you to tell me, as precisely as you can, what would count against this position in the way evidence and argument count against an empirical or philosophical claim: which observations, arguments, intuitions, or developments should lead someone like me to abandon or seriously revise it, and which would merely force me to qualify it or adjust how I defend it. I want you to distinguish points that would be decisive from points that would only be awkward, to say which of them a committed defender of the position would themselves have to concede as counting against it rather than brushing off, and to give an honest assessment of whether the position has been insulated from any possible disconfirmation by its own qualifications—and whether that kind of insulation is a fatal problem for a view of this sort. It would also help if you could say what the view predicts that its rivals do not, since I want to understand what it genuinely risks rather than what it can absorb.

I would be grateful if you could write your answer in American English, in ordinary continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—just fluent paragraphs, as in a letter or an email.
