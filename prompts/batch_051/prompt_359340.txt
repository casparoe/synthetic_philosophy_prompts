I am a student in a course on philosophical methodology, and I keep coming back to a view I will call case-first particularism: intuitive verdicts about concrete cases are the fundamental evidence in philosophy, and general principles such as transitivity (if A stands in R to B and B stands in R to C, then A stands in R to C) or simplicity (other things being equal, prefer the theory that posits less) earn their keep only by systematizing those case judgments. When a principle collides with a confident case judgment, as when a long chain of pairwise comparisons pushes toward a conclusion that intuition firmly rejects, the principle, not the case judgment, is what gets revised. The rival picture, which I have not yet ruled out, treats intuitions about general principles as independent evidence that may legitimately override case judgments.

I would like you to stress-test the case-first view rather than survey the debate. What would have to be true for it to be false, or at least badly compromised? Please distinguish evidence that would be decisive from evidence that would merely be awkward, and say what a defender of the view would themselves have to concede counts against it, not only what an external critic would claim. I am especially interested in whether the position can be insulated, for instance by treating every recalcitrant result as showing that someone has misdescribed a case or failed to have the appropriate intuition. Is the view capable of losing, and if it turns out to be well insulated, is that a strength or a defect?

To make the question concrete, use transitivity and simplicity as the two pressure points. For transitivity: could there be formal or empirical results showing that a sufficiently rich set of case-level judgments is jointly unsatisfiable, so that a transitivity-like constraint must be imposed from above rather than read off from the cases below? For simplicity: does the view leave room for simplicity to function as a real constraint, or does it reduce simplicity to a taste for whatever systematization of the cases feels least profligate? I want an honest map of exactly where the view is exposed, and an assessment of how seriously a defender should take each line of attack.
