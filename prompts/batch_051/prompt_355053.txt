I've been going back and forth on something from a philosophy of medicine class and I'm hoping you can help me sort it out. The class framed things as a fight between naturalism and normativism about health and disease. On the naturalist view, diseases are basically value-free biological breakdowns: a part or process isn't doing what it should do, where the "should" is read statistically, relative to how that part functions in most members of the species at the relevant life stage. On the normativist view, that's smuggling in a value judgment through the back door, because calling something a malfunction already assumes some state of the organism is the good or desirable one, and the whole category of disease tracks what we happen to disapprove of or want fixed.

What bugs me is cases where the two views pull apart. For instance, hypertension is extremely common in older adults, but we treat it as a disease and chase it as a risk factor before anything has actually gone wrong. On the flip side, some statistically rare variations (say, certain harmless anatomical quirks) never get classified as diseases at all. I also wonder about conditions like chronic pain or ADHD, where the diagnosis leans heavily on behavior and self-report rather than a clearly broken biological mechanism.

So here's the actual prompt: compare naturalism and normativism about disease, but then take a definite stand. Which of the two gives the better account of health and disease, and why? Dont hedge by saying they each capture something—pick one and defend it against the strongest objection you think the other side would raise.
