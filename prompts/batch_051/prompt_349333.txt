The Uniform Determination of Death Act (1981), Section 1, states:

"An individual who has sustained either (1) irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions, or (2) irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brain stem, is dead."

I want a working definition of death that I can use to decide cases that seem to put pressure on this statutory formula. Propose a definition, run it through the following cases, and say where it gives the wrong result or no result. You may revise the definition as you go, and you may tell me that some of my provisional classifications are wrong, but if you do, defend that move.

Cases:

1. A patient on a ventilator whose entire brain and brainstem have irreversibly stopped functioning, maintained because the family has authorized organ donation.

2. A patient in a persistent vegetative state: no awareness, but the brainstem is intact and the patient breathes spontaneously.

3. A patient with locked-in syndrome: fully aware, but almost completely paralyzed, with respiration support but an intact brain.

4. A patient on veno-arterial ECMO after cardiac arrest: no cardiac output, no circulation, and no respiratory effort, but the patient is awake and responsive because the ECMO circuit is perfusing the brain.

5. A patient in deep hypothermic cardiac arrest: cold enough that circulation and respiration have stopped, but potentially resuscitable with rewarming.

6. A person declared dead by circulatory criteria five minutes after asystole under a controlled donation-after-circulatory-death protocol, with no apnea test and no brain death examination performed.

I provisionally think case 1 is dead, case 2 is not, case 3 is obviously not, case 4 is the hardest and I am unsure, case 5 is not dead, and case 6 is dead if the no-touch period is a fact about death rather than a waiting period for organ eligibility. I want to know whether my pattern is defensible under a single criterion, and what criterion it commits me to.

Do not restate the statutory disjunction as your definition. I already know it and it does not by itself settle the hard cases. Instead, extract from it the best underlying concept of death — or argue that no single concept does the work — and then apply your definition to all six cases. End with a single line of the form \answer{...} containing your proposed criterion as a short phrase.
