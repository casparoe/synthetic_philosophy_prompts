The question was: state the independence axiom of expected utility theory in your own words. Then explain how the Allais paradox is supposed to bear on it: give one standard pair of choices, say what the usual pattern of responses is, and show whether that pattern violates the axiom. Finally, give one argument that the usual Allais preferences are rationally permissible despite violating the axiom, and one argument that they are not.

Student's answer: The independence axiom says that if you prefer one lottery to another, then mixing both lotteries with a third lottery in the same proportion shouldn't change your preference, because the common component cancels out. For example, if you prefer a sure $100 to a 50/50 shot at $200, you should still prefer it when both options carry an extra independent 50% chance of $10. In the Allais paradox, people are asked to choose between A, a sure $1 million, and B, which gives an 89% chance of $1 million, a 10% chance of $5 million, and a 1% chance of nothing. Most people take A. They are then asked to choose between C, an 11% chance of $1 million, and D, a 10% chance of $5 million, with nothing otherwise in both cases. Most people take D. If you rewrite B as an 89% chance of $1 million plus an 11% chance of a lottery that pays $5 million with probability 10/11 and nothing with probability 1/11, then A and B share the same 89% chance of $1 million, and C and D share the same 89% chance of nothing. Independence says the A/B choice and the C/D choice should line up, because both really come down to the same 11% component: $1 million for sure versus the 10/11 gamble. The usual pattern doesn't line up, so it violates the axiom. That shows expected utility theory is descriptively inaccurate, and since a principle that people systematically reject can't be rationally required, the axiom should also be given up normatively. Savage tried to defend the axiom as a principle of consistency, saying that someone who violates it could be made into a money pump, but Allais's preferences aren't actually inconsistent because the two choices involve different background risks. The best argument for allowing the Allais pattern is that certainty has a special value: in the first choice the sure million matters precisely because it is certain, and the independence axiom's cancellation of the common 89% branch just assumes certainty shouldn't matter, which begs the question against the Allais preferences.
