A friend of mine has been waving off what he calls "etiquette-level respect" with a single analogy. He says the difference between genuinely respecting someone and just performing the gestures is a difference in attention, not behavior. On his account, to disrespect a person is to fail to see them: the spouse read-while-replying "mhm," the boss who sees a role instead of a person, the neighborhood whose deaths get shrugged at all come down to being made invisible. From the analogy he concludes that recognition is an attitude first—a steady, unflnching looking at another person as a person—and that respectful actions are just its outward symptoms. He uses this to wave off codes of conduct and civility training: don't teach people the right moves, he says, teach them to see.

I know the invisibility line isn't just his; it shows up all over talk about recognition. But I can't find the exact point where it wobbles. Take the seeing metaphor apart for me. What maps onto what—the seer, the thing seen, the light, looking, blindness? Where does the mapping snap, and which breakdowns actually threaten his conclusion rather than just being loose thread? One thing already bothers me: literal seeing is largely involuntary, while he wants not-seeing to be a moral failing, and I can't tell whether that's a deep crack in the metaphor or just a part he doesn't mean to press. Does his claim survive—that what we owe each other is an attitude before it's a set of actions? And if you think a better metaphor would carry the truth of his view further, tell me what it is. Also, say how confident you are in the main points and single out the thing you're least sure about.
