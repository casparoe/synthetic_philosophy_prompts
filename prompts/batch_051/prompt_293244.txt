I've been turning over a tension in philosophical logic, and I'd like you to help me negotiate between the two sides rather than just tell me which one wins. I'm strongly attracted to a verificationist or broadly anti-realist principle of knowability: if a proposition is true, then it is possible for someone to know it — formally, for all p, if p, then possibly Kp. The principle appeals to me because it ties truth to what inquiry could in principle reach, and it rules out facts that are somehow true from nowhere yet inaccessible to any possible knower. I also have a firm, ordinary intuition that there are unknown truths: right now there is some definite number of sheets of paper on a particular desk in a closed office, and it is entirely possible that no one knows that number; more generally, I routinely accept claims of the form "p, and no one knows that p." But Fitch's derivation seems to show that these two commitments cannot coexist. Let q be "p and no one knows that p." Since q is true, the knowability principle says q could be known. But knowing q would require knowing p and knowing that no one knows p; knowledge distributes over conjunction and is factive, so knowing the second conjunct entails that no one knows p, while knowing the first conjunct entails that someone does — a contradiction. So q is unknowable, a true proposition that cannot be known, directly contradicting the principle. Worse, the principle then appears to force the conclusion that there are no unknown truths at all.

What I want is a map of the possible settlements and what would justify each. Should I debunk or restrict the intuition — for example, by holding that sentences like "no one knows p" are not the sort of humble truth the principle was meant to cover, or that the allegedly unknown truth was being specified in a self-undermining way? Should I amend the principle — perhaps by restricting it to a privileged class of basic or observational propositions, or by reformulating "could be known" so that the truth would have been knowable in some suitably related situation even if it is not known in the actual one? Should I instead give up one of the background assumptions used in the derivation, such as classical logic, distribution of knowledge over conjunction, or factivity? Or is there a reading of "possible for someone to know" — in principle, at the ideal limit of inquiry, from no particular point of view — on which the principle and the existence of unknown truths both survive? Please answer as a professional philosopher of logic and epistemology. Take your time, begin by stating the strongest version of each commitment, then work through which move is most defensible and be explicit about the residual costs: what do I give up, respectively, if the intuition goes, if the principle goes, or if I keep both only by weakening something else?
