I'm a retired chemist who has been teaching an introductory ethics course down at the local college, and I'm working through a batch of exams from the metaethics unit. I cant quite decide how to grade one of them, so I'd appreciate a second opinion. The question I set was:

Explain the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism. Use an example to show the difficulty, and then sketch at least one way an expressivist might try to respond to it.

One student wrote:

Expressivism is the metaethical view that moral sentences like "Murder is wrong" don't describe the world or report facts. Instead, they express the speaker's non-cognitive attitudes, such as disapproval of murder. So on this view, "Murder is wrong" means roughly the same as "I disapprove of murder." The Frege–Geach problem concerns what happens when such sentences are embedded in larger sentences. In a conditional like "If murder is wrong, then getting your little brother to murder is wrong," the speaker is not actually disapproving of murder; the clause is only being considered hypothetically. But modus ponens requires the embedded "murder is wrong" to mean the same thing it means when it stands alone. If the meaning of a moral sentence is just the attitude it expresses, then the antecedent of the conditional and the free-standing premise have different meanings, and the inference

(P1) If murder is wrong, then getting your little brother to murder is wrong
(P2) Murder is wrong
(C) Therefore, getting your little brother to murder is wrong

becomes an equivocation between an expressed attitude and a merely mentioned one. This is a serious problem because valid moral reasoning plainly happens all the time. Expressivists have tried to fix it by developing a "logic of attitudes," in which conditionals express higher-order attitudes, such as disapproval of combining disapproval of murder with a lack of disapproval of getting your little brother to murder. But in my view these fixes all fail, since a logic of attitudes still cannot explain how moral sentences compose truth-functionally, and the only remaining option is to give up expressivism and admit that moral sentences are truth-apt after all.
