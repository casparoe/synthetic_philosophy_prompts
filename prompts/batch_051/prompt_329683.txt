I am a novelist. I am working on a book about a contemporary master craftsman and the people who employ him, and I have been reading G.W.F. Hegel's The Phenomenology of Mind in the J.B. Baillie translation to think about power, service, and recognition. I came across this passage in the discussion of lordship and bondage, paragraph 197, and it has become central to how I imagine the craftsman's inner life:

The repressed and subordinate type of consciousness, on the other hand, becomes, in the formative activity of work, an object to itself, in the sense that the form, given to the thing when shaped and moulded, is his object; he sees in the master, at the same time, self-existence as a real mode of consciousness. But the subservient consciousness as such finds these two moments fall apart — the moment of itself as an independent object, and the moment of this object as a mode of consciousness, and so its own proper reality. Since, however, the form and the self-existence are for us, or objectively in themselves, one and the same, and since in the notion of independent consciousness the inherent reality is consciousness, the phase of inherent existence (Ansichsein) or thinghood, which received its shape and form through labour, is no other substance than consciousness. In this way we have a new attitude or mode of consciousness brought about: a type of consciousness which takes on the form of infinitude, or one whose essence consists in unimpeded movement of consciousness. It is one which thinks or is free self-consciousness.

Here is the argument I have distilled from it, which I want to use as the psychological engine of the novel but need to check first:

(1) In the formative activity of work, the bondsman sees the thing he has shaped as his own object, and in that object he sees himself as an independent object.
(2) If a consciousness has itself as an independent object, then it is related to itself and is not merely dependent on another self-consciousness.
(3) To be related to itself in this way, rather than merely dependent on another self-consciousness, is what Hegel means by free self-consciousness.
(4) Therefore, through work, the bondsman becomes a free self-consciousness.
(5) But a consciousness that is free self-consciousness in this sense would no longer be the consciousness of a bondsman bound to the master's will.
(6) So the passage seems to imply that the bondsman's labor has already dissolved his bondage, which would collapse the very relation of lordship and bondage the passage is supposed to explain.

I suspect the problem is in step 2 or step 5, but I cannot locate it precisely, because each step seems to be using Hegel's own concepts. Is the argument valid as it stands? If not, exactly which step fails, and on what reading? What corrected version of that step would preserve what Hegel wants to say about work and self-consciousness without implying that the bondsman has become socially free?
