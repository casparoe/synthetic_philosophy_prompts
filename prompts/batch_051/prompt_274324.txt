i have a principle ive been using in arguments about embryo selection and want it stress-tested before i keep deploying it. call it the why-did-you-pick-me test: a reproductive choice (embryo, sperm donor, whatever) is allowed only if the parent could honestly explain it to the child who results as something done for the child's own sake, not just for the parents' taste or convenience. so screening out a disease that would mean a short life in and out of hospitals passes — you can say to the kid 'i picked the embryo i did because i did not want you to spend your life like that' — but picking for eye color or a certain height fails, because you can't say that to the child without lying or treating her like a factory option. i think this splits the difference between procreative beneficence, which sounds like it orders us to pick the best child, and the intuition that parents shouldn't treat children like products from a catalogue. but im not sure the principle does any actual work: maybe every trait preference can be re-described as 'for the child's sake' (picking a child who will be pretty or tall or high-status really is for her sake, given how shallow the world is). and the non-identity thing makes 'for your sake' weird, because the alternative was never a better-off version of you, just a different child who wouldnt exist. is this a real principle or just a verbal constraint that any choice can pass? give me the strongest case it gets wrong, and say whether it collapses into something people already defend or is genuinely distinct.
