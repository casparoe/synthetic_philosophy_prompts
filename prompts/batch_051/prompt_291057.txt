A colleague has been insisting that global workspace theory proves too much, and I cannot locate the step at which his reasoning fails. The argument runs as follows.

(1) Global workspace theory says that a mental state is conscious if and only if its content is globally broadcast in a neural workspace, making that content available to a wide range of consuming systems—memory, planning, language, and the mechanisms of report.

(2) Global availability of this kind is a functional condition. It concerns what the state does and what it makes possible within the cognitive economy, not what it feels like.

(3) Availability to memory, planning, language, and report is precisely what access consciousness is.

(4) Therefore, if global workspace theory is true, a state's being conscious is identical with its being access-conscious.

(5) But phenomenal consciousness—what it is like for the subject—is distinct from access consciousness. It is at least coherent that information is globally available to all the right consuming systems while there is nothing it is like to be the system in question; and many would also allow phenomenal states at the edge of attention that are not globally available for report.

(6) So global workspace theory, if true, would make the distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness collapse. The very gap that a theory of consciousness is supposed to explain would be ruled out by the theory's definition of the target.

(7) Hence global workspace theory does not explain consciousness so much as replace it with a functional surrogate and declare the problem solved.

I find that conclusion unacceptable, yet each step seems to be doing genuine work, and I cannot see which one to give up without losing something I care about. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it commit you to?
