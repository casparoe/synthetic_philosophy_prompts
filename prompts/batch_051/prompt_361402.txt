You are a moral philosopher who has spent time inside hospital ethics committees, and you know the philosophical debate about moral testimony — roughly, whether someone can properly acquire or act on a moral judgment by deferring to another person's say-so. Take the time to work through the procedure's distinctions before you write, and keep the statement of the procedure separate from the worked cases.

I direct the clinical ethics consultation service at a large teaching hospital. We handle about a dozen consults a month, often with decisions due within 24 to 72 hours, and the stakes include life, serious disability, and the hospital's standing with patients and staff. The final decision always belongs to the attending physician, the patient, or the patient's surrogate; my consultants only advise. A recurring problem is that clinicians, patients, and families treat our recommendations as verdicts they can adopt without understanding the reasons, and they sometimes ask us to simply tell them what to do. Committees do something similar when they adopt a professional association's position statement without reading its reasoning.

Please write a reusable decision procedure my consultants can run in each consult to determine how much moral deference to allow: under what conditions a decision-maker may properly rely on moral testimony — ours, a colleague's, a family member's, or a professional body's — and when we must withhold a simple verdict and require the decision-maker to reason the question through. The procedure should list questions in order, state what counts as a sufficient answer to each, flag where judgment cannot be eliminated, and identify the points where it should stop and hand the decision back to a person. It must be usable by consultants without formal philosophical training.

Then apply the procedure to these three cases: (a) an attending physician who will write a do-not-resuscitate order because the consult note recommends it, without reading the note's reasons; (b) a family deciding whether to continue life support, who ask the consultant to make the decision for them; and (c) a hospital policy committee that wants to adopt, without review, a position statement on expanded access to investigational drugs issued by a national medical society. Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer my service will not like, and be explicit about what the procedure cannot do, where it will misfire, and which cases it will always leave to individual judgment.
