I re-read Conrad's Heart of Darkness last month while preparing a small seminar on sovereignty, and one question has stayed with me. Some political and legal thinkers argue that a state is best understood as a corporation: a legal person created by a charter, with perpetual succession, the capacity to own and alienate its territory, and limited liability in the form of sovereign immunity. The clearest historical exhibits are the chartered trading companies, which levied taxes, waged war, and governed people while remaining commercial bodies. In the novel, the Company sits at the same boundary: it runs stations, extracts ivory, coerces the people along the river, and behaves as a small sovereign in the interior, while Kurtz oscillates between employee, governor, and warlord. Read the novel through this lens. Where does the state-as-corporation reading genuinely illuminate what Conrad shows, and where does the novel resist it — where does it suggest that sovereign power remains something other than corporate hierarchy with a different mask? Anchor the reading in the major episodes rather than in general themes, and end by saying what Conrad contributes to the debate about corporate sovereignty that the theory alone could not say.
