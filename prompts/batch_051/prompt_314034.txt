Two people in my logic reading group have been going around in circles about Lewis Carroll's Achilles and the Tortoise for weeks, and I want you to write the document their adversarial collaboration would end with if they sat down and tried to be fair to each other.

Quick setup so nothing has to be looked up. Achilles gets the tortoise to accept A: if it rained, the path is muddy; and B: it rained. Then he says Z follows: the path is muddy. The tortoise accepts A and B but won't accept Z until he also accepts C, namely that if A and B are true, Z is true. Once he's accepted C he still won't accept Z until he accepts D, namely that if A, B, and C are true, Z is true. And so on forever.

The two positions:

— Rule camp: modus ponens is a rule of inference, not a premise. The tortoise is confusing a license to move with a thing that could be written into the premise list. Adding C doesn't help because the move from A, B, and C to Z either uses modus ponens anyway or it's a different move. The regress only runs because the skeptic keeps insisting that a rule be stored as a premise before it can do its work. Deduction can't be justified from outside because it's part of what it is to reason at all.

— Skeptic camp: the tortoise wins. Any attempt to justify deduction either uses the very rule it's trying to justify, which is circular, or it doesn't, in which case the conclusion doesn't follow. There's no non-circular answer to the question of why we should reason this way; at bottom we just do it, and our later story is a rationalization.

Write the joint statement they could both sign. Format it as:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section. These should be the strongest claims both sides still accept, not just truisms — about the scenario, about what the regress does and doesn't show, about what modus ponens is, and about what's at stake.

2. A disagreements table. For each row: a neutral statement of the point of disagreement, then the formulation each side insists on for its own view, then whether the disagreement is genuine or verbal. I in particular suspect part of their fight is only about the word justification — say wether you think that's right.

3. A cruxes section. For each crux, a sub-question such that settling it would move at least one of them, give a concrete case, argument, or test both would agree bears on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance. I suspect one crux is whether the tortoise's stance is even a coherent position rather than a refusal to play, and another is whether rule-circularity and premise-circularity are genuinely different. Don't adjudicate or pick a winner; the point is to make the residual disagreement exact and pre-registered.
