I chair a university academic integrity board that meets weekly to decide how to respond to student misconduct (plagiarism, exam fraud, falsified data), with sanctions ranging from a warning to expulsion; board members include faculty and students with no philosophical training, each case must be handled in under an hour, and the dean reviews every outcome. We keep oscillating between treating a student as a moral agent to be blamed and treating them as someone to be managed and referred for help. Write a reusable decision procedure, grounded in P. F. Strawson's distinction between the participant attitude, from which reactive attitudes such as resentment and indignation are appropriate, and the objective attitude, in which we see a person as something to be managed, treated, or controlled, together with his distinction between excuses, which leave the participant attitude in place but block blame for a particular act, and exemptions, which place the person outside the participant attitude altogether. Translate the theory into ordered questions, specify what counts as a sufficient answer to each, say where the procedure stops and where it must hand the decision to a person because judgment cannot be eliminated, and mark what the procedure cannot decide. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a first-year who copied a paragraph and plausibly did not understand citation rules; (2) a final-year who paid someone to sit an exam and has since been hospitalized with a severe psychiatric episode; and (3) one of your choosing in which the procedure pushes the board toward an answer it will not like. End with a one-sentence summary of the procedure's core decision rule as the final line.
