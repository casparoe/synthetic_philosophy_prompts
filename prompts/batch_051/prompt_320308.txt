I am teaching a unit on the actual infinite to advanced undergraduates who have just gone through Hilbert’s hotel, Cantor’s diagonal argument, and a short strict-finitist polemic. The students keep sliding between “completed infinite,” “potential infinite,” “transfinite,” and “actual infinite” as though these all pick out the same thing, and they want a way to see what would settle the issue. I would like you to write the desiderata that a theory of the actual infinite—or a principled refusal of it—must satisfy if it is to be adequate for classical mathematical practice and a serious philosophical contender rather than a merely verbal position. For each desideratum, give the motivating phenomenon, case, or argument from the material: a one-to-one correspondence between a collection and a proper part, Hilbert’s hotel’s ability to free a room by shifting every occupant, the diagonal theorem, the contrast between a rule for generating a sequence and the finished totality, the puzzle of how a finite procedure can refer to an infinite domain, and so on. Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable across all serious positions, which are negotiable preferences of particular camps, and which are smuggled-in demands that beg the question. If some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable or would force acceptance of one side over another, say so explicitly; that is the part I most want to use in class. I am less interested in a scorecard of named theories than in the criteria themselves, so keep the weight on the desiderata and their motivation. Please lead with the bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
