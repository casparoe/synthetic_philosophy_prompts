I'm a journalist working on a piece about whether history explains things in the same way physics does, and I've gone down a rabbit hole on the covering-law vs. narrative explanation fight. A philosopher I was interviewing gave me this argument and I can't tell whether it's airtight or whether there's a slide buried in it. Here it is, cleaned up:

(1) A narrative explanation of an event traces the event back through a chain of earlier actions and circumstances, showing how each stage arose from the one before.
(2) For one stage to be intelligible as arising from the stage before, the connection between them has to rest on some generalization about how people or institutions behave in situations like that.
(3) If every link in the chain rests on such a generalization, then the whole narrative is a chain of little covering-law explanations.
(4) A chain of little covering-law explanations just is a covering-law explanation of the final event, with the laws left implicit.
(5) So narrative explanation is nothing over and above covering-law explanation: anybody who explains an event historically is doing the same thing, logically, as a physicist, just with the laws unstated.

I keep poking at step (2) and step (4) and they bend instead of breaking, wich makes me worry I'm missing something. Is the argument valid?
