I serve on the council of a small confessional church. Our constitution, adopted long before the internet existed, contains this article on prayer:

"Because God is unchangeable and already knows all things, prayer is not a means of informing Him or of changing His decree. Prayer is the turning of the believer's own mind and will toward God; it is to be made by each believer in his own person, from a sincere heart, and with an understanding of what is asked."

A member of the congregation, an engineer, has built what he calls a prayer agent. The system uses a large language model to compose and post a fresh petition to our online prayer wall every hour on behalf of the whole congregation, drawing on the directory and the week's prayer requests. He does not write the prayers himself. Each evening he skims a digest of the day's petitions and occasionally vetoes one, but he concedes that he has never held most of these requests before God in his own mind at the moment they are offered. He argues that, given God's omniscience and immutability, it cannot matter whether the words issue from a human mind at the moment of utterance or from a machine he has set running with a sincere and reverent intention: the petitions are addressed to God, they are doctrinally sound, and the arrangement merely fulfils the scriptural command to be constant in prayer. Several of us on the council believe this is not prayer at all but a manufactured imitation of it.

How should the article be applied to this case? Work through what its words require, what its apparent purpose suggests, what its drafters would most likely have said if they had been asked about an autonomous prayer system, and what an ordinary reasonable reader of the article would understand it to permit. Then say which of these approaches ought to govern here, and why. Finally, tell me which argument or consideration would be most likely to change your judgment, or where your reading of the article is most strained.
