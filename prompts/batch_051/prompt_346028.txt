I've been chewing on something Peter Abelard says in his Ethics, the part where he argues that the whole sin is in consent: once a person has agreed to do a bad thing inside themselves, the sin is already complete, and the outward deed adds nothing to the guilt. If that's right, then our ordinary way of treating attempted murder as less bad than murder looks hard to defend. Picture two people with the same anger, the same plan, the same willingness; one bullet hits, the other is knocked off course by a pigeon flying past at the last second. Is there any real moral difference between the two of them as agents? I get that the law has practical reasons to punish completed crimes differently, because intentions are hard to prove and a death causes different grief than a near miss, but im asking about the guilt itself, not the legal handling. Is there a defensible justification for the asymmetry we all feel, or is it just leftover superstition about outcomes that a clear-eyed view would drop? Give me the strongest defense of the asymmetry you can find, and then tell me honestly whether you would keep it.
