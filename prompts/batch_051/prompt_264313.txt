The exam question that was set:

"Consider the following case. A powerful demon credibly threatens to punish you severely unless you admire him, though there is nothing about him that merits admiration. There is a plain sense in which you now have a reason to admire him, and a plain sense in which he is not the least bit admirable. Explain what this case shows about attempts to analyse value — for example, admirability — in terms of reasons for attitudes. Draw the distinction that such a case is supposed to force upon us, and assess whether the analysis can be saved by drawing it."

The student's answer, reproduced in full:

"Admirability is commonly analysed in buck-passing terms: x is admirable if and only if there is a reason to admire x. The demon case is a problem for this analysis because the threat supplies a reason to admire the demon even though the demon is not admirable. The standard response is to restrict the right-hand side to reasons of the right kind. Right-kind reasons bear on whether the attitude fits its object; wrong-kind reasons bear only on whether having the attitude is advantageous. This lines up with the object-given/state-given distinction: right-kind reasons are facts about the object, while wrong-kind reasons are facts about the state of admiring, such as the fact that admiring would avert the threatened punishment. Once the analysis says 'x is admirable if and only if there are object-given reasons to admire x,' the case is handled, because the demon's threat is about my mental state rather than about him, and the restriction mentions only the object rather than value, so it avoids circularity. This is all the distinction we need. It is clear that the demon really does give me a reason to admire him; it is merely a wrong-kind reason. Whether we go on to call such considerations 'reasons' at all is a purely verbal matter, since they clearly count in favour of having the attitude in the practical sense. With the restriction in place, the buck-passing analysis survives intact."
