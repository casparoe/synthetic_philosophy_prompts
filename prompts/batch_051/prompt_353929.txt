I want to know what principle, if any, is doing the work behind the way I react to five people. My verdicts feel mostly coherent, but at least two of them pull against each other, and I would rather have an honest theory than keep all five.

1. M. M was a gifted cellist with a wide circle of friends, and at thirty-one she gave up performing and most of those friendships to run a malaria-prevention charity in a country she had never visited. She is warm, funny, and undogmatic; she says she does not miss the cello because, given the expected lives saved, the choice seemed boringly obvious. I admire her more than almost anyone I know, but I would not wish her life on my daughter, and my admiration is edged with an unease I cannot name.

2. R. R is, by any impartial measure, the most morally good person I know. He has no special attachments, no hobbies, no interest in beauty, and no humor about himself. Each of his actions is the one that, by his honest calculation, best promotes aggregate welfare. He dislikes much of it and does it anyway. I find him hard to be around, and I think something has gone wrong in his life, even though he is morally better than M and better than I am.

3. L. L is not grim like R. She delights only in serving others, and nothing else appeals to her; she is repressing nothing because, she says, there is nothing else there. People call her radiant. But I cannot talk with her about music, or fear, or wanting two incompatible things. I admire her without the unease I feel about M, but I still would not call her life a fully human one.

4. J. J is a brilliant novelist and a devoted father and friend. He is decent, truthful, and often generous, but he is not maximally good: when a book is going badly, he neglects his obligations and is a little cruel to the people near him. I find J more admirable than R and more enviable than M, even though I would never say he is a better person.

5. S. S is maximally good, but every non-moral thing in his life — friendships, humor, taste — is kept on a leash he will drop the moment the numbers say to. There is nothing grim about him; he enjoys the leash. I like S, and I can find nothing to criticize in any particular choice, yet I will not call his life the ideal of a human life, and I cannot say why without it sounding like "he is too moral," which I do not believe.

What principle, if any, captures these reactions? Please do the following: state the strongest candidate principle or two, not as slogans but precisely enough to test against the cases; show how each handles all five cases, including which of my verdicts it preserves and which it would have me overturn; say whether my verdicts about R and M commit me to thinking that maximal moral goodness is not desirable, or only that a morally best life can lack something else worth having; and apply the best principle to a sixth case — a person who is moderately good, deeply excellent at something non-moral, capable of one serious cruelty, and on the whole happy — and tell me what it predicts.

Please organize your answer as Candidate Principles, then Case-by-Case Fit, then Prediction and Consequences. Keep the whole response to at most 800 words.
