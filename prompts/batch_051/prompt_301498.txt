After a long argument with a friend about whether Scandinavian trust in government or American suspicion of it is the more reasonable attitude, I wrote down the commitments I found myself defending. I would like a consistency check before I rely on them. The commitments are:

(1) Trust in government is reasonable only to the extent that it tracks how the relevant institutions actually perform — their competence, honesty, and impartiality — rather than resting on a standing cultural disposition to trust or distrust.

(2) Generalized interpersonal trust is largely an outcome of institutions rather than an independent source of them: Scandinavian countries have high social trust because their states have been comparatively impartial, honest, and inclusive, not the other way around.

(3) The characteristically American suspicion of concentrated power is, in the contemporary United States, a defensible response to a real record of institutional failures rather than merely an inherited founding-era ideology.

(4) An adversarial political culture — checks and balances, investigative journalism, organized skepticism toward officials — is one of the main mechanisms that keeps power honest and exposes misconduct where it occurs.

(5) A standing posture of suspicion toward government carries real costs: it depresses compliance, cooperation, and willingness to fund shared projects, so a society whose default is suspicion forgoes some collective goods it could otherwise secure.

(6) The question whether Scandinavian trust or American suspicion is the more reasonable attitude is not best settled as a question about national character; it should be settled case by case, by looking at how institutions in each country actually perform.

My friend argued I cannot hold all six together. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, and which only seem to be? Please rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equal, and for each say what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost me.
