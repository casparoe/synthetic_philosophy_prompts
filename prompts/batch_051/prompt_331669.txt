I've been trying to think about the 1999 Kargil War through the stability-instability paradox and Schelling-style brinkmanship, and the more I push on it the less sure I am what the theory is doing. Here's the episode as I understand it: India and Pakistan both tested nuclear weapons in May 1998, so by spring 1999 they were brand-new declared nuclear powers. Pakistan nonetheless infiltrated soldiers and militants across the Line of Control in the Kargil sector to grab high-altitude posts India had vacated for winter, which put the Srinagar–Leh highway, India's supply route up to Siachen, in artillery range. India fought back hard with infantry and airpower but conspicuously refused to cross the LoC or open another front, even though that restraint made the operation slow and bloody. It ended after the U.S. leaned hard on Nawaz Sharif in July 1999. What I want is a disciplined mapping, in both directions, between the case and the theory. Who are the actual players once nukes sit in the background, and what did their options and payoffs look like? The stability-instability paradox predicts that stable mutual deterrence at the strategic level licenses limited conventional aggression at the sub-nuclear level — does Kargil confirm it's predictions, complicate them, or is it just a badly chosen test? What work is India's refusal to cross the LoC doing in the model: rational escalation control, a costly signal, domestic politics, something else? And then be honest about where the theory goes quiet — the U.S. pressure, the terrain, and especially the way Pakistan's civilian prime minister and its army seem to have been playing different games, which sits badly with the unitary-actor assumption running through deterrence theory. Does the war show the stability-instability paradox is basically right about South Asia, or does it show the paradox only does real work if you pretend states are single players?
