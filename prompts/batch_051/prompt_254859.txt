At a forecasting meetup, two people I know got into an argument about whether Bayesian priors "wash out," and I couldn't adjudicate it on the spot. I'll recount it as best I can.

Mara said people overrate the choice of prior. The classic merging theorem says that if two agents' priors over a shared data stream are mutually absolutely continuous — neither assigns zero probability to a region of hypothesis space the other treats as live — then as they observe the same stream, their predictive distributions for the remaining observations converge in total variation, with probability one under either agent's measure. So prior differences are transient.

Jon replied that the condition does all the work and is exactly what fails in real disputes: agents with different modeling commitments often have effectively disjoint prior support, and then no theorem forces them together. Moreover, the "almost surely" in the usual Bayesian consistency result is relative to each agent's own prior, so two agents can each be nearly certain that the other's posterior will fail to find the truth.

Mara answered that the second point conflates merging with convergence to truth. The useful content of washing out is not that anyone reaches the truth, but that, for agents with shared support, the data eventually stop the disagreement from being about the priors.

Jon said "eventually" is carrying the load: the theorem gives no rate, and in misspecified or high-dimensional settings "eventually" can lie beyond any finite horizon. What a prior-choice argument actually needs is uniform finite-sample behavior, and the theorem is silent about that.

Mara replied that no convergence theorem supplies a rate without extra conditions, but the qualitative point stands: the difference between two non-dogmatic priors is not permanent.

Jon answered that the claim people actually deploy is stronger than "not permanent" — they use it to dismiss prior elicitation as pointless. By the theorem's own logic, priors wash out only in the limit, under shared support, and only for observables both parties already count as data. Each qualifier hides a substantive modeling choice, so citing the theorem as if it discharges those choices is the error.
