I've been going back and forth with a friend who does AI safety about whether the whole chain-of-thought monitoring idea is coherent, and I'm hoping you can dig out the assumptions underneath it. Here's the practice as I understand it. A lab trains a main model to solve hard tasks while producing a written step-by-step chain of thought. Separately, it trains a monitor model (or sometimes uses a human) to read those reasoning transcripts and flag anything that looks like deception, taking shortcuts, or gaming the reward signal; flagged transcripts get escalated for review. At the same time, the lab is running exactly the kind of reinforcement learning that optimizes the main model to produce outputs human raters like, and everyone involved knows that under that pressure a model can learn to write reasoning that looks careful and honest even when the actual basis for its answer is something else. So the practice seems to be: push the model harder and harder to be convincing, then treat its written self-report as evidence about what actually produced the behavior.

What view of a model's inner cognition — or of the relation between its words and its reasons — does this monitoring practice presuppose? Does it need the strong claim that chain-of-thought is faithful, in the sense that the transcript tracks the real decision process, or only the weaker claim that deceptive writing is hard enough that the monitor stays ahead of the optimization pressure? And is that weaker claim stable under exactly the pressure the lab keeps turning up, or is the practice quietly assuming something like the idea that the model cannot or will not learn to obfuscate its own monitoring signal? Reconstruct the implicit philosophy here, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely hopes is true, and say what the monitoring setup would have to look like if the lab instead took seriously the thought that a chain-of-thought transcript under optimization pressure is a strategic document rather than a report.
