I've been on a local heritage commission for a few years and we keep going around in circles about a museum exhibit. The old exhibit tells the town's founding story in a way that's warm and unifying but has a few things the documentary record contradicts or at least sharpens: the founding was messier, some of the heroes did bad things, and some of the community's cherished story is later embroidery. A group of academic historians says the exhibit should be corrected because public institutions shouldn't lie. Another group, including many descendant families, says the shared story is what holds the town together and the museum isn't a peer-reviewed journal. The weird thing is both sides agree on the facts in the archive; they disagree about what the museum is for.

I keep thinking about Renan's essay *What is a Nation?* He says, in effect, that forgetting and historical error are essential to the creation of a nation, and that the progress of historical studies can be a threat to nationality. That sounds like he's siding with the memory-is-functional crowd, but he also says a nation is a daily plebiscite, which sounds more voluntarist and presentist than just "preserve the old story." I'd like you to diagnose the kind of disagreement this is, not referee which side is right. Is the commission stuck because of a hidden factual question, because the two sides are using history for different purposes, or because the underlying disagreement is about whether identity has to be grounded in accurate recall at all? Is my reading of Renan even fair, or does he actually draw a distinction that would cut through our local standoff? And given the diagnosis, what would actually move it—or is it the kind of thing where the honest answer is that no new evidence would move it? Also, keep a clear seperation between what's settled about Renan or the debate and what's genuinely contested; don't pretend there's a consensus where there isn't.
