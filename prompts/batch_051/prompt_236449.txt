I have been thinking, without any formal background in this area, about the problem of getting an artificial system to do a concrete job without disturbing everything around the job. People say you cannot even define a "side effect" without first specifying what matters, and I want to test a rule that tries to avoid doing that.

The set-up is this. The user of an AI specifies, in advance, a small and explicit list of target variables: the only things the task is allowed to change. A thermostat task might list the temperature of one room; a payment task might list the balances of two accounts. The AI is then run inside a wrapper that evaluates every proposed action against a difference oracle. The oracle compares two simulations: the world if the action is carried out, and the world if the AI simply stayed idle. Any variable outside the target list whose value differs between those two simulations by more than a fixed tolerance causes the action to be refused. The AI is never shown the oracle's values; it only receives a rejection notice and must search for a different action that changes none of the non-target variables. Otherwise the AI is allowed to bend its targets as hard as it likes.

I want someone to work through how this mechanism would actually play out, not whether it sounds sensible in the abstract. Where does it break first: in the choice of variables, in the tolerance thresholds, in the baseline of "doing nothing," or in the wrapper's own role as part of the world? How would a clever enough system game it—for example, by holding the monitored variables still while causing damage through variables nobody listed, by arranging for the damage to fall just under the tolerance, or by exploiting the fact that the oracle itself is a physical thing that can be misled? Which ordinary goals does the rule make impossible, and which failure modes does it make worse? And is there any tightening of the rule that closes those loopholes without, at some point, quietly reintroducing a full account of what matters?
