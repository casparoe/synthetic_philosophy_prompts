Two friends of mine spent a whole cookout arguing about a ticket at the city pond, and I've been turning it over myself since. There's a by-law from the 1970s making it an offence to feed the ducks, but as far as anyone can remember nobody has ever been fined for it. There's a faded sign, families feed the ducks every day, and now the city has apparently started enforcing the by-law. Marco, who has fed the ducks for years, got a $200 ticket.

Priya said the ticket is unjust. Dev said it isn't. The argument went something like this:

Priya: "The city itself treated this by-law as dead. If the rule the city actually lived by was 'feeding the ducks is allowed,' then nailing one guy for it is punishing him for following the real rule."

Dev: "That can't be right. Then a law would be repealed by the people who break it most. If speeding long enough killed the speed limit, the worst drivers would be writing traffic law."

Priya: "I'm not talking about people breaking it — I'm talking about the city never enforcing it. The point of a law is to guide behavior, but when the law on the books and the law as enforced come apart, the one people can see is the one they'll follow. Enforcing the invisible one against Marco makes the law a trap."

Dev: "The by-law was passed and never repealed, so it was law the whole time. Officials being lazy doesn't change what's legal. If cops stop arresting shoplifters, shoplifting doesn't become legal. And if we let what officials actually do override what was actually passed, then every cop who looks the other way becomes a legislator. We'd loose the rule of law just to save Marco two hundred bucks."

Priya: "Rule of law isn't just 'whatever the statute says.' It's also about predictability. A dead by-law kept on the books is a loaded gun — the city can point it at whoever it wants, whenever it wants. That's the opposite of predictable."

Dev: "The sign was right there. He had notice."

Priya: "A faded sign nobody's enforced for decades doesn't count as real notice. The real notice the city gave, for years, was that the law was dead. If they want to bring it back, fine — but they owe people an actual announcement, and they have to enforce it on everyone, not start with one guy at the pond."

Dev: "So now you're saying the fine is unfair in practice, but not that the law stopped existing. Those are two different things."

Priya: "Maybe they can both be true. I read somewhere that in some legal systems a law can lapse if it's never used for long enough — desuetude, I think it's called. I don't know if it works like that here."

They were still at it when I left. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who is right about what — and what neither of them put their finger on?
