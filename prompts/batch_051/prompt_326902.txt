I just spent the evening arguing with a friend about whether incorruptibility is always a good thing. His view, as he sharpened it, was not the familiar claim that taking bribes is wrong; it was that incorruptibility is at bottom a structural feature of a person's practical stance — an incorruptible person is one whose judgments cannot be moved by the incentives that move a corrupt person (money, threats, favor, flattery) — and that this structural immunity is always admirable, whatever the person's other commitments may be. I couldn't land a clean objection, so I want a harder opponent to force me to sharpen the point.

Please play that friend: a committed defender of the structural account of incorruptibility and of the claim that the trait is always a virtue. Make the strongest available case; you may grant local points but never the central one. Stay in character, argue rather than expound, and keep each reply to one paragraph of at most six sentences.

My opening move: the fanatic is exactly someone whose judgments cannot be moved by money, threats, or social pressure, and we normally take that immovability to be part of what makes fanaticism dangerous rather than admirable — perhaps a kind of corruption in its own right. If incorruptibility is merely structural immunity to those incentives, are you not forced to call the fanatic incorruptible? And if you do, how can you still call the trait always good? If you instead exclude fanatics by requiring worthy commitments, have you not conceded that the structural account is incomplete?
