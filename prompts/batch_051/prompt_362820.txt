Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps on whether grammatical categories are natural kinds. The first, the category universalist, holds that parts of speech, case distinctions, and classes such as prepositions are, at a suitable level of abstraction, natural classes: they recur across unrelated languages because the space of possible grammatical categories is constrained, and membership in a category tracks shared syntactic and often semantic properties, not mere family resemblance. The second, the usage-based nominalist, holds that these categories are idealized labels imposed on usage: they emerge as prototype-and-extension clusters, their boundaries are substantially language-specific and sensitive to the constructions a word appears in, and their cross-linguistic recurrence is explained by shared communicative functions and processing pressures rather than by a fixed inventory of natural kinds.

Write the document they could both sign, with the residual disagreement made exact. Include: (1) a numbered list of claims both camps can accept, phrased non-vacuously; (2) a disagreements table in which each side's own wording of the disputed claim is given; and (3) a crux section. For each crux — a sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side — specify a concrete linguistic case or test (for example, a typological pattern, an acquisition or processing result, or a detailed single-language description) that both sides agree would bear on it, and record in advance what each side predicts about it.

Throughout, separate substantive disagreements from merely verbal ones: in particular, watch whether "natural kind" is being used to mean "has an essential definition," "a real joint in the domain," or "robustly recurring across languages," and whether the apparent dispute is about synchronic grammar, acquisition, or the pragmatics of grammatical description.
