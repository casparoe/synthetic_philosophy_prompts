My study group has an argument that seems to show that no rescue is ever morally required. It runs:

(1) In a lifeboat scarcity, each occupant may permissibly refuse to give up a place to a stranger who will otherwise drown.  
(2) The principle that justifies this refusal cannot be that the rescuer would die; it must be the more general principle that a person may always prefer their own survival to a stranger’s.  
(3) If a person may always prefer their own survival to a stranger’s, then they may refuse to save a stranger whenever doing so would lower their own chance of survival, even by a small amount.  
(4) Nearly every rescue imposes some nonzero risk or cost, so the principle in (3) covers nearly every rescue.  
(5) But what is permissible to refuse is not required to perform.  
(6) Therefore, nearly no rescue is ever morally required.

I do not accept the conclusion, but each step seems forced by the lifeboat case. Tell me which step to give up, why it looked fine, and what the main positions in the lifeboat ethics debate would each have to pay to reject it.
