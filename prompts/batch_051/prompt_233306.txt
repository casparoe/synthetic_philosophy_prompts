I keep noticing an asymmetry in how people think about training that I can't quite decide is justified. My friend has trained in karate for years, and a large share of his practice is kata: fixed, solo sequences of strikes, blocks, and stances that no real opponent would ever let him perform. He has no doubt that this transfers to actual fighting, and he would find it odd to be told that he should only practice full-contact sparring because only that resembles the target skill. Yet when I described how I had been training my own reasoning—weekly calibration quizzes on trivia, re-deriving modus tollens and Bayes' rule on toy cases, and reading arguments only to write out the exact inference before I respond—he dismissed it as artificial. Real arguments, he said, are messy, emotionally loaded, and full of unstated premises; tidy drills would never carry over. I could not tell whether he was being inconsistent or whether he had identified a genuine difference between bodies and reasoning.

That is the asymmetry I want you to examine: we readily accept that martial artists drill forms that look nothing like real combat and expect the skill to transfer, but many of the same people doubt that formal or artificial exercises can improve real-world reasoning. Is there anything that actually justifies this asymmetry? For example, is there a principled feature of physical skill acquisition—motor patterns, muscle memory, the closed environment of the drill—that makes kata transfer while epistemic drills do not, or is the reasoning case just newer and less visible? Are there nearby cases that break the asymmetry in either direction—chess puzzles for chess, simulated emergencies for pilots, memorized proofs for mathematicians—and do they force us to revise the ordinary judgment? I am not asking whether my exact self-designed exercises work; I am asking whether the default skepticism toward reasoning drills, in contrast to the default trust in physical drills, is defensible once we hold the cases parallel.

Please give me a verdict I can act on, not just a survey of considerations. In particular, say which argument or consideration would most plausibly change your mind, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
