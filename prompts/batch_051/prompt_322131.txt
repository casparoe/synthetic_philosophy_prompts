Two friends of mine got into it a few nights ago over whether you can forecast how much longer the United States stays the dominant world power by looking at how long past empires lasted, and I couldnt referee it, so I'm hoping you can. I kept wanting to step in, but each of them kept making points I couldn't immediately knock down.

Here's roughly how it went, with their moves numbered.

(1) Dan said the right way to think about it is the outside view: instead of reading current events and telling a story about why this particular empire is different, you collect a bunch of past empires or great powers, see how long each one lasted, and then see where the US sits in that distribution. He said people are way too confident in their narratives and ignore the base rates.

(2) Maria said that sounds objective but it isn't, because it all depends on which past empires you count. Do you include Rome? The Ottomans? The Soviet Union? Does the British Empire count as finished, or did it just change form? The number you get out depends on the reference class you pick, so she said it's not a forecast, it's a choice of framing.

(3) Dan said choosing a reference class is always a judgment, but that isn't a knockdown objection, because the inside view is a judgment too, just a more flattering one. At least the outside view anchors you. If almost every plausible class of historical empires tops out within a few hundred years, and the US has already been the leading power since the end of World War Two, then the claim that it'll last another 500 years has to be a low-base-rate bet, whatever the nice story.

(4) Maria said that ignores the fact that the US has already survived a civil war, a depression, and a nuclear standoff. Once something has already lasted this long, the unconditional base rate is the wrong number. She said for some things, lasting longer is evidence it will keep lasting, not that it's due to fall apart.

(5) Dan said she was right, and that's exactly why you don't use the raw average; you condition on having already made it to the current age. He said when he did that with a broad class of historical empires, the median remaining time was still alot shorter than what people's stories imply. So her correction makes the outside view better, not the inside view.

(6) Maria said that just pushes the problem back. Once you condition on age, the relevant reference class shrinks to something like "empires that survived to 80 years," and if you then try to match the US on any of the things that actually matter — democracy, nuclear weapons, a service economy instead of conquered tribute — there are almost no precedents left. The outside view works for car crashes, where there are millions of similar cases, but for a one-off global order it's pretending to have a sample size it doesn't have.

(7) Dan said that means more uncertainty, not that you should throw out the base rate and go back to narrative. Even a handful of cases teaches you something, and if that thin sample is all you've got, it's still better than no baseline at all.

(8) Maria said no: if every defensible choice of reference class gives a wildly different number, then the outside view isn't a method, it's a mirror. You haven't escaped storytelling; you've just moved the storytelling into the choice of class, and that choice is hidden so it feels scientific.

They asked me to settle it and I couldn't.
