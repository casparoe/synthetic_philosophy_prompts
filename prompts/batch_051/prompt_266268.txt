I run the policy analysis unit in a mid-sized city's planning department. My analysts review several proposals a week, mostly land use, transport, flood control, and housing, and our memos go to the planning commission and city council, who actually sign off. The decisions commit public money and land for decades, the work is usually done under a statutory deadline, and my analysts are planners and engineers rather than anyone trained in philosophy. Last night I had a long argument with a friend who taught philosophy of science, and he claimed our memos repeatedly dress up value choices as technical findings. He leaned on Carnap to make the point: that there is a difference between theoretical questions, which evidence can in principle settle, and practical questions, which are settled by decisions rather than discoveries; that value judgments are not factual reports but expressions of a practical stance, an attitude, or a will; and that even the choice of a framework for analysis is itself a practical choice made for pragmatic reasons rather than because the framework is true. He was careful to add that this is not an argument against planning, since Carnap himself was sympathetic to planning and to socialist politics; the failure he was pointing at is our failure to keep the two kinds of question apart.

I would like you to turn this into a reusable decision procedure my unit can actually run when a proposal arrives. The procedure should tell an analyst, in order, which questions to ask about the proposal, how to decide whether a question is a theoretical one about means and consequences or a practical one about ends and values, what counts as a good enough answer to each kind of question, where the procedure stops with a no or with a request for more evidence, and where it must hand the matter to the planning commission or city council because the question is one of values rather than facts or analysis. I also want it to mark the points where even Carnap's own distinction will not resolve things, and the points where an analyst's judgment cannot be eliminated and should be made visible to the people who sign off. Then apply the procedure to three representative cases: a congestion charge whose real dispute is over who bears the burden and who gains; a flood-control project that my staff has been calling the only technically sound option; and a proposal to remove a bus lane that the transport models support but that a neighborhood says would damage its daily life. Please make at least one of the worked applications produce an answer I will not like, most likely by forcing me to call something an irreducibly political decision that we have been presenting as a technical necessity. Do not structure the answer with headings, bullet points, numbered lists, bold text, or tables; write it as continuous prose, the way you would write an email or a letter.
