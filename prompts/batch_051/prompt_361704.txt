Bertrand Russell, in his Introduction to Mathematical Philosophy, explains the multiplicative axiom—his name for the axiom of choice—by contrasting two imaginary cases. In the first, you have an infinite collection of pairs of shoes, and you can specify a rule for choosing one from each pair by always taking, say, the left shoe. In the second, you have an infinite collection of pairs of socks, and because the two socks in a pair are exactly alike, no such specifying rule presents itself. A selection from all the sock-pairs at once seems to require an infinite number of arbitrary acts rather than a finite rule. Russell uses this picture to show that the axiom says something nontrivial: there are selection functions even when no rule is available.
