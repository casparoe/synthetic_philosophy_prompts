After an arguement with a friend about whether 'the square root of −1' picks out a definite object, I tried to write down the rule I'm actually using for when a choice in a definition is harmless, and I couldn't. Here are my gut calls, mostly about roots.

When someone says 'let i be a square root of −1,' I'm completely fine with it, even though there are two roots and nothing in the reals tells them apart. When they say 'let i be the positive square root of −1,' I reject it: there's no ordering of the complex numbers that plays nice with the arithmetic, so 'positive' is doing no work. But 'let √2 be the positive square root of 2' is fine to me, because among real numbers exactly one root is positive. In the abstract field Q[x]/(x²−2) — the field you get by adjoining a square root of 2 to Q without specifying which real root it is — I'd balk at calling either root the positive one until you choose an embedding into R; the two embeddings give opposite verdicts, and I don't see one as more canonical than the other. Once i is fixed, defining √(−4) = 2i feels fine, but defining a single-valued square root on all of C without a branch cut feels not fine, since any global choice has to break somewhere. Similarly, I'm fine with 'log(−1) = iπ' only relative to a chosen branch; bare, it feels exactly as arbitrary as 'log(−1) = −iπ.'

My tentative principle is: a choice among roots is harmless exactly when the alternatives are permuted by automorphisms of the ambient structure and every property I go on to use is invariant under those automorphisms. But the Q(√2) case already makes me nervous: the automorphism of Q(√2) that sends √2 to −√2 turns one embedding into the other, yet the two embeddings disagree about which root is positive, so invariance fails because positivity itself isn't preserved by the automorphism. Is the principle right, and if not, what is the repaired version? Test it on two cases I keep going back and forth on: defining 'the' square root of a nonzero complex number as the root with the smaller argument in (−π, π], and labeling the three roots of an irreducible cubic α₁, α₂, α₃ in the middle of a proof. And tell me which of my verdicts the principle, once repaired, forces me to drop.
