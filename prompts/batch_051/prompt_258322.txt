I'm a journalist who covers health and social science, and the line my editor and I fight over more than any other is the one where I reached for the word "caused." When I get it wrong we get correction requests, and readers make health and money decisions on these sentences, so the stakes are real. I have about twenty minutes per study and no stats training beyond reading a results table; the copy editor signs off on whatever rule I hand them.

I'd like a reusable decision procedure — a checklist I can run on a single paper — for deciding whether a finding supports causal language in my piece, grounded in whatever the philosophy of causation and the causal-inference folks actually know: regularities, counterfactuals, interventions, mechanisms, difference-making, and even the eliminativist thought that "cause" is a habit of speech that has outlived its usefulness. I don't need the metaphysics lecture, but I do need to know which of those distinctions survive translation into a rule a tired copy editor can apply. Most of my stories need "raises the chance of" more than "guarantees," so the procedure has to say whether probabilistic causation still counts as causation, and what to do when a paper doesn't report the number a step demands. Please mark the steps that are really judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the checklist on three findings from this month:

1. A cohort of 20,000 people finds daily multivitamin users have 12% lower dementia incidence over a decade, after adjusting for age, education, diet, exercise, and baseline health. Can I say multivitamins cut dementia risk? I suspect the answer is no and I'm not thrilled, so tell me exactly which steps force the no and what causal-ish sentence they still permit.
2. A state expands Medicaid and its uninsured rate falls eight points while a neighboring non-expansion state stays flat, with no other obvious policy changes. Can I say the expansion caused the drop?
3. A randomized trial of a memory supplement, with high adherance and a clear between-group effect on one memory test. Is "caused memory improvement" allowed here, or only with a caveat about who it was shown in and under what protocol?

Last, say where the procedure should hand the decision to a person because it stops being factual — like how much error I'm willing to put in front of a non-expert reader — and what the checklist genuinely cannot do for me.
