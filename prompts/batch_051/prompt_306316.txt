I'm a philosophy PhD student working in decision theory and population ethics, and next month I'm presenting a paper at a workshop on the long-run future. By fanaticism about the far future I mean the view that, because the number of future lives could be astronomically large, the expected value of our actions is dominated by their influence on the long-run trajectory rather than by any near-term effects. My thesis is that the simulation argument does not dampen this fanaticism; it relocates it, and anyone who found the fanatic's original position uncomfortable should be no more comfortable after the simulation argument does its work. The audience is mostly longtermist philosophers and existential-risk researchers who already accept that long-run effects dominate expected value, plus two epistemologists who read the simulation argument as a contemporary descendant of external-world skepticism, plus one decision theorist who thinks expected-value reasoning over an unbounded future population is ill-defined rather than merely fanatical.

Here is the argument they have seen a draft of. The standard trilemma runs: if posthuman civilizations would have both the computing power and the desire to run vast numbers of ancestor simulations, then either almost all civilizations at roughly our stage go extinct before reaching that capacity, or such civilizations almost never run ancestor simulations, or we are almost certainly living in one. The deflationary reading holds that this should temper far-future fanaticism: on the third disjunct our causal reach into the base universe's far future is negligible, and on the first disjunct there is likely no far future of ours to shape. I defend three claims. First, on the third disjunct the morally relevant far future does not disappear; it is the far future of the simulation itself, which is exactly the kind of astronomical population the fanatic cared about, unless one denies that simulated conscious lives matter. Second, if the first disjunct is what prevents the simulation conclusion from being near-certain, then the fanatic's object becomes existential-risk reduction in whichever world we actually occupy, because whether we survive to the relevant threshold is itself part of a long-run trajectory our present actions might influence. Third, the only version of the simulation argument that genuinely dampens fanaticism is one that assigns little or no moral weight to simulated minds, and that moral claim is independent of the simulation argument's probability assignments.

Prepare me for the Q&A. Give me the objections I will actually get from this audience, in roughly the order in which they will come, phrased the way these people would phrase them — the epistemologists will not sound like the longtermists, and the decision theorist will not grant the expected-value frame. For each objection, tell me how much damage it can do, what I should concede on the spot, what I should hold, and give me a short reply plus an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Include the question I should most hope nobody asks. Assume I have two to three minutes per reply. At the end, tell me how confident you are in this objection map and which single point in it you are least sure of.
