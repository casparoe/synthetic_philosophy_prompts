ok so i'm a phd student in practical ethics, TAing an intro to moral philosophy section with about 18 undergrads, 75-minute session, and i want a classroom activity for the complicity unit — participating in wrongdoing without causing it. the thing i'm trying to get them to feel, not just hear, is that the "i made no difference" defense does one kind of work as a causal claim and a very different kind of work as an excuse. and that there's a real question about whether you can be on the hook for participating in something even when your act was causally redundant.

i definately don't want this to be a lecture, and i don't want the idea named up front. the activity should be arranged so that (a) the class produces some mildly bad outcome together, (b) each individual contribution is unnecessary — subtract any one of them and the outcome is exactly the same — and (c) it's not one of those diffuse harm cases where no individual is identifiable. a committee decision where votes are aggregated could work, or a production line, or a little role-play with soliciting/encouraging/ratifying roles. they should walk out seeing that joining a plan or making themselves part of the group doing it can matter morally even when it doesn't matter causally.

and i'd like you to work in something less standard: the idea that what makes you complicit might be less the causal difference your act makes and more what you do with others — joining the plan, signaling willingness to help, making other participants feel their part is fine — even when that contribution fails even the weak test (if not for this person's act, the harm is exactly the same). i want at least one moment in the activity where a student has clearly made themselves part of the group doing the wrong thing, but their act is causally inert.

specifics: 18 students plus me, 75 minutes, a room where we can move chairs, no real money — points or candy are fine. please give me a complete runnable thing: setup and materials, roles and rules, a payoff scheme, a facilitator script for how i actually run it, and a debrief that maps what happened onto distinctions like causing vs. enabling vs. joining, overdetermination, and culpability vs. answerability.

then i want an honest "what will go wrong" section. in particular: how do i handle the student who says "so if my act doesn't matter, i might as well take the payoff," and the student who says "this is just peer pressure, not wrongdoing." and please be plain about which part of the idea a classroom activity can't reproduce — i don't think a game can carry the moral seriousness of real complicity cases, and i'd rather you say that than let the debrief pretend otherwise.

one more thing: wherever you take a stand on a contested point (for example whether the redundancy excuse actually fails, or whether non-causal participation can ground culpability at all rather than just answerability), tell me what would change your mind on that point or where your reasoning is most likely to fail. i don't want the activity quietly resting on a dogmatic answer.

format-wise, write it in sections in this order with approximate lengths: (1) Setup and materials, (2) Roles and rules, (3) Payoffs — each ~150–250 words; (4) Facilitation script, (5) Debrief, (6) What will go wrong — each ~300–400 words; (7) What the activity can't teach — ~150 words. if the section breaks feel awkward, keep the names and order but don't obsess over the word counts.
