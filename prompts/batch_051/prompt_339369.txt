I've been reading a lot lately about how we can know anything about the distant past, and I keep tripping over the same gap. The textbook contrast is Herodotus happily passing along what people told him versus Thucydides insisting on eyewitnesses and cross-examination — but that contrast is all about a single source's care. It doesn't address the thing I'm stuck on, which is what happens when two sources agree. I've been calling my worry the corroboration trap, and I want it checked.

The idea: when an ancient claim is 'confirmed' by a second source, historians and laypeople alike treat that as independent evidence and raise their credence. But for a lot of pre-modern material the two sources aren't independent at all. They share an earlier source, or a later compiler harmonized them, or they were both copied and kept precisely because they made the same neat point. So what looks like multiple attestation is often a single attestation that survived the transmission lottery and then got dressed up as several. My toy example: two chronicles both report that a king did a miracle on a Tuesday. Impressive, until you learn they both copied a lost third chronicle; then the agreement is evidence for a common ancestor, not for the miracle. And since the texts that survive are exactly the texts someone bothered to copy, agreement should be the default expectation rather than a surprising coincidence.

So my questions: is this a real distinction or am i just badly reinventing textual criticism? What's the strongest version of the obvious objection (because I have a feeling it collapses into 'never trust agreeing sources'), and where exactly does it break — are there features that make agreeing sources genuine independent evidence after all, even when you can't reconstruct the stemma?
