I have been working out a distinction I want checked. I call it centripetal versus centrifugal looping. Hacking's looping effect, as I understand it, says that classifying people changes the people classified, and because the people change, the kind itself changes. But Hacking says less about the direction such loops take, and I want to say there are at least two. A classification loops centripetally when being sorted into it pulls people toward its criteria: the label supplies a script, institutions notice people who fit it, and the classified come to resemble the criteria more over time. A classification loops centrifugally when being placed in it pushes people away from its criteria: the label is experienced as a threat or a stigma, people hide the features that would satisfy it, and their self-understanding gets built around not being that kind of person. In popular culture, borderline personality disorder seems to loop centripetally, with the label becoming an identity and the identity amplifying the relevant behaviors. A stigmatized diagnosis that people actively conceal would loop centrifugally instead; the classification persists not because people enact it, but because they evade it.

My stronger claim is that psychiatric institutions keep categories stable by quietly converting centrifugal looping into centripetal looping. When stigmatized people avoid diagnosis, the avoidance gets read not as a reason to doubt the category but as evidence of poor insight or of underdiagnosis, and the criteria get widened rather than questioned. I want to know whether the centripetal–centrifugal distinction is real or whether I have just described the same feedback loop from two angles. Is the stronger claim coherent? And where does the distinction break down: can a single classification loop in both directions at once, and if it can, does separating the two directions still earn its keep?
