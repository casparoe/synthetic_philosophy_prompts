You are a philosopher of medicine who also understands how randomized trials are actually designed and run. I sit on the governance board of a health system that is drafting a policy for when it may ethically enroll patients in randomized comparative-effectiveness trials—trials comparing two treatments that are both in standard use but about which competent clinicians genuinely disagree. We need a principled basis for the policy, not a regulatory checklist. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of the ethics of randomizing individual patients must satisfy, where the theory's purpose is to guide decisions of exactly this kind.

Begin by brainstorming candidate requirements, then interrogate each one. For each candidate, ask whether it is a genuine adequacy condition—such that a position that rejected it would no longer count as a theory of the ethics of randomization—or a preference of one defensible camp that others may reasonably reject. Do not discard contested requirements merely because they are contested; mark them as contested and keep them.

Then produce the desiderata. For each requirement, give the case, phenomenon, or distinction that motivates it, and state whether it is non-negotiable or a camp preference; where it is a camp preference, say which camp holds it and how its opponents would resist it.

The list should respect, and not collapse, at least these distinctions: the individual physician–patient dyad versus the research community or institution running the trial; a clinician's belief that one arm is superior versus the state of the evidence; and a patient's being offered a trial as one option among several versus being cared for under a protocol the institution has already adopted. It should also force a position, or at least show where a position is needed, on the locus of equipoise: whether the relevant equipoise attaches to the enrolling physician, to the expert community, or to something else.

Among the cases the list must handle cleanly: a physician believes Drug A is better for the patient in front of her because of a subgroup consideration the trial is not powered to test, while the clinical community as a whole is genuinely divided; and the health system randomizes entire clinics to one of two care protocols, so no individual physician personally offers randomization, yet patients are still allocated by chance.

Now the part I most want. I suspect the desiderata cannot all be satisfied at once. In particular, consider (i) a physician may enroll a patient only if doing so is compatible with that physician's considered judgment about what is best for this patient, and (ii) a trial's design and enrollment cannot be held hostage to each physician's private clinical impressions, which shift with every anecdote and would make rigorous trials impossible to complete. Together with the rest of your list, these two seem to pull against each other. Either show that they are jointly satisfiable, by stating precisely what each must mean so that the tension dissolves, or identify the subset of requirements that is jointly unsatisfiable, say which requirement must be relaxed and by how much, and specify what the theory must do to prevent that relaxation from allowing patients to be used as mere means to generalizable knowledge.

Finally, identify the one or two requirements that do the most work excluding candidate theories, and state what they would rule out. Do not score named theories against the list; the criteria themselves are the deliverable.
