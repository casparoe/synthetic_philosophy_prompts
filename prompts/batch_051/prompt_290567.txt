I've got a homemade theory about why hypocrites sometimes still get to blame people and sometimes don't, and I want it checked before I keep using it. I'm calling it the escrow account of standing. The idea is that your standing to criticise someone isn't a lightswitch that hypocrisy turns off; it's more like a balance. Blaming someone for a wrong you also commit draws down the account, and if the balance goes below zero, your criticism loses standing even if its factually correct. What rebuilds the balance is confession made in advance and made specific: if I say 'I've done this too, and here's exactly when I did it,' I've paid into escrow, and third parties can draw on that escrow to keep taking my criticism seriously. Vague 'nobody's perfect' doesn't count because it's a promise to pay, not a deposit. A third party can also top up your account by vouching for you, but that costs them something, which is why being asked to vouch for a hypocrite feels like being asked to co-sign a loan. The part I actually like: it predicts that the more specific and costly your confession is, the more standing you retain, and that a hypocrite who confesses after being caught doesn't restore much, because paying after the loan is called in is cheaper. I know the ordinary concept of 'standing to blame' exists and that some people think hypocrisy doesn't affect whether a criticism is true. Is my escrow thing a real distinction or just a metaphor? What's the nearest existing view, and where does the account break? Give me the three strongest objections and tell me if any of them is fatal. Also, seperate what's actually settled in this corner of philosophy from what people still genuinely fight about, and don't wave a contested view at me as if it were consensus.
