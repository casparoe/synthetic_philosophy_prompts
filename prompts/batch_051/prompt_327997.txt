I am a retired acoustician — I spent my career measuring how a vibrating string or a column of air disturbs the air around it and what exactly reaches the eardrum — and I have only recently begun reading philosophy of mind and aesthetics with any care. Please act as a professional philosopher of art and mind and work through this with me slowly. You may find it useful, before assessing the argument, to separate three things: what it is for a person to feel sad, what it is for a face or a willow to look sad, and what it is for music to sound sad. Then examine the argument step by step.

Here is an argument I keep returning to. I half-suspect it is too quick, but I cannot say exactly where it goes wrong.

(1) The sound of a piece of music is nothing but a temporally structured sequence of pressure waves and silences, fully describable by frequency, amplitude, phase, and duration.
(2) If a whole is nothing but certain physical parts in a certain arrangement, then any property it has literally is either a physical property of those parts or a structural or relational property of their arrangement; there is no extra ingredient.
(3) Felt sadness is an occurrent phenomenal state of a subject of experience; it is not a physical property of any pressure wave, and it is not a structural or relational property of a sequence of pressure waves.
(4) Therefore the sound of a piece of music cannot literally be sad in the sense in which a person is sad.
(5) But if the sentence "This music is sad" is literally true, as ordinarily used, then the music must have the very property a person has when they are sad.
(6) Therefore no music is literally sad.
(7) Yet we do say, apparently truly, that some music is sad.
(8) So either (1), (2), (3), or (5) is false, or the sentence "This music is sad" is not literally true — it reports a disposition, a projection, a convention, or a metaphor.

My question is not which step you would prefer to reject, but which step actually fails, and why. Is the argument valid as it stands? If it is invalid, identify the exact step at which it breaks, and give the correct replacement for that step. If it is valid, identify which premise should be rejected and reconstruct the strongest remaining account of how the sound itself — rather than merely the listener — can be sad. In your answer, reason as an expert philosopher rather than appealing to authority.
