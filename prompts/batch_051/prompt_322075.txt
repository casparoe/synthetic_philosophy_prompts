I’ve been trying to sort out what exactly should count as moral relativism, and a passage from Hume is making trouble for the taxonomy. I was taught that a view is a form of moral relativism if it satisfies four marks: (1) it treats moral judgments as truth-apt rather than merely expressive; (2) it makes their truth or falsity relative to some standard—individual, cultural, historical, or species-specific; (3) it denies that any one such standard is uniquely correct from a standpoint outside all standards; and (4) it allows for faultless moral disagreement when standards conflict. Now consider this passage from David Hume, An Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals, Section I:

“There has been a controversy started of late, much better worth examination, concerning the general foundation of Morals; whether they be derived from Reason, or from Sentiment; whether we attain the knowledge of them by a chain of argument and induction, or by an immediate feeling and finer internal sense; whether, like all sound judgement of truth and falsehood, they should be the same to every rational intelligent being; or whether, like the perception of beauty and deformity, they be founded entirely on the particular fabric and constitution of the human species.”

Hume seems to be posing a disjunction: if morality came from reason, it would be the same for every rational intelligent being; if it comes from sentiment—as he will argue—it rests on the particular fabric and constitution of the human species. That doesn’t look like agent- or culture-relative relativism, but it does make moral truth hostage to the contingent makeup of one kind of creature. Is that enough to make the view a borderline case of relativism, or is it better classified as something else—say, a species-wide universalism, or a form of relativism that stops at the species boundary?
