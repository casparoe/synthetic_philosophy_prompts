Please assess the following argument about pain asymbolia and the badness of pain. A colleague insists that the argument is fallacious, but she will not identify the fallacy, and I cannot tell whether the problem lies in the argument's form or in one of its premises.

(1) Pain asymbolia is a condition in which a person can identify a noxious stimulus as painful, localize it, and characterize its sensory qualities, while showing none of the ordinary aversive responses: no withdrawal, no grimacing, no avoidance, and no report that the sensation is unpleasant or that the person minds it.

(2) Such a person has the sensory-discriminative component of pain while lacking the affective-motivational component that normally makes pain bad for the one who feels it.

(3) If pain is intrinsically bad, then any state that is not bad for its subject is not pain, even if it shares sensory features with pain.

(4) Pain asymbolics are in a state that shares the sensory features of pain but is not bad for them.

(5) So, on the best clinical description, pain asymbolics have pain without badness, which contradicts premise (3); therefore pain is not intrinsically bad.

(6) The natural explanation is that the badness of pain belongs entirely to a separable affective-motivational component, not to pain's sensory-discriminative core.

I would like you to say whether the argument is deductively valid as written. If it is not valid, say exactly which step fails and whether a small revision would repair it. If it is valid but unsound, identify the weakest premise, state the strongest defensible version of that premise, and say whether the conclusion still follows on that version.
