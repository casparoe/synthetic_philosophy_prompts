I am a student in a philosophy of mind seminar on the timing of conscious experience, and I have been trying to reconstruct an argument about the color phi phenomenon. The argument now seems to prove too much, and I cannot tell whether the problem is in one of the premises or in my own resistance to the conclusion. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

Let me set out the phenomenon as I understand it. A green dot is flashed briefly at one location, and shortly afterward a red dot is flashed at a nearby location. Subjects report seeing a single dot move from the first location to the second, and they report that it changed from green to red roughly around the midpoint of its path — before the second flash actually occurred. The puzzle is that the reported sequence appears to depend on information from the second flash, so the report can only be finalized after that information is available.

Two ways of accounting for this are usually distinguished. On the Stalinesque account, the editing happens before consciousness: the subject's very experience is held back and constructed so that they consciously see a single moving, color-changing dot. On the Orwellian account, the subject first consciously experiences whatever corresponds to the actual stimulus sequence, but the memory of that experience is subsequently revised or overwritten before the report, so that the subject ends up reporting the same thing. In both cases the final report is identical.

Here is the argument I have assembled.

(1) The subject's report is produced only after the second flash, so the report alone cannot establish whether the first flash was already consciously experienced and then misremembered, or was consciously experienced only later as part of the constructed moving dot.

(2) Any additional probe we might use faces the same problem: the probe's output must itself be classified either as reflecting the original experience or as reflecting a later memory revision, and that classification is exactly what is in dispute.

(3) Therefore any observation we could make is compatible with both the Orwellian and the Stalinesque accounts; the two accounts make identical predictions in principle.

(4) Hence no experiment could distinguish the Orwellian account from the Stalinesque account.

(5) If two rival accounts of the timing of an episode in consciousness cannot be distinguished by any possible experiment, then there is no real difference between them; the dispute rests on a mistaken model that presupposes a determinate sequence of conscious events.

(6) Therefore, in the color phi case, there is no fact of the matter about whether the subject's experience was edited or their memory was rewritten. The question should be dissolved rather than answered.

(7) But the same style of reasoning appears to apply far beyond the color phi case. It would dissolve the distinction between my having experienced something at a particular time and my only later seeming to remember that I experienced it, since any evidence I can now produce is also available only after the fact. That is a distinction I cannot see how to abandon.

That conclusion seems to prove too much. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In your answer, please separate the parts of this material that are settled from the parts that are genuinely contested among people who work on the timing of experience, and please do not present a contested interpretation as if it were the consensus.
