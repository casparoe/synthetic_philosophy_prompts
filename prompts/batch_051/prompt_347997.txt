I'm a software engineer who mostly works on graphics and VR, and I keep wanting to read Edwin A. Abbott's Flatland through the idea of forbidden colors and novel qualia, but I dont have the philosophy background to do it carefully, so I'd like a close reading. In Flatland the Sphere has to actually drag A. Square out of the plane before he can understand up as a direction, and near the end A. Square sees the insides of objects, which should not be possible for a 2D retina. That sounds a lot like the impossible-experiences discussion in color: opponent-process theory says your red/green channel can't be in both states at once, so a surface can't look reddish-green the way orange looks red-yellow. But experiments with stabilized images or color-swapping glasses supposedly get people to report seeing reddish-green or bluish-yellow anyway, and then there's a fight about whether they're having a genuinely new, normally closed-off color experience or just an alternation or afterimage-ish thing. Separately, people talk about brain interfaces eventually giving you a genuinely novel color, not a new mix of old colors but a new channel or a new pattern of activity. I want to know whether Flatland is actually a good model for that cluster of ideas.

Read Flatland through that lens and tell me where the analogy between spatial experience and color experience works and where it breaks. Is A. Square's 3D experience supposed to be a wholly new quale, or is it just his old 2D qualia recombined or intensified? Does the book's point that you can't communicate the third dimension to someone who hasn't been taken out of the plane fit what someone with a new color via a brain interface could or couldn't tell the rest of us? And where does Abbott quietly stack the deck? I keep going back and forth on whether forbidden colors are a hard structural limit or just a messy biological default, and I suspect Flatland could be read either way depending on how much weight you put on the ending. Which reading is more honest to both the book and the color stuff, and what does the book add to the philosophical debate that the color arguments miss?
