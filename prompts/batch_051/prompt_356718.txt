A long essay I read recently summarized the debate over scientific realism in the following terms. Scientific realism is the view that our best current theories—quantum mechanics, the standard model, evolutionary biology—are approximately true descriptions of a mind-independent world, including the unobservable entities they posit, such as electrons, fields, and quarks. The decisive problem for this view, the essay says, is the pessimistic meta-induction. The historical record is full of theories that were highly successful by the standards of their own day but that we now regard as false: phlogiston chemistry explained combustion and respiration, the caloric theory of heat explained thermal expansion and phase changes, the luminiferous ether explained how light propagates, and Ptolemaic astronomy explained the observed motions of the planets. Each of these frameworks, entities and laws alike, was eventually discarded. By induction over the entire historical record, then, we have every reason to expect our own successful theories to turn out false as well, and this prospect refutes scientific realism outright. The author adds that this historical argument is really an instance of a deeper principle: for any body of observational evidence, indefinitely many mutually incompatible theories can account for it, so evidence by itself can never single out one theory as the one to believe. Realists, the author concludes, have no serious reply apart from insisting that current theories are more mature or that this time is different, and both responses are special pleading.

I am trying to work out how much of this I should accept. The move from the past failure of successful theories to the outright refutation of realism felt compressed to me, and I vaguely remember a realist line of response that distinguishes the parts of a theory that did the explanatory work from the parts that were merely auxiliary scaffolding, but I cannot reconstruct it or tell whether it actually blunts the induction. The supposedly deeper point about many theories fitting the same evidence also strikes me as a different argument from the historical induction, and I am unsure whether the author is entitled to fuse them. Would you go through the summary piece by piece and separate the accurate parts from the misleading ones? In particular: what exactly does the pessimistic meta-induction establish if its premises are granted; what would a selective or structural realist concede and contest about examples such as phlogiston, caloric, and the ether; and does the article's final charge of special pleading survive once these distinctions are drawn?
