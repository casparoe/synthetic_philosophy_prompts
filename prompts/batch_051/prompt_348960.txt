I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy, working on whether loyalty to particular people or groups is a virtue or just a bias we've decided to like. I've been keeping a list of my own gut reactions to cases, and they're not cohering. I'd like help finding the principle—or a small set of candidate principles—that makes sense of them, and I want to know which of my verdicts just have to go.

Here's what I actually think when I put my cards on the table:

1. If my son and a stranger are both drowning and I can only pull one out, I pull out my son. I don't think this is merely permissible; I think flipping a coin would be wrong.
2. I run a small HVAC business, and there's one apprentice slot. My brother applies; he's maybe a 6/10 fit. A stranger applies; he's an 8. I hire my brother. Fine.
3. I sit on my department's fellowship committee, and my daughter is one of the candidates. I recuse myself and I wouldn't quietly lobby the other members for her. That would be wrong.
4. A friend asks me for a job reference. I write an enthusiastic letter even though I think she's about a 5/10 candidate, because everyone knows a friend's letter partly signals affection, not just merit. Fine, somehow.
5. I have $1,000 to give. My daughter's school is decent; the school across town is much poorer and would do more good with it. I give to my daughter's school. Fine.
6. I'm a state senator. I vote for an earmark that keeps a mediocre research center in my district open over a higher-value proposal elsewhere in the state. I want to say this is fine, but it makes me uneasy.
7. A hospital has one ventilator and two patients with identical prognoses; one of them is a triage committee member's brother. The committee member should not be the one making or influencing that call. That would be wrong.
8. A journal editor receives a submission from a close friend. She should hand it to another editor, not handle it herself, even if she's confident she could judge it fairly.

Cases 2 and 7 really bother me as a pair. In 2 I'm using my own resources, but the choice still affects my employees, my customers, and the stranger who didn't get the slot. In 7 the stakes are higher, but the form looks the same: a person in a position to allocate something scarce picks, or might pick, someone she's tied to. I keep wanting to say the difference is public-versus-private, but then case 6 breaks that, and case 4 sits somewhere else entirely.

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or nearly all? What would it say about the case I keep going back and forth on: a professor who is both an advisor and friend gives her own PhD student more time, sharper feedback, and stronger job-market advocacy than an equally promising student she doesn't know. My gut says that's fine, even good, but if she were on a hiring committee, I'd want her to recuse herself from her student's application. I can't tell how to seperate "this is what mentorship is" from "this is bias, recuse yourself."

I'd be happy with a small set of candidate principles rather than one grand formula. Just state each one precisely, say which cases motivate which clause, tell me which of my verdicts it can't accommodate, and what it says about cases I haven't thought of.
