Consider four settled institutional practices in which competent adults are expected to defer to another person's judgment, and in which the same institution also expects them, at some point, to stop deferring and push back.

1. Military orders. A soldier is expected to carry out a lawful order even when he privately thinks it is tactically misguided, and he can be punished for refusing it. But he is expected to refuse an order that is unlawful, and the plea that one was merely carrying out a superior's orders was rejected as a defense in the war-crimes trials after the Second World War. The soldier is thus meant to be a deferential executor on matters of military judgment and a moral gatekeeper on matters of law.

2. The hospital chain of command. A nurse or junior doctor is expected to carry out a consultant's instructions without re-arguing the clinical reasoning, yet many hospitals now run safety campaigns telling the same staff to challenge a superior's order when it looks like a medication error, a wrong dose, or surgery on the wrong site. The same hierarchy that demands deference also demands interruption.

3. The cockpit. Early airline culture made the captain's decision final. After investigations into crashes in which a junior crew member had noticed the problem but said nothing, airlines adopted crew resource management: first officers are trained to state a concern firmly and repeatedly, and captains are trained to invite and reward contradiction. The junior pilot defers in normal operations but is expected to override social deference when the aircraft is at risk.

4. Monastic obedience. In several religious orders a novice is trained to obey a superior's instruction without first judging whether it is prudent or even intelligible, on the understanding that renouncing one's private judgment in small matters is itself a discipline. The same tradition typically adds that a command to do something plainly sinful must be disobeyed. Deference is cultivated as a virtue right up to a moral boundary that the subordinate is still expected to recognize.

Each of these practices treats deference as a virtue in one register and as a vice in another, but they locate the boundary differently. Write an essay that reconstructs the implicit theory of authority and obedience each practice presupposes, and then compare the four. In particular, do the following:

(a) For each practice, state what view of legitimate authority it strictly presupposes, and separate that from what it is merely compatible with.

(b) Identify the points on which the four practices presuppose one shared view, and the points on which they genuinely conflict.

(c) Offer the most defensible unified principle for when deference is a virtue that fits the pattern across all four settings, and state explicitly which cases that principle leaves unresolved.

After the essay, append a table with one row per practice and these columns: Practice; Implicit view of legitimate authority; What it strictly presupposes; What it merely fits with; When deference counts as a virtue or a vice here.
