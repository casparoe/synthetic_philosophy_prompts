I've gone down a rabbit hole on Kepler this year—started with the three laws and ended up reading a lot about his astrology—and next month I'm giving the short presentation at my local astronomy club. My thesis is basically this: Kepler's astrology wasn't just a side gig to pay the rent, and it wasn't a mystical relapse we should quietly forgive. It was part of the same project as his astronomy. He thought the planets genuinely affected things down here through physical causes: the geometry of the rays, the angles planets make with each other, the way the earth as a whole responds to those patterns. He spent real effort trying to reform astrology into something with actual causes, a kind of celestial weather science, while throwing out the arbitrary stuff—the zodiac house rules and the like—that he thought had no basis. The guy who worked out Mars's orbit and the guy who wrote treatises about how aspects work are the same guy, and spliting him into good Kepler and embarrassed Kepler is a mistake.

The audience is my club: mostly retired engineers, a few astrophotographers, one high-school physics teacher. They know Kepler as the hero who gave us the three laws, and they mostly treat his astrology as nonsense he did for money. So I know how the Q&A will go. I don't want a general defense; I want a prep sheet. What are the objections thier likely to raise, in roughly the order they'll come, and which kind of club member is most likely to raise each one—retired engineer, astrophotographer, physics teacher? Phrase each objection the way that person would actually put it. For each one: how much damage it does, what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and where my reply is softest. And end with the question I'm hoping nobody asks, the one that exposes the weakest part of the whole position.
