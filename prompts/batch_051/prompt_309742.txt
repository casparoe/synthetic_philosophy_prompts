In his 1953 essay The Methodology of Positive Economics, Milton Friedman considers the hypothesis that the leaves on a tree are arranged as if each leaf deliberately sought to maximize its exposure to sunlight. He grants that leaves do not make calculations or choices, yet he argues that the hypothesis can still be scientifically valuable if it yields accurate predictions about where foliage will be dense or sparse. Friedman’s broader claim is that the realism of a model’s assumptions is not the right test of the model; what matters is whether the model predicts well over the range of phenomena it is meant to cover.

I want to know whether that argument survives a specific later development. Consider a contemporary computational model of the same tree canopy that simulates each leaf as a physical subsystem responding to local light gradients, water stress, and mechanical constraints, with no intentional or as-if vocabulary. The simulation is more assumption-realistic but also far less compact. It may predict the same foliage patterns, and it may also predict patterns the leaf-as-agent hypothesis cannot.

Does Friedman’s argument still justify preferring the simpler as-if-deliberate hypothesis, or does the availability of high-fidelity simulation shift the relevant criterion from predictive success alone to some trade-off involving realism, compactness, and scope? Which precise premise in Friedman’s argument is under pressure, and is there a repaired version that remains defensible?
