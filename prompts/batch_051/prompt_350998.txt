I've been thinking on my own about whether it really makes sense to say that one false picture of the world is closer to the truth than another. The question got its grip on me through everyday corrections rather than through reading philosophy: to a child who believes the Earth is flat, I would say the Earth is roughly a sphere. But "the Earth is a sphere" is also false, strictly speaking, since the Earth bulges at the equator. And yet almost no one would treat "the Earth is a sphere" and "the Earth is flat" as equally false. The phrase "closer to the truth" seems to be doing real work there.

That led me to a harder case. Newtonian mechanics and Aristotelian physics are both, by current lights, false as accounts of motion: one replaced the other, and both have since been superseded. But I find it very hard to shake the intuition that Newton is closer to the truth about motion than Aristotle is, and I don't think that's merely because Newton came later or because I was educated into Newton.

My current credence is about 0.65 that the claim "Newtonian mechanics is closer to the truth about motion than Aristotelian physics is" is true in a robust, interest-independent way: that there is a fact of the matter here which does not reduce to convenience, training, or which problems we happen to care about. A friend of mine puts his credence at about 0.2. He says closeness to the truth is only a metaphor until you have specified a purpose, and that with no purpose specified the comparison is neither true nor false.

I would like your own number, but more importantly I would like the reasoning decomposed. Here are the sub-questions I can see, and I would like a rough probability for each:

- Is being closer to the truth a property a false statement or theory can have in the first place?
- Should closeness be measured in terms of true and false consequences, and if so, should the false consequences count against a theory, and do they all count equally?
- Are two theories with such different conceptual vocabularies and such different ideas about what motion is even comparable on a single scale?
- Is there a single correct way to measure closeness to the truth, or is any measure necessarily relative to an interest or task?
- How much is the comparison really about the theories themselves, rather than about our judging them from the standpoint of a later theory?

Then I would like to know how these combine to produce your number, which sub-question your final credence is most sensitive to, and which sub-question most likely explains the gap between me at 0.65 and my friend at 0.2. Finally, tell me what evidence or argument would move your number the most, and what in particular would move you toward about 0.2 or about 0.65. If you think the main question is ill-posed as stated, say what would need to be fixed for it to become well-posed, and factor that into your decomposition rather than refusing to answer.
