I am a novelist who writes literary historical fiction. In every chapter I decide, over and over, how to treat the historical record: whether to state something in the narrator's own voice as settled fact, let a character say it as someone who may be wrong, present it as contested, fill a gap with invention, or cut it because it will not withstand scrutiny even by fiction's looser standards. The decisions are individually small, but collectively they determine whether the book deserves the reader's trust and whether a historian reading it finds me scrupulous or sloppy. I have about six months of research per book, I will not clutter the prose with footnotes, and an editor and a fact-checker will ask me to defend every sentence I presented as fact.

I want the procedure grounded in a real disagreement in the philosophy of history rather than in my own habits. Ranke is associated with the conviction that the historian's discipline can recover the past as it actually was. Carr answered that facts do not simply lie in the record waiting to be picked up: they become historical facts when a historian selects them and gives them significance. Hayden White went further and argued that a historical narrative is always plotted, as tragedy, comedy, romance, or satire, and that the plot is the historian's contribution, not simply what the sources dictate. I do not want to treat a novel as if it were a monograph, but I also do not want to use these skeptical points as a license for invention passed off as fact. The procedure should respect all three positions: it should tell me where the record genuinely constrains me, where the uncertainty is already in the record, where I am free to invent because there is a real gap, and where I am making an interpretive choice that no factual test can settle.

Please write it as a sequence of questions I can run on a claim or a scene, in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each. Mark where the procedure stops with a decision, such as state it plainly, qualify it, attribute it to a character, or drop it, and mark where it stops because the decision is interpretive and must be handed back to me. Then run the procedure on three worked cases: (1) a battle whose date, location, outcome, and principal commanders are confirmed by multiple independent sources; (2) a private motive attributed to a real person in a recent biography but unsupported by that person's own letters or diaries; (3) a vivid, widely repeated remark attributed to a historical figure that survives in a single late and interested source and would make a superb scene. Let at least one worked case be one where the procedure tells me something I do not want to hear. Finally, say explicitly what the procedure cannot do: the questions that look factual but are really Ranke's method being asked to settle a problem that belongs to Carr or White.
