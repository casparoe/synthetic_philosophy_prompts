Me and a friend got into a long arguement last night about what it actually means to understand a proof, not just be able to follow one. He kept reading me this bit from Poincaré's *Science and Hypothesis*, chapter 1, where Poincaré says:

"No theorem can be new unless a new axiom intervenes in its demonstration; reasoning can only give us immediately evident truths borrowed from direct intuition; it would only be an intermediary parasite. Should we not therefore have reason for asking if the syllogistic apparatus serves only to disguise what we have borrowed?"

My friend thinks this means the only thing that matters for understanding is being able to locate the one non-syllogistic move that does the real work, and that everything else is just checking. I don't buy that fully, but he did make me realize I've got alot of gut verdicts that might not add up. Here are the cases we kept going back and forth on, all about whether a person understands a proof.

1. A student memorizes the proof that the square root of 2 is irrational and can reproduce every step in order, including the contradiction setup and the parity argument. But when you ask why it matters that p and q are coprime, or what would break if they weren't, they just repeat the line from the proof. I say they don't understand it.

2. A mathematician sees a diagram for a proof of the Pythagorean theorem and says "oh, now I see what's going on," and can explain the idea in plain words, but if you ask them to write the formal algebraic derivation they make small mistakes and have to be nudged. I say they understand it.

3. A graduate student goes through a long analytic number theory proof line by line, can justify every inference, and can even formalize it in Lean. But they describe the whole thing as "a long slog of estimates" and can't say what the main idea is or what estimate is doing the heavy lifting. I want to say they don't understand it, but then my friend asked what's left to understand if they can justify every step. I didn't know what to say.

4. A topologist gives a proof by drawing a series of pictures of a homotopy. Another mathematician checks the pictures against the definitions and says the proof is not rigorous as drawn because some continuity details are skipped, but after an hour they fill in the details. I think the first topologist understood the proof before the details were filled in, but I'm less sure about the second one.

5. A theorem prover verifies a proof with no human ever looking at the code. The system can use the proof to certify the theorem. I don't think "the system" understands the proof, though I can't say exactly why not, since the verification is complete.

6. A student reads a proof of the fundamental theorem of algebra and can repeat it, but they also say "the key idea is that the function behaves like z^n at infinity and like a constant near zero, so it must hit every complex number in between." That is not really what the proof literally says, and maybe it's only a heuristic. I still want to say they understand the proof better than someone who can only check the algebra.

Is there a single definition of "understanding a proof" that gets all these verdicts right, or close? If not, which of my verdicts would it force me to overturn? And does the Poincaré passage actually support my friend's one-big-idea view, or does it create more trouble for him than for me? I'd also like you to run whatever principle you propose on a case I keep going back and forth on: a student can explain the proof of the Chinese remainder theorem by saying "the moduli are coprime, so you can solve the congruences independently and patch the solution together," but if you change the statement slightly to where the moduli aren't coprime, they can't say what goes wrong. Do they understand the original proof? Please write your answer in American English.
