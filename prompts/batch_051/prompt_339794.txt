I teach an upper-level undergraduate aesthetics seminar, and when we reach the paradox of tragedy — why people deliberately seek out and report enjoying artworks that make them feel sad, afraid, or disturbed — I want to run a sorting exercise rather than lecture through the taxonomy. Please write me a decision tree that sorts a respondent into one of the standard families of explanation: (1) catharsis or purgation, (2) conversion or compensation, on which the displeasure is transformed into, or outweighed by, the pleasures of artistic representation, (3) control or security, on which we enjoy the felt negative affect because we know we are safe from real consequences, (4) meta-response or rich-experience views, on which the global experience is valued with the negative emotion as a necessary component, and (5) the deflationary view that denies there is any paradox to begin with. I do not want a tree whose nodes are these labels turned into questions. I want it built from the logical space of possible answers. Every internal node should be a concrete, self-contained case or claim a student can answer without knowing any of the theory — for example: whether the appeal survives when the ending, and hence the pity and fear, is known in advance; whether a wordless instrumental piece in a minor key with no narrative or represented characters can still produce the phenomenon; whether a technically masterful production that leaves one emotionally flat is preferred to a cruder one that reliably moves one; whether a documentary showing real, unscripted suffering is enjoyed in the same way as a fictional tragedy; and whether whatever explains the appeal of sad art must also explain the appeal of fear-inducing horror. Phrase each node as a first-person judgment about one of these cases rather than as a theoretical commitment.

Begin by laying out the questions and which combinations of answers are coherent, and only then locate the five named positions as leaves in that space; other defensible leaves may be added if the space calls for them, for a total of six to nine leaves. Where a coherent combination of answers has no known defender, mark it clearly and add one sentence diagnosing why it is unoccupied — incoherent on reflection, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Where two of the named positions land on the same leaf because the chosen questions cannot separate them, say so and state the further question that would separate them. This is for a ninety-minute seminar exercise, so each question must be answerable quickly, from pre-theoretical response to the cases alone, without any outside reading.
