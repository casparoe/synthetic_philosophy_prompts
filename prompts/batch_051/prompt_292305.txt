Professionally, I spent four decades in geophysics, studying fault systems and the slow accumulation of strain before a rupture. Since retiring I have turned to epistemology, and I have become absorbed by a short book, Wittgenstein's On Certainty. I want your help diagnosing a debate in its secondary literature that, to an outsider trained in the natural sciences, looks as though it has been circling the same ground for years without settling.

The passage that anchors me is §94. I do not have a translation to hand, so I shall paraphrase and mark it as such: Wittgenstein says, as I understand him, that he did not arrive at his picture of the world by first confirming that it was correct; nor does he hold it because he has confirmed its correctness. Instead, that picture is simply the inherited backdrop against which he draws the distinction between true and false. In the sections nearby he develops a related image, of certain propositions being like the bed of a river while others flow over them in the current of ordinary judgement. What intrigues me, as someone who has spent a career thinking about what holds things up, is the idea of a ground that is not itself supported from below but nevertheless makes all local support possible.

Now the debate. One school of interpreters treats these hinge commitments as a species of belief: they are propositions held with a peculiar kind of certainty, neither justified nor doubted, but still propositional states whose contents can in principle be assessed for truth or falsity, even if their epistemic role is unlike that of ordinary empirical beliefs. The other school denies this and treats hinges as non-propositional: not beliefs at all, but a form of practical sureness that shows itself in what agents do, in which doubts they never raise, in how they take certain things as given. On this second view, to ask whether the hinge is true is already to make a category mistake, rather like asking whether a rule of measurement is itself long or short.

What troubles me is that both sides seem able to absorb the same textual and epistemic phenomena. The oddness of saying that one knows this is a hand when no sceptical doubt is in play; the near-unintelligibility of radical doubt about whether the Earth has existed for many years; the fact that hinges stand fast for us without our feeling their lack of justification as a defect — each side claims all of this as support. I have not found an argument that one side treats as fatal to the other, and the dispute persists.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is at bottom a dispute over the word 'belief', and over how widely to draw the boundary of the propositional: each side fixes its terminology so that its preferred theory follows, and the apparent conflict is therefore partly verbal. But I am not confident in that, because I can see two other possibilities. The first is that the disagreement is genuinely substantive, turning on what kind of state — doxastic or practical — can in fact play the role of a hinge. The second is that the two camps are answering different questions, one about individual cognition and one about public, rule-governed practice, while sharing the heading 'hinge epistemology'.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a survey, and not a verdict about which reading of Wittgenstein is most likely exegetically correct. Specifically: what kind of disagreement is this — verbal, substantive, or cross-purposed? What would count as progress? That is, is there an argument, a distinction, or even an empirical finding from developmental psychology or cognitive science that could in principle move one side to abandon its view, and if not, why not? And if the debate as I have posed it is ill-posed, what is the better question to be asking about hinges?

I should add two constraints. First, I am not asking for a bibliography or a map of the literature; I want your own reasoning about the structure of the disagreement. Second, if my description of the positions rests on a confusion, or if I have mischaracterised one of the camps or Wittgenstein's own view, please say so plainly rather than tidying it up and complying for the sake of compliance.
