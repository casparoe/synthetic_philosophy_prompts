I'm in a seminar on gratitude — the ethics side and the psychology side — and I've been using a distinction in my notes that I can't tell is actually original or just me rediscovering something definately obvious. Play the role of a philosopher of emotion who's actually read the gratitude literature: steelman what I'm about to say before you tear into it, and start by brainstorming what would have to be true for it to work.

I've been calling it "benefactorless gratitude." The idea is that the felt state of being grateful doesn't really need a benefactor. When I'm grateful that the tumour turned out benign, or that my flight made it through that storm, or toward my past self for finally quitting, there's no one — or no one separate — to thank, but the feeling is the same as when I'm grateful to a person. So I've started thinking that all gratitude is basically propositional: it's a response to good fortune, full stop, and gratitude-to-a-person is just the special case where you can trace the good fortune back to an agent. Person-directed gratitude isn't the paradigm; it's the derivative case.

The actual claim I want checked is stronger. Whatever you owe someone — thanks, reciprocation, loyalty — attaches to the traced-back cause, not to the felt state. So if the cause is luck, you owe nothing; if it's a person, you owe them. That would mean feeling gratitude and owing gratitude can come apart completely, and any theory defining gratitude as a debt to a benefactor is only handling the traced-back case, not gratitude itself.

So: does this hold up? Is this already a named thing in the literature that I've somehow missed — and if so, what does the standard version get right that I'm fumbling? And where does it break: what does it wrongly count as gratitude, or what can't it explain?
