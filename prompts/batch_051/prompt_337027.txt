I've been rereading Hume's economic essays in the Political Discourses alongside a policy argument, and I keep going back and forth about what he would actually say. The question I'd like you to work through is whether Hume, if asked to be consistent with his own earlier rulings, would approve of a modern central bank using domestic asset purchases—quantitative easing—to reduce unemployment during a recession, in a country with a floating fiat currency and no foreign wars.

Here is the track record I'm working from. In "Of Money," Hume holds that, within a single kingdom, the quantity of money is of no real consequence, because prices proportion themselves to it. The passage I keep coming back to is this one, from "Of Money":

"If we consider any one kingdom by itself, it is evident, that the greater or less plenty of money is of no consequence; since the prices of commodities are always proportioned to the plenty of money... It is only the public which draws any advantage from the greater plenty of money; and that only in its wars and negociations with foreign states."

In the same essay he adds a doubt about banks and paper credit, treating them as a kind of counterfeit money that raises prices without conferring the advantage that precious metals give a nation in its foreign dealings. In "Of the Balance of Trade," he argues that a nation cannot permanently lose its money as long as its people and industry remain, because prices and trade flows adjust and the money returns. In "Of Interest," he insists that low interest is caused not by abundance of money but by the growth of commerce, industry, and frugality.

So the new case is the quantitative-easing case: a central bank in a country with a floating fiat currency, no major foreign wars, and a domestic recession buys long-term government bonds to lower long-term interest rates and encourage employment. I'm less interested in a bare guess than in the rule Hume seems to be running. In particular, two readings both look consistent with the track record. One is that money's only real advantage is for the public in foreign wars and negotiations, so domestic monetary expansion aimed at domestic goals is pointless or harmful. The other is that Hume's consistent test is whether a policy supports industry and commerce, in which case a monetary intervention that prevents a commercial collapse might pass. Do those two rules diverge on the QE case, and which rule is the better reading of the track record? Which of Hume's essays is the closest analog to the QE case, what principle would he be adopting under each reading, and what further case would best tell the two rules apart?
