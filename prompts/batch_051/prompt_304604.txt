I'm a novelist, and I'm working on a near-future book where a government replaces its senate with a citizens' assembly chosen by sortition — by lottery, basically. To keep the politics from sounding like a think-tank pamphlet, I've been collecting real arguments. The other night two of my friends got into it over this, and I took notes like a creep; I need an honest, point-by-point adjudication, because I want to know which of these moves are strong enough to put in a character's mouth and which ones would make a politically literate reader roll their eyes. Here's the argument, transcribed about as well as I could manage.

(1) M: Sortition is a technocrat's fantasy. Legislating is a craft: you have to know how a bill gets marked up, how an amendment dies in committee, how to read a fiscal note. Random citizens don't know any of that, so they'd just be puppets of the permanent staff and the lobbyists, who are the only people in the room who actually know how the machine runs.

(2) R: That's the career politician's self-serving story. The "expertise" elected legislators pile up is mostly expertise at fundraising, working donor rooms, and getting re-elected. A body drawn by lot at least wouldn't spend half its time begging for money, and it would actually look like the population instead of a convention of lawyers and small-business owners.

(3) M: But elections create accountability. If I hate what my rep did, I vote them out. A citizen chosen by lot serves for a year or two and then goes back to their day job, so they never face the voters; there's no check on laziness or corruption. Strip away re-election and you've stripped away the only discipline politicians actually have.

(4) R: That accountability is mostly notional. Voters pick on party label, name recognition, and a couple of issues; they almost never punish someone for a committee vote they never heard about. A career politician, by contrast, is accountable to donors at all times and to the public only at election time. A citizens' assembly has a different kind of accountability — to its other members, and to the public through published reasons for what it decides.

(5) M: Term limits show what happens when you cycle amateurs through. After term limits, power flows to the permanent staff, the lobbyists, and the governor's office, because they're the only ones left who know how the place works. At least a career politician has institutional memory and can be watched by journalists and opponents.

(6) R: That's an objection to term limits, not to sortition. Sortition doesn't just drop amateurs into the existing machine and leave the machine untouched; it changes who gets to write the rules. And where it has been tried — the Irish Citizens' Assembly, and the constitutional convention before it — ordinary citizens handled genuinely hard questions once they got expert briefings, time, and structured deliberation.

(7) M: The Irish example is my point. The assembly could recommend whatever it liked, but the government and a referendum still had to sign off. The politicians kept the final say. That's an admission that sortition only works as a consultative sideshow, not as an actual replacement for elections.

(8) R: That's a feature, not a bug. Nobody serious wants to replace every election with a lottery. Sortition is for the questions where elected officials have a direct conflict of interest: drawing their own districts, setting their own pay, imposing term limits on themselves, constitutional rules. On those, letting incumbents decide is like asking a restaurant to grade its own kitchen.

(9) M: But then you have a capture problem. Whoever decides what goes to the assembly will just send it the questions they don't care about, or the ones they can overrule. It becomes a pressure-release valve that legitimizes the same old class.

(10) R: So design it so the assembly can put things on its own agenda, or be triggered by a citizen petition. The design can be fixed; the principle stands. A random sample of citizens has a democratic claim that a career politician with a donor list doesn't.

Alright. Please adjuticate this move by move: for each of the ten moves — or pair them where they're replies — rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then say which specific points each side actually wins, and what, if anything, they're both missing.
