I'm taking a class on how archaeologists reconstruct early beliefs and institutions from material culture, and the same recurring problem keeps bugging me: how do you decide that a repeated pattern of objects reflects something like a shared belief or a ritual institution, rather than just a practical convention or a household quirk? Our instructor gave us five working criteria for calling an archaeological pattern evidence of a belief/ritual as opposed to mundane practice. The pattern should be (1) standardized and rule-governed across many separate contexts, not one-off or idiosyncratic; (2) costly in time or materials without an obvious practical payoff; (3) found in special locations — foundations, thresholds, burials — rather than ordinary refuse; (4) materially non-utilitarian: miniature, unusable, deliberately broken, made of rare stuff, or redundant; and (5) not fully explainable by any plausible mundane account that also handles its distribution.

Here's the borderline case I can't stop thinking about. At a small late Neolithic settlement — call it Ash Hollow — excavators find, beneath the north wall of every one of the twenty-three houses in the main phase, a small clay-lined pit containing the same three things: a single deliberately snapped clay figurine of a human torso, two tiny clay cups too small to hold more than a thimble of anything, and a few charred seeds of a plant that doesn't grow in the region and has no documented food value. The pit is always capped with a broken quern stone, grinding face down, aligned north–south. The deposits never occur in the settlement's refuse pits or middens, never occur in later phases, and don't correlate with house size, age, sex, or grave goods in any way anyone can detect. One of the pits also contains, just this once, an extra token: a small clay disc marked with four parallel incised lines.

Run this case through the five criteria and tell me whether it qualifies as evidence of a shared belief or ritual institution — or whether a shared record-keeping convention or a household identity marker would fit equally well. I'd also like to know whether you think these criteria can actually do the work we're asking of them, or whether the line between belief, ritual, and mundane institution is one that objects alone just can't draw. Keep your answer under 500 words.
