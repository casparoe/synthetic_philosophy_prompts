Hey — I'm a novelist, not a philosopher. My current book is near-future AI, and the safety lead at the fictional lab has a habit of building the strongest case she can for views she hates, because she thinks it keeps her honest. Right now she's working on the fast takeoff position: the claim that once a system reaches roughly human-level general intelligence, it will go to superintelligence in a matter of weeks, not decades. So far her list of arguments has four entries:

1. Recursive self-improvement. Once a system can do AI research as well as a human can, it can modify its own architecture, which makes it better at modifying itself, and each round shrinks the time to the next round.

2. The overhang argument. For years the field builds up hardware and untapped algorithmic ideas faster than the software can absorb them, so a single breakthrough could let one system cash in a huge backlog of latent capability all at once.

3. The removal of human bottlenecks. Humans are stuck with fixed neurons, slow signaling, small working memory, and terrible bandwidth between brains. A digital system can clone itself, run itself faster, add compute, and share exact mental states directly, so its version of doing research isn't slowed down by the things that make human science take decades.

4. The competitive-pressure argument. Even if the first lab wanted to go slow, other labs and governments won't; once the race is on, nobody gets to choose a slow takeoff, and a lab that tries get overtaken.

I have a feeling there's a fifth argument that has a genuinely diffrent structure — not a restatement of any of these, not a special case, and not a combination of two of them. It's the kind of thing a small group of engineers at the lab would raise over lunch. What is that argument? And be straight with me: is it actually any good, or does it collapse into one of the four when you press on it?

You're a professional philosopher of science and technology who thinks carefully about the logic of AI-risk arguments. Please brainstorm two or three candidates internally before settling on one, and check each candidate against the list to make sure it earns its place.
