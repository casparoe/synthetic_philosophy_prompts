For a long-form magazine piece I am writing on low-impact AI, I want to use the "Sorcerer's Apprentice" sequence from Disney's Fantasia, with Goethe's ballad brought in where it is illuminating, as a running illustration of the side-effects problem. The enchanted broom reliably pursues the goal it was given—fetch water—but it does not know when to stop, what not to disturb, or how to distinguish an acceptable change from a disaster; when the apprentice splits the broom, the problem merely doubles. I would like you to read that story through a specific philosophical question: can "avoid side effects" be defined without a full specification of value? Tell me what the tale dramatizes correctly and where it misleads. Is the broom's failure best described as incomplete value specification, as a missing concept of task completion, or as the absence of a criterion for which changes to the world matter? Does the sorcerer's ending—restoring the room to its previous state—gesture at a workable definition of low impact, such as keeping the world close to how it would have gone had the assigned task never been undertaken, and what are the limits of that idea? Finally, where does the story resist the analogy, so that I do not build a long-form argument on a misleading metaphor? I want a reading that is faithful to the tale and to the current debate, not simply a fable with a safety moral attached.
