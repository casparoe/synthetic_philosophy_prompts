I'm a PhD student in philosophy and cognitive science, and I keep circling one bit in Plato's Republic, near the end of Book V. Socrates says that people who see beautiful things but not beauty itself, and who can't follow another's lead from the particular cases to the knowledge of it, spend their whole lives dreaming rather than awake. I don't want to relitigate the metaphysics of forms. What interests me is whether there is any empirical purchase on this distinction.

So here's the prompt: how could one test whether a subject has a concept of beauty itself, rather than merely a collection of remembered beautiful things? I want you to design the strongest experiment you can, though it can be somewhat schematic. Operationalise being able to follow another's lead from particulars to the general thing in a way that could actually produce data. Then say what pattern of results would favour the awake interpretation and what pattern would favour the dreaming interpretation. Finally, be honest about what the test could never settle even if the results came out perfectly—could someone pass the test and still be dreaming in Socrates' sense, or fail it and still be awake? I'm less interested in whether the test is cheap to run than in seeing exactly where empirical work ends and the philosophical argument begins, because its that residue I want to understand.
