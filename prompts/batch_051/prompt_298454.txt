In a social choice seminar last week, two classmates got into a dispute about Sen's so-called liberal paradox — the impossibility of a Paretian liberal — and they asked me to referee. I said I'd think about it, but after replaying the argument I still can't sort out who is right about what. Here is the dispute as faithfully as I can reconstruct it.

Ana: "Sen's result strikes me as a genuine discovery. Take two principles that almost everyone finds attractive. Pareto: if every individual strictly prefers one outcome to another, the social evaluation should rank it above. Minimal liberalism: at least two people should each be decisive over some pair of options that concern their own lives — for instance, whether they themselves read a certain novel. In the Lady Chatterley case these two principles cycle. The prude's private right gives 'nobody reads' over 'the prude reads,' because he prefers not to read. The lewd's private right gives 'the lewd reads' over 'nobody reads,' because he prefers to read. And Pareto gives 'the prude reads' over 'the lewd reads,' because both prefer that outcome. So we get: the prude reads beats the lewd reads, which beats nobody reads, which beats the prude reads. Rights plus unanimity, both minimal, produce intransitivity. Something has to give, and that is not a trivial observation."

Ben: "But look at what the intransitivity is an intransitivity of: a social preference ordering. A right to read or not to read is not a claim about where alternatives should sit in a public ranking. It is a constraint on interference: the prude may not be forced to read, and the lewd may not be stopped from reading. If rights are constraints of that kind, then no single ranking of 'the prude reads,' 'the lewd reads,' and 'nobody reads' needs to exist in the first place, because there is no collective agent deciding who reads what. Sen's minimal liberalism forces rights into the wrong shape — a decisive vote over pieces of the social ordering. The cycle is an artifact of that formalization, not a conflict between liberalism and Pareto."

Ana: "That dodges the problem rather than solving it. On matters where private choices have spillovers — a library decides what to stock, two people argue about whether a book stays in the house — some collective stance or procedure is unavoidable. For those decisions we aggregate preferences, and Pareto is about as uncontroversial as aggregation gets. Sen's theorem shows that once you add even a small sphere of individual decisiveness to an aggregation that respects unanimity, cycles can appear. You can say 'don't aggregate personal reading,' but that simply refuses to apply the framework exactly where it seems needed."

Ben: "No, my point is more specific. It is about the model of rights, not about whether aggregation is ever needed. The cycle depends on counting preferences about how other people live. The prude's preference that the lewd not read, and the lewd's preference that the prude read for his own good, are preferences about other people's private conduct. A liberal theory has independent reasons not to treat such meddlesome preferences as inputs to social choice. If you restrict the admissible preferences to self-regarding ones, the Pareto leg of the Lady Chatterley cycle simply isn't available, and the alleged conflict dissolves."

Ana: "But that restriction is doing serious work, and it is a substantive liberal doctrine, not a technical repair. Pareto, as normally stated, says unanimity should count regardless of the content of the preferences. To block the example you have to amend Pareto with a filter on which unanimous preferences count, and that itself concedes the point: a liberal cannot accept the unrestricted Pareto principle. The formal theorem remains a discovery about why these conditions cannot all stay. It doesn't prove one should ditch rights rather than Pareto, but it does show they come apart."

Ben: "I don't dispute that the axioms are jointly inconsistent. What I dispute is the interpretive step from the axioms to a conflict between liberalism and respect for unanimity. The 'minimal liberalism' condition is not a faithful rendering of what rights are. It makes a right into one more ingredient of a social preference function, in the same currency as tastes. That is the very picture of rights that liberals reject. Read properly, Sen's result is a reductio of the decisiveness formalization of rights, not a discovery about the values themselves."

Ana: "The side-constraint picture has its own serious gap. Rights collide: your right to speak may interfere with my right to enjoy my home in quiet; a right to practice religion can collide with another's right to equal treatment. A pure constraint model says nothing about how such conflicts are to be resolved. The social-choice framework, for all its artificiality, at least forces those resolutions into a public ranking and asks whether the ranking can be coherent. Until the side-constraint view gives an account of conflict and adjudication, 'rights are constraints' is an opening move, not a refutation of the paradox."

Ben: "I'll grant that. But it is a different problem from Sen's. The paradox advertises an impossibility from Pareto plus minimal liberalism. My objection is that the impossibility is purchased by a misdescription of rights. If the correct formalization is constraint-based, then the advertised impossibility has not been established. Liberalism may still have hard problems with externalities and conflicting rights, but Sen's theorem is not the proof of a deep antinomy."

Neither of them moved. They asked me to referee, but I could not.
