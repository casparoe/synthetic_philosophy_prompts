I recently found myself defending the anti-realist idea that every truth is in principle knowable, and a friend tried to correct me by walking through Fitch's paradox. His summary went as follows:

"Suppose there is some true proposition P that nobody knows. Then the conjunction 'P and nobody knows that P' is also true, so by the knowability principle it must be knowable: in some possible situation, somebody knows that conjunction. Since knowledge distributes over conjunction, that person would know P and would know that nobody knows P. Knowledge is factive, so their knowing that nobody knows P entails that nobody knows P. Yet the same person knows P in that very situation—contradiction. Therefore the assumption that there is an unknown truth is impossible, which means every truth is already known. Notice that the argument uses only two uncontroversial assumptions, the distributivity of knowledge over conjunction and the factivity of knowledge, so there is no room left for the knowability principle. Since we are obviously not omniscient, the principle is simply refuted."

I don't have enough modal logic to tell whether this is a fair presentation of the result. Which parts of his summary are accurate, which are subtly wrong, and does his conclusion—that the knowability principle is simply refuted—survive the corrections? If the conclusion overreaches, what exactly is left standing once the summary is corrected?
