Consider this passage, from Chapter IV ("Comparison of the Mental Powers of Man and the Lower Animals—continued") of Charles Darwin's *The Descent of Man*:

"In the same manner as various animals have some sense of beauty, though they admire widely different objects, so they might have a sense of right and wrong, though led by it to follow widely different lines of conduct. If, for instance, to take an extreme case, men were reared under precisely the same conditions as hive-bees, there can hardly be a doubt that our unmarried females would, like the worker-bees, think it a sacred duty to kill their brothers, and mothers would strive to kill their fertile daughters; and no one would think of interfering."

Treat this passage as the empirical core of the evolutionary debunking argument: the thought that the contents of our moral judgments would have been different if our lineage had faced different selective pressures, and that moral truth as such need never enter the Darwinian explanation.

Definitions and target proposition. By robust moral realism I mean: there are stance-independent moral facts; our moral judgments are sometimes correct in virtue of accurately representing those facts; and their being correct is not constituted by the outputs of idealized or improved practical reasoning. Let P be the proposition:

P: The Darwinian explanation of the content of our moral sense gives an undefeated reason to think our moral judgments are unreliable indicators of robust moral facts, and a reasonable evaluator who fully accepts that explanation should consequently reduce her credence in robust moral realism by at least half.

(b) Decompose your credence. At minimum your decomposition must assign a probability and a one-line justification to each of the following, but you may add terms if you think they matter:

1. Darwinian-variation premise: moral dispositions at the relevant level of abstraction would vary substantially with the ancestral selection regime, so the moral sense is better explained by adaptive pressures than by sensitivity to robust moral facts.
2. Insensitivity premise: natural selection, and the social instincts it shaped, explain our holding the moral judgments we hold without robust moral facts playing any role in the explanation; our judgments would be largely as they are whether or not robust realism were true.
3. Debunking principle: if a class of beliefs can be fully explained by a process insensitive to the truths the beliefs purport to track, then those beliefs are not justified or not reliable, absent an independent reason to expect the process to track.
4. Realism's escapes fail: the robust realist cannot supply that independent reason; neither a tracking account, nor a third-factor account, nor a conceptual-entailment account, nor the claim that moral truths are what we would judge under good conditions rescues reliability at the level needed.
