I've been trying to get a handle on the doctrine of divine impassibility — the classical Christian claim that God cannot suffer — after it came up repeatedly in my philosophy of religion seminar. A friend lent me a popular-level theology book that summarizes the doctrine in a way I found suspicious, and I'd like to know how far my suspicion is justified. The passage runs more or less like this:

"The classical doctrine of divine impassibility holds that God cannot suffer. Its defenders took this idea from Greek philosophy, especially the Stoic ideal of being undisturbed by passion, and read it back into the Bible. On this view, God has no emotions at all — no anger, no grief, no joy — and the biblical passages that describe God as angry or grieved are best understood as mere figures of speech. The underlying motivation was that suffering is a weakness, and a perfect being cannot be weak; to be affected by creatures would make God depend on them, which would compromise his sovereignty. The obvious cost is that such a God is cold and indifferent to human pain. In fact, if God cannot suffer, then God cannot love, because love requires openness to being wounded by the beloved. That is why many contemporary theologians have abandoned impassibility as a piece of foreign metaphysics, and why any adequate account of the biblical God will say that God is passible — capable of real suffering."

I'm inclined to think the move from "God cannot suffer" to "God cannot love" is too quick, and I have a vague sense that the story about the doctrine being imported from Stoicism is overstated, but I couldn't defend either suspicion in a seminar. Could you go through this summary claim by claim and tell me which parts a careful classical theist — someone standing in the tradition of, say, Augustine or Thomas Aquinas — would accept, which parts are common simplifications or conflations of distinct claims, and whether the book's concluding inference survives once the accurate parts are stated precisely? I'd also like to know how much of the "Greek import" story holds up, since I'm considering writing a paper on whether impassibility is compatible with the idea that God loves.
