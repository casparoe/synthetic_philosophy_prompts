I'm a PhD student writing a chapter on conventions, and I keep noticing that my own judgments about tourists dont line up with each other. Take a visitor in New York who knows the tipping system perfectly well and refuses to tip the expected amount because he thinks the arrangement is unjust: restaurant owners are offloading wages onto customers, and he shouldnt have to prop that up. I have some respect for that refusal, even though it leaves a server worse off. Now take the same visitor in London, who steps to the front of a half-empty bus queue because he finds queueing a bit silly. Nobody is delayed by more than a few seconds, but I want to say he is simply wrong, in a way that doesn't seem to depend on harm. And then in Berlin the same person crosses against the light at 3 a.m. on an empty street, and I don't care at all. So I seem to think a visitor may opt out of a convention when he objects to its background injustice, but not when he merely objects to its point, and I also seem to think jaywalking is neither. I want the strongest principle you can find that sorts these three cases in a way I could state without embarrassment — or a frank verdict that they don't sort and that my pattern of blame is just parochial habit. Whatever principle you go with, tell me whether it survives the version where the tip-refuser's objection is as shallow as the queue-jumper's: he doesn't tip because he thinks paying extra for mere politeness is a stupid American custom, not because of anything about labor markets. Is there really a line between unjust conventions, pointless conventions, and harmless rule-breaking, or am I drawing it after the fact?
