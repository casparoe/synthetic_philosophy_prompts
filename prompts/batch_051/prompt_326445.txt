We admire the person who, knowing she will later want to break a considered resolution, binds herself in advance: Ulysses lashed to the mast, the gambler who self-excludes from the casino, the patient who signs an advance directive while still competent. We describe these as ways of exercising autonomy, not surrendering it. But when a political community does the same sort of thing—entrenches rights, requires supermajorities, or makes constitutional rules difficult for future majorities to change—many of the same people call it a dead hand on democracy and an assault on self-government. In both cases, a present self imposes a constraint on a future self whose judgment it predicts will be distorted by temptation, fear, or faction. Is there a principled asymmetry between the two cases, or does consistency force us either to accept constitutional precommitment as a legitimate form of collective freedom or to condemn Ulysses contracts as self-betrayal?
