I'm a PhD student in political theory, mostly early modern political thought and legal personality, and I keep half-using a distinction in seminar that nobody has told me is stupid, which makes me suspicious. I call it the 'orphan charter' view of the state. The claim is: a chartered company and a state are not two different kinds of thing; they are the same kind of thing at two different points in a charter's life. The VOC or the East India Company by the late eighteenth century had private armies, collected taxes, ran courts, made treaties, and held territory—by 1780 in Bengal you'd be hard pressed to say which sovereign powers they still lacked. Yet everyone still called them companies. On my view the only thing that made them companies was that their constituting instrument had a named grantor (the Crown, Parliament) who could amend or revoke it, and the instrument expired. A state, then, is a corporation whose charter has outlived its grantor and lost its expiry date. The charter was never revoked; it got orphaned. Sovereignty is not a special kind of authority at all—it's a corporate charter with no living issuer and no sunset. Constitutions, on this view, are just charters with the grantor line left blank. Revolutions write constitutions rather than petitions because you can't ask for a new charter from the person you are overthrowing; you issue one that names no grantor. And citizenship starts to look like what shareholding becomes when the shares can't be sold and the corporation can't be wound up.

I know this is probably Hart's rule of recognition in fancy dress, or Hobbes's artificial person, or something Maitland already said about the Crown, and I want to know how embarassingly close I am to an actual position. So: is this a coherent theory or just a metaphor? What does it get right? Where does it break—what does it wrongly count as a state, or fail to count? Give me the strongest objections, and say whether any of them is fatal rather than uncomfortable.
