A member of my reading group has argued that the standard historian's injunction against anachronism, if taken seriously, commits us to a conclusion none of us wants. I cannot find the faulty step. The argument runs:

(1) In reading past science, we should not attribute to a past author a claim couched in concepts unavailable to them. We should reconstruct the claim as they could have meant it, against their own conceptual background.

(2) So when a text of Aristotle says heavier bodies fall faster than lighter ones, we should not treat it as asserting a Galilean or Newtonian proposition about gravitational acceleration. We should treat it as asserting something about natural motion toward the centre in a cosmos divided into earth, water, air and fire.

(3) Having reconstructed the claim that way, to ask whether Aristotle was right is to ask whether that reconstructed claim is true.

(4) But the truth of that reconstructed claim is answerable only within the conceptual scheme from which it came. By our standards it is not so much false as not straightforwardly translatable, and by his own lights it was well supported.

(5) Therefore Aristotle was not wrong about falling bodies. He was talking about something different, and later mechanics did not refute him but replaced one framework with another.

(6) The same reasoning applies across the board: to phlogiston, caloric, the celestial spheres, and every other discarded doctrine. On this position phlogiston chemistry was not false; it carved the phenomena in a different way.

(7) But that cannot be right. Some past scientific doctrines really were wrong, not merely different. That is part of why the history of science matters and why we now do chemistry and physics differently.

So the injunction against anachronism seems to prove too much: it protects past authors from error only by giving up the idea that science corrected itself. I would like to keep step (1), understood as a principle of historical reading, and I also want to keep step (7). Something between them has to bend. Which step do you reject or revise, and what does rejecting or revising it cost? In particular, say whether the argument's mistake, if there is one, lies in its account of meaning, its account of truth, or its account of what historical reading requires.
