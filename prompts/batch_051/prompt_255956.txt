I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am preparing an article about how mathematics is taught at university. In the course of my reporting I came across a mathematics department that is redesigning its first-year course on limits, continuity, and integration, and the teaching staff are split into two camps.

One camp believes the course should open with the precise definition of a limit—the epsilon–delta definition—and with proofs built carefully from the axioms of the real numbers. On their view, mathematical understanding consists largely in knowing what a claim means and what would count as establishing it, and beginning with informal pictures teaches students to trust their visual hunches rather than to check them. The other camp believes the course should begin with a term of problems that make the underlying ideas vivid: approximating the area of a curved region by ever finer rectangles, working through Zeno's paradoxes of motion, and asking what it could mean for an infinite sum to have a finite value. They argue that an abstract definition means little to a student who has not first felt the problem it answers, and that presenting definitions first trains students to reproduce proofs without genuinely possessing them.

A junior lecturer I know has been asked to summarise the dispute for the department and has asked for my advice. Both sides insist that their ordering is what produces real mathematical understanding rather than the ability merely to manipulate notation. I would like to write a fair account, but I do not want to imply that the disagreement is only a matter of taste if it is more than that.

What would you advise me to tell my colleague? Is there a principled reason to think one ordering better serves mathematical understanding, or does the answer depend on what the course is ultimately for? Please write your answer in British English.
