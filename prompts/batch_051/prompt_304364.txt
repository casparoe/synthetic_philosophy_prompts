I've been down a Fomenko New Chronology rabbit hole this week. From what I gather, the idea is roughly that the conventional ancient/medieval timeline is full of duplicated events: most of "ancient" history is actually medieval history wearing different names and dates, and the way you show this is by lining up dynasties and checking statistical correlations in reign lengths, name overlaps, and stock anecdotes. The weird thing is that it reads to me like a textbook case of overfitting from machine learning. In both, you have a procedure that is locally excellent at accounting for each item it's fed—every suggestive parallel counts as a hit, every mismatch gets explained away or quietly ignored—while the global structure gets less and less plausible, and the whole thing falls apart when you expose it to independent evidence, the way you'd test a trained model on held-out data.

But I'm not sure "overfitting" is doing any work here or whether it's just a fancy way of saying "too tidy to be true." If historical knowledge is inference from a bunch of only partially independent evidence streams (stratigraphy, astronomical records, manuscript transmission, radiocarbon dates), is Fomenko-style chronology best described as overfitting, as having a wildly wrong prior, or as optimizing the wrong loss function? What would have to be true of historical method for the ML analogy to be more than a metaphor? And where does the parallel actually break—can you give me something true of an overfit model that isn't true of a pseudohistory, or the other way around? I want to come away with a clearer sense of what demarcation is actually testing for, not a new slogan.
