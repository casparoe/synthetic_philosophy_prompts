I've worked in finfish aquaculture for about a decade, but I recently got put on our company's welfare committee and now I'm running into a pattern I don't have a word for. The closer an animal is to a familiar vertebrate, the more protection people seem to assume it needs: salmon get dissolved-oxygen targets, humane slaughter rules, the works. But as you move down the tree, the default flips, and crabs and shrimp have almost no one arguing for them, even though the physiological evidence doesn't drop off at any clean line. The odd bit is that the line keeps moving. Someone will boil a crab alive without thinking twice, but when a study comes out showing crabs avoid a chamber where they were shocked before, the same person doesn't say "ok, maybe crabs count." They just move the cutoff: "but do they have a cortex?" And if a later study suggests shrimp will give up food to avoid a shock, the line moves again. I honestly can't tell whether the cutoff is tracking the evidence or just tracking where it would get expensive to admit the animal can suffer — and for us, the expensive admission is crustaceans, since we'd have to redo our handling and slaughter. I know it's not exactly moving the goalposts, because the goalposts here are the boundary of "can suffer," and they seem to move in one direction only, toward whatever would cost us the most. Is there a name for this particular thing — the continually retreating cutoff for "probably can't suffer," where each new group that gets hard to dismiss is met with a new requirement, so the evidence never quite catches up? If there's an established term, I'd like to know how it differs from plain speciesism, motivated reasoning, and the precautionary principle. If there isn't one, I'd want a term coined with a definition and a few boundary cases. I'm not satisfied with just a label; I want to know exactly what the phenomenon is and isnt, and where it shades into those neighbors.
