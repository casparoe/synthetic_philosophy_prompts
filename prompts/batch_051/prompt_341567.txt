Retired physical chemist here; i've only been reading philosophy seriously since I stopped working, and Heidegger's stuff about tools has stuck with me. The idea that a hammer doesn't show up as an object while you're using it, that it withdraws into the task, and that the whole workshop only becomes visible when something breaks — I can't stop applying it to the CrowdStrike mess on July 19, 2024. If you don't know the IT side: CrowdStrike pushed out a bad configuration update for its Falcon sensor, roughly 8.5 million Windows machines blue-screened or went into bootloops, airlines and hospitals and emergency services got knocked sideways, and the fix partly came down to sysadmins manually booting machines into safe mode and deleting one file. Not an attack, just a bad update.

So here's what I want. Treat the CrowdStrike outage as a case study in readiness-to-hand and the breakdown of equipment, and run the analysis in both directions. First, do the mapping carefully and separate the levels. What exactly is the 'equipment' here — the laptop, the Falcon sensor, the check-in kiosk, or the airline's whole operational system? And who is the 'user' whose world gets disrupted: the gate agent, the passenger, the sysadmin, the airline? I think the analysis changes a lot depending on the level, so don't just merge them. Second, say what the theory gets right about the episode. I imagine thousands of people suddenly staring at infrastructure they'd never noticed, and a whole hidden network of tools becoming visible as a broken totality. Does readiness-to-hand actually predict that, and does it explain what people found so unsettling? Third, say where it misses or misdescribes the case. A bootlooping laptop doesn't reveal itself the way a snapped hammer handle does; it just refuses to boot and gives an error code. And the people who understand what failed are often far away, reading a vendor bulletin. So maybe this is breakdown without disclosure, or disclosure only for a different class of user. Fourth, flip it around: what does the case do to Heidegger's story? Does it confirm that breakdown reveals the world, or does it show that this only works in a small, transparent workshop where someone can pick up the broken tool and understand it — and that the account gets nostalgic fast when the broken tool is a remote kernel driver? Finally, be candid about your own read: tell me where your analysis is most likely to fail, and what argument or alternative reading of readiness-to-hand would change your verdict.
