I've come to hold the position that language is best defined by modality-neutral structural properties—duality of patterning, productivity, hierarchical syntax, conventionality—and that the vocal–auditory channel is a contingent feature of attested languages rather than part of the definition. On this view, signed languages are full natural languages, and any definition of language that requires sound or linear speech is false, not merely parochial. Tell me what would count against this position. Look past the stock objection that sign is elaborate pantomime, and consider homesign and village sign languages, iconicity in signed lexicons, supposed differences in neural processing and acquisition, and the greater simultaneity in sign morphology. Which of these would a defender of the modality-neutral view concede as counting against it, and which would they dismiss as facts about the channel rather than the language faculty? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say what the view predicts that a speech-based rival does not, and give an honest verdict on whether the view is exposed enough to be falsifiable or insulated enough to be untestable. End the answer with a last line of the form Verdict: [short label for how exposed the position is].
