I am a law lecturer preparing a workshop paper for a faculty seminar at a U.S. law school, and I want to walk in ready for a difficult question period. The paper defends a limited judicial doctrine of desuetude in the criminal law: where a criminal prohibition has gone entirely unenforced for a substantial period, and the underlying conduct is now treated as morally indifferent in the relevant community, a court should presume the statute unenforceable unless the legislature expressly reauthorizes it. My argument is that dead-letter statutes are not harmless. They enable selective prosecution, chill conduct that everyone in practice tolerates, and open a gap between the law as written and the law as lived that damages the rule of law itself. Fair notice, which my audience accepts as a starting point, cannot be satisfied by a law that no reasonable person expects to be applied to them; legality should, within strict limits, track the public's settled understanding of what is actually prohibited. I am careful to defend only a rebuttable presumption that shifts the burden back to the legislature, not a judicial power to repeal or to block a newly reauthorized enforcement effort.

The audience will be mostly public law theorists and legislation scholars whose work centers on legislative supremacy, the separation of powers, the canons of statutory interpretation, and the formal requirements of legality. Several are textualists who hold that the only legitimate ways for a statute to die are repeal or sunset; a few are criminal law scholars who have argued that the proper cure for uneven enforcement is transparent prosecutorial guidelines and political accountability, not a change in the law's validity. They will tend to treat non-enforcement as an executive-policy problem, and they will be suspicious of any doctrine that lets judges make legal validity turn on shifting social practice rather than enacted text. I need to know what they will actually say to me.

Prepare me a question-period prep sheet, not a restatement of the paper. Give me the objections I am most likely to hear, ordered by a rough combination of likelihood and damage, and phrase each one the way this specific audience would phrase it. For each objection, give a short reply I can deliver in the available time and a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me which objection I should concede, or narrow my thesis to accommodate, up front, and which one I should not concede even if it sounds reasonable. Finally, tell me the question I should be quietly hoping nobody asks, and how to answer it if someone does.
