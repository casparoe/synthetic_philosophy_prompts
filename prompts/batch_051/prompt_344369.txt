I am a retired evolutionary biologist, and since leaving the bench I have been reading the history and sociology of science. A debate I keep meeting, in forms ranging from careful to careless, is whether scientific discoveries are inevitable and whether it matters who made them. The standard exhibits are the near-simultaneous formulation of natural selection by Darwin and Wallace and of the calculus by Newton and Leibniz. As best I can reconstruct it, the debate contains at least three positions.

The first position holds that a discovery is the product of its intellectual and material environment: prior results, methods, instruments, shared anomalies, and the pressure of open problems. Once those ingredients are assembled, the discovery is overdetermined; the individual discoverer is a vehicle for an event that was going to happen in roughly that form at roughly that time. Darwin and Wallace are two independent products of the same causal brew, and the question of who first got there is a lottery at the level of persons but a near-necessity at the level of the system.

The second position denies that this survives contact with the details. What Darwin produced was not merely the abstract mechanism but a mass of evidence, argument, and synthesis built over decades, and it was that mass that moved the field; Wallace arrived at the mechanism without that weight, which is precisely why the two cases are not interchangeable. Newton and Leibniz did not produce the same discovery at all but two distinct formalisms with different notations, conceptual commitments, and downstream trajectories. On this view, the individual shapes the content, form, and reception of a discovery, the counterfactual that someone else would have made it is untestable, and the examples of multiples are half-remembered against a background of singletons that never get counted.

The third position tries to divide the question. It concedes that the content of a discovery may be overdetermined while still insisting that priority is not a vain social overlay: priority systems determine which version of an idea becomes canonical, who receives resources, and which problems are pursued, so priority matters even when inevitability is true. The two claims operate on different levels and need not compete.

I want you to restate these positions in the vocabulary I actually think in. Treat me as fluent in adaptation and selection pressures; fitness landscapes, local optima, and peak shifts; convergent and parallel evolution; homology and homoplasy; exaptation; path dependence, contingency, and constraint; drift; and the distinction between a trait whose evolution is highly probable under a given selective regime and a trait that is a contingent singularity.

Do not merely draw an analogy. Translate each position into explicit claims about what corresponds to a trait or a lineage, what corresponds to the niche or fitness landscape, what a simultaneous discovery corresponds to, and what priority corresponds to, if anything. Then give me the residue report: which claims translate cleanly, which claims require stretching the evolutionary vocabulary beyond its proper use, and which claims have no counterpart at all. In particular, I want to know whether the claim that a discovery was inevitable amounts to anything more than the claim that a trait is likely to evolve when a niche is occupied, and whether that latter claim is testable or is just the original historical claim reworded. And I want your honest verdict on the untranslatable remainder: is it something my evolutionary vocabulary is missing, or is it something the original debate imports — credit, institutions, counterfactual necessity — that has been doing the real work all along?
