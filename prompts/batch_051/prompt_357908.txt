I'm a software engineer with no formal background in this area, so I keep reaching for implementation language that may be doing more work than I realize. A friend walked me through the following argument and I can't locate the step that bothers me.

(1) In the standard set-theoretic foundation for arithmetic, the natural numbers are encoded as particular sets: 0 is ∅, 1 is {∅}, 2 is {∅, {∅}}, and 4 is {0, 1, 2, 3}. (2) On that encoding, 2 is one of the elements of the collection that is 4, so 2 ∈ 4 is a theorem. (3) If 2 ∈ 4 is a theorem of a correct foundation, then 2 really is that set, and membership between 2 and 4 captures a genuine property of the numbers themselves. (4) But there are equally workable encodings — for instance, the one where each number is the singleton of its predecessor, so that 2 is {{∅}} — and under that encoding 2 is not a member of 4. (5) So 2 ∈ 4 comes out true under one equally legitimate encoding and false under another, yet whether 2 is in 4 should not depend on an arbitrary choice of representation. (6) Therefore the standard encoding does more than define: it settles by fiat a question that has no determinate answer, and treating 2 ∈ 4 as simply true proves too much. (7) But if I reject the standard encoding, I seem to lose the reduction of arithmetic to set theory that gives numbers their foundation at all.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I'd most like to keep the implementation-independence intuition behind (5), but I can't tell how much of the standard set-theoretic account I have to give up in order to do so.
