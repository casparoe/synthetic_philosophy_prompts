Ok so I keep coming back to imaginative resistance — how people find it way harder to go along with a fiction that asks them to imagine something morally awful is fine (like a story where torturing children is presented as just another good thing) than with a fiction that asks them to imagine something merely physically impossible. And I happened on this bit from Hume's Of the Standard of Taste:

The merit of delivering true general precepts in ethics is indeed very small. Whoever recommends any moral virtues, really does no more than is implied in the terms themselves. That people, who invented the word charity, and used it in a good sense, inculcated more clearly and much more efficaciously, the precept, be charitable, than any pretended legislator or prophet, who should insert such a maxim in his writings. Of all expressions, those, which, together with their other meaning, imply a degree either of blame or approbation, are the least liable to be perverted or mistaken.

It struck me that Hume is basically describing the same machinery from the other end: the evaluative charge is built into the word itself, not added by the sentence. So when a fiction tries to make "virtue" mean something different, or asks us to imagine a world where cruelty just is what "justice" means, the resistance isn't really about the act of imagining — it's about trying to use a word that carries its own approval as if it were evaluatively neutral. Is this parallel deep or just superficial? In particular, does Hume's claim about moral terms help explain the asymmetry in imaginative resistance (that we can easily imagine cats that talk but not that murder is right)? And where does the parallel actually come apart — what would a fiction have to do to make Hume's point irrelevant to the resistance people feel?
