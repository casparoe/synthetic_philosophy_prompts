I co-teach a small philosophy of science seminar, twelve students, one 90-minute session, and we spend a week on Bell's theorem. The students are sharp but most of them know Bell from pop-science accounts, so they can say hidden variables and nonlocality without being able to state what the theorem actually rules out. I'd like an activity where, inside the session, they try to be a local hidden-variable set-up and fail in a way they can feel. The centerpiece I'm picturing is the two-player guessing game sometimes called the CHSH game: Alice and Bob can agree on any strategy they like before being separated; then each is handed a bit, x for Alice and y for Bob; each must output a bit, a and b; the pair scores when a XOR b equals x AND y. No communication after separation, but any amount of shared pre-agreed rules or lookup tables is allowed. I want the activity designed so that students, by proposing strategies and having a referee score many rounds, crash into the fact that no deterministic pre-agreed scheme does better than 75 percent, and that randomization before the round doesn't beat that either. Only after they hit that wall does the tutor reveal that a shared pair of entangled qubits can win about 85 percent of the time, and that this gap is the force of Bell's theorem.

Give me runnable rules, materials that don't require actual entangled photons (numbered cards are fine), a scoring scheme that doesn't use real money, a minute-by-minute timeline for 90 minutes, and a debrief script that maps the 75 percent bound onto the idea of a local pre-existing-answer strategy, while making clear what the game is not a bound on (flat-out determinism? signaling? superdeterminism?). Also tell me what predictably goes wrong first time out: I can already see someone suggesting Alice and Bob share a stream of correlated random numbers, and another person treating beating other teams as the point instead of beating 75 percent. Be honest about one feature the activity can't reproduce: the game makes Bell's theorem look like a coordination or programming limitation, whereas the real sting is that quantum mechanics actually reaches the 85 percent value in experiments. Am I over-specifying this, or does it still leave enough for the students to discover on their own?
