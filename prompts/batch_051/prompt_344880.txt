Question (20 marks):

Wang Yangming holds that knowledge and action form a unity (zhixing heyi). Explain how he would respond to the apparent counterexample of a person who sincerely says that they know filial piety requires caring for one's parents, yet never cares for them. Compare his position with (a) the Zhu Xi-style view that one first completes knowledge and only then acts, and (b) Socratic intellectualism. Finally, state whether the unity thesis is best restricted to some kinds of knowledge or applied to all knowledge, and why.

Student's answer:

Wang's thesis is aimed at the stepwise picture he associates with Zhu Xi, on which a learner first investigates things and extends knowledge, and only later converts that knowledge into action. Wang takes this picture to license delay: people excuse themselves from acting because their learning is not yet complete. Against it, he argues that knowing and acting are two descriptions of one reality, not consecutive stages. A person who can recite the requirements of filial piety but has never attended to their parents does not genuinely know filiality; what they have is verbal or hearsay knowledge. The example is therefore not a counterexample to the unity thesis, but a case of incomplete knowledge presenting itself as complete.

Because genuine knowledge already contains the act, Wang is, in effect, a Socratic intellectualist: he holds that no one who truly knows the good will fail to do it, and hence weakness of will is an illusion. His only real departure from Socrates is that he develops the point inside the Confucian program of self-cultivation rather than through Socratic examination.

Wang also keeps the Great Learning's program of extending knowledge through the investigation of things, and he reads it in line with Zhu Xi as an investigation of the principles in things; his distinctive addition is only the insistence that the investigation be carried out with a sincere will, so that the unity shows up first at the level of the will.

The doctrine is most defensible if it is restricted to self-involving, practical knowledge — knowledge of what one's own situation calls for. Wang's examples are of this sort, and it is not clear that the thesis needs to cover merely descriptive or theoretical knowledge.
