I have been defending retributivism against consequentialist theories of punishment, but the following argument has me cornered, and I cannot tell which step to give up.

(1) Punishment is retributively justified only to the extent that the offender deserves it for a moral wrong.
(2) What a person deserves cannot depend on facts outside their control. Once we hold fixed what the person chose — what they intended, knew, and willed in defiance of another's rights — the chance results of that choice cannot change how much punishment they deserve.
(3) So a shooter whose bullet grazes the victim and a shooter whose bullet kills are equally deserving: attempt and completion are morally on a par.
(4) But the line between attempt and mere preparation, and the line between preparation and a fully formed intention, is also drawn by features outside the agent's control in the same way: a locked door, a passerby, a message that interrupts before the final step. If (2) is right, how far the world happened to let the plan proceed cannot matter to desert.
(5) So the retributive principle seems to require punishing a settled, concrete intention to kill just as severely as a completed killing.
(6) The only principled place to stop that conclusion is an act requirement. Yet once quality of will has been admitted to be the whole ground of desert, the act requirement itself needs a different justification — reliable proof of resolve, public offense, deterrence, avoiding punishment of the harmless — and those justifications look consequentialist rather than retributive.
(7) Therefore either retributivism is committed to punishing bare intentions, or it must borrow its stopping point from the very consequentialist considerations it claims to reject.

I want to keep both the no-luck principle in (2) and the act requirement. Which step in the argument do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
