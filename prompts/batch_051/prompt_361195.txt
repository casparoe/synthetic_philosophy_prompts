I'm thouroughly stuck on the best woman's speech I'm meant to give at my friend's wedding on Saturday, and it's because of an argument I got into last night with my flatmate about Émilie du Châtelet's Discourse on Happiness (the little essay sometimes titled Discours sur le bonheur or Réflexions sur le bonheur). I'd been reading the bit where she says:

"Il faut pour être heureux, s’être défait des préjugés ; être vertueux ; se bien porter ; avoir des goûts et des passions ; être susceptible d’illusion ; car nous devons la plupart de nos plaisirs à l’illusion, et malheureux est celui qui la perd."

I kept coming back to the claim that most of our pleasures come from illusion, and that the unhappy person is the one who loses it. My flatmate said I was reading it too crudely, and that there's a difference between being susceptible to illusion and being soft-headed; she thought du Châtelet was saying we should deliberately learn to keep the varnish on things, not that we should be passive dupes. We went back and forth for ages about whether a rational person can honestly choose to protect an illusion, and it ended with her saying, fine, then try putting that into a wedding toast without making the bride sound deluded.

So that's exactly the speech I need. The occasion is a wedding of about eighty people: parents, grandparents, the groom's teenage kids, plus a handful of our philosophy friends. The couple aren't cynical, but they've both said in private that part of promising "forever" is deciding to believe certain things that the evidence can't fully underwrite. I don't want to talk down to anyone, and I don't want a generic toast. Please write me a five-minute best woman's speech that uses du Châtelet's point about illusion and happiness as the actual spine of the speech, lands warmly, and doesn't insult the couple or reduce their love to a self-deception.
