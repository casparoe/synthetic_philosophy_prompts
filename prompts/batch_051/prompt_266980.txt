I am a third-year undergraduate in a seminar on Rousseau and the moral critique of the arts, and we have been reading his Letter to M. d’Alembert on the Theatre. I keep noticing a parallel I cannot settle, and I would like an evaluation of it. As I understand Rousseau’s position, the problem with theater is not merely that particular plays depict vice sympathetically, but that the theatrical situation itself makes moral improvement impossible. The spectator is placed in a relation of emotion without practical involvement: compassion, admiration, and indignation are excited in order to be consumed as aesthetic experience, while the spectator’s practical character remains unaffected. In his discussion of Molière’s Le Misanthrope, Rousseau seems to argue that the comic form does not punish vice but instead makes Alceste’s uncompromising virtue look ridiculous while making the sociable deceivers appear reasonable and likable. The audience is thus trained to enjoy its own tolerance for polished insincerity rather than to become more virtuous.

This looks to me structurally identical to something in contemporary digital life. On social media, expressing outrage at a distant injustice, sharing a first-person testimony, or posting a statement of solidarity is often described as activism. But much of this appears to function as emotional display addressed to an audience, where the performance of moral feeling substitutes for the more costly practical actions that would actually respond to the wrong. The platform, like the theater, seems to place users in the position of spectators to their own and others’ displayed virtue. It rewards a moving self-presentation rather than a stable practical commitment, and it makes moral emotion something to be performed, received, and consumed. And just as Rousseau thought the theater’s failure was intrinsic to spectatorship and mimesis rather than a matter of bad playwrights, I suspect the moral failure of social media is intrinsic to its structure of profile-based identity and audience feedback rather than to the laziness of individual users.

I am trying to decide whether this is a genuine structural identity, a loose analogy, or a conceptual confusion. Please assess the parallel in a formal way. State the proposed mapping precisely, say what would need to be true on both sides for Rousseau’s conclusions to transfer to social media, and identify at least one disanalogy that the parallel predicts away but that is actually important. End with a one-sentence summary of your verdict as the last line.
