Since I retired from the lab I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group, and last week a friend presented an argument that has been keeping me up. It starts from something I recognize from laboratory life: a group of people, each with a private and imperfect sense of whether some action is worth doing, where any one of them is allowed to act alone—publish the risky result, ship the strain, start the petition. Suppose the action is in fact harmful, and each member has a small independent chance q of mistaking it for acceptable. The chance that none of them makes this mistake is (1-q)^N, which collapses toward zero as N grows; with a big enough group, someone is almost sure to say yes. If that person can act unilaterally, the harmful thing happens. So far I follow him.

Then he pushed it, and now I can't find the exit. He set it out like this:

(1) For any action a large enough group could take, it is almost certain that at least one member judges the action net-positive, whether or not it really is.
(2) If the group's norms let any member act unilaterally on that judgement, then for each such action, someone almost certainly performs it.
(3) So a large group with decentralized decision rights will, with near certainty, perform every harmful action available to it.
(4) Therefore the only rational rule for a large group is to require something close to unanimous agreement before anyone can act.
(5) But near-unanimity is a non-starter: one cautious holdout can block a good action, and you can't even get a ten-person commitee to agree unanimously that a hallway should be repainted.
(6) So the demand that single actors defer to group judgement leads to the absurd conclusion that nobody in a large group may ever act on their own judgement.

I want to keep (4)—I spent too many years around biosafety committees to trust one person's private call—but I worry keeping it costs me (6). He says the conclusion is ridiculous, so something has to go; I just can't see which step. Which step goes wrong, if any? If instead the argument is valid, which premise is least costly to give up? And for whichever move you recommend, say what it gives up or buys.
