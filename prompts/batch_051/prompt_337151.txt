Last night I got into a long argument with a friend over a method our consciousness professor spent two lectures defending. The professor says the science of consciousness has a deep problem: all we ever have from the outside is what subjects say—there's a red stripe moving left, the pain is in my hand. So, he says, the only honest move is to treat those verbal reports the way a literary critic treats a novel: you don't ask whether the narrator's claims are true of the real world, you ask what the world is according to the narrator, and you describe that world as carefully as you can. He calls this heterophenomenology. The scientist is the critic, the subject is the author of a fictional text, and the subject's conscious experience is the world of the story. And he used that analogy to argue that the method is completely neutral—it doesn't smuggle in dualism or physicalism—and that it's the one safe starting point for a science of consciousness.

My friend found this infuriating. He said the analogy is doing all the work and doing it illegitimately: a novel's narrator is an invented part of the story, but a lab subject is a real reporting system whose reports are caused by actual perceptual machinery, and calling the report fiction quietly builds in the conclusion that there is no first-person fact of the matter beyond the text. I sort of see both sides and I cant figure out where the analogy actually breaks. Could you walk through what maps onto what in the fiction analogy, which features of the literary case it quietly imports that the lab case lacks, which disanalogies matter for the professor's neutrality claim and which are just superficial, and whether the claim that heterophenomenology is a neutral starting point survives once the disanalogies are made explicit?
