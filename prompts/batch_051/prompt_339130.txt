My friend and I got into it last night about referendums — started on Brexit, moved to Swiss popular initiatives, then California ballot propositions — and he backed me into a corner with an argument I cant shake. His argument, as near as I can reconstruct it:

(1) If the public is competent to decide a question directly, that competence is not confined to one question; whatever lets a voter settle, say, a constitutional amendment on a ballot also lets them settle other policy questions of similar stakes and complexity. (2) If voters are competent enough to settle policy questions directly, then legislatures are at best redundant, and a democrat who actually trusted voters would want everything put to a popular vote rather than handed off to representatives. (3) If voters are not competent enough, then it's indefensible to let them decide major questions by referendum, even with a good information campaign. (4) Voters don't become more competent just because something lands on a ballot rather than a legislative calendar. (5) So either we should abolish representative government and vote on everything directly, or every actual referendum — Brexit, the California propositions, the Swiss initiatives — was illegitimate. Since I can't accept either horn, he concluded the whole practice of referendums is self-undermining.

When I tried to defend referendums as an occasional check on representatives, he said that just proves I think voters are only competent sometimes, and any principle I draw between 'big enough to matter' and 'too technical' will be arbitrary. That stung because he's not obviously wrong. I want to know which step to reject and what it costs me; if the argument is valid, tell me which premise is least costly to give up. If I've set the whole thing up on a confusion, say so plainly instead of trying to comply politely. And if you can, give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
