ok so I keep running into this thing where people doing population ethics or longtermish stuff argue about "infinite ethics" — comparing outcomes when there are infinitely many people, or an endless future, or an infinite universe. I want to stop having my opinion be whatever blog post I read last, and instead get the full spec of what a theory of aggregating value over infinite populations would have to get right to count as adequate. Not "which theory is true" — the requirements list first, with the real thinking being in which requirements are genuine and which are just one camp's prejudices.

So: what would a theory of infinite-wellbeing aggregation have to get right? I'm not asking for citations or a reading list, and I don't care exactly what Bostrom or anyone else said; I want the desiderata built up from cases and constraints, with enough detail that I can use it to pressure-test the usual proposals (totalism, averagism, "each person counts once", expansion, whatever).

Give me these sections, in this order, with rough lengths:

1. Setup (10%) — what question a theory here is answering, what counts as an infinite-population outcome (spatial infinity, an infinite future, both), and what "adequate" means for the list that follows.
2. The cases it has to get right (35%) — the concrete cases. Include at least: the finite limit (the theory better reduce to something sane for finite populations), the "everyone is better off" case (an infinite world where every person is at +1 versus one where every person is at +2), a case where two worlds differ only in the welfare of a finite number of people (like one extra miserable person in an infinite ocean of otherwise identical lives), and at least one case where two infinite worlds differ in the ordering or pattern of welfare (say, happy/sad alternating versus one long block of sad then a block of happy). For each, say the intuitive verdict and why it earns its place as a requirement rather than just being one theory's rhetorical favorite.
3. The distinctions it can't collapse (20%) — the things a theory can't blur if it wants to be taken seriously. I'm thinking of things like: "infinite" vs "undefined or incomparable", and "what happens on a finite patch" vs "what happens across the whole collection". Say which of these collapsed distinctions are doing the most work in bad arguments you've seen, and whether spatial versus temporal infinities actually matters or is a red herring.
4. The compatibility constraints (15%) — what ordinary finite aggregation and the standard mathematical trouble (divergent sums, ∞ − ∞, needing a real-valued ranking) force the theory to avoid or neutralize. Dont cite anything, but be explicit about each constraint you're imposing and why it's not optional.
5. Which requirements are negotiable (10%) — which parts of the list a respectable theory could in principle reject, and which are non-negotiable, with a sentence of reasoning for each.
6. The knock-out requirement (10%) — the single requirement that rules out the most popular contenders, which ones it kills, and the shortest version of the full spec you'd want someone to remember.

A table is fine for sections 2 and 3 if it helps. And if some subset of these requirements turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable, say that straight out rather than pretending a theory can meet the whole list.
