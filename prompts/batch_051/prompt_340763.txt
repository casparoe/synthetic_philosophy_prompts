I'm working on a novel set in a small fictional republic, and the constitutional crisis at the middle of the book has to actually hold together or the whole ending wobbles. I wrote a constitution for the place, and the relevant bit is:

"The term of the President shall begin at noon on the fifteenth of March next following the election. If the President-elect shall die before taking office, the Vice President-elect shall be sworn as President. If the office of President becomes vacant by death, resignation, or removal, the Vice President shall succeed to the office for the remainder of the term."

The scenario I'm stuck on: two days before the inauguration, the President-elect is in a bad car crash. He isn't dead — he's in an induced coma, and the doctors say he's likely to survive but may not regain consciousness for months, maybe never. March fifteenth arrives. The outgoing president's term expires at noon, and the outgoing vice president's term expires at the same moment. Who is president at 12:01?

My instinct was that the VP-elect just gets sworn in, but the text only hands the job to the VP-elect if the President-elect dies, and the last sentence lets the sitting Vice President succeed only when the office of the President becomes vacant — except the office here wasn't vacated, it was never occupied. And the constitution never defines incapacity at all, and even if it did, that language seems to attach only to a sitting president, not a president-elect.

So, bracket the novel for a second: whether its legally coherent, whether there's actually a gap at noon, and how it should be resolved. I want you to argue it from the plain words, from the purpose or structure of the succession scheme, from what the drafters would probably have said if you'd cornered them, and from what a reasonable reader at the founding would have taken the text to mean. Then tell me which approach should win here and why. I care less about a tidy answer than about which interpretive move is really doing the work.
