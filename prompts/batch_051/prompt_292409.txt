Been chewing on this for a group chat that's half rationalist-blog alumni and half film people, and I want a take that doesn't talk down to either crowd. I want to read Arrival (Denis Villeneuve's film, from Ted Chiang's Story of Your Life) through Kant's distinction between intuitions and concepts. The Kant part, roughly: intuitions are the receptive side that gives us objects, and for finite beings like us space and time are the a priori forms of intuition; concepts are what the understanding uses to think those objects. His slogan runs something like: thoughts without content are empty, and intuitions without concepts are blind (I'm paraphrasing from memory, so treat that loosely). In the film, the heptapod written langauge is nonlinear — an utterance shows up as one symbol without a direction in time — and Louise's fluency in it seems to change her experience so she can see her own future the way we recall a past. That looks like a language rewiring either her concepts or the form of her intuition, and I honestly can't tell which one the film actually commits to, or whether Kant can even allow it.

So the ask: write me a loose-but-serious reading that stays scene-level where you can and concept-level throughout. Where does the film actually play nice with the Kant picture (e.g., acquiring the heptapod script could be read as gaining new concepts, or new ways of applying old concepts, rather than touching intuition itself)? Where does it force a choice Kant doesn't let you make — on the Transcendental Aesthetic the temporal form of inner intuition is prior to empirical learning, so no amount of language exposure should let a finite discursive mind start experiencing time non-sequentially? Is the most charitable reading that the heptapods have a different form of intuition and Louise maps her old concepts onto it (which sounds like something Kant would call a category mistake), or is the film effectively handing Louise intellectual intuition, which Kant reserves for a non-discursive intellect and not for us? And if the most honest answer is that Villeneuve and Chiang are dramatizing exactly the move Kant rules out, that's fine — just say where you're fudging the philosophy to save the film or fudging the film to save the philosophy, and what the fiction adds to the abstract distinction for someone who thinks the transcendental machinery mostly doesn't cash out in experience.
