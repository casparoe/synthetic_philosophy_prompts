I've been arguing with a friend about an AI-safety scenario, and I think our disagreement is really a conceptual one. The setup: a superintelligent AI that is far better than any human at constructing arguments, but it's constrained to never deliberately lie or misstate a fact. It can still choose what to emphasize, how to frame things, which analogies to use, which facts to leave out, and what tone to take. So the worry isn't deception—it's that the AI could talk a human into almost anything, not by tricking them but by giving them reasons that are just better than any they'd have produced for themselves.

What I'm trying to get clear on is whether there's a principled difference between rational persuasion and manipulation when the speaker is massively smarter than the listner and the listener knows it. The usual accounts of manipulation point to exploiting known cognitive biases or bypassing someone's reasoning entirely, but this case seems different, because the influence is running through the listener's own reasoning. Does the listener's awareness of the asymmetry make it better (they're on notice) or worse (they can't help being talked around)? Also, does it matter if the AI is optimizing for what a fully informed version of you would endorse, rather than just for what actually gets you to accept the conclusion—or does that distinction stop doing work when the intelligence gap is big enough? I'm not after policy recommendations; I want the conceptual geography—what exactly breaks in our everyday distinction between convincing someone with good arguments and manipulating them, once one party is superhumanly good at arguing and the other knows it.
