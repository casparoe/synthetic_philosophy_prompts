I am preparing a film club discussion of Kathryn Bigelow’s 2008 film *The Hurt Locker* and want to read it through William James’s essay “The Moral Equivalent of War.” James writes:

“Showing war's irrationality and horror is of no effect on him. The horrors make the fascination. War is the strong life; it is life in extremis; war taxes are the only ones men never hesitate to pay, as the budgets of all nations show us.”

I want you to treat that passage as a lens, not as a thesis the film simply confirms. In what scenes does the film dramatize James’s claim that the horrors make the fascination and that war is “the strong life”? Does the film’s protagonist, Sergeant First Class William James, embody the martial type James thinks we inherit? Where does the film resist the essay—for example by making the addictive intensity of combat look less like a transferable public virtue than like a private, self-destructive compulsion? And would James’s proposed moral equivalent, a peacetime conscription of youth into an army enlisted against nature, actually speak to the need the film portrays, or would it strike the character as precisely the kind of civilian consolation the film refuses?

End by saying what the film adds to James’s argument that the essay alone cannot deliver. Please also tell me where your interpretation is least certain: which scene, if read differently, would most change your overall reading, and what would that rival reading be?
