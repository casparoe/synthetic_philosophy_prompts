I have been working through a variant of the two-player centipede game and would like a careful analysis of it. The basic game runs for five decision points. The first player, A, moves at the first, third, and fifth decision points; the second player, B, moves at the second and fourth. The pot starts at £1 and doubles whenever a player passes. If a player takes, the game ends and the taker receives 70 per cent of the current pot while the other player receives 30 per cent; if the game reaches the fifth decision point, the rules require A to take at that point. In the unmodified game, backward induction leads to A taking at the first decision point, since at every node the mover prefers 70 per cent of the current pot to the 30 per cent they would receive after letting the other player take the doubled pot.

Now alter the game in one respect. Play is observed by an audience, and every pass, by either player, is publicly observed and yields an audience premium of r pounds to each player, paid after the play ends; each player therefore receives, in addition to the share described above, r times the number of passes that occurred before the game ended. The premium is common knowledge, and the players remain narrowly self-interested, caring only about their own total monetary payoff; r is best thought of as a reputation return rather than an other-regarding taste. Please analyse how the backward-induction outcome changes as r rises from zero. At what values of r, if any, does the prediction stop being that A takes at the first move, and at what values, if any, do later decisions begin to be postponed? Is the change gradual or does it move through discrete thresholds, and why should any thresholds increase as the pot grows? Finally, would you say this variation dissolves the classic paradox, or does it simply show that the original analysis was suppressing a payoff that was there all along?
