Write the joint statement that a committed Haidt-style moral foundations theorist and a committed debunker about the moral psychology of left and right could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Their question isn't just "how do liberals and conservatives differ" — they're past that — it's whether any of that psychology tell us anything about who's right.

The foundations person is a pluralist. They think the standard MFT picture — liberals lean hardest on harm/care and fairness/cheating, while conservatives also weight loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, and sanctity/degradation, with liberty/oppression as a candidate sixth — is not just descriptive. They think it suggests both sides are tracking distinct real moral concerns, so the correct view is probably a pluralism that houses all the foundations rather than the liberal two or three with the rest as noise.

The debunker thinks this is exactly backwards. They're willing to stipulate most of the broad descriptive pattern for now, but they deny it has any payoff for who is right. On their view the extra conservative foundations are mostly post-hoc rationalization of loyalty, submission, and disgust, and the whole psychology is more likely to debunk both sides' moralizing than to vindicate a happy pluralism. They also suspect the pluralist quietly sneaks in the normative conclusion by calling every foundation a "real moral domain."

Write the document they should have produced, not a dramatized back-and-forth. Start with a numbered agreed-claims section (aim for 8–12 claims; each has to be something both can actually sign, not a truism, not a compromise no one believes). Then a disagreements table. Each row needs: the disagreement in one neutral sentence; the foundations person's exact wording of their side; the debunker's exact wording of their side; the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one of them; and a test, case, or evidence both agree bears on the crux, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Separate the descriptive rows from the normative rows, and flag any row where you think the two are actually just using a word differently once the wording is pinned down.

Some cruxes I'd expect to be in the neighborhood of: whether the five-ish factor structure replicates cross-culturally or fragments once you're outside WEIRD samples; whether moral dumbfounding (e.g., the incest-with-contraception cases) actually shows intuition runs ahead of reasoning or just shows people are bad at producing reasons under time pressure; whether a moral judgment whose variance is well predicted by disgust sensitivity is thereby debunked; and whether the move from "both sides have different sensitivities" to "both sides are tracking distinct real values" is an empirical finding or a normative claim the psychologist hasn't paid for yet. Don't feel bound by my list if better cruxes emerge from the wording, but every row has to have a real test or case with predictions, not a restatement of the disagreement.

No citations, no reading list. They're writing something to sign, not a lit review.
