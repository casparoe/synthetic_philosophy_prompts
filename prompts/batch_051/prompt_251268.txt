I have been developing a distinction I call counting versus sorting, and I want to know whether it dissolves the problem posed by Black's two-sphere universe. My proposal is that the identity of indiscernibles should be split into two principles. The counting principle says that two things may be distinct only if some purely qualitative feature of the world guarantees that there are at least two things. The sorting principle says that two things may be distinct only if some purely qualitative feature attaches to one and not the other. Black's spheres satisfy the counting principle, because the relation being two miles apart is irreflexive, so its instantiation requires two relata; but they violate the sorting principle, since every qualitative description true of one sphere is true of the other. I take this to show that a world need not be sortable in order to be countable, and that distinctness requires only counting-style discernibility. Have I merely renamed a familiar position? Is the split stable, or does it collapse in hard cases, such as objects for which no irreflexive relation holds, or relations whose irreflexivity already presupposes distinctness? I would like an honest assessment of what this gets right and where it breaks.
