Since retiring from a career in physical chemistry, I have been reading my way through the philosophy of mind and the more conceptual side of the machine learning literature, and I keep circling back to one question: can a system trained only to predict the next token ever produce something genuinely new — a real insight or theoretical leap — rather than an artful recombination of what was already latent in its training data? I find myself drawn to the following negative argument, which I will set out as a numbered sequence, but I also half-suspect it is too quick and I cannot say exactly where.

(1) After training, a transformer implements a fixed function: at inference, its output distribution is determined entirely by the prompt and the weights, which no longer change. (2) The entire informational content of those weights was acquired from the finite training corpus and the objective of minimizing next-token prediction error; no other source of information reaches the network. (3) Therefore any pattern the model can produce is, in some sense, already contained in the training corpus and its statistical structure; the model cannot produce anything that outruns its training distribution. (4) A genuine intuitive leap — the kind of move I used to associate, in my own laboratory, with a colleague seeing that two apparently unrelated phenomena fall under a single mechanism — is precisely a pattern that outruns the data: one that is underdetermined by the observations rather than interpolated within them. (5) Therefore a next-token-predicting transformer cannot make a genuine intuitive leap; its apparent novelty is always recombination, interpolation, or the surfacing of something the data already contained, however striking it looks.

Premises (1) and (2) strike me as almost definitional, and (4) seems to capture what genuine novelty has to mean. So if the conclusion is false, the fault should lie in (3). But I cannot articulate precisely what is wrong with (3), and I suspect the phrase "contained in the training corpus" is doing more work than it can bear — or that my forced choice between "mere interpolation" and "a pattern no one deliberately put in the data" is itself the mistake. Please diagnose the argument: is it valid, and if not, which step fails? State the strongest reading of (3) on which it is true, and say whether the conclusion still follows on that reading. If the argument needs a distinction I have missed in order to be made correct, give it.
