I keep noticing what looks like the same distinction in two different subjects, and I want to know whether it is really one distinction or just a surface-level resemblance. In formal logic, strings of symbols are manipulated by purely syntactic rules and mean nothing until someone supplies an interpretation—a model that assigns objects, properties, and truth conditions to the symbols. The syntax is self-contained: a well-formed formula does not by itself refer to anything in the world. In artificial intelligence and the philosophy of mind, the symbol grounding problem appears to begin from the same observation: a computer program, like a dictionary, manipulates symbols that are defined only in terms of other symbols, and if the whole network floats free of the world, then none of the symbols has meaning. But formal logic seems to already have a solution to the logical version—namely, interpretations, or model theory. So my question is this: is the symbol grounding problem simply the syntax/semantics distinction wearing different clothes, or is it asking for something more? If it is asking for something more, what exactly is the extra element: a causal connection to an environment, a perceiver whose primitive categories are not themselves symbolic, or something else? And does supplying a model-theoretic interpretation to a formal system actually make any progress toward grounding symbols in a robot or an embodied system, or are the two problems merely parallel-sounding? If the parallel is superficial, I want to know the precise point at which syntax/semantics and symbol grounding diverge.
