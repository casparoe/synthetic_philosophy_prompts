I teach philosophy of mind and I've been going back and forth with a colleague who does AI alignment about whether any existing system literally has beliefs. We agree on the Dennettian starting point: adopt the intentional stance when it earns its keep as a predictive strategy. The disagreement is over which features of a system should push you from treating belief-talk as a useful shorthand to saying the beliefs are really in there. I scribbled a list of candidate features on a whiteboard, and I'd like you to rank them, or sort them into tiers, by how much independent evidential weight each should have in that move. Give me a sentence for each placement, and if two items are basically the same feature, tell me.

1. The system has a stable internal world model it uses to anticipate the downstream consequences of its possible actions.
2. The system is fluent at producing plausible after-the-fact rationales for its output, including on inputs it has not seen before.
3. The system's self-reported confidence tracks its actual error rate across tasks.
4. The system's preference ordering stays roughly fixed across contexts instead of being recomputed locally for each prompt.
5. The system notices contradictions among its own outputs and revises without external feedback.
6. The system has real causal effects on other agents, so misdescribing its beliefs would have practical costs for us.
7. The system's self-reports are robust against superficial rewording of the input.

I'm not asking for necessary or sufficient conditions; I want a ranking of how much each should matter ceteris paribus when a philosophers trying to decide whether to go beyond the instrumental story. Don't be polite about any item you think deserves zero weight.
