I've been trying to sort out something I used to think was straightforward, and my own examples keep breaking the framework, so I'd like you to help me repair it or replace it. I have always assumed there are two kinds of truth. Some statements are true just because of what the words mean: you don't have to inspect the world to know them, and someone who denies them is misusing the words rather than disagreeing with a fact. Other statements are true only because the world happens to be arranged a certain way, so you can find out only by investigating. I'll call these word-truths and world-truths.

Here are my current verdicts.

1. "All bachelors are unmarried." Word-truth, and I'm confident. Denying it would just show that the speaker doesn't know what "bachelor" means.

2. "A fortnight is fourteen days." Word-truth. This is a dictionary matter.

3. "All bachelors are unhappy." World-truth at best. Nothing about the word "bachelor" decides this; you would have to study actual people.

4. "Water is H2O." World-truth, mostly, since chemists had to discover it. But this one bothers me: now that it is settled knowledge, people use it almost as a definition of "water," so it feels like a world-truth that has hardened into a word-truth over time.

5. "Either it will rain in Tulsa tomorrow or it won't." Neither box, which is my first real problem. I don't need to wait for tomorrow or check a forecast; it is true no matter what. But it isn't true because of what the words mean the way "bachelor" is. It seems guaranteed by the structure of "or" and "not," whatever that kind of guarantee is.

6. "Nothing can be both perfectly round and perfectly square at the same time and in the same respect." I know it without looking, so it feels like a word-truth. But it also seems to say something about how shapes can be, not just about how I use "round" and "square." I can't decide which box it belongs in.

7. "There are infinitely many prime numbers." Neither box again. I didn't arrive at it by counting, and you can't settle it by looking at the world, so it isn't an ordinary world-truth. But I can't see how it is true merely because of the meanings of "prime" or "infinitely many" either.

8. "All cats are animals." In one light, word-truth, because that's how I would explain "cat." In another light, world-truth, because it records a fact about how living things are organized, not an arbitrary convention.

So I have confidence in some verdicts and confusion in the rest. What bothers me most is the pair of verdicts 1 and 5: both can be known without checking the world, so by my original test they should go in the same box, but I don't want to say that logic is just word-meaning. The pair 4 and 8 also bothers me: both seem to mix word and world in ways my two boxes can't hold.

I'd like a principle—or a small set of them—that sorts these cases better than my two boxes do. If the only way to save every one of my verdicts is to build a complicated patchwork, I'd rather hear which verdicts I should overturn and why. Please also tell me what the resulting principle says about a case I still can't place: "Every event has a cause." And should I stop expecting a sharp boundary between word-truth and world-truth at all? Please write your answer in American English.
