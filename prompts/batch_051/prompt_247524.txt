I am a first-year graduate student in a seminar on realism and anti-realism, and this week we discussed Fitch's paradox of knowability. A classmate who had read a survey article summarized the result roughly as follows. Fitch showed that the anti-realist principle that every truth is knowable collapses into the absurd claim that every truth is actually known by someone at some time. Suppose, for reductio, that p is a truth no one knows. Then p and no one knows p is itself a truth, so by the principle it must be possible to know it. But knowing a conjunction requires knowing each conjunct, so it would be possible for someone to know p and also to know that no one knows p. Since knowledge is factive, knowing that no one knows p entails that no one knows p, which contradicts the first conjunct; so the conjunction cannot be knowable after all, contrary to the principle. Hence there can be no unknown truths. Since there plainly are unknown truths, the principle, and with it any anti-realism that makes truth answerable to what we could in principle verify, is refuted.

I am not sure I trust the last two moves more than the formal core. I would like an accuracy audit: which parts of this summary would someone working on the paradox accept, and which parts overstate or subtly misdescribe the result and its intended target? In particular, I want to know whether the argument refutes the knowability principle outright or only a strong unrestricted formulation of it, whether deriving that every truth is known commits the anti-realist to something as plainly false as the summary suggests, and whether the step from the theorem to every truth is known by someone at some time involves an equivocation about who knows, or when. Go piece by piece through the summary, and say what a defender of the anti-realist position would concede and what they would resist.
