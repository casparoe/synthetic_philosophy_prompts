I am taking a course on the ethics of markets, and we have been weighing the condemnation of greed as a vice against the argument that the pursuit of private gain is an engine of prosperity. I keep wanting to draw a distinction that I think this debate collapses, and I would like an honest assessment of it. I call the first phenomenon unbounded acquisitiveness: a disposition to want and pursue more of a good without any natural stopping point, where the object of desire is the good itself or what it affords—security, options, comfort—rather than anyone else's holdings. The second I call positional greed: a desire whose object is essentially comparative, such as a larger share, a higher rank, or more than one's peers possess. My suspicion is that the prosperity argument needs only the first, while the moral critique of greed is most convincing against the second, so the debate tends to talk past itself because one word does two jobs. Someone who keeps accumulating savings because no amount feels sufficient for future security seems unboundedly acquisitive without being positional, whereas someone who wants a raise only because a colleague received one seems positional without being acquisitive. Is this a real distinction or just a redescription? Does it already exist under another name? And where does it break—can the two really come apart in actual market behaviour, or does unbounded acquisitiveness reliably slide into positional competition?
