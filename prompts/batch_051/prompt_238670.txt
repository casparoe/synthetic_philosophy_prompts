In a political philosophy seminar, a fellow student has been defending a strong view with an analogy. He says rights are trumps, in something like the way a trump card is a trump in a card game: just as the ace of trumps takes any card from any other suit regardless of how much aggregate strength the other side has assembled, a rights claim beats any appeal to aggregate welfare, majority preference, or the general good. From this he concludes that once a policy violates a right, the argument is over — no amount of collective benefit can justify it, so a surveillance program that violates a right to privacy is impermissible even if it would plausibly save many lives.

I suspect the card-game picture is doing more work in his inference than he realizes, and I would like an audit of exactly how far it holds. Lay out what maps onto what in the analogy — the suit, the rank of the card, the hand, the game, the players, the rules, the score, what it means to win a trick, what it means to win the game — and then say where the mapping breaks down. Separate the disanalogies that matter for his conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. In particular, I want to know whether the conclusion — that a rights claim automatically outweighs any appeal to aggregate welfare — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Where the trump picture misleads, say what it was trying to capture and whether a different picture — for instance, rights as side constraints on what may be done, or as instruments for protecting interests — captures that insight without the distortions.
