I've been teaching myself epistemic utility theory—the program that attempts to ground norms on degrees of belief in a single demand: maximize expected accuracy, where accuracy is simply closeness to the truth. The more I work with it, the more I want to inject a second score into the ledger. I've been calling it earned accuracy, though I'm not attached to the name.

Here is the proposal. Accuracy, as the theory defines it, scores a credence only against how things actually are: assign 0.9 to a proposition and you score well if it is true and badly if it is false, no matter how you reached 0.9. It is, so to speak, blind to the route. But compare a juror and a coin-flipper. The juror assigns 0.9 to the defendant's guilt after carefully weighing weeks of evidence; the coin-flipper assigns 0.9 because a fair coin happened to come up heads. In the world where the defendant is guilty, both receive exactly the same accuracy score. I have the strong intuition that these are not epistemically equal, and that the difference should show up inside the score itself rather than as a separate moral or pragmatic matter. So I define the evidence score of a credence q at a world w as the closeness of q to the evidential probability at w—the credence a fully competent agent with precisely the agent's evidence would have at w. The total score of q at w is then (1−λ)·accuracy + λ·evidence score, with λ between 0 and 1, and rational credence is whatever maximizes expected total score. As λ approaches 1, rational credence collapses onto the evidential probabilities; as λ approaches 0, it collapses back onto plain accuracy-first. I can already feel the obvious objection: I've dressed up evidentialism as a scoring rule, and everything is being done by the under-defined notion of "the evidential probability at w." There is also the reply that accuracy-first is a theory of credence rationality, not of agent evaluation, so juror and coin-flipper are equally rational from the inside and the difference belongs to the theory of reliability or virtue. I want an honest assessment: is earned accuracy a coherent addition to the epistemic-utility framework, does it reduce to something already out there, and does it buy anything that ordinary accuracy-first machinery cannot get more cheaply—say, by invoking long-run calibration or reliability rather than a second one-shot score?
