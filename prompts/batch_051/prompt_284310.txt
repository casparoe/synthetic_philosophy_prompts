Consider a rule proposed for a small self-governing community: a truthfulness statute under which every adult must answer any direct question from another member with what they honestly believe, with no exception for benevolent motives — not even when the questioner announces an intention to kill a third person. Someone who answers truthfully is immune from all liability for any harm that follows from that answer, however foreseeable the harm was; someone who answers falsely is strictly liable for whatever harm follows, even if it could not have been foreseen. The statute's preamble paraphrases Kant's late essay against a supposed right to lie from benevolent motives: there Kant argues that truthfulness is not owed only to those with a right to the truth, but is an unconditional duty to humanity in general, because a lie, even one that injures no particular person, attacks the trustworthiness on which enforceable rights depend; and that if you tell a truth to a murderer and the victim dies, the death belongs to the murderer, whereas if you lie you become answerable for what your lie sets in motion. Walk through how this statute would actually behave: what it incentivizes in ordinary conversation, in close relationships, in interrogation-like encounters, and in confrontations with threatening members; where it is gamed first; what second-order norms or evasions emerge; and whether the failure modes can be fixed without abandoning the core immunity/liability asymmetry. In doing so, interpret the Kantian reasoning behind that asymmetry, and end by saying how confident you are in your analysis and which point you are least sure of.
