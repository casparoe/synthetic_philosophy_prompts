I chair the hazard review committee at a company that manufactures laboratory equipment — centrifuges, autoclaves, and vacuum pumps — mostly for university and hospital labs. Each quarter we triage roughly fifty field reports and internal design memos, each of which asserts that a device 'could' fail in a particular way, 'has the potential' to injure an operator, or 'cannot be ruled out' under sufficiently extreme conditions. Our task is to decide which of these possible failure modes go into the risk register and trigger engineering follow-up. We have about twenty minutes per report, and the committee includes engineers, a field-service technician, and one lawyer; no one has formal training in risk theory.

The committee is caught between two habits. One camp treats a possibility as actionable only when someone can supply at least an order-of-magnitude probability or a mechanism with a recognized base rate; otherwise, they say, the register fills with unfalsifiable 'what ifs' and the real hazards disappear. The other camp argues that a sufficiently severe outcome can be actionable simply because it is physically possible and not excluded by anything we know, even if no probability can yet be assigned. I suspect the underlying dispute is about what 'possible' contributes once probability is unavailable, but I need a working instrument rather than a lecture on that question. Please write a decision procedure the committee can actually execute inside the twenty-minute window: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, explicit stopping rules for no action, and explicit points where the procedure must hand the decision to a senior engineer or to legal because the remaining issue is one of values or residual uncertainty rather than fact. Make the procedure say plainly which distinctions among kinds of possibility and degrees of probability it preserves and which it collapses, and where the procedure itself is likely to misfire. Then run it on three reports, and include at least one outcome the committee — including me — will not like: (1) a technician who says a centrifuge rotor could shatter its housing under a rare combination of load imbalance, bearing wear, and a particular vibration frequency; (2) an internal reviewer who says a vacuum pump could injure a user through a failure mode nobody has been able to specify; and (3) a customer who says an autoclave door could open mid-cycle because a pressure sensor fails in a way that has never been observed but cannot be proven impossible.
