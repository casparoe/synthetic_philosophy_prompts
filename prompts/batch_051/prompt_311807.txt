Construct a thought experiment for a decision-theory lecture in which maximizing expected utility and the natural-order summation rule for gambles with undefined expected values come apart in the following direction: a single fair-coin gamble, specified in two different ways, receives a positive finite natural-order value under one specification and a negative finite natural-order value under the other, even though both specifications describe exactly the same distribution over dollar payoffs. Constraints: only a fair coin is used; each specification's expectation is a Pasadena-style conditionally convergent series, so standard expected utility has no verdict under either; the two specifications must assign identical probabilities to identical payoffs and differ only in the order in which the possible first-tails outcomes are listed; and the two listings may be given by recursive rules rather than written out in full. After constructing the two specifications, do the check: write the contribution term for each listed outcome, compute the natural-order partial-sum limits under both specifications, confirm that expected utility is undefined under both, and state which specification the natural-order rule would prefer. Finish by identifying the single feature of the case that does the work, what happens to the disagreement if the contribution series is made absolutely convergent, and how a defender of natural-order summation would best resist the conclusion that the rule is arbitrary.
