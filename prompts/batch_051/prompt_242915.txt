I am a novelist. The protagonist of my current novel is a painter who repeatedly, with full awareness of what she is doing, lets a small immediate pleasure (a night of drinking, a spiteful phone call, an irrelevant errand) displace work toward the exhibition that would realize the ambitions she professes to have. In trying to make her behavior psychologically precise rather than merely theatrical, I arrived at an argument I cannot shake.

(1) An act is weak-willed only if, at the moment of acting, the agent acts against her own all-things-considered judgment about what she should do.
(2) With hyperbolic discounting, the smaller-sooner reward can have greater discounted value at the moment of temptation than the larger-later reward, even when the larger-later reward had greater discounted value at every earlier point.
(3) For an option to have greater discounted value for an agent at a moment is for the agent to judge, all things considered, that it is better at that moment; there is nothing else for such a judgment to track.
(4) So when a hyperbolic discounter takes the smaller-sooner reward, she acts on her all-things-considered judgment at that moment, not against it.
(5) Therefore, in all such cases, what presents as weakness of will is not weakness of will at all, but the ordinary working of a time-inconsistent discount function.

Is this argument valid? If it is, then I must write the character as someone moved by arithmetic rather than by a failure to do what she thinks best, which changes the moral shape of the book. If it is not, say exactly which step fails and how it should be corrected.
