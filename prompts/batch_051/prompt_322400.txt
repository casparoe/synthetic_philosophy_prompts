At next month's meeting I have one shot to convince the other trustees of the community foundation I serve on that we should add a distinct grantmaking priority: increasing broad prosperity itself, rather than only funding services or reducing poverty. The claim I'm defending is that a community's overall wealth and economic liveliness have moral value over and above the particular goods those resources purchase, so we should sometimes fund work whose main effect is to make the local economy bigger and more energetic even when we cannot say exactly which person ends up better off or in what way. I've never studied this formally; I've just thought about it on my own for years, and I want to be ready for how this particular group will push back. The trustees are mostly public-health nurses, social workers, and two environmental advocates. They treat money as valuable only when it shows up in measurable outcomes like fewer asthma cases, better school readiness, or less food insecurity, and a couple of them hear the word 'growth' as code for developers and sprawl. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or nine objections I'm genuinely likely to get, roughly in the order they'll come, phrased the way these particular people would phrase them, each with a reply I can give in about two minutes and a note on the point where that reply is softest. Include the question I most hope nobody asks. The argument they will be aiming at goes something like this: prosperity is a general-purpose cushion against shocks that no list of current outcomes captures; a visibly moving economy creates confidence and a willingness to experiment that people value in itself; and a wealthy community can absorb newcomers and setbacks without making anyone beg for help.
