Next term I'm teaching a seminar on why mathematical definitions look the way they do, and I want one week on the standards of evaluation themselves rather than another lecture on how the open-set definition won. So write me the desiderata. Suppose we have a pre-formal concept—continuity, dimension, symmetry—and several candidate definitions on the table, none extensionally equivalent. What does a good definition have to do? I want the list, with the case behind each: which agreed examples it must include and which agreed non-examples it must exclude; why it should be invariant under the relevant notion of sameness (isomorphism, homeomorphism, whatever else); whether it should be closed under the operations we care about (restrictions, products, quotients) and what it means when it isn't; when generality is a virtue and when it merely lets pathologies in; how minimal the axioms need to be and whether a redundant clause is a real sin; and whether a definition is allowed to build in a theorem—compactness defined as closed-and-bounded is the classic warning. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just taste; I suspect a good deal of 'naturalness' talk is just 'this is the one I learned first.' Then flag the pairs that pull against each other, like generality versus keeping the old theorems, or economy versus the definition actually saying something. The part I most want: tell me which single desideratum does the most work in excluding a candidate, and run it on continuity—epsilon-delta, open-set, sequential—plus one other definitional dispute of your choosing. Keep the weight on the criteria, not the scorecard.
