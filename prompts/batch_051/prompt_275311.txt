I am preparing a short position paper on how much authority a person's earlier self should have over their later, cognitively impaired self, and I would like an audit of my current commitments before I have to defend them. I find each of the following claims individually plausible, but I do not trust my sense that they add up to one coherent view.

My present commitments are these:

1. A competent adult's advance refusal of life-sustaining treatment should ordinarily be honoured even when the same person, now living with dementia, expresses a contrary wish to go on living. The reason it should be honoured is respect for that person's autonomy.

2. Those later contrary wishes deserve to be taken seriously, but they cannot by themselves revoke the earlier directive, because the later self lacks the sort of global, life-organising capacity that made the original decision an exercise of autonomy.

3. A person is best understood as a temporally extended whole, not as a succession of separate selves; the object of our concern is therefore not one moment's preference but the person's life taken as a whole.

4. The deeper point of honouring an advance directive is not to satisfy a past preference for its own sake but to respect the person's enduring critical interests, by which I mean interests in how the whole of their life goes; the earlier competent self is ordinarily the most reliable source of what those interests are.

5. Some people with mild-to-moderate dementia retain genuine capacity for a wide range of everyday decisions — what to eat, whom to see, how to spend the day — and we should honour those decisions, even while maintaining that the advance directive stays in force for major medical treatment.

6. A directive is most authoritative when it is specific and anchored in the person's long-held values; vague directives, or directives drafted in the middle of a passing crisis, have considerably less authority.

Could you please audit this set: identify which commitments are in tension with which, rank the tensions by severity, and say which apparent tensions dissolve once the relevant distinctions are drawn. Where the cheapest repair is a small qualification, state it and say what it costs me; where a whole commitment has to give, say which and why. If my position tacitly relies on an assumption I have not listed, tell me what it is. Finally, say how confident you are in the diagnosis you have given, and single out the point you are least sure of.
