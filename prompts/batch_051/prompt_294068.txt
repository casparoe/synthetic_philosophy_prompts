I find myself pulled in two directions about whether a corporation can be a genuine object of moral blame, and I want help negotiating the conflict rather than a verdict that simply sides with one pull against the other.

On one side, I am attracted to a functionalist account of corporate agency. On this view, an organization is a moral agent to the extent that it has a stable internal decision procedure that lets it form judgments and intentions attributable to the organization itself rather than merely to its members at a given time. The view earns its keep by offering a single criterion for when phrases like "the company decided," "the company knew," and "the company is to blame" are literarily true rather than convenient shorthand. It also captures the way a corporation can apologize, adopt a new policy, or run a cover-up as a continuing entity, and it avoids the forced claim that whenever a company does wrong the fault always decomposes into a roster of individually culpable employees.

On the other side, I have an intuition I cannot shake: full-blooded moral blame requires a subject who can take the blame in the first person, recognizing the wrong, feeling remorse, and making amends as the same agent that did the wrong. A company can pay a fine, dismiss executives, and rewrite its policies, but it cannot feel remorse, and punishing it seems to spread the burden onto shareholders and employees who had no causal or moral part in the wrong. The intuition is sharpest with resentment: I can judge that a corporation should be sanctioned, but I cannot make sense of resenting the corporation itself in the way I resent a person. The asymmetry with praise makes this even more puzzling, since I have no comparable difficulty saying that a university ought to be proud of a result or that an institution has a reputation to protect.

The theory and the intuition appear to be in direct conflict. Give me the strongest version of each side: the case that the intuition is a debunkable by-product of machinery built for tracking individual agents and that blame can be extended to corporate agents without loss, and the case that the theory has overgenerated by conflating agency with liability and must be contracted so that corporations can be held accountable without being full objects of blame. Also tell me whether there is a reading of blame on which both survive, perhaps by distinguishing blame as an entry in a moral ledger from blame as a reactive attitude. For each possible resolution, say what would justify it: what distinction, evidence, or deeper principle would show that the intuition should be dropped, that the theory should be amended, or that the two were never really in conflict.
