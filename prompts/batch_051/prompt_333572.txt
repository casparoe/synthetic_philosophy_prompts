I serve on the five-member appointing board for my city's Integrity Commissioner, and next week we vote on whether to give the current commissioner another term. The case is not about corruption; it is about what incorruptibility is worth.

In his three years, the commissioner has been genuinely incorruptible. During the development-permit bribery scandal, a firm under investigation offered him a consulting contract; he reported the approach the same afternoon. He later recommended sanctions against two officials who had been his friends since before he took the post. He accepts no gifts of any value, recuses at the smallest appearance of conflict, and keeps a log of every meeting and phone call so the office can survive any audit.

But the same rigidity is beginning to damage the office. He has opened formal files on junior staff who reported a forty-dollar working lunch a few days late, and on an incoming hire who once chaired a fund-raiser for a mayor two administrations ago. He refused to settle three routine claims against the city because each settlement would have let the other side get slightly more than the guideline range; in his refusal he wrote that a negotiated discount pays someone to stop claiming harm they may not have suffered. Two experienced investigators have quit, saying he cannot tell the difference between a bribe and a compromise. The mayor's office, which the commissioner is supposed to monitor, has stopped attending the quarterly compliance meetings, calling him unreasonable.

The board is split, and I can see both sides. One side says his resistance to every pressure is exactly why the office was created, and that any loosening would slowly rot the city's enforcement. The other side says he has confused being incorruptible with being unbending, and that an official who treats every ordinary compromise as corruption is not clean but merely inflexible, and is costing the city more trust than he protects. The question I keep circling is whether incorruptibility is just the absence of being buyable, or whether it also requires some positive sense for when to bend, settle, and use judgment — and whether it can turn into a fault without the person ever taking a penny.

Please write the short opinion I will circulate before the vote. Begin directly with the opinion itself, with no preface or restating of the facts, and make the first sentence of the opinion your recommendation. It should recommend a concrete action — renew, decline to renew, or renew with stated conditions — give your reasoning, and take seriously the strongest thing that could be said for the side you recommend against. Argue from the facts of this case; no sources or citations are needed.
