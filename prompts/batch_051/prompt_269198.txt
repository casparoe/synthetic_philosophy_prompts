I'm a retired statistical physicist who's spent the last couple years reading philosophy and decision theory instead of doing calculations, and I've gotten very interested in the ergodicity economics line of thinking about time averages versus ensemble averages. Before I bring a little writeup to my reading group, I'd like an outside check on whether my commitments actually hang together. I've numbered them so you can poke at them precisely.

(1) When a decision is genuinely repeated and wealth compounds — you keep risking some fraction of what you currently have — I think the rational target is the time-average growth rate of wealth, not the ensemble-average expected value. In practice that means maximizing expected log wealth, Kelly-style.

(2) But I also think expected utility theory with a concave utility function over final wealth is the right general normative theory of choice under risk, including one-shot gambles where there's no repetition at all.

(3) I think the St. Petersburg paradox is dissolved by noticing that the gamble is non-ergodic in the relevant sense: the ensemble-average payout diverges, but no actual player repeating the game can realize that average, so the intuition that you should pay an infinite price rests on a quantity that isnt available to any real trajectory. I like this better than the bounded-utility reply.

(4) I think loss aversion isn't a bias to be debiased. The asymmetry where losses loom larger than equal gains tracks a real structural fact: under multiplicative dynamics, a 50% loss needs a 100% gain just to break even, so the asymmetry is built into the dynamics before any psychology shows up.

(5) I think buying insurance is rational even though it has negative expected monetary value, and I'd defend it by the same logic: paying a premium to truncate the left tail raises the geometric growth rate of wealth.

(6) I'd turn down a single coin flip that pays $12,000 on heads and costs $10,000 on tails, even though its expected value is positive, but I'd happily take a hundred independent flips of that same bet, provided I could handle the swings. I dont think that pair of verdicts is a consistency problem — one is a gamble, the other is a line of business.

(7) Methodologically, I want the dynamics to do the heavy lifting in explaining risk preferences. Psychological quirks should be the residual, not the starting point.

What I'd like is an audit, not a lecture. Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like they are. For the genuine tensions, rank them by severity and say what would have to give in each case. And then tell me which single revision would buy the most coherence overall.
