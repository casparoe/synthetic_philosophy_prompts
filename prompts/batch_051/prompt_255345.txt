I recently joined my town's planning commission, and doing so has made me notice a rule I had apparently been following for years without stating it. Whenever a debate about public policy bottoms out in a religious claim — that a zoning change violates God's law, that a housing program is required by the created order, that a practice is forbidden by scripture — I stop treating the claim as a reason. I do not interrupt, and I do not assert that the person is wrong. I simply assign the consideration zero weight when forming my own view and when explaining my vote. Empirical evidence, cost–benefit arguments, and appeals to rights count for me; appeals to revelation or theological doctrine are treated as if they had been made in a private language I have no way to assess.

A fellow commissioner, who is religious but never argues in that register, tells me this is not neutrality. She says I am confusing the source of a reason with its quality, and that my rule would exclude from public debate precisely those citizens whose deepest commitments are religious — not because their arguments are bad but because they are theirs. She adds that several secular premises I accept without argument are no more publicly assessable than a neighbor's claim about God's will; they are just more familiar to me.

I want to know whether my policy is defensible. Treat it as a policy rather than a feeling: under what conditions is it a reliable way to reach good public decisions, and under what conditions does it fail? Is there a real distinction between judging religious reasons false (which I do not claim to do) and judging them inadmissible in this forum? Can the rule be restated to keep what it gets right — for example, the demand that coercive policies be justifiable to citizens of different faiths and none — without treating religious citizens as second-class participants? Or is my rule bias dressed up as a principle, such that the honest position is to evaluate each argument on its merits whatever its source?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
