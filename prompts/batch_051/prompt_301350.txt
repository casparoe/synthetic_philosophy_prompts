I've been trying to write something on whether hope and optimism should count as epistemic vices or as moral virtues, and I keep getting stuck earlier than I expected: I don't actually have a definition of hope. It gets used for a judgment about odds, for a kind of mood, and for whatever it is that keeps people going after the judgment turns bad, and sometimes all three at once. I suspect 'hope' is doing at least two jobs — one cognitive (a take on whether the outcome is possible or likely) and one practical (an investment in the outcome that shapes what you do and attend to). If that's right, two people could assign the same probability to an outcome and only one of them hope for it, and someone could hope while thinking the odds are below what they'd call realistic. But I'm not sure that's right, so I want you to build a definition and test it.

Propose a working definition of hope, run it against each case below, tell me where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and either revise the definition or defend the verdicts it forces on you:

1. A woman whose child is missing after a flood. The official search has been called off, she knows the survival odds by now are terrible, and she does not expect anyone to be found. She stays at the site, organizes volunteers, and refuses to leave. She says she has to hope.

2. A bank analyst puts the chance of a merger closing at 85%, wants it to close because his bonus depends on it, and is mostly just waiting for the announcement. Is he hoping it closes, or expecting it, and does your definition care about the difference?

3. A friend says 'I hope it doesn't rain tomorrow,' but he has no outside plans, doesn't check the forecast, and wouldn't alter a single behavior either way. It seems sincere. If your definition excludes this, it's cutting against ordinary usage; if it includes it, hope is looking pretty thin.

4. A gambling friend is optimistic he'll break even this year; the actual numbers make that vanishigly unlikely, and he's clearly reweighting the evidence so the bad cases stop counting. Is he hoping? Is this the same as optimism? Where does self-deception land?

5. A prisoner with no realistic prospect of release says he is not optimistic and isn't making any prediction, but he keeps a routine, exercises, and teaches other prisoners to read because he says hope is something you do, not something you estimate. Does he hope, or has he just renamed discipline?

6. An oncologist puts a patient's chance of remission at 70%, treats the case competently, and genuinely has no personal stake in how it turns out beyond doing her job. Does she hope the patient recovers, and if not, what's missing?

Once you've got a definition that survives these, say briefly whether it makes the original vice-or-virtue question easier to ask, and where optimism ends up relative to hope on your account.
