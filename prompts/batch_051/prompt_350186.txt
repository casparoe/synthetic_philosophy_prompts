I keep noticing a structural resemblance between Lakatos's account of scientific research programs and the way software engineers manage legacy systems, and I want to know whether it is a real isomorphism or a superficial analogy. On Lakatos's side, a program consists of a hard core of central theoretical commitments protected by the negative heuristic: anomalies are never allowed to refute the core directly, and must instead be absorbed by adjusting a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses, initial conditions, and modeling assumptions. The program is progressive while those adjustments generate novel predictions that are subsequently confirmed, and degenerating when they become ad hoc patches that merely accommodate recalcitrant observations. On the software side, the strangler-fig and anti-corruption-layer patterns give a legacy core much the same treatment: the old system is treated as off-limits for direct modification, new functionality is routed around it, and changing requirements are absorbed in adapters, facades, and translation layers until the core can be retired. The proposed mapping is hard core to legacy core, negative heuristic to the architectural rule against touching the old system, protective belt to the adapter layer, anomaly to change request, and ad hoc patch to wrapper sprawl. Is this mapping exact enough to be interesting, or does it break as soon as one asks what counts as a novel prediction in software, or why the two cores are protected in the first place? State where the structures match and where they come apart, and then say whether anything transfers in either direction: for instance, a Lakatos-style criterion for when a legacy core should be abandoned, or a software-engineering insight about replacing the core incrementally that the Lakatos picture of theory change cannot express.
