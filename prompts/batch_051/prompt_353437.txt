I direct the resource-allocation analysis unit at a state health agency. Several times a quarter my team of half a dozen analysts must compare proposals that benefit very different groups—perinatal home-visiting that prevents infant deaths, a drug that slows the progression of ALS, expanded cognitive-behavioral therapy for mild depression, a screening program that catches early-stage kidney disease—and recommend which to fund out of a fixed budget, with our recommendations going to the agency's executive board for sign-off. The decisions involve tens of millions of dollars and large changes in health and life expectancy. We have outcomes data and some self-reported quality-of-life ratings, but we lack any defensible way to compare how much good a proposal does for one group against how much good another proposal does for a very different group, so in practice the proposals with the most effective advocacy tend to win. Please give me a reusable decision procedure my analysts can actually run in an afternoon, using only the data we already have, that operationalizes what formal welfare economics and social choice theory can genuinely support about interpersonal utility comparisons. The procedure should be a sequence of tests or questions; it should tell us when a number may be treated as comparable across persons and when it may not; how to construct an explicit calibration—such as a zero-one normalization anchoring each person's best and worst feasible states, or a veil-of-ignorance/impartial-observer test—without pretending the choice of calibration is value-free; which steps are measurement and which are equity judgments that must be surfaced to the board; and where the procedure must stop because a comparison cannot be defended. Keep the distinction between comparing levels of well-being and comparing gains or differences in well-being, and show what follows from it for choosing between a large benefit for a few and a small benefit for many. Then run the procedure on three proposals: (1) a costly drug for a small number of people with a rapidly fatal disease versus expanded talk therapy for a large number of people with mild depression; (2) two programs that produce the same measured health-state improvement, one for people who have long adapted to a disability and one for newly impaired people who rate the same health state as catastrophic; and (3) a high-profile program the agency leadership has already signaled it wants to fund. Include at least one case in which the procedure, correctly applied, blocks or delays a funding decision the agency is inclined to make, and end by saying plainly what the procedure cannot do—in particular, that it cannot manufacture a common cardinal scale from purely ordinal data and cannot settle how much priority to give to the worse off.
