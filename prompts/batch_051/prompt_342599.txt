I'm a PhD student working on the epistemology of counterfactual history, and I've distilled an argument from several things I've been reading that now looks to me as if it proves too much. I would be grateful for a precise diagnosis rather than a one-line dismissal.

Here is the argument.

(1) Suppose determinism is true: the complete state of the world at any time, together with the actual laws of nature, fixes the entire subsequent course of history.

(2) A historical counterfactual — for instance, the claim that if the Persians had won at Salamis, then such-and-such would have followed — is to be evaluated, like any counterfactual, by looking at the closest possible worlds in which the antecedent obtains and asking whether the consequent obtains there.

(3) Consider a world that exactly matches the actual world up to the night before the battle and obeys the actual laws. By (1), that world realizes the actual outcome. So any world in which the Persians do win must diverge from actuality in one of two ways. Either it differs from the actual past before the battle (a backtracking divergence), or it contains at least one localized violation of the actual laws at or near the point of divergence (a miracle).

(4) Suppose the divergence lies in the earlier past. Then the antecedent is not achieved by refighting the battle under its actual background conditions; it is achieved by altering those background conditions. And once alterations to the earlier past are permitted, it seems that for any consequent one cares to secure, some suitable earlier divergence from the actual past will yield that consequent. The counterfactual becomes unconstrained, and any verdict can be made true by ad hoc selection of the divergence.

(5) Suppose instead that the world contains a localized miracle. Then the actual laws are violated at precisely the point where the inference from conditions to outcome was supposed to run. Since the actual laws do not determine which miracle occurs, which miracle we insert is not compelled by the evidence either, so the resulting verdict is unsupported.

(6) Therefore every historical counterfactual is either unconstrained or unsupported by evidence.

(7) But that cannot be right. Historical explanation is saturated with counterfactual claims — for example, that without the embargo the regime would have fallen — that are evidently sensitive to evidence and open to rational dispute. If the argument were sound, this entire practice would be empty.

I would prefer to keep (1), since I see no independent reason to give up determinism here, and I am not willing to accept (6). So my question is whether some step between (1) and (6) fails, or, if you think the argument is valid, which premise is least costly to abandon and what abandoning it commits me to. Please do not just say the alternatives are vaguely problematic; I want the step identified, an explanation of why it looked attractive, and an account of the price of accepting your diagnosis.

At the very end of your answer, on its own line, write \answer{step N} if you diagnose a failed inferential step, or \answer{premise N} if you judge the argument valid and recommend abandoning premise N. Put nothing else on that line.
