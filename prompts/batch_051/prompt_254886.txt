I am an undergraduate in a moral philosophy course on social conventions, and we have spent several weeks on whether everyday conventions—tipping, queueing, and jaywalking—are moral obligations, and whether a visitor to a place is bound to follow its local conventions. Two students in the course have been arguing about this for most of the term and have decided to write an adversarial collaboration instead of two opposed papers. I have been asked to produce the draft joint statement for them to respond to.

One of them defends what he calls the convention-first view: an established convention—a practice people in a setting generally comply with and reasonably expect of one another—itself changes what its participants owe each other, so that a visitor who enters the setting inherits at least a defeasible obligation to conform over and above the benefits and harms in the particular case. The other defends what she calls the underlying-reasons view: a convention never adds an obligation of its own; it is at most fallible evidence of the non-conventional considerations—coordination, reciprocity, fairness, harm, consent, reasonable expectation—that alone generate obligations, and a visitor is bound only to the extent those considerations actually bind.

Please draft the document they could both sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration, formatted as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table. The agreed-claims section should list what both camps accept about what conventions are, how the tipping, queueing, and jaywalking cases are alike and where they differ, and what either view would have to explain; it should be neither vacuous nor slanted. The disagreements table should give, row by row, the precise residual disagreement in wording each side would accept for its own position, the crux it tracks, a concrete case or piece of evidence both camps would agree bears on that crux, and each side's prediction recorded in advance.

I suspect the cruxes include whether the existence of a convention adds a reason over and above the underlying considerations; whether a visitor's relation to a convention is relevantly like an insider's; and whether any obligation depends on participation, benefit, or mere reasonable expectation. Amend or replace these if you find better ones. Vary the cases: a visitor who does not know the local convention, a queue that reliably solves a coordination problem, a tipping norm that has partly outlived its original rationale, a convention that tracks a background injustice, and a jaywalking norm locals themselves flout. If you conclude that some part of the dispute is merely verbal, identify which part and why. The statement should read as something both could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact, rather than as a debate transcript or a ruling about who is right.
