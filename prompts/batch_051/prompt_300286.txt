Last night I stayed up far too late arguing with a close friend about interpersonal comparisons of utility. I found myself defending the strict ordinalist position: a person's utility is only a device for representing their preference order, valid only up to any strictly increasing transformation of that person's own numbers, so there is no common unit or zero across persons, and a sentence such as "Ann gains more than Ben loses" cannot report a fact. My friend defended the comparabilist position: one cannot make any distributional judgement — who should receive a scarce treatment, whether a transfer from a richer person to a poorer one is an improvement, whether one person's loss is worth another's gain — without such comparisons, and a welfare economics that refuses to make them has not thereby stayed neutral; it has quietly deferred to whatever allocation already exists. We could each state the other's view back to them accurately, and neither of us moved.

What we decided would be more use than another round of argument is the document an adversarial collaboration between the two camps would actually produce. Please draft that document itself, not an essay about it. Keep the two positions as "strict ordinalist" and "comparabilist", but state what each commits to. Structure the document as follows.

(a) A numbered list of claims both camps can sign, with enough formal content that the agreement is not vacuous. The list should include: the nature of individual preference orderings and their utility representations; the invariance properties both sides accept within a single person; the sense in which Pareto efficiency is comparison-free; and the precise sense, if any, in which a non-Paretian rule for evaluating distributions commits itself, perhaps implicitly, to interpersonal comparisons once it is required to treat persons symmetrically.

(b) A table of the residual disagreements. For each row, give the strict ordinalist's wording of their own position, the comparabilist's wording of theirs, and a sentence recording, for each camp, what would have to be shown before they would revise it. Aim for wording that is neither vacuous nor so strong that the other camp could truthfully say "that is not what I hold."

(c) A list of cruxes: the sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one camp. For each crux, record each camp's prediction in advance, and name the forms of argument or evidence both camps could agree would bear on it; where a crux is purely conceptual rather than empirical, say so.

(d) A concluding assessment of which parts of the dispute, if any, are merely verbal — a disagreement about which stipulations to adopt rather than about what follows from them — and what remains substantive once that part is set aside.

Make it a document that a committed strict ordinalist and a committed comparabilist could each, with mild discomfort, put their name to, while locating the remaining disagreement exactly rather than blurring it.
