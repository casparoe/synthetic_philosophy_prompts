In my medieval philosophy seminar we spent a session on Abelard's claim that sin lies in consent to an act rather than in the outward deed itself. Two students, Miriam and Julian, ended up arguing about a test case, and they began from the same reading of Abelard but disagreed sharply about what follows from it. (1) Miriam put the point through a pair of cases: two people each consent, with equal resolve, to kill someone; one is stopped at the door, the other carries it out. On Abelard's view, she argued, the two have sinned equally, because the outward act adds nothing to a sin that is already fully present in the consent. (2) Julian objected that this cannot be the whole story. In the completed case a person is dead, and the agent has more to answer for — grief to repair and further wrongs set in motion — so the completed act adds something morally significant even if it adds nothing to the quality of the will itself. (3) Miriam replied that he was confusing the sin with the harm and the reparation. Abelard's claim, she said, is about what makes a person bad, not about what makes a state of the world worse. The completed act is only the outward execution of a will already fixed, and any greater punishment or penance for completed crimes tracks deterrence and public order, not a greater degree of sin. (4) Julian then pressed the point differently: if sin is being defined that narrowly, Abelard has turned it into a technical term that no longer tracks what the tradition and ordinary moral judgment mean by it. Human judges and ordinary moral judgment treat completed wrongs as worse than merely attempted ones, and not merely for pragmatic reasons; if Abelard's principle makes attempted and completed wrongs equal in sin, that is a reason to doubt the principle.

Adjudicate their exchange move by move. For each of the four moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right, justify the rating by saying which subclaims are right and which are wrong, and identify the distinctions — between sin, harm, and reparation, and between internal guilt and public punishment, for example — that would resolve or sharpen each point. Finally, say what, if anything, both of them are missing about the Abelardian position.
