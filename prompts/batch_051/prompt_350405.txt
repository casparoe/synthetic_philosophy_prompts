Last night a friend and I spent hours arguing about anthropic reasoning and ended up nowhere, in a way that left me unsure what kind of argument we were even having. The dispute concerned two ways of handling theories that imply different numbers of observers. My friend defended the self-indication assumption: the fact that one exists is itself evidence, so that, other things being equal, a theory on which the universe contains vastly more observers is much more likely, simply because one's own existence would be more probable under it. I defended the self-sampling assumption: one should reason as if one were selected at random from among all actual observers, so one's bare existence does not by itself favour a larger population, and one may not conclude that a theory with an enormous number of observers is true in the absence of ordinary observational support.

The sticking point was the thought experiment called the Presumptuous Philosopher. Suppose two theories accommodate all the empirical data equally well and differ only in how many observers they predict: one predicts, say, 10^12 observers, and the other 10^24. My friend held that one should be near-certain that the larger-observer theory is true, even though no measurement has told us this, because our existence would be vastly more likely under that theory. I held that this is absurd, since it licenses near-certainty about a cosmic population on the strength of nothing but the fact that one is here to ask the question. We circled for hours: my friend accused me of smuggling in an arbitrary reference class and refusing to condition on all my evidence; I accused him of double-counting his own existence and treating a precondition of any observation as if it were a fresh observation.

We both know the standard moves, and neither of us budged. What kind of disagreement is this, really? Is it a hidden formal disagreement about how to condition on indexical facts, a substantive disagreement about what counts as evidence, a verbal dispute about the word evidence, or a clash of bedrock epistemic intuitions that neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, is there anything—a formal result, a sharper distinction, a change of question—that would actually count as progress, or should we expect reasonable people to remain split?
