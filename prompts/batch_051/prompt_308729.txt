Marx's story about machines under capitalism has a few standard parts. Machines are dead labor — past human work frozen into equipment — but under capitalism the owner deploys them as capital against living workers. They make it possible to replace skilled workers with unskilled ones or with the machines themselves, which swells the reserve army of the unemployed and pushes wages down; and because production keeps tilting toward dead labor over living labor, there's supposed to be a long-run tendency for the rate of profit to fall. Now take a development Marx obviously didn't have in mind: an AI system trained on a huge amount of actual human work—text, images, code, analysis—that can then churn out passable versions of that same work, and that is available to nearly anyone for almost nothing, including the workers whose past output helped train it. A beginner with the model can now produce work that used to require years of skill. Does Marx's argument survive this? If it does, say exactly which parts still go through and why they do the real work. If it doesn't, say which premise the AI system actually breaks, and whether there's a repaired version of the argument that still captures something Marx was right about. I'm interested in the logic of the argument, not in what policy should follow. Write your answer in American English.
