I'm a UX researcher who works on growth design — onboarding flows, habit loops, weaning users off bad patterns, that sort of thing. I've been reading about Maimonides' take on the animal sacrifices in the Torah, and I can't shake the feeling that he's doing design. As I understand him, Maimonides thought God never actually wanted the whole slaughter-and-burn system; the Israelites where so shaped by Egypt and the surrounding nations that sacrifice just was what worship meant to them, and cutting it out all at once would have been like telling a user to delete their entire workflow overnight. So God kept the sacrifice feature but pointed it at himself, restricted it to one temple and one priesthood, loaded it with ritual friction, and — on Maimonides' reading — was steering people over centuries toward prayer and inward devotion. Restate that view in my vocabulary: legacy features, transition flows, user habituation, progressive ritual load, stated intent vs. shipped product, maybe dark patterns. Be as faithful as you can. Then give me the residue report. Which parts of Maimonides' view map cleanly onto design concepts, which parts did you have to fudge, and is there anything in his view that design language simply can't say? In particular I want to know whether this translation quietly turns God into a product manager second-guessing his users' capacity, and whether Maimonides himself would have rejected that framing as a distortion.
