For a graduate seminar on practical reasons, I want a case that isolates the difference between there being a reason to perform an action and some agent's having a reason to perform it. The target is a free-floating reason: a decisive normative reason for a particular token action at a particular time, where no agent in the scenario can correctly be said to have that reason. Please construct such a thought experiment to the following specifications.

Scenario constraints. The setting must be ordinary — a small business, a civic committee, a hospital unit, or something similar — with at most three human agents, no group minds, no exotic technology, no perfect information, and no appeal to an omniscient or ideal spectator. The scenario itself should be no more than one paragraph. The reason must attach to a token action (signing a particular document, transferring a particular item, closing a particular gate by 5 p.m.), not to a general state of affairs such as the money's being returned or someone's seeing to it that the gate is closed.

Construct the case so that all of the following hold:

1. There is a decisive reason to perform the action.
2. For every agent in the scenario, "A has a reason to perform the action" is false — not because A happens to be ignorant or unmotivated, but because no agent stands in the relation to the action that would make the reason A's.
3. The reason is not reducible to any agent's reason to bring the action about through a proxy, an instruction, an authorization, or an institutional change; the reason would still be present if every agent capable of such mediation were removed.
4. The reason is action-guiding rather than merely evaluative: it counts in favor of the action's being done, not merely in favor of the outcome's being good, desirable, or required.

The case should generate these verdicts from its structure rather than simply stipulate that the reason has no bearer. Now take these three views and state, for each, the verdict it delivers about the case and the reasoning that leads there:

(i) Reason-first realism, on which "there is a reason to φ" is the fundamental normative unit and "S has a reason to φ" is derivative, so that a reason can exist before any agent stands in the relevant relation to it.
(ii) Possession-first psychologism, on which "S has a reason to φ" is the fundamental unit and "there is a reason to φ" is merely elliptical for some agent's having it, so that a reason without a bearer is no reason at all.
(iii) A deliberative-standpoint view, on which a reason is actual only relative to the standpoint of a possible deliberator, so that absent any such standpoint the favoring relation is at most a potential or would-be reason, not a real one.

Finally, identify the single structural feature of the scenario that is doing the work, say what would happen if that feature were removed — in particular, whether the case would collapse into a familiar external-reason case about an ignorant or unmotivated agent — and give the strongest objection a possession-first psychologistic theorist could press against treating this as a genuine free-floating reason rather than a misleading way of talking about something no one has reason to do.
