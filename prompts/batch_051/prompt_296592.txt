Analyze the French 35-hour workweek — introduced by the Aubry laws of 1998 and 2000, which cut the statutory workweek from 39 to 35 hours — through the lens of Max Weber's Protestant work ethic, with the Japanese phenomenon of karoshi (death from overwork) as a comparative pressure point. I know the standard economic disputes about whether the policy created, destroyed, or merely redistributed work; set those aside. What I want is a disciplined conceptual analysis of this episode in the moral standing of work and leisure.

Take Weber's thesis as a theory about the moral psychology of work: methodical labor in a worldly calling came to function as a duty and a source of moral standing, while idleness carried a stigma, and this religiously formed disposition outlived its theological basis in modern attitudes toward employment. Work through the case with that theory. First, set out the mapping. Who were the actors, and what were the actual options — not only reduce hours or not, but what packages of hours, pay, flexibility, and exemptions were on the table? What evidence would count for or against the claim that the work ethic shaped how the policy was designed, defended, resisted, or experienced?

Second, say what the theory would lead you to expect from a legal reduction of the workweek, and what it would have prescribed by its own lights. Would the actors experience it as liberation, as a loss of status or purpose, or as something mixed? Where would the displaced work discipline reappear — intensification of the remaining hours, overtime, permanent availability, or a two-tier split between exempt employees whose work is treated as a calling and hourly employees whose time is regulated?

Third, say which features of the French episode the thesis explains and which it misses or misdescribes. On one side, the Aubry laws were partly a work-sharing reform under mass unemployment, treating work as a scarce good to be rationed rather than a burden to be minimized — a logic the work-ethic thesis should recognize. On the other side, consider what it cannot comfortably absorb: the state's macroeconomic and electoral motives, union strategy, the exemption of many professional and managerial employees from the 35-hour regime, the gender dimensions of shorter hours, and the question of whether a culture changed or only a legal number did.

Fourth, bring in karoshi. Is it best read as the same ethic pushed to a lethal extreme — work as identity and obligation so total that it overrides health, family, and life — or as a product of organizational coercion, employment insecurity, and Japanese labor-market institutions that a story about inner conviction distorts? What does the karoshi comparison reveal about the limits of the work-ethic lens for understanding the French case?

Finally, run the analysis in the other direction. Does the 35-hour week confirm Weber's thesis, refute it, or require it to be revised — for instance, by showing that a society can shorten working hours while leaving the moral primacy of work intact, or that what persists is less an ethic of work than a hierarchy among kinds of work? End with a last line beginning "Verdict:" followed by a single sentence stating your overall finding about the thesis in light of the case.
