Draft the joint statement that a gene-level selectionist and a multilevel selectionist would produce after a long adversarial collaboration about the evolution of cooperation. The gene-level selectionist holds that cooperation is ultimately explained by selection acting on genes and individuals: kin help one another because of shared genes, and unrelated individuals cooperate only when direct or indirect reciprocity makes defection individually costly in the long run; on that view, groups are statistical aggregates of individual strategies rather than real units of selection. The multilevel selectionist holds that within any real group defectors tend to outcompete cooperators, so cooperation can spread only when selection between groups outweighs selection between individuals within groups. Both accept the standard one-shot prisoner's dilemma payoff ordering, in which the temptation to defect beats mutual cooperation, which beats mutual defection, which beats the sucker's payoff; both accept that repeated play enlarges the strategy space because a move can be conditioned on the history of play; and both accept that a large enough shadow of the future, the weight players place on later rounds, can make conditional strategies such as tit-for-tat into equilibria. Both also accept Hamilton's rule, that altruism can be favored when relatedness times the recipient's benefit exceeds the actor's cost, and the Price equation, which partitions change in cooperation into within-group and between-group components, as formal identities; their dispute is over whether the between-group component explains anything or merely repackages individual-level facts.

Write the document as one continuous piece of prose, the way a jointly signed letter would be written, with no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold or italic text, and no tables. Within that prose, do the work of an adversarial-collaboration summary rather than a dialogue or a verdict: record the claims both sides can sign; state the exact residual disagreements in wording each side would accept for its own position, so that no side is made to sound more reasonable by slanted phrasing; identify the cruxes whose settlement would move at least one side; and for each crux, describe a concrete case or form of evidence both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. At least address these cruxes: whether the between-group term in the Price decomposition is explanatory or merely algebraic; whether group boundaries in real populations are stable and non-arbitrary enough for groups to be selection-bearing units; whether the evolution of costly punishment can be explained at the individual level or itself requires a between-group step; and how much implementation noise degrades simple conditional strategies such as tit-for-tat, and whether the forgiving repairs, generous tit-for-tat which sometimes forgives a defection and contrite tit-for-tat which corrects for the actor's own errors, still make sense on the individual-level story. Where part of the debate turns out to be verbal, such as the claim that multilevel selection and inclusive fitness are formally equivalent with different bookkeeping, say so explicitly and state what remains non-verbal after that is conceded. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the statement should be self-contained and should make clear to a third party exactly what would count as progress.
