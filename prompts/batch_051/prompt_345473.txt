For the past year I have been designing a study on individual differences in voluntary mental imagery, and in doing so I stepped into a quarrel my department has been having for decades. The argument is the familiar depictive-versus-descriptive one often associated with Kosslyn and Pylyshyn. One side says that when people rotate a figure in their head or scan across a remembered map, the time it takes scales with distance or angle because the mind is really moving over a picture-like representation, and that tells us something real about the format of the underlying code. The other side says those results can be explained by what participants tacitly know about how vision and physical pictures work, together with their expectations about the task, so the performance tells us nothing about whether the underlying code is pictorial. On this second view the code could be symbolic or descriptive, and introspection is the wrong instrument for reading off its format. Both sides treat the other's objections as long since answered, and neither has moved in years.

What unsettles me is not deciding which side is right, but that I cannot tell what kind of disagreement I am watching. Sometimes it looks like a verbal dispute about what should count as an image or as pictorial. Sometimes it looks like the two sides are answering different questions: one about what experience is like from the inside, one about what code the brain is running. Sometimes it looks as though a hidden empirical question—about what mental rotation and scanning tasks actually measure—is doing all the work, and no one has designed the experiment that would settle it. My own study forces me to commit to one side's methods and interpretation, and my committee members disagree about which commitment is defensible.

I am asking for a diagnosis rather than a verdict. What kind of disagreement is this: verbal, factual, methodological, about which cases count as data, or between starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? And given that diagnosis, what would count as progress—a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a change of question—or should we expect no movement, and why?
