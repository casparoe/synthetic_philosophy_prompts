Analyze Volkswagen's diesel-emissions fraud through the lens of Hannah Arendt's account of Eichmann and the banality of evil, and then tell me where the mapping breaks down. I do not need a summary of either the scandal or the book; I want a disciplined case analysis that runs in both directions, using the theory to illuminate the case and the case to test the theory.

Take the banality thesis not as the loose slogan that ordinary people can do terrible things, but as a set of specific claims. On Arendt's telling, Eichmann was not a sadist or a fanatic; what she thought she saw was a failure of thinking — an inability to imagine the world from someone else's standpoint, a mind stocked with clichés and officialese, absorbed in the administrative task of moving people from place to place. For her, that thoughtlessness was not a mitigating circumstance but the core of the offense, and it had to be answered with individual judgment rather than dissolved into role-playing. Fold in the further distinction in her work between personal moral-legal guilt and collective political responsibility, because the corporate setting will put the most pressure on it. Set aside the historical dispute about whether Arendt underestimated Eichmann's own ideological commitment; the question here is whether the Arendtian description can get any grip on a different kind of case.

The scandal, as I understand the facts, is this. From roughly 2009 to 2015, Volkswagen sold diesel cars whose engine-control software detected when the car was undergoing emissions testing and switched into a compliant mode, while in normal driving the cars emitted nitrogen oxides far above legal limits. The defeat device was not the work of a small rogue group: it was engineered, tested, and kept secret across teams and through outside suppliers over several years, in service of a strategy to sell diesel cars as both clean and high-performing. The scheme was exposed by an environmental NGO working with academic researchers, not by Volkswagen's own compliance or testing procedures. The company eventually admitted the deception and set aside billions for fines and settlements.

Now map the Arendtian claims onto the episode as precisely as you can. Who, if anyone, occupies the Eichmann position — and is it one position or several? For each relevant level of the company — design engineers, compliance staff, middle managers, senior executives — say what the theory predicts about why people went along, and what the theory would have demanded of them instead. Then say where thoughtlessness genuinely illuminates the case and where it fails. The obvious difficulty is that the defeat device had to be designed with technical sophistication, concealed for years, and protected from the company's own oversight mechanisms: that looks more like organized and intelligent wrongdoing than an absence of thinking. Meet that difficulty head-on rather than explaining it away. Use the distinction between personal guilt and political responsibility to sort out how responsibility should be distributed: what is the difference between the guilt of the engineer who wrote the code and the responsibility of a manager who plausibly did not know the details, and does the distinction have any purchase when the relevant system is a corporation with profit targets rather than a state bureaucracy? Finally, say what the episode does to the theory. Does it confirm the banality thesis, narrow its scope, or expose a rival explanation for what happened — and which features of the case push hardest against reading it as ordinary people failing to think?
