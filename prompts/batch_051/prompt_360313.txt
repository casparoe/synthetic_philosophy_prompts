I cover science and ideas for a living, and last month at a behavioral-economics conference I ended up at the hotel bar next to two econimists who were going at it about whether the sunk-cost fallacy is always a fallacy. I took notes on my phone, so this is pretty close to verbatim.

One of them—Dana, the more orthodox one—said: A friend of mine won't drop a master's program she actively dislikes because she's already finished two years of it. She actually said she can't let those years be wasted. I told her: if you wouldn't sign up for the final year as a standalone program, at the same price and with the same classes and job prospects, then finishing just to avoid "wasting" the first two years is irrational. The years are already gone; they can't be wasted, they can just be over.

The other, Priya, answered: No, the comparison is rigged. Two years in, she has a half-done thesis, an adviser who's written her letters, specific skills, and relationships that only pay off if she completes. A stranger enrolling fresh in that final year wouldn't have any of that. So "would you start it from scratch?" isn't the relevant test; the past investment changed what completing the last year is worth.

Dana came back: But everything you just listed is a future benefit of continuing, not the sunk cost itself. Fine—count those. But then the real reason to stay is those future payoffs, and the moment they don't cover the cost of the last year, the fact that she's spent two years on it adds nothing. So my point stands: the past spending itself is doing no work.

Priya shrugged: Analytically, sure. But deciding isn't analysis. Time already spent is a noisy signal for how close you are to the payoff and how much the project matters to you. Most people can't compute future benefits from scratch when they're tired and unsure, and a rule like "two years in, you're probably near the payoff" can beat winging a fresh future-only calculation. Calling that a fallacy because it sometimes misfires is like calling overbooking irrational because some flights end up oversold.

Dana frowned: Then you've dissolved the fallacy rather than refuted it. If leaning on past investment is justified whenever it beats explicit calculation, then "ignore sunk costs" is either false or just another way of saying "calculate carefully"—and every case where someone finishes a hated degree because of the years behind them gets relabeled as a wiser-than-it-looks heuristic. One of those two readings has to give.

- `move`: 1 through 5
- `speaker`: Dana or Priya
- `claim`: their position in one sentence
- `rating`: one of `clearly mistaken`, `mostly mistaken`, `partly right`, `mostly right`, `clearly right`
- `justification`: your reason, one or two sentences

After the table, give a `missing_from_both` paragraph (under 150 words) and a one-sentence `bottom_line` field.
