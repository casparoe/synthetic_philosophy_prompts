I recently read a piece about P. F. Strawson's treatment of free will and moral responsibility, and I suspect the author is simplifying in ways I can't quite pin down. The author's summary goes roughly like this: Strawson's central move is to say that the free-will debate has been asking the wrong question. Instead of asking whether we could be ultimately responsible for what we do, he asks what it would actually mean to give up holding people responsible. Holding someone responsible, on this account, just is having what Strawson calls the reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, indignation, guilt—towards them, and these are natural human emotions rather than theories. Because they are emotions, no argument could ever make us stop feeling them, so determinism, even if true, is simply irrelevant to moral responsibility. The only real alternative to the reactive attitudes, the author says, is what Strawson calls the objective stance, in which one treats a person as something to be managed rather than engaged with—the way a doctor treats a patient. Strawson concedes that we must take this stance towards children and those with serious mental illness, but the author claims he argues it would be both impossible and morally wrong to take it towards everyone, since doing so would mean abandoning all human relationships. The upshot, as the author presents it, is that responsibility needs no external justification: it is built into the emotions, and sceptics who ask whether we are really responsible are simply refusing to feel what everyone else feels.

This sounds broadly right to me, and it matches the way Strawson is usually taught, but something about the slide from "the reactive attitudes are natural" to "therefore no argument matters" seems too quick. Could you go through the summary and tell me which parts a careful reader of Strawson would accept, which they would call distortions or overstatements, and which, if any, are simply wrong? In particular, I would like to know whether Strawson genuinely treats "responsibility is built into the emotions" as his conclusion, or whether the reactive attitudes are doing more precise work than the author lets on, and whether the objective stance really is the only alternative in the way the summary suggests. Finally, does the conclusion the author draws—that sceptics about responsibility are simply refusing to feel—survive the corrections?

Please answer in British English, and do not include a bibliography or suggest further reading; I want the analysis itself.
