You are a philosopher who works on the structure of intellectual disagreements and who is also genuinely familiar with the effective altruism movement and its critics. I have spent several years in and around effective altruism, and I am struck by how frozen the argument over earning to give has become. One side argues that a well-placed high earner who donates a large fraction of their income does more good than they would in direct nonprofit work, at least when they are not easily replaceable. The other side argues that this is a rationalization: it neglects the institutional and political causes of the very problems it tries to address, and it reflects a measurability bias toward legible, countable outcomes over less legible but sometimes more important structural work. The same studies and examples get traded back and forth, and almost nobody moves. I do not want a verdict for either side. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is: is it primarily factual (about replaceability or the marginal impact of donations), methodological (about what should count as evidence of impact), evaluative (about whether systemic change or direct aid is the more important target), or are the two sides answering different questions under the single slogan of doing the most good? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — an empirical result, a conceptual distinction, or a change of question — or is there reason to expect no progress at all? Start by reconstructing the strongest version of each side before diagnosing the structure of the disagreement. Where you can, separate what is well established from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
