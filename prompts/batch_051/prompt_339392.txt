I run a workshop for early-career science journalists — around 18 people, one 75-minute block. I want an activity that makes them feel why a vivid coincidence ('my aunt called the instant I thought of her', 'two people in my yoga class had the same rare cancer') is terrible evidence for a hidden connection, even though it feels overwhelming in the moment. Not a lecture on probability: they should stumble into the coincidence themselves and be tempted to tell the 'there must be something going on' story before it gets dismantled. Design a game or simulation — rules, materials (index cards or a shared spreadsheet, nothing fancy), a timeline, and a debrief mapping what happened onto the birthday paradox and the idea that with enough people and enough events, striking matches are basically guarenteed. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be straight about where the analogy strains: the birthday paradox concerns a matching pair among many possible pairs, while a real anecdote is a specific striking match noticed after the fact — does the activity genuinely earn that harder point, or do I have to just assert it? And tell me what would change your mind about the whole design — which participant objection would make you think the activity teaches a math trick instead of the epistemology of anecdote.
