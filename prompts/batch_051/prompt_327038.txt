I am preparing a unit on the ethics of complicity, and I would like a close philosophical reading of Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day. I will frame complicity as the way an agent may become answerable for wrongdoing she does not initiate or cause — through facilitating it, carrying out instructions, lending it an appearance of normality, or remaining silent when a protest is called for. Stevens does not cause Lord Darlington's appeasement diplomacy or the anti-Semitic episodes in the house, and he seems to share none of Darlington's aims; he only performs his professional role, with a conception of dignity that trains him to suppress his own judgment. I want you to argue for and against the claim that the novel presents him as complicit in Darlington's wrongdoing, and then defend a verdict. Attend especially to the episode in which the two Jewish housemaids are dismissed: Stevens carries out the order without protest while Miss Kenton objects and threatens resignation. What, if anything, makes his part or his silence wrongful, and does the novel endorse or undermine his contrast between dignity and ineffectual opinion? Consider as well the narration itself: Stevens re-describes his own conduct in self-exculpating terms, so the form of the novel seems built around how complicity hides from itself. Conclude by saying what the novel adds to a philosophical treatment of complicity that arguments about causation and intention alone would miss, and where the work resists the reading.
