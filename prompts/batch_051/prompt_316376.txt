I'm on the board of my housing co-op and this has gotten weirdly theoretical. We have a rule that guests cant stay more than four nights a month. Everyone agrees it's annoying and nobody remembers why it was put there. I proposed getting rid of it, and another board member hit me with Chesterton's fence: don't tear down a fence until you know why it was built. She's a bit of a small-b Burkean about this stuff and says old arrangements are owed a presumption because they're inherited, not invented. Fine. But then she turned it into an argument, and the more I look at it the more it seems to prove way too much. It goes like this:

(1) If a rule or custom has stuck around for years, there's usually some actual reason it was put there, or at least why it survived.
(2) If you don't know what that reason is, you aren't in a position to say it no longer applies.
(3) If you aren't in a position to say it no longer applies, you shouldn't remove the rule.
(4) We don't know why the guest rule exists.
(5) So we shouldn't remove it.

But the same argument covers every old rule we don't fully understand, which is a lot of them. Worse, it would have covered every old arrangement that the people who made our current rules replaced. On this logic the current guest rule could never have gone up, because whoever made it was changing a prior arrangement whose reasons they probably didn't fully know either. So a principle that defends the fence seems to forbid the fence from ever having been built. That has to be too strong, but I cant figure out where exactly the screw turns. It feels like the argument converts "there might be a reason" into a permanent veto, which is confusing due diligence with a stopping rule.

I don't want to be the guy who dismisses Chesterton's fence just because it's inconvenient. I'd like to keep the modest point that old rules often encode information and you should look for it before you tear them out. But the argument as stated gets us to "your dumb overnight-guest rule is basically unrepealable," and that can't be right.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Which premise do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? And is there a version of (2) or (3) that keeps the caution but doesn't collapse into a veto?

Oh, and please write your answer in American English.
