I'm a high school student trying to settle, for an essay I'm writing, whether the profit motive — as it operates specifically under capitalism — is the root cause of the dangers from advanced AI, or whether those dangers are better explained as a coordination problem that would arise under any system in which several actors can build powerful systems and none can safely slow down alone. I'd like you to compare these two positions, with both sides stated at full strength, and then defend the one you think better accounts for the specific dangers of advanced AI — in particular the pressure to deploy AI systems whose capabilities outrun our ability to ensure they do what we intend.

The comparison should turn on the following passage from Karl Marx's Grundrisse, from the section usually called the "Fragment on Machines" (translated by Martin Nicolaus):

"The exchange of living labour for objectified labour – i.e. the positing of social labour in the form of the contradiction of capital and wage labour – is the ultimate development of the value-relation and of production resting on value. Its presupposition is – and remains – the mass of direct labour time, the quantity of labour employed, as the determinant factor in the production of wealth. But to the degree that large industry develops, the creation of real wealth comes to depend less on labour time and on the amount of labour employed than on the power of the agencies set in motion during labour time, whose ‘powerful effectiveness’ is itself in turn out of all proportion to the direct labour time spent on their production, but depends rather on the general state of science and on the progress of technology, or the application of this science to production."

Here are the two positions as I understand them. The first reads the passage as a diagnosis of capitalism. Capital is caught in a structural contradiction: it is constantly driven to automate — to reduce the labour time that goes into production — while it continues to treat labour time as the measure and source of wealth. On this view, advanced AI is the latest expression of that drive, and the reason it is developed and deployed faster than safety can keep up with is that under capitalism the direction, speed, and uses of the technology are dictated by the pursuit of profit. The root cause of AI risk is therefore specifically capitalist.

The second position grants that the passage describes a real long-run historical shift — production coming to depend more on the progress of science and technology than on quantities of direct labour — but denies that this establishes capitalism as the root cause of AI risk. The decisive dangers come instead from more general features: whenever multiple groups can each build powerful systems, and each knows that slowing down means falling behind the others, you get racing dynamics, incentives to cut safety corners, and pressure to deploy before you fully understand what you have. On this view, those incentives would plausibly reappear under any economic system that still had separate labs, firms, or states competing while the downside of losing the race looked severe, so the root cause is a coordination failure rather than capitalism.
