Me and a friend where going back and forth about the semantics of "if" the other day, and he pointed me to Lewis's triviality results, which I've now gone a little too deep into. As I understand it, the result is something like this: if you treat an indicative conditional "if A then C" as a regular proposition whose probability behaves normally, and you also insist that its probability should equal the conditional probability P(C|A), then under some pretty mild assumptions the whole thing collapses — you end up forced into trivial values, with everything independent or pinned to 0 or 1 or something like that. My friend took this to show that we literally cannot have degrees of confidence in ordinary conditionals, and that whenever I say something like "it's probably true that if the test comes back positive I should start treatment," I'm not actually doing anything coherent.

I'm skeptical, because in medical and legal contexts people reason with such if-then claims all the time and it really doesnt feel like empty talk. So what exactly did Lewis show? Does it actually mean we can't assign probabilities to conditionals in everyday life, or is that a misreading? And if it is a misreading, what's the right way to make sense of partial confidence in a conditional — is there a view that keeps probabilities meaningful without collapsing into triviality?
