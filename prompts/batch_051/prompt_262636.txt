George Berkeley's monetary writings — principally the series of queries published in the 1730s as The Querist — press a distinctive view: a nation's wealth consists in the industry, skill, and labor of its inhabitants rather than in its stock of gold and silver; money is at bottom a kind of ticket, counter, or circulating credit whose function is to command that industry; and a well-designed paper currency, issued through a public national bank, can perform that function at least as well as scarce specie, and often better. From these commitments he took a dim view of hoarding and of treating the precious metals as intrinsically valuable.

Now suppose Berkeley could be shown three features of the contemporary monetary world, all unknown in his day: (1) fiat currencies that are not convertible into any commodity, along with central bank digital currencies, in which the state's promise rather than a commodity anchors trust; (2) cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin, whose supply is algorithmically fixed and which are issued by no public authority; and (3) the modern account on which commercial banks create much of the money supply endogenously when they extend loans. Reasoning as consistently as possible from the commitments above, what verdict would Berkeley most likely reach about each — an improvement on the spirit of his proposals, a misunderstanding of what money is, or a development his framework cannot comfortably classify? Where do his commitments pull in different directions, and at what point do his eighteenth-century assumptions cease to be a reliable guide?
