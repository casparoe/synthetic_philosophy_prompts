I am a retired physical chemist who has come to philosophy only recently, and I have been working through the problem of how identity statements can be informative. I have drifted into the following set of commitments, and I would like them audited for joint consistency before I present them to a reading group.

1. The sole semantic contribution of a proper name is its bearer; a sentence expresses a structured proposition whose constituents are the objects and properties referred to by its parts.
2. Because of (1), 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' expresses the very same proposition as 'Hesperus is Hesperus': Venus's identity with itself.
3. Nevertheless, the two sentences differ in cognitive value. The first could express a genuine discovery; the second could not.
4. In ordinary extensional contexts, replacing one coreferential name with another never changes truth value.
5. In belief ascriptions this is not so: Lois may believe that Hesperus is visible in the evening while failing to believe that Phosphorus is visible in the evening.
6. A belief ascription relates a subject to the proposition expressed by the embedded clause.
7. The difference in (3) is a genuine semantic difference that a theory of meaning should explain; it cannot be written off as mere psychology or mere pragmatics.

I cannot tell where this package breaks down. Which pairs or triples are in genuine tension, and how severe is each? Which conflicts are only apparent? What is the least costly single revision, and what does it cost?
