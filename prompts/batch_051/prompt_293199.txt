For a novel I am drafting, I need a moral argument worked through with real rigor rather than the kind of summary a bright undergraduate could reproduce. My central character is deciding whether to have a child, and the scene turns on an asymmetry I find in ordinary thought and want either justified or exposed.

The asymmetry is this. Nearly everyone treats the prospect that a child would have a life of terrible suffering as a decisive reason not to bring that child into existence: a life so bad that the person would be better off never existing should not be started. Yet almost no one treats the prospect that a child would have a wonderfully happy life as a symmetrically weighty reason to bring that child into existence: a couple may decide not to conceive a child who would have had a splendid life, and virtually no one thinks they have wronged the absent child or made the world worse. The possible miserable life seems to generate a command; the possible happy life seems to generate only an option.

I need the strongest available account of whether this asymmetry can be defended, or whether it is an inconsistency we simply live with.

Please write, as the intellectual backbone of the scene, a sustained analysis in this order. First, state the asymmetry precisely enough that its two halves cannot be explained away by easier distinctions such as act versus omission, causing versus allowing, or doing harm versus failing to benefit. Then reconstruct the best candidate justification for it, whichever side you ultimately take; consider at least a person-affecting explanation (reasons must be reasons for someone), a view that gives special or exclusive weight to the avoidance of suffering, and the thought that obligations extend only to those we could wrong. Next, test that candidate against three cases that appear in veiled form in the novel:

(a) a couple who can conceive now or later, where conceiving now yields a child with a moderately good but limited life and waiting yields a different child with a much better life — does the asymmetry imply that choosing the first child would not be wrong at all, and is that a reductio or a consequence to accept;

(b) a life that is overall very good but contains stretches of serious and entirely foreseeable suffering — does the explanation you favor end up making ordinary, well-intentioned procreation impermissible in too many cases;

(c) an already existing person whose remaining future would be, on balance, more suffering than happiness, but who wants to go on living — the standard asymmetry concerns beginning lives, so say whether your justification extends to continuing or ending them, and if it does not, what difference between existing and merely possible people prevents that extension without circularity.

Conclude with a verdict: is this asymmetry one we should keep, reverse, or give up, and, on your verdict, what should a reasonable version of my character conclude about having the child?
