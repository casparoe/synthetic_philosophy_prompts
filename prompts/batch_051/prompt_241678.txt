In my course on direct democracy and referendums, a classmate offered the following argument; I have tried to reconstruct it faithfully and cannot locate where it goes wrong.

(1) What is supposed to give a referendum its special democratic authority is that the people directly author the law, rather than merely electing someone else to do so.
(2) If direct authorization by the governed is what confers democratic authority, then any statute directly authorized by popular vote would possess that same authority, and a system that put more of its laws to a direct vote would be more democratically authoritative.
(3) So, on this view, the ideal democracy would submit essentially every law to a binding popular vote.
(4) But almost no defender of Swiss initiatives or California ballot propositions accepts that conclusion: emergency appropriations, technically detailed tax rules, and rights-protective statutes are often deliberately shielded from majority votes, and we do not regard that shielding as a democratic defect.
(5) Therefore the defender of referendums must either abandon the idea that direct authorization confers authority and admit that referendums are merely one filtered instrument among others, or accept an all-laws plebiscite that almost everyone rejects.

I would most like to keep both the idea that referendums can have genuine democratic value and the idea that representative institutions should decide most ordinary statutes. Which step or inference should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost?
