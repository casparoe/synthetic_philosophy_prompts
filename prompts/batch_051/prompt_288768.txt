I am a novelist, currently at work on a book set in a recently founded city-state whose founding charter commits the state to equalizing something among its citizens, but deliberately leaves the something unspecified. The politics of the novel turns on the factions that form around rival answers to the question "equality of what?" I have been reading political philosophy in order to make these factions plausible, and I have assembled a list of the positions I already understand well enough to dramatize. I do not need those repeated; I need something I can attach to a new faction.

The positions I already have are these:

- Equality of welfare: the state should aim to make people equally happy, or equal in the satisfaction of their preferences.
- Equality of resources, in the Dworkinian spirit: people should begin with equal shares of transferable resources, and differences in their productive talents should be corrected through something like a hypothetical insurance market, after which the results of their choices are their own affair.
- Equality of primary goods, in the Rawlsian manner: the state should equalize the all-purpose means — rights, liberties, opportunities, income and wealth, and the social bases of self-respect — that any rational person is presumed to want, whatever their particular plan of life.
- Equality of capabilities: people should be made equal not in resources or felt satisfaction, but in their real freedom to do and be the things they have reason to value.
- Equality of opportunity for welfare: people need not end up equally well off, but they must begin with equal chances to obtain welfare, so that any remaining inequality in welfare traces to genuine choice rather than unchosen circumstance.
- Equal access to advantage: advantage, understood as something broader than either welfare or resources alone, is the right target of equality, and people should be made equal not in their final advantage but in their access to it.

I would like a seventh candidate, the kind of view a character in my novel could advocate. It must be a genuine answer to the question "equality of what?", and it must not be on this list: that is, it must not be a restatement of one of these six, a special case of one of them, or a combination of two or more of them. It may be an existing position from the philosophical literature that I have omitted, or a position you construct yourself, provided it is coherent enough to survive being stated by an intelligent character in a novel.

Having offered it, tell me honestly whether the view is defensible. In particular, say how it handles the objection that it may reward expensive tastes or punish diligence (the standard worry for welfarist views), and the objection that it may mistake a mere means for the end (the standard worry for resourcist views). If it is not defensible, say so rather than dressing it up.
