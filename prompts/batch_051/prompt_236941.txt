I teach a graduate seminar on common knowledge and rationality, and I've noticed I've got a habit. When someone—a colleague, a grad student—tells me there's a fatal problem with a paper, but can't produce a faithful, strong version of the paper's argument in the moment, I discount the objection almost to zero. The thought is: in a group of broadly rational agents with common priors and common knowledge of rationality, if a really good objection existed, it would already be public—silence is information. But I also know the formal no-trade and agreeing-to-disagree results only go through under very delicate common-knowledge assumptions, and a real department isn't a formal structure. Is my discounting policy defensible, or am I confusing a stylized equilibrium result with how actual epistemic agents should behave? What exact conditions would make the absence of a publicly articulated objection evidence that there is no objection, and are those conditions ever satisfied enough in a real seminar room to justify how heavily I discount?
