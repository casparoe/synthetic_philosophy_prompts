For the past year I have been in a reading group on vagueness, and I keep coming away from our discussions convinced that the two dominant positions—epistemicism and supervaluationism—have reached a standstill, even though they seem to share nearly all the relevant linguistic data. I would like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, rather than a verdict on who is right.

Both sides agree on the surface phenomena. In a Sorites series, competent speakers cannot identify a single grain whose removal turns a heap into a non-heap. For many intermediate items, speakers feel unable to assert either that the item is a heap or that it is not. And the tolerance principle—one grain cannot make the difference between being a heap and not being one—is overwhelmingly compelling when considered case by case. Both sides also end up rejecting the tolerance principle in its universal form, but for different reasons.

Epistemicists hold that the semantics of vague predicates is classical. There really is a sharp cutoff for "heap," but we cannot know where it is: knowledge requires a margin for error, and any belief about the exact cutoff would be too close to cases that would falsify it under slight variation. Borderline claims are therefore either true or false, and our inability to say which is a purely epistemic limitation. The cost is a commitment to semantic facts that are in principle beyond our reach and that look arbitrary from any standpoint a speaker can occupy.

Supervaluationists hold instead that a vague predicate is only partially defined. There are many admissible ways to make it precise, and a sentence is true simpliciter just in case it is true on all of them, false simpliciter just in case it is false on all of them, and otherwise neither true nor false. Since every admissible precisification of the predicate draws a boundary somewhere in a Sorites series, the universal tolerance premise is false on every precisification and hence false simpliciter, even though no particular instance is false—each is true on some admissible precisifications and false on others. The view preserves classical consequence while giving up bivalence and truth-functionality: a disjunction can be supertrue even when neither disjunct is supertrue. The cost is the apparatus of admissible precisifications. If there is a definite set of admissible ways to sharpen a predicate, then "admissible" itself seems to have a sharp boundary, and the problem reappears as higher-order vagueness.

As I have watched the debate, each side's central move is to argue that the other side has merely displaced the mystery. Epistemicists say the hidden boundary is the price of keeping language classical and content truth-functional; supervaluationists say gaps and non-truth-functionality are the price of not making meaning radically verification-transcendent. Each insists the other's revision is more costly. Neither side, as far as I can tell, can point to a piece of linguistic behavior the other cannot accommodate, and neither is moved by the other's cost–benefit arithmetic.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement has stalled because it is not really about what English is like. It is about which theoretical virtues and costs should govern semantic theorizing: classical logic and bivalence set against a semantics that respects the epistemic limits of speakers and the apparent seamlessness of vague categories. But I keep doubting this diagnosis, because the two sides also describe the semantic facts differently in the first place—sharp boundaries exist or do not—and that sounds like a substantive disagreement rather than a dispute about explanatory taste.

Could you diagnose what kind of disagreement this is? Say whether it is substantive, verbal, or a difference in explanatory standards; where exactly the two positions contradict each other as opposed to merely trading costs; what, if anything, would count as progress; and whether we should expect the debate to remain stalled.
