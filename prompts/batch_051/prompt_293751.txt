I'm a journalist on the ideas beat, so I read a lot of arguments like Glaucon's in the Republic and then find myself seeing them in the news. The passage I keep chewing on is from Book II. Glaucon says something like: give two people, one just and one unjust, the power to be invisible, and before long their behavior is indistinguishable—the just one helps himself to whatever he wants, enters houses, sleeps with whoever, frees prisoners, as if he were a god among men. Glaucon's conclusion is that no one is just willingly; people are just only because they're compelled, and remove the fear of consequences and the mask slips. (That's my paraphrase, not a translation.)

The real case I want to put next to it is the LIBOR rate-fixing scandal of roughly 2008–2012. Traders at major banks were colluding to move an interest-rate benchmark that underpins a huge amount of global lending, and they did it in private chats with an almost cartoonish belief they wouldn't get caught. Analyze that episode through Glaucon's lens: what does his argument predict correctly, where does it strain or misdescribe what actually happened, and what should the scandal do to his deeper claim about justice being only a matter of external pressure? Go both directions—let the theory illuminate the case and let the case test the theory. If I'm picking the wrong case or reading Glaucon badly, just say so instead of forcing it.
