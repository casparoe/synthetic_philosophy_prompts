I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but I've been turning over a parallel between the raven paradox and the way we reason about test confidence, and I'd like help deciding whether it's substantive or just pattern-matching.

As I understand the paradox: a generalization like 'all ravens are black' is confirmed by its positive instances, black ravens. But that generalization is logically equivalent to 'all non-black things are non-ravens,' so a white shoe — a non-black, non-raven thing — ought to confirm that all ravens are black, if logically equivalent claims are confirmed by the same observations. Most people want to resist that last step, and the puzzle is to explain why without giving up some very basic principle about how instances support generalizations.

I keep seeing the same shape in testing. Suppose I want confidence in a property like 'every request that reaches the parser is either handled or logged.' A passing test that drives a normal request through the parser is a black raven: a direct positive instance. The proper white-shoe analogue would have to be a case that is neither handled nor logged and does not reach the parser — something like a request silently dropped at the edge. My first instinct was that the white shoe is simply a passing test in an unrelated module, but I now think that's wrong, because in such a test the request is handled, just by other code, so it isn't a non-handled, non-parser instance at all. The edge-dropped request, though, does fit the contrapositive, and I find myself wanting to say it confirms nothing about the parser even though the logic seems to say otherwise — which is exactly the paradox's pull. My suspicion is that the standard moves from confirmation theory (background knowledge about reference classes, confirmation as comparative, whatever the Nicod criterion has to become once you restrict it to relevant instances) are doing the same work as the engineer's insistence that confidence about a code path requires exercising that code path rather than merely not violating the property. Is this a genuine structural identity, a loose analogy, or a confusion? If it is mostly real, does anything from confirmation theory transfer back to how I should reason about regression suites, coverage, and how much a passing test actually licenses? If it is mostly not real, tell me precisely where the structures come apart — for instance, whether the finite and enumerable sample space of a codebase, or the fact that we know the program's structure in advance, gives the engineer something the ornithologist lacks. Please don't preserve the analogy if it's flawed, and please don't let a mistaken premise pass silently: if I've misstated the paradox or set up the testing side in a way that can't be answered, say so plainly, and indicate which parts of your answer are settled in confirmation theory and which are your own reconstruction.
