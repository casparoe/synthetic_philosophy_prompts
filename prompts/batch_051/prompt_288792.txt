I am a doctoral student working on formal models of emotion regulation, and I keep returning to a parallel I cannot settle. In control engineering, a negative-feedback regulator is specified by a set point and an error signal: the design objective is nothing but the elimination of discrepancy, and the system has no representation of a positive state to accumulate — success is just sustained low error. A family of ethical views, call it tranquilism, claims a structurally similar thing about well-being: only the elimination of craving and suffering has intrinsic value, while positive states figure at most instrumentally or as fallible indicators that the disturbance is gone. On the mapping I have in mind, craving and suffering are the error signal, the undisturbed state is the set point, and corrective practices are the control action. Is this a deep point about the logic of value — that well-being is better modeled as regulation toward a neutral baseline than as maximization of a positive magnitude — or is the parallel superficial? For instance, a real regulator treats deviation on either side of the set point as error, which seems to predict that intense pleasure would count as a disturbance to be corrected; does that false prediction sink the analogy, and is anything worth keeping either way?
