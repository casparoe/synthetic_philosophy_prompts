I’m writing a paper on whether the naturalistic fallacy is one fallacy or several, and I’m trying to get a working definition that is more precise than the loose way the term gets used in applied ethics. In *Principia Ethica*, G. E. Moore argues that no proposed identification of “good” with a natural object—pleasure, being more evolved, being desired—can be correct, because for any such candidate X it always remains an open and substantive question whether X is itself good, whereas if “good” simply meant X the question would be closed; he calls the mistake of identifying good with a natural object the naturalistic fallacy. I’d like a working definition of that fallacy that lets me decide whether each of the following commits it, and whether it is best understood as a definitional mistake, a kind of invalid inference, or a distinctively metaphysical error:

1. A hedonist defines “good” as “pleasant.”
2. An evolutionary ethicist argues that because moral dispositions evolved to support group cooperation, cooperative actions are good.
3. A divine command theorist says “good” means “commanded by God.”
4. A Cornell-style reductive naturalist says “good” rigidly designates a natural property, with the identity discovered empirically, as with water and H₂O.
5. An emotivist says “good” expresses approval rather than ascribing any property at all.
6. A policy commentator argues that because jealousy and ingroup preference are natural human dispositions, institutional attempts to eliminate them are doomed and misguided.

Run your definition against each case, say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and then either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. In particular, I’d like to know whether your definition also captures the gap between descriptive premises and normative conclusions, or whether that is a separate problem.
