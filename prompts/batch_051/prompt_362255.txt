I am working through the philosophical literature on psychiatric classification, and before I read another defense of realism, social constructionism, or a purely neurobiological account of mental disorder, I want the specification that any adequate theory of classification has to satisfy. Write the desiderata: the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the historical or empirical findings it must be consistent with. For each requirement, give the consideration or case that makes it a requirement, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or a demand of a particular camp. Then say which single requirement most decisively excludes the currently popular views, and whether any two requirements appear to be jointly unsatisfiable — that last point, if true, is the part I most need to see.

Anchor at least one requirement in this passage from Ian Hacking, "Making Up People," London Review of Books (2006). Your use of the passage should be interpretive, not decorative: show what it forces a theory of psychiatric classification to accommodate that a purely biomedical account or a purely conventionalist account would miss.

This story can be placed in a five-part framework. We have (a) a classification, multiple personality, associated with what at the time was called a ‘disorder’. This kind of person is now a moving target. We have (b) the people, those I call ‘unhappy’, ‘unable to cope’, or whatever relatively non-judgmental term you might prefer. There are (c) institutions, which include clinics, annual meetings of the International Society for the Study of Multiple Personality and Dissociation, afternoon talkshows on television (Oprah Winfrey and Geraldo Rivera made a big thing of multiples, once upon a time), and weekend training programmes for therapists, some of which I attended. There is (d) the knowledge: not justified true belief, once the mantra of analytic philosophers, but knowledge in Popper’s sense of conjectural knowledge, and, more specifically, the presumptions that are taught, disseminated and refined within the context of the institutions. Especially the basic facts (not ‘so-called facts’, or ‘facts’ in scare-quotes): for example, that multiple personality is caused by early sexual abuse, that 5 per cent of the population suffer from it, and the like. There is expert knowledge, the knowledge of the professionals, and there is popular knowledge, shared by a significant part of the interested population. There was a time, partly thanks to those talkshows and other media, when ‘everyone’ believed that multiple personality was caused by early sexual abuse. Finally, there are (e) the experts or professionals who generate (d) the knowledge, judge its validity, and use it in their practice. They work within (c) institutions that guarantee their legitimacy, authenticity and status as experts. They study, try to help, or advise on the control of (b) the people who are (a) classified as of a given kind.

I want the criteria themselves stressed, not another summary of DSM debates. For each of the following, say whether it is a genuine desideratum or a prejudice of one camp, and why: the requirement that a theory distinguish kinds that are changed by being classified (interactive kinds) from kinds that are indifferent to their classification; the requirement that it explain why some classifications seem to multiply their own instances, as multiple personality did in the 1980s, while others burn out or vanish, as the late nineteenth-century fugue epidemic seems largely to have done; the requirement that it distinguish a category's being constructed from its being merely fictional or unreal; the requirement that it explain what makes a revision of a classification an improvement, given that the earlier classification has already changed the people it picks out; and the requirement that it remain consistent with the comorbidity and heterogeneity observed within psychiatric categories. Do not merely list these; adjudicate them and defend the non-negotiable core.

If you include a scorecard of the main current contenders, keep it compressed — the list itself is the deliverable.

If the task as I have posed it is ill-posed — if "psychiatric classification" is too heterogeneous to have one set of adequacy conditions, or if Hacking's framework cannot bear the weight I want to put on it — say so plainly and give the corrected question, rather than forcing an answer. Write in American English.
