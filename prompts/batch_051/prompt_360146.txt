In my politics of language seminar we've actualy been stuck on Orwell for two weeks now, and I honestly can't tell whether the room is disagreeing about the text or about something else. The passage everyone keeps coming back to is this, from Orwell's "Politics and the English Language":

"But an effect can become a cause, reinforcing the original cause and producing the same effect in an intensified form, and so on indefinitely. A man may take to drink because he feels himself to be a failure, and then fail all the more completely because he drinks. It is rather the same thing that is happening to the English language. It becomes ugly and inaccurate because our thoughts are foolish, but the slovenliness of our language makes it easier for us to have foolish thoughts."

Half the class reads this as Orwell making a strong causal claim: linguistic slovenliness actively corrupts thought, so cleaning up language is itself a political intervention. The other half reads it as him insisting the ultimate causes are political and economic, with language only reacting and feeding back. We can't settle whether he thinks language has independent causal power or is just a loop attached to deeper causes. My working theory is that the stall isn't really about misreading Orwell; it's that the two sides have different ideas about what would count as evidence for a claim about language influencing thought. Is that right? What kind of disagreement is this actually—an empirical one about how language shapes cognition, a hermeneutic one about how to read a writer as slippery as Orwell, or a normative one about what language reform would even be for? And if my diagnosis is roughly right, what would move the debate?
