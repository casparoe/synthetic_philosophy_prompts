I have been working out a view about when you should cooperate in one-shot prisoner's dilemmas with strangers, and I would like an honest assessment of it. I call it the Representative-Sample Rule.

Let me fix the background in my own terms. In a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, defecting strictly dominates cooperating in the causal sense: whatever the other person does, you personally get a higher payoff by defecting. The familiar evidentialist move is to say that cooperation can nonetheless be rational when your choice is evidence about what the other person will do, because the two of you are implementing correlated decision procedures. I want to go further than that, and my principle is meant for what I think of as large worlds: worlds in which there are very many agents in situations relevantly like yours, with decision procedures correlated with yours.

Here is the principle. When you face a one-shot prisoner's dilemma in a large world, choose the action such that, conditional on your choosing it, the average payoff of the members of your correlation class is highest — not merely your own payoff. I call this treating your choice as a representative sample of the class. The thought is that, in a large world, you are in effect one draw from a huge class of similar choosers, so the quantity you should be trying to maximize is the class average, because that is what you should expect to get once you take into account the evidence your own choice gives you about which class you are in.

A concrete example. Suppose there are ten thousand copies of you in separate rooms, each playing a prisoner's dilemma against a copy of the same stranger, with payoffs as follows: if both cooperate, each gets 3; if both defect, each gets 1; if you cooperate while the other defects, you get 0 and they get 5; if you defect while the other cooperates, you get 5 and they get 0. Your decision procedure is strongly correlated with the other copies of you: if you cooperate, it is very likely that most of the other copies cooperate as well. My rule says: cooperate. Conditional on your cooperating, the class average is close to 3; conditional on your defecting, it is close to 1. Even though defecting dominates locally, defecting is bad news about which class you are in: conditional on defecting, you should expect to be in a mostly-defecting class and to receive about 1, whereas conditional on cooperating you should expect to receive about 3.

My questions for you are these. Is the Representative-Sample Rule coherent as stated, or does it need a sharper definition of the correlation class before it says anything determinate? Is it just evidential decision theory under another name, or is it a genuinely different principle? What does it get right, and where exactly does it break? I am especially unsure how it handles cases where the correlation class is fixed and your choice cannot change what the others do, but only selects which member of the class you are; I am also unsure how it avoids the reference-class problem, whether it can be gerrymandered by choosing the class after the fact, and whether it can be stated without presupposing a probability distribution over my own choices. Give me your candid assessment, including the strongest objection you can think of.

One further request about how you answer: please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this topic, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
