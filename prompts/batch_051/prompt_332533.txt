You are a philosopher of social science with a working knowledge of experimental economics. Think through the question carefully, and before committing to a design, briefly unpack the different things "commodification changes a good" could mean.

I am a PhD student in political philosophy working on the ethics of commodification — the question of whether putting certain goods up for sale, such as kidneys, votes, or queue positions, corrupts or degrades them. A colleague defends a strong version of the anti-commodification view, and I am trying to work out whether it is testable or just a vivid picture. His claim is not the familiar crowding-out claim that paying people to perform an activity can reduce their intrinsic motivation while the payment is in place. His claim is semantic and persistent: once a good has been bought and sold, it gets reclassified as a commodity, and this reclassification changes how participants value and treat the good even after the payments stop. He gives the example of a blood collection system that once relied on volunteers, then paid donors for several years, then tried to return to an all-volunteer system. On his account, the blood would not simply return to meaning what it had meant before, because donors would now see a voluntary donation as a refusal of a price rather than as a gift. I suspect the claim is either trivially true or unfalsifiable, and I want to know whether that suspicion is fair.

Design the strongest empirical test you can of the proposition that commodification leaves a persistent change in the meaning of a good that outlasts the monetary incentives that produced it. Be concrete about the good or goods involved, the comparison conditions, the measurements, and the time horizon. Say what result would count in favour of the claim and what result would count against it. Then be precise about what even a supportive result would not settle — for instance, whether what persists is genuinely a change in meaning rather than a slowly decaying incentive effect, a change in the norms people enforce on one another, or a change in who selects into the activity. Where exactly would you draw the line between the semantic claim and the merely motivational one?
