In classical electromagnetic theory, physicists routinely treat two vector potentials that differ by a gradient as descriptions of one and the same physical situation, because they yield the same electric and magnetic fields. The choice among these potentials is called a gauge choice, and the practice is to treat it as a matter of convention: one fixes a convenient gauge, such as the Coulomb gauge or the Lorenz gauge, solves the equations, and then reads off predictions from the fields or from other gauge-invariant quantities. The potentials themselves are standardly described as unphysical bookkeeping. But the practice is not perfectly uniform. In the Aharonov–Bohm effect, an observable shift in an electron interference pattern is computed from the line integral of the vector potential around a closed loop, even though the electron travels through a region where the magnetic field is zero, and the local values of the potential along the electron's path are not fixed by the local fields. So the profession treats the potential as unreal in one setting, yet allows quantities defined in terms of the potential to figure in an observable prediction in another.

What theory of physical reality does this two-sided practice presuppose? In particular, does the practice strictly presuppose that only gauge-invariant quantities exist, or does it merely presuppose that gauge-variant quantities never enter predictions? Which rival views—for example, that the potential is physically real, that gauge-equivalent descriptions are representations with surplus structure, or that gauge equivalence is a case of the general equivalence of symmetrically related possibilities—does the standard practice quietly rule out, and which is it merely compatible with? Reconstruct the implicit criterion of physical content at work in the practice, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined. Finally, say what would have to change—in the practice, or in the metaphysics, or both—if the Aharonov–Bohm effect were taken to show that the vector potential is real after all.
