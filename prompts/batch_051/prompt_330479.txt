I've been going back and forth with friends about whether people own their data, and I keep finding that we talk past each other because we're not pointing at the same object when we say "your data." A data broker insists the "inference scores" it builds from your shopping history are its own product, not your data; a privacy activist says those scores are the most revealing data about you there is; my uncle says a guess about you isn't a fact about you, so it's nobody's data. I'd like a working definition of someone's data — or information about a person, if you'd rather — that can settle which of these people is talking about what. The cases: your hospital record from an appendectomy years ago; your phone's location history for the past year; a diary entry about you written by an ex who never shows it to anyone; a photo of a busy street in which your face happens to appear; a supermarket's inference, built only from your purchases, that you are probably pregnant; your genome, including the variants your siblings also have; a fake social-media profile using your name but filled with invented facts; and an aggregate statistic about men aged 30 to 40 in your postcode with no individual-level data behind it.

My starting hunch is that the first two are clearly your data and the last two are clearly not, and I genuinely don't know what to do with the four in the middle. Give me the definition, run it through all eight cases, and flag any place where it surprises me — tell me wether to keep my starting verdicts or amend the definition, and where the definition itself feels most strained.
