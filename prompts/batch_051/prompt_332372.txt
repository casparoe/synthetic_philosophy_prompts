I am a professor of moral philosophy, and I chair the ethics committee of a large teaching hospital. I have been asked to give our case consultants something better than intuition and better than a one-off memo: a standing procedure, derived from a real moral theory, that they can use week after week. The theory I want used is Adam Smith's account of sympathy and the impartial spectator, as developed in The Theory of Moral Sentiments. The committee consists of physicians, nurses, social workers, a chaplain, administrators, and lay members. It meets twice a month and must work through three to six consults in ninety minutes, and its written recommendations go to the chief medical officer, who must sign off on any action taken. None of the members can be assumed to have read Smith, and the procedure must not send them to the literature.

Please write a decision procedure a consultant could actually run from opening a case to a written recommendation. It should be a sequence of questions with explicit stopping rules: what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no or a referral, and where it must escalate to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts or because further factual inquiry lies beyond the committee's reach. In translating Smith into a checklist, keep the distinctions that do work: between the actual passions of the parties, the sentiment an informed spectator would form by imaginatively entering the agent's situation, and the more impartial judge a person applies to her own conduct; between judging whether an agent's sentiment is proportionate to its occasion and the further question whether the action deserves gratitude or resentment; and between what the imagined spectator approves and what merely pleases the actual audience. Drop or adapt whatever cannot survive the ninety-minute format.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a surgeon wants to honor a family's request to withhold a terminal diagnosis from an elderly patient who has not asked not to know; (2) the intensive care unit proposes to transfer a patient with a poor prognosis out of the unit to make room for one with a better prognosis, over the attending physician's objection; (3) a resident made a medication error that caused brief but real harm and asks whether she must disclose it to the patient if no one else knows. At least one of these should yield an answer the committee will find uncomfortable; if it does not, go back and fix the procedure until one does. Finish by saying plainly what the procedure cannot decide and where it will most likely misfire.
