I keep returning to Kavka's toxin puzzle and cannot tell whether the following argument is sound, or where exactly it fails. Set up the case in the usual way: I am offered a large reward that will be paid tomorrow morning if and only if, at midnight tonight, I intend to drink a non-lethal but genuinely unpleasant toxin tomorrow afternoon. I need not actually drink the toxin to receive the reward; by the time drinking would occur, the money has either been paid or not.

My argument that a fully rational agent cannot collect the reward:

(1) By tomorrow afternoon the payout is already fixed; drinking the toxin changes nothing except to add a day of illness.

(2) A fully rational agent will not, at the time of acting, perform an action whose expected value then is strictly negative when a clearly better alternative is available.

(3) So the agent will not drink tomorrow afternoon; and because she is rational and self-aware enough to predict her own future choice, at midnight she knows she will not drink.

(4) An agent cannot intend to do something she knows she will not do when the time comes.

(5) Therefore the agent cannot form the midnight intention, and since the reward is conditional on exactly that intention, a fully rational agent cannot win the reward — even though winning would be a pure gain.

I half-suspect that step (4) carries the argument, yet the principle behind it feels close to definitional. I also worry that step (2) quietly assumes that a prior intention cannot itself give the agent a reason to drink. Is the argument valid? If it is invalid, which step fails, and what is the strongest corrected version of that step on which the conclusion would, or would not, still follow?
