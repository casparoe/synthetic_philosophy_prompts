I have a rule about political arguments: when someone — a politician, a pundit, even a friend — defends a proposal by appealing to "the will of the people" or says "the public wants this," I treat that part of the argument as carrying zero independent weight, maybe negative weight. My view is that there is no such thing as a unified popular will; it's a rhetorical device that smuggles a faction's preference into democratic language, and even "the public wants X" usually just means some poll asked a particular question to a particular sample in a particular week. Last week I deployed the rule with my cousin, who was arguing for a new law (the details don't matter). He said it should pass because the public overwhelmingly supports it. I refused to count that as a reason at all, and told him the public doesn't support anything — some weighted sample of respondents did, with varying intensities and reasons, under one wording of the question. He said I was doging, and that in a democracy mass opinion is exactly the kind of thing that should move me, and that my rule is just a way to ignore evidence I don't like behind a fake philosophical scruple.

So: is my rule defensible, or is it bias dressed up as conceptual sophistication? If there is a defensible version, how should it be stated — when should "a lot of people want this" count as a reason, and at what weight, given that the people isn't a unified agent but aggregated preferences do carry some information and some democratic authority? And is there a difference between the descriptive claim that most people favor the policy and the loftier claim that it's "the will of the people," such that I should discount them at different rates?
