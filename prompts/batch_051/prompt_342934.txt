I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I have been reading Bayesian confirmation theory on the side. The piece I cannot get straight is the problem of old evidence. As I understand it: suppose a fact E is already in the dataset with probability 1 before a hypothesis H is ever formulated. The standard incremental measure of Bayesian support says E confirms H only if P(H | E) > P(H), which is equivalent to P(E | H) > P(E). But if P(E) = 1, conditionalizing on E changes nothing, and the machinery reports zero support. Yet this seems wrong: the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion was old news when general relativity was proposed, and it is routinely counted as strong support for the theory. So the formalism misses something.

Before I adopt any of the standard fixes I keep seeing — counterfactual reconstructions that pretend E was not already known; additions of logical learning to the state space, so that what is new is not E itself but the proposition that H entails E; or the move of assigning every contingent proposition a probability slightly below 1 — I want the requirements list first. Write the desiderata that any adequate solution to the problem of old evidence must meet, where adequate means good enough to serve as a design spec for an ideal Bayesian reasoner, not a description of how a sloppy human happens to reason.

For each requirement, give the concrete case or consideration that forces it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as a camp preference. Then flag any pair of requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied; if some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, say which requirement a candidate solution is most entitled to drop. Do not give a full scorecard of the standard fixes, but do say which single requirement does the most work in excluding them.

Begin with the bottom line — whether the requirements are jointly satisfiable, and if not, which requirement has to give — and then give the list and its supporting reasoning. No preamble, and do not restate the question.
