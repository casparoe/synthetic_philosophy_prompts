I've been arguing with a friend about the hard problem of consciousness, and we keep circling Mary's room, but the standard black-and-white version has started to feel worn out. I'd like to test how much work the complete absence of chromatic experience is actually doing in that case. In the familiar setup, Mary has had no chromatic color experience at all and knows every physical fact about color vision; when she leaves and sees red, the question is whether she learns something new. The three standard positions are: she does (so not all facts are physical); she gains only an ability, such as recognizing or imagining red; or she gains a new phenomenal concept for a fact she already knew. Now change one feature of the case. Mary's room is not black-and-white. She has grown up surrounded by color—blue, green, yellow, purple—but has never seen red. She has direct introspective familiarity with green above all. She also has complete physical knowledge, including a full specification of color processing. Stipulate that, on the best processing account, red and green are opposite poles of a single opponent channel: seeing red drives the channel in the opposite direction from seeing green, and this exhausts the physical difference between the two. So, before ever seeing red, Mary has both the physical description of the red-producing state and first-person acquaintance with the opposite pole of that channel. When she finally sees red, does the knowledge argument still go through? Could she have worked out what red would be like, and if she still learns something new, which of the three strategies best explains why?

Run the knowledge argument, the ability hypothesis, and the phenomenal-concept strategy against this modified case, and say whether it weakens, strengthens, or leaves unchanged the anti-physicalist conclusion. I'm especially interested in where the variant puts pressure on each standard response—for example, whether the ability hypothesis must say Mary already had the relevant ability, and whether the phenomenal-concept strategy must say her new concept is of a different type here than in the original case. Throughout, separate what is settled about the original case from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested diagnosis of why Mary learns as the consensus.
