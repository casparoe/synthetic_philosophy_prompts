In the concluding chapter of The Methods of Ethics, Sidgwick lays out the impasse he calls the dualism of practical reason: both rational egoism and utilitarianism seem to him to be equally rational methods, but when one's own happiness and the general happiness come apart, they issue opposite verdicts and he can find no higher principle to arbitrate. I want a thought experiment built to test whether that dualism really runs deep, for a paper on whether the conflict survives in ordinary practical contexts. Construct a realistic, ordinary-life scenario (one paragraph) in which an agent faces a genuine conflict between the egoist's verdict and the utilitarian's verdict, but with two constraints: the egoist must be smart and long-term, not myopic, so the conflict can't be dismissed by saying she miscalculated her self-interest; and the case can't sneak in any moralized preferences or duties on the egoist's side. After the scenario, walk through what each method says and why, then identify which feature of the case is doing the work to keep the conflict alive. Remove that feature and say whether the case collapses into a mere clash of motives or a resolvable conflict of interest. Finally, how would a defender of Sidgwick's dualism answer someone who says the egoist could just cultivate wider sympathy or stable cooperative habits, making the apparent conflict only a temporary practical problem?
