I have been trying to decide whether mutable objects in a running program really persist through their changes, or whether "mutation" just relabels a succession of distinct objects. Consider this argument:

(1) All there is to an object in a running program at a given time is its state at that time: the values of its fields, the configuration of its parts.

(2) To mutate an object is to make its state at a later time differ from its state at an earlier time.

(3) If the state at the later time differs from the state at the earlier time, then what exists at the later time differs from what existed at the earlier time.

(4) For the thing that exists at the earlier time and the thing that exists at the later time to be one and the same object, exactly the same thing must exist at both times.

(5) By (3) and (4), the thing after the mutation is not one and the same object as the thing before it.

(6) Therefore, a so-called mutable object does not survive its own mutation; what programming languages call changing an object is really replacing one object with another, perhaps one that happens to occupy the same name, address, or reference.

I half-suspect the argument proves too much, but each step feels hard to deny. Is the argument valid? Are the premises true? If a step fails, say exactly which one, and identify the reading of state, object, or identity on which it fails. Also say under what reading the argument would go through, and what a view of objects would have to look like for that reading to be acceptable.
