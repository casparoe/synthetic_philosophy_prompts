I keep coming back to the problem of evil on hard mode: not humans, angels. The usual free will defense leans on stuff that only applies to us — ignorance, having a body that can be starved or hurt, social pressure, evolution, being confused about what's good. Angels don't have any of that, they're supposed to be created already knowing God, no scarcity, no death, no weakness. So how does the first evil decision ever get made? Milton's Paradise Lost looks like a whole epic trying to solve exactly this: Satan starts out in heaven, sees the Son promoted, and freely picks rebellion. I want to read the poem as a theodicy for angelic evil, not just as a dramatic backstory. Does Milton actually give a coherent mechanism for how a perfect rational creature can sin, or does he just wave at 'pride' and 'envy' and hope the poetry covers it? Which bits are doing the real philosophical work — the scene where Abdiel argues with him, the war in heaven as Raphael tells it, the speeches in Pandemonium, the preferring-to-rule-in-Hell declaration — and where does the poem quietly give up? In the online arguments the angel case gets treated as a gotcha and then dropped, but Milton actually stays with it, and I cant tell whether he's cheating by hiding the hard part in vividness, or whether the vividness is the argument that a good creature's will can turn for reasons that are real to it but not good reasons full stop. What would I be missing if I treated Paradise Lost as just an illustration of the philosophers' free will account, and what would I be missing if I let the poem replace the argument?
