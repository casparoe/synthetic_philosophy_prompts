Two colleagues of mine, Mara and Daniel, work in the same field and recently found themselves on opposite sides of a dispute about whether planning is overrated. They reported it to me afterward, and I have been going back and forth about it ever since.

Mara opened by saying that planning is systematically overvalued. On their last project the team spent three weeks producing a detailed plan, and within the first days of execution they had already departed from most of it because the situation on the ground did not match their assumptions. She said the plan had mainly served as a ritual for reducing anxiety before the real work began, and later as a way to assign blame when things went wrong. The actual progress, in her view, came from people making local judgments against feedback, not from the plan.

Daniel replied that this was a criticism of a bad plan rather than of planning. A plan is not a prophecy, he said; its value lies in forcing you to think through constraints, dependencies, and resources before you are under pressure. The fact that the team departed from the plan was not evidence against planning, because the plan gave them a baseline from which to depart intelligently. He added that a good plan is revised, not obeyed.

Mara answered that this makes planning unfalsifiable. If the plan is followed, it gets the credit; if it is abandoned, it also gets the credit, because supposedly it provided the baseline. She also pressed the cost side: the weeks spent planning could have been spent acting, and the feedback that comes from acting is the real teacher. You cannot learn the texture of a situation from a room, she said.

Daniel responded that some feedback arrives too late and some lessons are too expensive to buy twice. Pilots and surgeons plan precisely because certain errors cannot be treated as iterations. On their project, he pointed out, the planning process had caught a permitting requirement that would otherwise have been discovered only after money was committed. He said he was not defending elaborate documents for their own sake, but the distinction between preparation and improvisation is not the same as the distinction between waste and competence.

Mara granted much of that, but said Daniel was defending thinking ahead, not planning. Listing known constraints and rehearsing risks is fine, she said; what she objects to is the artifact, the detailed schedule with dates and durations nobody could know, because that produces false precision and then people confuse the plan with the reality. Daniel replied that a schedule is a coordination device rather than a prediction. Eight people need a shared default about who does what next, and revision requires something to revise. Group improvisation without any plan, he said, tends to become direction by whoever is most confident or most persistent.

Mara countered that a plan can freeze exactly that problem in advance, recording the confident person's assumptions before the evidence is available. She also argued that once a plan is made it becomes a commitment, and people anchor on it even after its rationale has gone. Daniel said that commitment is a feature rather than a bug, because if nobody commits to anything until the last moment, others cannot prepare dependably, and some arrangements cannot be improvised later at all. He summed up by saying that the rational function of planning is to let prior intentions structure later deliberation, not to produce clairvoyance. Mara said they probably agreed on the concept but disagreed on the weight, and that in her experience the cost of making plans is systematically underestimated while the benefit of just beginning is underestimated. Daniel said that in his experience projects fail more often from uncoordinated activity and overlooked dependencies than from overly rigid plans, so the burden should stay on whoever wants to skip planning.

They have each asked me to sort out which of them is actually right, and I find I cannot do it cleanly. Please explain, in continuous prose, which specific claims from each side should be accepted, where Mara and Daniel are talking past one another, how much of the dispute is a difference in weighting rather than a difference in principle, and who is on balance better supported, if either. I suspect the phrase planning is overrated is hiding several distinct claims that should be separated, but I would rather you do that sorting than rely on my own sense of it.
