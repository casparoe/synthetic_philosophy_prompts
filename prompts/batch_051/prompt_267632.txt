I'm teaching an intro philosophy of science course next term and I'd like to open the unit on Popper with something interactive rather than another lecture. The unit is specifically on Popper's famous wobble about Darwinism: early on he argued that natural selection and survival of the fittest are not really testable scientific claims but more like a metaphysical research programme, since fitness seemed to be defined by whatever survived, so the whole thing looked close to a tautology. Later he changed his mind and said Darwinism does make testable predictions after all, and the secondary literature still argues about which Popper is the real Popper. My students won't have read any of this before the quiz, so I don't want it to just test weather they've memorized the two readings.

Could you write me a 'which reading of Popper on Darwinism are you actually committed to?' quiz, eight to ten questions, where every question presents a concrete case about evolution or natural selection and has two or three answer options? The point is to sort students by students actual intuitions about whether Darwinian explanations are testable, empty, or somewhere in between, before they know the labels. No question should mention Popper, falsifiability, tautology, or metaphysical research programme. I'd like the cases to include at least: rabbits in the Precambrian (would one out-of-place fossil sink common descent?), a just-so story about why humans evolved to like music, shared pseudogenes between humans and chimps, and a trait that looks like a byproduct rather than an adaptation. The scoring key should map answer patterns onto four positions: the early-Popper metaphysical research programme reading, the late-Popper testable theory reading, the blunt tautology or empty-content reading, and the view that the core theory is testable in principle but that evolutionary biology in practice trades in just-so stories. Also, please flag where the quiz can't cleanly separate two of those positions, and say what mixed answer patterns should be read as instead of force-fitting everyone into a leaf. I'm picturing a handout I can give before the assigned reading, so keep the answer options in ordinary language, and under each result in the key give a one-sentence blurb explaining why that pattern fits that reading. If one of the four positions turns out to be a combination nobody actually picks, say so and say what that suggests about the position rather than papering over it.
