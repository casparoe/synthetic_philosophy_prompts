So I cover behavioral science and ideas for a living, and I've been nursing a concept I can't shake — derided by my editor, but still. I call it "past-self honoring." The thought is that a sunk cost isn't just a number already lost; it's also a kind of commitment you made as a slightly different person, and abandoning it means breaking faith with that person. The standard advice is that the money or years are gone, so ignore them. But when my friend refuses to abandon a novel she's spent five years on and knows is bad, I don't think "merely irrational" captures what she's doing. She's not pretending the time will come back; she's acting as if her past self gets a vote. Same with a doctor who stays in a specialty she's grown to hate because she trained a decade for it, or someone who won't back out of a wedding at the last minute — not because of the deposit, but because the deposit stands in for a promise to the person who made it. My rough principle: honoring sunk costs is irrational when you're optimizing the present, but rational when you're trying to be continuous with your past self, because past investments partly constitute who you are now. I know this sounds like loss aversion with better marketing, and I can see it rationalizing staying in a dying industry or a bad relationship simply because you chose it once. So, honest assessment: is there a real distinction here, or am I just giving a fancy name to the usual bias? What does it get right, and where does it stop being a principle and start being an excuse? And has someone already worked this out under a better name?
