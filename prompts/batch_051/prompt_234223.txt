I want to make a particular argument in a paper, and I’d like you to work it out fully enough that I can see its real force and its main weakness. The passage I want to anchor the argument to is this, from David Hume’s An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Section XII, Part I:

These sceptical topics, indeed, are only sufficient to prove, that the senses alone are not implicitly to be depended on; but that we must correct their evidence by reason, and by considerations, derived from the nature of the medium, the distance of the object, and the disposition of the organ, in order to render them, within their sphere, the proper criteria of truth and falsehood.

I want to argue that this passage is not a harmless description of how we correct sensory error, but an unacknowledged answer to the problem of the criterion—and a circular one. The correction by reason and by considerations about medium, distance, and organ is only justified if those correcting resources have already earned their authority; but their authority is itself established through the very sensory evidence whose reliability is in question. So Hume’s move merely relocates the Cartesian evil-demon worry rather than answering it. Develop that argument as strongly as you can. Then give the best Humean reply: that the demand for a non-circular transcendental justification is a philosopher’s artifact, and that talk of “proper criteria” is internal to the practice of judging rather than an answer to the sceptic from outside that practice. Finally, explain whether that reply succeeds or quietly concedes my original point.
