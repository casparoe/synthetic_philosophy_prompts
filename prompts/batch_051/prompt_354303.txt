For a works-in-progress seminar I'm presenting on the foundations of welfare economics, I've been asked to write out my "decision-theoretic profile" — a list of commitments — so the group can audit it before I build the paper around them. Here is the profile:

1. Revealed preference is the right epistemic foundation: a person's choices under ordinary, uncoerced conditions are our best and default evidence about what they prefer, and preference attributions should be grounded in choice data rather than introspection or self-report.
2. Utility is not merely a label for what choice rankings summarize. It refers to a real magnitude of how much things matter to a person — at least ordinal, and I increasingly think cardinal and interpersonally comparable — which choice behavior tracks only imperfectly.
3. Completeness and transitivity are requirements of rationality, not empirical descriptions of behavior. It is legitimate for economists and policymakers to treat coherent preferences as a normative standard even though actual choice violates it.
4. Where choice, self-report, and well-being come apart — addiction, framing, menu dependence, akrasia — a person's "true" preference is what they would choose under suitably idealized conditions: full information, adequate reflection, and freedom from bias. Actual choice can then be corrected against this benchmark.
5. A person's well-being just is the satisfaction of their (possibly idealized) preferences — not their hedonic state and not any objective list of goods.
6. The behavioral literature doesn't refute revealed preference. It shows that raw choice data are noisy and frame-sensitive, and the remedy is better elicitation, debiasing, and careful adjustment for context — not abandoning choice as the evidence base.

I'd like you to audit this set for internal consistency. Treat it as given that I want to hold on to at least the spirit of (1) and (5): if the cleanest fix abandons one of them, tell me, but also say what revision would preserve as much of each as possible.

Give a committed response: take a definite position, don't lay out a menu of options. Discriminate between genuine tensions and merely apparent ones rather than flagging every pair. Rank the genuine tensions by severity, with the worst first, and say for each what would have to give and what the cheapest fix costs; then tell me which single revision you would make and defend it as the one that cleans up the most.

Format your answer as a single JSON object with exactly these fields:

"genuine_tensions": an array of objects, each with "id" (a string), "commitments_involved" (array of strings, e.g., ["1","4"]), "severity_1_to_10" (integer, 10 = outright contradiction), "diagnosis" (two or three sentences on the exact source of the strain), "cheapest_fix" (one sentence), "cost_of_fix" (one sentence on what you give up). Rank the array worst-first.
"apparent_tensions": an array of objects, each with "id", "commitments_involved" (array of strings), "why_it_looks_like_a_tension" (one sentence), "why_it_dissolves" (one or two sentences). Include only pairs that would strike a good reader as strained but on inspection are not.
"verdict": an object with "single_recommended_revision" (one sentence naming which commitment to revise and how), "why_it_cleans_up_the_most" (two or three sentences), and "what_you_give_up" (one sentence).

Do not pad the arrays with trivial pairs; if a pair involves no real or interesting apparent friction, omit it.
