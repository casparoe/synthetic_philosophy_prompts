I've talked myself into a position I don't fully believe, and I want to know what it would take to fix that. Bradley's regress, as I see it, shows that you can't account for the unity of a proposition by piling up relata and relations. Take the proposition that the cup is on the table. You'd like it to be a single thing built from cup, on, and table. But [cup, on, table] is just three things, not a proposition. Add a relation of instantiation to bind them and you now have [cup, on, table, instantiation], which is four things needing binding; add another relation and you're off. So the systematic, tidy conclusion seems to be: there is no unity of this sort to be had — talk of 'the proposition' is shorthand for a plurality of elements that never quite become one. This view appeals to me because it avoids positing a mysterious primitive glue and it dissolves a whole class of pseudo-problems.

But the intuition won't go quietly. When I grasp that the cup is on the table, I'm grasping one thing. It's the kind of thing that can be true or false as a whole, negated as a whole, put in a conditional. A plurality of cup, on, and table isn't true or false; the proposition is. The difference between 'on' having done its unifying work and merely sitting in a list with cup and table feels like a difference in the world, not just in how I experience it.

So either the intuition is a psychological illusion to be debunked, or the eliminativist conclusion is too quick, or there's a third reading where both survive: maybe the regress shows only that unity can't be analyzed, not that it can't be real. I want to know what would justify each move. What evidence should make me give up the intuition? What mistake in the argument should make me give up the eliminativism? And can the compatibility story be stated precisely enough to be more than comforting? Which should yield, and how would I tell?
