I'm taking a phil of language course and we just did Haslanger on ameliorative analysis. The core argument, as I understand it: asking what a concept actually means is less interesting than asking what it is doing for us, and when a concept is doing badly we can and should revise or replace it to serve our legitimate social and political purposes. That always seemed to lean on a picture where a community holds its concepts as public tools and can deliberately retool them—through law, education, activism, changing the dictionary.

But here's what's bugging me. The biggest engine of conceptual change right now might not be a community reflectively retooling anything. It's the large language models and platforms that now shape a huge amount of what people read and write. Concepts end up being whatever a model has absorbed from the training corpus and then propagates at scale through search, autocomplete, chatbots, recommendations. You can't pass a law that makes the model's latent space stop encoding 'woman' the way it does, and even a serious activist campaign might be a rounding error in the next training run.

So the question: does Haslanger's ameliorative argument survive this development, or does it need repair, or is it dead? I want you to be specific about which premise of the origional argument the LLM development actually pressures, and whether there's a repaired version of conceptual engineering that still makes sense once concepts live in model weights and data pipelines rather than in norms a community can reflectively revise. If it's dead, say what the practice of deliberately shaping these systems should be called instead.
