Here's the thing I'm chewing on. One of the classic defenses of conditional amnesty — think of the South African TRC, or debates about Argentina and Rwanda — goes roughly like this: after mass atrocity, the most damaging truths are locked up in the heads of perpetrators and their networks. If you credibly threaten prosecution, they'll shut up, destroy evidence, or lie. So you offer amnesty in exchange for full, public testimony. The truth you buy is then supposed to do a lot of the moral work: it names the dead, corrects the official record, lets families know what happened, and creates a shared baseline for reconciliation. Amnesty isn't mercy in this picture; its a purchase — you're trading punishment for knowledge.

Now here's my worry. We now live in a world where a huge amount of that knowledge no longer lives only in anyone's head. Mass atrocities are routinely documented by phone cameras, dashcams, satellite imagery, bank records, biometric databases, social media posts, and cloned hard drives, and a lot of it circulates before a truth commission ever convenes. In some future transition, investigators might already know who did what to whom, when, and with what weapon, from the digital record alone. What they may still lack is perpetrator testimony: the perpetrator's own account of why, what they were ordered, what they thought they were doing, what happened behind closed doors.

So my question: does the classic truth-for-amnesty argument survive that development? A lot of its force seemed to depend on a scarcity assumption — that only perpetrators can supply the truth, so it must be bought from them. If the scarcity assumption is false or greatly weakened, does the argument collapse into a purely political bargain about stability and deterrence, or can it be repaired? Say exactly which premise the digital-record development bears on, and whether the argument still goes through, needs repair, or should be described as a different argument altogether.
