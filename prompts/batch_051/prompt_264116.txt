You're a statutory interpretation scholar, and I want you to think like a judge's law clerk reconstructing a consistent doctrine from a messy record. I'm a 2L in a seminar on legislation, and our professor has spent the semester giving us one-off absurdity-doctrine cases. Next week she's springing a new case on us, and we can't tell which rule she's actually running. Her previous calls, as best I can reconstruct them:

- Scrivener's error: a recodification changed a licensing statute from "the board may issue a permit" to "the board may not issue a permit." She said a court should read the "not" out; obvious drafting slip.

- Service dog: an ordinance says "no animals in any courthouse." She said it doesn't bar a blind witness's service dog: applying it would make the courthouse inaccessible in a way no county board could have intended.

- Copy fee: a public-record law says agencies "shall charge 25 cents per page." A journalist argued the transparency purpose requires waiving the fee for indigent requesters. She said no; the text is clear and a 25-cent-per-page fee isn't absurd. Courts shouldn't rewrite a fee schedule off a broad statutory purpose.

- Disaster deadline: a relief statute says applications "must be filed within 30 days." An applicant filed on day 32 because a hurricane flooded her house. She said no; deadlines may be harsh but they're not absurd.

Now the new case. A state park rule says "no person shall remove any native plant from a state park." A licensed wetlands restoration crew, working under a restoration permit, removed an overgrown native willow that was choking a rare fen. The rule's own purpose clause says it exists "to preserve native plant communities." The text has no exemption; the department admits it should have drafted one but didn't. The enforcement action is pending.

If she's consistent, which way does she come out? Give me a bottom line in the first sentence. Lay out the candidate rules she could be running (I can see at least two: a narrow "text unless absurdity or drafting slip" rule and a broader "text unless it defeats the statute's own purpose" rule), and say what each predicts here.
