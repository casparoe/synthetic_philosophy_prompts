For a column I'm writing, I need to get a Sleeping Beauty argument straight before I publish anything about it. Beauty is put to sleep on Sunday and a fair coin is tossed. If heads, she's woken once, interviewed, and that's it. If tails, she's woken Monday, interviewed, given a drug that wipes the memory of that waking, then woken Tuesday and interviewed again, so neither waking carries a day-stamp from the inside. When she wakes, what's her credence in heads?

The 1/2 camp says waking tells her nothing new about the coin, since she was sure she'd wake at least once. A friend insists this view proves too much:

(1) On waking, P(heads) = 1/2.
(2) If heads, she's only ever woken Monday, so P(heads and Monday) = 1/2.
(3) If tails, the Monday and Tuesday wakings are indistinguishable, so they split the remaining 1/2 evenly: P(tails and Monday) = P(tails and Tuesday) = 1/4.
(4) So P(Monday) = 3/4.
(5) So if she's then told it's Monday, P(heads | Monday) = (1/2)/(3/4) = 2/3.
(6) But being told the day shouldn't move a fair coin's probability from 1/2 to 2/3. So (1) has to go, and the right waking credence is 1/3.

I can't tell whether the soft spot is (3), (6), or something earlier, and I don't want to fix it by quietly giving up more than I meant to. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it commit you to? And could you write the answer in British English?
