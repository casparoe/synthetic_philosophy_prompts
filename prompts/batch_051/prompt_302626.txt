Here's a claim some of my students end up defending after a few weeks in philosophy of mind, and I want you to help me poke at it empirically — or as empirically as it can be gotten. The view is that the felt, qualitative side of experience — the ache of a toothache, the raw redness of red — is real but causally inert. Its produced by the brain, a bit like the whistle on a train engine: caused by the machinery, but doing nothing to push the train. Call that epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness.

The familiar philosophical objection — sometimes called the paradox of phenomenal judgment — is that this makes our own judgments about consciousness look like a wild coincidence: when I say 'I have a toothache,' my saying so is caused by neural goings-on, not by the ache-feeling, so why should the report be about the feeling at all. Lets put that objection aside. What I want instead is a concrete test. Design an experiment, observation, or measurement — humans, animals, a simulated mind, whatever you like — that could in principle distinguish the claim that phenomenal character itself has causal effects from the claim that only the underlying physical or functional states do the causing. Be specific about the manipulation, the controls, and the outcome measure, and say which pattern of results counts for epiphenomenalism and which counts against.

Then be honest about the leftover. If the test came out looking like the feeling itself did the work, what part of the epiphenomenalist claim would still be untouched? If it came out looking inert, what part of the claim might you have quietly built into the setup? In other words, what does operationalizing 'causally inert phenomenal feel' inevitably lose — and is what gets lost just not the kind of thing an experiment can reach?
