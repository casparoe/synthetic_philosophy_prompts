You are a philosopher of economics with expertise in the Scottish Enlightenment and in reconstructing the normative commitments of trade policy. I am a trade policy officer in a government department that negotiates economic agreements with former colonies. Our standard template includes preferential market access for our own exporters, tied procurement, and technical assistance whose stated aim is development but whose design is heavily influenced by our firms’ interests. In The Wealth of Nations, Book IV, Chapter VII, “Of Colonies,” Adam Smith considers the claim that the utility of colonies consists in supplying the mother country with goods and a market. He responds, in substance, that founding a great empire for the sole purpose of raising up a people of customers may appear fit for a nation of shopkeepers, but is in truth unfit even for them, and is rather fit for a nation whose government is influenced by shopkeepers. I want you to treat our department’s practice as the object of analysis. Work carefully and begin by distinguishing what the practice explicitly says it aims at from what its structure presupposes. Then reconstruct the implicit theory of national interest and of economic value that the practice presupposes, and say whether it is the same theory Smith is attacking. Where does the practice strictly presuppose that view, and where is it compatible with several theories? Finally, tell me what would have to change operationally if we adopted the Smithian critique, and flag the point where that change turns on a value judgment rather than a factual claim. Please write the answer in British English, and end by stating how confident you are in the reconstruction and which part of it you are least sure of.
