I'm working on a long-form article about clinical AI and the difference between knowing a diagnosis and understanding it, and I need a thought experiment built to a precise specification, with the construction shown rather than stipulated.

Construct a one-paragraph, realistic scenario in which a physician adopts a well-validated diagnostic decision-support system and, over the course of a year, both her diagnostic accuracy and her ability to give correct explanations of those diagnoses improve measurably. The scenario must nevertheless make two accounts of understanding come apart in the following direction. Take a reliabilist about understanding to hold that understanding why is, roughly, true explanatory belief produced by a reliable process, where the reliable process may be partly external to the agent—an instrument, a database, a colleague, or an AI system. Take a cognitive-achievement theorist to hold that understanding why requires the agent herself to grasp the explanatory relations, such that she can answer novel why-questions and manipulate the explanation without merely deferring to the system. The reliabilist should say her understanding has improved, or at least not eroded; the cognitive-achievement theorist should say it has eroded. Constraint: the erosion must not come from deskilling, laziness, or blind deference. The physician keeps studying, keeps passing her board examinations, and checks the system's suggestions critically; the divergence should trace to the structure of the reliance itself.

After the scenario, state her explanatory belief in one representative case, the method by which it is formed, and why each account gives the verdict it gives. Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the divergence if that feature is removed, and how a defender of the reliabilist account would resist the conclusion that understanding has been lost.
