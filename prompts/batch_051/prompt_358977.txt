You are a professional philosopher who works on aesthetics and the philosophy of humour. I have been trying to get clear about what I actually believe about humour, and I have ended up with the following seven commitments. I would like an audit of whether they hang together. Please think through them carefully and systematically; do not simply flag every point of contact. I need you to distinguish real tensions from merely apparent ones, and to tell me exactly what would have to give.

Here is some shorthand I will use. Superiority theory says humour is the pleasure of feeling superior to someone else's defect or misfortune. Incongruity theory says humour arises from the perception of an incongruity between expectation and actuality. Relief theory says humour results from the discharge of pent-up nervous or psychic energy. Benign violation theory says a thing is funny when it constitutes a violation of a norm, expectation, or interpersonal boundary while being simultaneously appraised as benign.

My commitments:

1. Benign violation theory is the best overall account of humour. It naturally handles tickling, puns, dark jokes, and cringe comedy: in each case something transgressive or boundary-crossing is appraised as safe enough.

2. Superiority theory is false as a general account, but it identifies a real class of cases — a friend's pratfall, a well-aimed roast, laughter at an enemy's setback. I think benign violation actually explains these better than incongruity does: the violation is social or physical, and it is appraised as benign in part because it is the butt, not me, who is exposed.

3. Incongruity is necessary but not sufficient for humour. Every genuinely funny thing involves an incongruity between expectation and actuality. Many incongruities are not funny — the uncanny, tragic absurdity, a badly mismatched outfit.

4. Relief theory explains laughter, not humour. Nervous laughter after a threat passes, or laughter during tense rituals, is a discharge of tension, but being relieved is not the same as being amused.

5. The funniness of a token joke is exhausted by its content plus the audience's appraisal of that content. Strictly speaking, then, the same joke cannot be funnier when told by one person rather than another, or at one time rather than another, unless the content or the appraisal changes. But I also find it undeniable that a joke about my own group is funny from an in-group member and not funny from an out-group member, and that a "too soon" joke becomes funnier as the event recedes even though the words have not changed.

6. Comedy is an aesthetic domain: a joke's moral badness as such never makes it less funny. A morally awful joke can be exactly as funny as a harmless joke with the same content and delivery.

7. The "benign" in benign violation is partly a normative and safety appraisal, not merely a matter of physical harmlessness. A joke that expresses or invites genuine cruelty toward a vulnerable target is therefore not benign, and so fails the condition for funniness: it is not merely morally tainted — it is not funny at all.

Please go through these commitments and tell me where the set is unstable. In particular: (i) identify which pairs or triples are in genuine tension and which only appear to be; (ii) rank the genuine tensions by severity; (iii) for each genuine tension, say which commitment or commitments would have to give and what the cheapest revision would be; and (iv) tell me which single revision across the whole set would buy the most consistency at the least cost. Where two commitments only appear to be in tension, show me the reading or mechanism under which they are compatible. Begin with a brief brainstorm of possible friction points before giving your final audit.
