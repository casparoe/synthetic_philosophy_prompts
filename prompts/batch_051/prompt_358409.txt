I'm taking a class on privacy and technology and I keep going in circles on something. The way the course has been setting it up, a privacy violation happens when at least one of these is going on: (1) someone gets access to information you never made public; (2) someone intrudes into a space where you had a reasonable expectation to keep things to yourself; or (3) someone uses what they know about you to harm you or to narrow your options. Each of these seems to catch real violations, but I've hit a case that none of them quite handles.

There's this startup, call it Hummingbird, that builds personality and mood profiles for advertisers. The thing is, it trains only on completely public behavioral residue: which posts you linger on, the timestamps of your likes, the order in which you scroll a feed, how you rephrase a search query. It never reads private messages, never buys medical or financial data, never installs anything in your home. Yet the profiles are scary good — the company claims it can tell with high confidence that someone is in early pregnancy, in the middle of a divorce, or in a manic episode, from patterns the person has no idea they're giving off. No one accessed non-public information, no one intruded anywhere, and the only thing Hummingbird does with the profile is decide whether to show you ads for maternity clothes instead of, say, a bankruptcy lawyer. My friend says this is definately a privacy violation and our criteria are just incomplete. So my question: work through the three criteria one by one and tell me whether this qualifies as a privacy violation on that account, and whether the case is a reason to revise the criteria — for example, should privacy cover information you didn't intend to reveal even when it was, in some sense, out in public? Or is my friend just confusing creepiness with privacy?
