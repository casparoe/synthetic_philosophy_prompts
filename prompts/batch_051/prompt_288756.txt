In my seminar on speech, offense, and censorship, students were analyzing a case in which a politician used a phrase that many listeners heard as a racial dog whistle, even though nothing in the sentence itself was explicitly racial and the speaker denied any such intent. A student who had been arguing that the utterance was racist tried to answer questions about the speaker's intent by saying, "racism is about impact, not intent," and several others treated this as decisive. I want to understand what that slogan could actually mean. Please lay out the distinct precise claims one might be making with it — for example, claims about what makes an utterance racist, about what makes a speaker blameworthy, about whose uptake settles the meaning, and about how institutions should respond — and for each reading, say whether it is a near-platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, or outright false. Then identify which of these readings would have to be true for the slogan to do the work it was doing in that discussion, namely to close off the question of the speaker's plausibly deniable intent in a dog-whistle case, and say whether the slogan, so read, survives.
