I keep going back and forth on an argument a friend gave me about Morgan's canon, and I can't tell whether it's a real problem or a sleight of hand.

(1) Morgan's canon says, roughly, that you shouldn't interpret an animal's behavior as involving a higher psychological process (insight, reasoning, intention) if the behavior can be fairly interpreted as involving only a lower one (trial-and-error learning, habit, association, reflex).

(2) The usual defense of the canon is just parsimony: don't posit fancy inner causes when cheaper ones will do. But parsimony is species-neutral — it doesn't say "be stingy only with nonhumans."

(3) For almost any human action, some lower-process story is available. I open doors out of habit, finish sentences on autopilot, flinch by reflex, and even a careful bit of reasoning can be re-described as a chain of trained associations firing off in sequence.

(4) If the mere availability of a cheaper story is enough to make the higher attribution unjustified, then the canon, applied even-handedly, says we are never justified in attributing beliefs, intentions, or reasoning to other people.

(5) But that's unlivable. I'm doing it right now, and I'd have to give up ordinary conversation, friendship, and most of psychology and social science to stop.

(6) So either Morgan's canon is false as a general rule of evidence, or it needs a qualification nobody ever states, or we're stuck with solipsism about other people's minds. I want to keep a canon-ish conservatism about animal minds and I also want to keep the ordinary attribution of minds to people. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
