I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, primarily around artificial intelligence. Recently I sat in on an argument between two researchers at a workshop, and I'm considering writing it up. They were arguing about whether an AI system can be built to avoid side effects without first supplying it with a full specification of what humans value. I reconstructed the back-and-forth from my notes as faithfully as I could, and neither of them seemed to have cleanly won. I'd like a neutral, point-by-point adjudication of what they actually said.

The exchange, as I recorded it:

One researcher, an engineer I'll call Mae, opened with this. "We don't need anything like a final theory of value to define low-impact behavior. Require the agent to keep the rest of the environment close to its baseline while it performs its task. Track a large set of everyday variables—positions, velocities, whether objects stay intact, temperature, whether doors and windows remain as they were—and penalize divergence from what would have happened without the agent's intervention. That is a workable definition of avoiding side effects, and it never says what is good."

The other researcher, a philosopher I'll call Ravi, replied. "But which variables go on the list? Performing the task requires changing some of them; that's the point of acting at all. Sorting 'task-relevant' from 'side-effect' variables is exactly where the value judgment lives. And there is no value-neutral way to draw the list. Even 'object integrity' assumes that intact objects matter more than, say, rearranged molecules. You've relocated the value specification, not eliminated it."

Mae came back. "That's a fair objection to the variable-list version, which is why I prefer a second approach. Don't enumerate protected features at all. Instead, measure whether the agent's action leaves as much of the future reachable as inaction would. Take a broad reference set of possible goals and ask whether the goals achievable before the action are still achievable after it. Penalize the agent for shrinking that space. The reference set functions as a measuring instrument, not as a moral theory."

Ravi answered. "But the reference set cannot be neutral. If you sample uniformly from all possible goals, almost all of them will be bizarre—counting paperclips, preserving a particular arrangement of pebbles—and the differences humans actually care about will be washed out as noise. If instead you curate the set so that the measure tracks the side effects that concern us, the curation is itself a partial theory of value, now hidden inside the instrument. So you face a dilemma: either the measure is irrelevant to the side effects we care about, or it is value-laden."

Mae responded. "That's a false dilemma. The reference set can be built at the level of generic capabilities rather than final values: the future should still permit reaching, moving, communicating, keeping temperatures habitable, and forming new configurations. Preserving capabilities is far thinner than specifying what matters. A crash-test dummy's accelerometers don't require a theory of which injuries are worst; they provide a useful proxy, and we accept them for exactly that reason."

Ravi finished. "But capabilities are not value-free either. A capability is just a means that some possible agent might care about, and deciding which capabilities are worth measuring reflects a view about which ends ought to remain available. More importantly, you've now conceded the conceptual point. You're defending a fallible, conservative heuristic, one that can misclassify a terrible change as low impact. That may be a reasonable engineering compromise, but it isn't a definition of 'avoid side effects' that gets by without invoking value. The definitional project, as originally stated, fails."

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each numbered move, rate the argument from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and say which speaker, if either, carries that particular point. Then address the final wrinkle: does Mae's retreat to a useful proxy concede Ravi's conceptual point, or does Ravi overstate the difference between a measurement instrument and a value specification? Finally, identify anything both parties are missing—for example, questions about baselines, irreversibility, or the difference between avoiding all side effects and avoiding catastrophic ones. The write-up I need is a precise distribution of credit and doubt across the moves as actually made, not a single winner.
