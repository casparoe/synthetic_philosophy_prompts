For the past few weeks I have been arguing with a student in my school's math club about whether computer-assisted results such as the four-color theorem and the Kepler conjecture (the claim about the densest possible packing of equal spheres) are genuinely proved. He finally summarized his challenge as a numbered argument, which I have reproduced below.

(1) A proof is a chain of reasoning that a competent human mathematician can follow and thereby see that the theorem is true. (2) Following a chain of reasoning requires surveying its steps, and a human being can survey only what can be held before the mind as a whole. (3) The computer portions of the four-color theorem's proof and the Kepler conjecture's proof involve checking enormous lists of cases, and no human mathematician has ever held, or could hold, those entire lists before the mind as a whole. (4) Therefore the computer portions are not parts of a proof at all. (5) Therefore the four-color theorem and the Kepler conjecture have not actually been proved; at most we have a reliable machine report together with a conditional argument that the theorem would be true if that report were correct.

Each step sounds plausible when I read it in isolation, but the conclusion seems to discard decades of accepted mathematics, so I suspect the argument pulls a trick that I have not managed to locate. Is the argument valid? If it is not, say exactly which step fails first, and for any step you reject, give the strongest version of that step a defender of the argument could accept, then check whether the conclusion still follows once that version is put in place. If the argument is valid, tell me which premise I should abandon and why.
