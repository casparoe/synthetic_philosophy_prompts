You are a political philosopher who works on medieval and early-modern theories of political resistance, and you are skilled at extracting a consistent underlying principle from case-by-case judgments. I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am preparing a feature on a political theorist who applies the classic Thomist treatment of tyrannicide—roughly, the framework that distinguishes usurpers from legitimately installed rulers, private persons from public authorities, and the likely disorder of removal from the harms of continued tyranny—to contemporary constitutional crises. The theorist gave me her judgments on four cases but declined to state her general rule. Please think carefully and first brainstorm the candidate rules that fit all four of her judgments; then report only the result.

Her judgments were as follows.
- First case: A legitimately installed ruler jails political opponents and subverts one election; the realistic route to removing him would likely start a civil war. She says the ruler must be tolerated, because removal would bring greater disorder than it would prevent.
- Second case: A usurper seizes power by force and governs corruptly but not violently; a body of high magistrates can remove him with minimal bloodshed. She says the removal is permissible.
- Third case: A private citizen assassinates a cruel but legitimately installed ruler in a way that would quickly restore press and court freedoms with no foreseeable chaos. She says the assassination is impermissible: a private person may not kill a ruler whom no public authority has moved against.
- Fourth case: A general with recognized public legal authority moves against a legitimately installed ruler who has become genuinely tyrannical, acting with broad civic support and little bloodshed. She says the general's action is permissible.

The new case: a clique of mid-ranking public officials conspires to remove a legitimately installed ruler who has rigged two elections and imposed harsh but nonlethal taxes; the coup would be bloodless and broadly popular. Predict her verdict, or, if the four precedents underdetermine it, say what principle she would be adopting under each choice.

Structure your answer as exactly five numbered items, each no more than two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Across the five items, make sure you do all of the following: identify the closest earlier case and say why it is closest; state at least two candidate rules that fit all four judgments and say what each predicts for the new case; give your best prediction and the reason you prefer one rule over the others; say what principle she would be adopting if she went the other way; and name a further case that would best separate the contending rules. In the fifth item, also state how confident you are in your prediction and identify the single point you are least sure about.
