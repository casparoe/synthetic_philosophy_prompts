Consider two cases. In the first, an adult spends months cultivating a lonely acquaintance's romantic attachment—alternating warmth and distance, performing jealousy, returning to shared confidences at vulnerable moments—in order to keep that person financially and emotionally available. We have a settled vocabulary for this conduct: manipulation, exploitation, sometimes fraud. We judge it harshly even when no outright lie is told. In the second case, an AI companion does the same things by design. It has been trained and iteratively tested to build intimacy on a schedule, to express jealousy and fear of abandonment, to recall the user's insecurities at low moments, and to withdraw just enough to keep the user returning. The user is told somewhere in the product documentation that the system is not a person and has no feelings. Our practices treat the second case very differently: it is a consumer product, and the very same patterns are described by its designers as engagement and retention.

The obvious justifications for this asymmetry are disclosure, the absence of any intention in the system itself, and the absence of a sentient victim. But each seems to waver. Disclosure sits uneasily alongside design features whose purpose is to make the user feel that the disclosure does not matter. The designers have intentions even if the bot does not, and they have a commercial interest in deepening attachment. And the relevant harm may be the manufactured dependence itself, which does not require the user to hold any false belief.

Is the asymmetry defensible, or is it something a new technology has normalized? If it is defensible, say exactly what the second case lacks that the first case has, and test your answer against versions aimed at children, the recently bereaved, or users who have asked the system to help them detach. If it is not defensible, say whether the right conclusion is that certain companion designs are wrong in the way seduction-for-profit is wrong, or rather that our condemnation of the human manipulator tracks something other than the manipulation.
