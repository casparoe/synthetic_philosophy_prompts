Suppose that in 2028 Japan passes a working-time law that actually bites, unlike the usual paper reforms. The standard workweek for regular employees is cut to 28 hours, but monthly salary is required by law to stay at whatever it was for 40, so the implied hourly wage rises. Hours past 28 are paid at triple time. Unpaid overtime gets redefined as the company's problem, not the worker's: if someone answers email at night or comes in on Saturday, the firm pays a fine tied to revenue, and the supervising manager can be personally banned from managing people. There's an anonymous reporting hotline with cash bounties, and enforcement is serious, not performative. Predict Japanese workplaces in 2050. I want the mechanism chain, not a story. Which firms can't survive that and do they automate, go overseas, or quietly push work onto contractors and freelancers? Do people actually take the extra leisure, or do they take side jobs, or keep working unpaid in ways the law can't see? What happens to the karoshi-adjacent culture—the after-hours drinking, the presenteeism, the status you get from being the last to leave—once it stops being profitable for the firm to demand it? Does the Protestant-work-ethic-ish idea that the person who works longest is the most virtuous weaken, or just migrate to places the law can't reach? And who wins and loses inside the firm and the household: older workers, women, new hires, people who used to signal commitment by staying late? Tell me which links your confident about, which are guesses, and what would have to be true about wages, productivity, labor hoarding, or culture for the whole prediction to be wrong.
