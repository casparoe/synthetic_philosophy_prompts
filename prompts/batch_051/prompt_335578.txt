In A Treatise on Probability, Keynes defends the view that a rational person's judgment of probability is often neither numerically determinate nor even comparable with every alternative judgment; it may have a more complex, partly non-quantitative structure.

I have been thinking about a distinction that seems to arise as soon as one tries to model such judgments formally. Suppose an agent's evidence constrains their degree of belief in a proposition to an interval, say from 0.4 to 0.7, without licensing any distribution over that interval. One can interpret that interval in at least two ways. On the first, the interval collects the precise probability values that the agent's evidence would permit if the agent chose to adopt a precise numerical probability; the agent has not done so, but any point in the interval would be an admissible precisification of their state. On the second, the interval is the state: the agent does not regard any point value as an admissible precisification, because their evidence itself does not single out a point value, and their doxastic commitment is exhausted by the interval.

These interpretations lead to different criteria for action. Under the first, one might compare acts by the range of expected utilities they produce as the probability ranges over the permitted points, and one might prefer the act with the better worst-case expected utility, or the better best-case, or some trade-off between them. Under the second, one would apply a dominance-like principle: an act is preferred only if its expected utility is at least as high for every probability in the interval, with strict inequality for some, so that the comparison is not a comparison of minima or maxima but of the whole functional profiles over the interval. The two criteria can disagree. One act may have a better minimum than another while nevertheless being worse for most of the interval, and another may dominate throughout the interval while having the same endpoints.

Is there an established name for this distinction — not for the general approach of representing belief by sets of probabilities, but specifically for the difference between treating the set as a range of acceptable precisifications and treating it as a single non-precise doxastic state? If there are standard names, I want to know how they cut the distinction, where the boundary cases lie, and whether one of the two readings is a better successor to Keynes's own distinction between probabilities that are non-numerical and probabilities that are merely not yet assigned numbers. If there is no standard name, then the task is to carve the distinction precisely and say where each interpretation fails.
