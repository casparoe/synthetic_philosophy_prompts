I am a novelist, and I am developing the intellectual backbone of a near-future novel in which a government, armed with machine-learning models and continuous streams of granular data from households and firms, attempts to replace market allocation with algorithmic central planning. I want one of my characters, an economist formed by the Austrian tradition, to reason in a way that is recognizably Hayekian without becoming a caricature. Please speculate about how Friedrich Hayek would assess such a regime if he encountered it today. Which of his objections to central planning would he regard as unanswered by richer data and more powerful computation, and where, if anywhere, might he grant that the technological situation differs in kind from the one he debated against Oskar Lange? I am interested in the underlying conceptual issue, namely whether dispersed, tacit, and constantly changing knowledge can in principle be made available to a planner, rather than in a summary of Hayek's biography or a list of his works.
