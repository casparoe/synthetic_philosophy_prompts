I write about science and ideas, and I've been using a friend, Maya, as a one-person panel for how ordinary people apply the word 'know.' Her calls are wierdly consistent. Here's the track record. She said Dana knows the library is open on Saturday (Dana just wanted to return a book, and Maya was idly wondering whether to go). She said Ravi doesn't know the train will make the connection (Ravi was deciding whether to take that train to a job interview, and Maya was headed to the same interview on the same train). She said Priya knows the coffee cart closes at four (both were just checking whether they'd missed it). She said Sam doesn't know the bridge is safe (Sam was deciding whether to drive over an old wooden bridge to his daughter's recital, and Maya was in the passenger seat). Notice that whenever the person in the scenario had a lot riding on the claim, Maya said 'doesn't know,' and whenever they had little riding on it, she said 'knows'—but her own stakes always lined up with theirs, so the pattern could be read as tracking the speaker's stakes (contextualism) or the subject's stakes (pragmatic encroachment). New case: Tom identifies a mushroom as a chanterelle. For Tom nothing much hangs on it—he's already eaten lunch and is just curious. For Maya, though, a lot hangs on his call: she's about to serve foraged mushrooms at a paid cookery class and will rely on Tom's identification. If Maya wants to stay consistent with her track record, should she say Tom knows it's a chanterelle? Spell out the candidate rules that fit her earlier verdicts, say what each predicts about Tom, identify the closest earlier case under each rule, and if the earlier cases genuinely don't settle it, say what principle she'd be adopting with each answer.
