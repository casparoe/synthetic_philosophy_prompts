In the classic Judy Benjamin problem, a soldier is dropped into a region partitioned by two binary features: whether she is in the Red or Blue sector, and whether she has landed near Headquarters or with the Second Company. She holds a prior over the four resulting cells. She then receives a radio message that does not tell her where she is, but constrains her conditional probabilities: given that she is in Red territory, the chance she is at Headquarters is some reported value q_R, and given that she is in Blue territory it is some reported value q_B. The standard challenge is that such a message is a constraint on a conditional distribution rather than an event in her algebra, so Bayesian conditionalization alone does not pin down a posterior over the four cells, and different proposed update rules yield different answers.

Consider now the following variation. The message is delivered in two stages. The first stage gives the usual point-valued conditionals. The second stage corrects the first: the radioman was reading from a damaged transmission, so the actual conditional values are known only to lie within intervals — say, q_R is between 0.6 and 0.9, and q_B is between 0.3 and 0.6 — and no further information about their exact values will arrive. The soldier is told to treat every point-valued pair inside that rectangular region as a live possibility. How does this interval-valued version change the analysis? Is the set of posteriors satisfying the interval constraint convex, and does a rule that selects the posterior minimizing some measure of overall departure from the prior, such as relative entropy, still yield a unique answer? Or does the interval version reveal that the uniqueness in the original point-valued case was an artifact of the reported exact numbers, so that the real problem was never fully solved? Which competing approach to the classic Judy Benjamin problem, if any, survives this modification intact, and which sub-question is the final verdict most sensitive to?
