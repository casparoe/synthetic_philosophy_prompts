I am writing a novel in which Émilie du Châtelet appears as a character, and while working through her essay on happiness I have repeatedly encountered a comparison that scholars and admirers seem to lean on: her account is like an estate ledger. The analogy runs roughly as follows. Happiness, for her, is not a windfall or an ecstatic transport but a solvent household. You begin with fixed inheritances—constitution, health, rank, sex, temperament, and external circumstances—and you cannot make the harvest come or force the tenants to pay. What you can do is keep accounts in order, refuse speculative investments, retire from ruinous debts, above all the debt of needing others' good opinion, and plant only what your soil will bear. On this reading, the essay's central advice—study, freedom from prejudice, the measured enjoyment of passion without surrendering independence, and the quiet renunciation of ambitions that depend on fortune—is advice not about changing the world but about administering an estate you never fully own. The editor who pressed this analogy on me then drew a conclusion: du Châtelet's ideal of happiness is conservative in the best sense, aimed not at enlarging life but at good husbandry of a limited one. I would like to know how far this really holds. Please give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the land, the fixed income, the unreliable tenants, the debts, the crops, and the seasons. Then show where the analogy breaks. In particular, does it smuggle in a picture of the self as fixed property rather than something shapeable, and does it understate what she thought could be cultivated in the mind? Finally tell me whether the editor's conclusion survives the disanalogies, or whether the analogy was quietly doing the ethical work.
