Ok here's one I'd love for a philosophy of math seminar. The students are upper-level undergrads, maybe 14 of them, mixed math and philosophy, and the session is 90 minutes. I want a classroom activity that makes them feel the force of the structuralist line on arithmetic — that numbers are positions in a shared structure rather than particular objects like sets — by making them run into the identity problem themselves. I don't want a lecture; I want them to discover that two perfectly good set-theoretic constructions of the natural numbers disagree on a question as basic as whether 2 is an element of 4.

The raw material I want you to use, however you present it, is the two standard reductions. Von Neumann's: 0 is the empty set, and each number is the set of all earlier ones, so 1 is {∅}, 2 is {∅,{∅}}, 3 is {∅,{∅},{∅,{∅}}}, and so on. Zermelo's: 0 is the empty set and each number is the singleton of its predecessor, so 1 is {∅}, 2 is {{∅}}, 3 is {{{∅}}}, and so on. Both support full arithmetic if successor and addition are defined in the obvious recursive way, but they disagree on whether 2 has one element or two, whether 0 is an element of 2, and whether 2 is an element of 4.

Design the activity as a game or structured exercise: how to split the groups, and whether to mix math and philosophy students; what they're asked to build; what questions the facilitator asks and in what order; how much time each phase gets; and what, if anything, goes on a handout. I want the moment where a group realizes another group got no on a question they got yes on, even though both are following there rules, to land before the word structuralism is said out loud. The debrief should map what happened onto the idea that these set-theoretic objects can't be what numbers are, or at least that picking one encoding over the other is arbitrary. Also include how to handle the student who says, well, in standard set theory 2 just is the von Neumann ordinal so the question has an answer — I don't want the activity to founder on that.

Finally be honest about what the exercise can't establish: two reductions disagreeing doesn't by itself prove there's no fact of the matter, so the facilitator will have to supply the step from arbitrariness between encodings to numbers as structural roles, and you should say so. And write the whole thing as continuous prose, like an email to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. I know a timeline wants a table, but resist it.
