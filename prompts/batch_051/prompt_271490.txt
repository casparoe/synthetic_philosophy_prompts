I commission and edit a school history textbook series and chair the review panel that must approve, revise, or reject every chapter before layout. The panel is made up of academic historians, classroom teachers, and a lawyer. It meets weekly and has to get through four or five chapters per meeting, normally with no more than an hour of discussion per chapter. We repeatedly stall on one question: a chapter can be accurate in every sentence — every date, quotation, and event verifiable — and yet still feel like a version of the past that is pushing a present-day moral or forcing the events into a single ready-made story shape. We cannot agree on a test for when that crosses from defensible interpretation into distortion.

Write us a reusable decision procedure grounded in the philosophical debate about historical objectivity, running from Ranke's ideal of showing the past as it actually happened, through E. H. Carr's argument that facts become historical facts only by being selected and interpreted by a historian who stands in the present, to Hayden White's claim that historical narratives are inevitably emplotted — cast as tragedy, comedy, romance, or satire — and that the plot is imposed by the historian rather than discovered in the evidence. The procedure must be usable by panel members who have never read Ranke, Carr, or White; your job is to translate whatever in those positions survives contact with a working review into questions an editor can actually ask a draft.

Include the questions in the order they are to be asked; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure should issue a firm reject or a mandatory revision rather than merely flagging a concern; and the exact points at which the panel is unavoidably making a value judgment that no procedure can eliminate. Then run the procedure on three hypothetical chapters: one narrating the origins of the First World War as a tragedy of miscalculation; one narrating early industrialization as a story of rising living standards that omits the factory inspectors' reports on child labor; and one narrating decolonization as heroic national liberation. Arrange at least one of the three so that the procedure returns an answer the panel will be inclined to resist. End by stating what the procedure cannot catch when every sentence in a chapter is individually true, and where the procedure's legitimate authority runs out.
