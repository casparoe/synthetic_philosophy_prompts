I'm a PhD student in philosophy working mostly on population ethics, and in our reading group we've been going around in circles about lexical threshold negative utilitarianism — the view that there's some intensity of suffering above which no amount of mild discomfort or positive welfare can outweigh it, so tradeoffs between severe and non-severe suffering aren't just steep, they're lexicographic. I keep thinking this is right in the abstract but have trouble picuring it as an actual decision procedure, so I've been imagining a mechanism and I want someone to actually run it through.

A large philanthropic foundation adopts a "tiered ledger" for all its global health and welfare grants. Every proposed intervention has to be scored in terms of expected changes in two categories: above-threshold suffering-hours (Tier A) and below-threshold welfare (Tier B), where the later includes mild pains, pleasures, and day-to-day flourishing. The foundation's rule is that any project expected to avert even one Tier A suffering-hour outranks any combination of Tier B projects, no matter how large; projects are only compared within tiers, where Tier B uses linear tradeoffs but Tier A lexically dominates. The threshold itself is fixed by a standing panel, but grant applicants can petition to have a condition reclassified as Tier A by presenting evidence of its intensity.

I don't want a verdict on whether this is good or bad in the abstract. I want a walkthrough of how it would actually behave: what grantees do in the first year, how classification disputes reshape the portfolio, what gets ignored or miscounted, whether the lexicographic structure survives contact with uncertainty and measurement error, and whether the failure modes can be patched without abandoning the core idea. Pretend you're writing to me like an email: continuous prose, no headings or bullet points or bold text or tables.
