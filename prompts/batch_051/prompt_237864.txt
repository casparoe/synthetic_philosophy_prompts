So another grad student in my epistemology cohort has been pushing this proposal for our department's weekly colloquium, and I want to know how it would actually play out, not whether it's a nice ideal. The rule: every time a speaker—or, in Q&A, anyone who asks a question that's really an assertion—makes a factual claim that isn't already established, they have to state a credence in it, between 0 and 1, out loud before they go on. A designated grad student logs the claim, the credence, and a resolution date (default two years out; the claimant can set it sooner). When the date arrives, a committee of three grad students rules the claim true, false, or unresolved, and unresolved claims are dropped. Each resolved claim gets a Brier score: (1−p)² if true and p² if false. At the end of the year everyone's average Brier score goes up on the department website, the best-scoring grad student gets a course release, and the three worst scorers have to be next year's resolution committee. The proposal's author keeps saying that since the Brier score is proper, honesty strictly maximizes your expected score, so no one has any incentive to report anything but there real credence. I know the theorem. I don't think it survives contact with the actual institution. So: what does this mechanism actually incentivize for speakers, for the audience, for the committee, and for whoever keeps the ledger? How would it be gamed? What kind of claims would it selectively reward, and which ones would quietly disappear from talks? And are the failure modes fixable—switch to log score, use outside adjudicators, kill the public leaderboard—or do they point at something deeper about trying to bolt epistemic utility theory onto an existing social practice and expecting propriety to do the work?
