Got into a long argument last night with a friend about a habit I have for handling if-then claims, and I still don't know which of us was right. The habit is this: when someone asserts a conditional in a debate — for instance, that if the government raises corporation tax, investment will fall, or that if we'd left later we'd have missed the train — I don't try to assign it a probability. Instead I imagine the closest situation in which the antecedent holds, with the rest of the background held fixed as far as possible, and I check whether the consequent is true there. If it isn't, I reject the conditional, no matter how confident the person sounded. I've done this for years, mostly in economic and political arguments where I dont trust my gut for probability, and it's saved me from accepting plenty of confident-sounding if-thens.

My friend says I'm using counterfactual machinery on ordinary indicative conditionals, where it doesn't belong. His example was the old Kennedy assassination one: given that somebody shot Kennedy, it's obviously right to say that if Oswald didn't do it, someone else did. But on my test I'd imagine the nearest world where Oswald didn't do it, and since on the version of events I believe he acted alone, that world would have Kennedy not shot at all, so I'd throw out a conditional I should plainly keep. He thinks I'm blurring two quite different questions — what to infer if A turns out true, versus what would have happened if A had been true — and that they come apart exactly in cases like this. I half-see his point, but I also think: if a conditional can't survive being tested in the nearest world where its antecedent is true, why should I believe it at all?

So: is the habit defensible, or is it a bias dressed up as a method? Where does it actually work, and where does it go wrong? Is there a repaired version that keeps the discipline without the mistakes, perhaps by restricting closest-world testing to conditionals that are meant counterfactually and doing something separate for ordinary forward-looking ones? And I'd like you to say what would change your verdict: which argument, example, or distinction would make you decide the habit is fine after all, or make you decide I should give it up entirely.
