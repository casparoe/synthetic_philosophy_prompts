I've been trying to sort out what I think about novelty and adventure as parts of a good life, and my gut verdicts on cases no longer line up with the tidy things I used to believe.

Consider two people with equal total felt enjoyment across their whole lives. Lena never leaves her hometown, keeps the same job, the same friends, and the same routines, and reports a steady mild contentment. Mina moves country every few years, changes careers twice, takes up new crafts and drops them, and reports exactly the same total enjoyment, just distributed in peaks and troughs. I think Mina's life is better for her, not merely more interesting to read about. Yet when I imagine Daniel, who makes himself travel constantly and take up new hobbies because he feels he should, and who finds it stressful and draining and enjoys almost none of it, I don't think his life goes better at all; he'd be better off staying put. And when I imagine a contented homebody for whom change would be distressing, I don't think her life is made worse by lacking adventure — novelty wouldn't be a component of her good. I also have a smaller-scope verdict: two week-long holidays with identical total enjoyment, one spent on the same porch looking at the same lake each day, the other with a different sight each day. I think the varied one is better, though I can't say why if the enjoyment is identical.

My provisional principle is something like: novelty is a good-making feature only when the person is disposed to welcome it, and then it adds value over and above the pleasure it brings. But that principle bothers me, since a good-making feature that switches off depending on taste sounds more like preference-satisfaction than an objective component of well-being. So I want to know what principle, if any, actually captures the pattern.

Reply as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the precise principle that best captures my verdicts, or the smallest set of candidate principles that do; (2) how it handles the Lena/Mina case and the homebody case together; (3) how it handles the Daniel and holiday cases; (4) its verdict on a case I haven't listed — Priya, whose life is varied on paper (new countries, jobs, relationships) but who experiences it all as a blur and later remembers almost none of it; (5) which of my listed verdicts, if any, the principle forces me to give up.
