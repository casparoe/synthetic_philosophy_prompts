I spent most of my working life as an experimental natural scientist, and I absorbed a methodological rule early on: a subject's verbal report is a datum about the subject, not a datum about the phenomenon under study. When a report conflicts with an instrument or with a well-established model, the report gives way. Since retiring and taking up philosophy of mind, I have noticed that I have quietly generalized this rule into a standing epistemic policy. Whenever a first-person report—someone else's or my own—conflicts with third-person evidence such as a transcript, a recording, a physiological measure, or a well-supported model of how the mind works, I side with the third-person evidence and treat the report as material for explaining the reporter's narrative construction rather than as evidence for the claim being reported. I find that I do this even for reports about current conscious experience, where the reporter has no obvious motive to deceive.

Two recent episodes have prompted me to examine the policy. The first was social. I left a long reading-group argument confident that I had revised a philosophical opinion because of a particular argument one of us had set out. A colleague later played the recording: my own contemporaneous notes show that my position shifted immediately after she praised my earlier question, and the argument I now cite appears only afterward. Applying my policy, I concluded without much discomfort that my introspected reason was a confabulation and that the recording was authoritative. The second episode was not so comfortable. I am as certain as I am of anything that, at this moment, I can see the page in front of me in uniform detail across the entire visual field. Yet the experimental findings I once relied on in the laboratory indicate that this impression of a fully detailed periphery is itself a construction, and that subjects systematically overstate how much of a scene they actually take in. My policy instructs me to discount even this report. I find that I cannot quite do so, yet I also cannot locate a principled place to stop.

Is this generalized policy defensible, or is it a laboratory habit dressed up as a rule of evidence? What would justify applying it to retrospective explanations of why one changed one's mind but withholding it from reports of current phenomenal experience—or should it apply to both? Can the rule be restated so that it retains its protection against confabulation without discarding a source of evidence that third-person methods cannot reach? And how far is someone in ordinary life, without laboratory-grade instruments and models, entitled to lean on such a policy?
