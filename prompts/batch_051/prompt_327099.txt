I've usually taken the standard Molyneux answer as obviously right: a man born blind, made to see, won't be able to say by sight alone which is the cube and which the sphere before touching them. But I've been rereading Locke and I'm struck that the bit just before he drops the usual problem says, [from Locke, An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, Book II, Chapter IX, section 8: "the judgment presently, by an habitual custom, alters the appearances into their causes."] That line is doing more work than people admit. If sight is already shot through with judgment and habitual custom, then the "no" answer is not a clean claim about raw sensation; it's a claim about what prior associations the person has formed. So I want to stress-test the no. What would actually count against it? I don't just mean the old Cheselden case or standard objections; I want a map of exposures. Which possible experimental results, accounts of perceptual learning, or findings about multimodal representations in early blindness would be decisive against the standard answer, which would just force a qualifed version, and which can supporters of no dismiss without making their position unfalsifiable? And is it a problem for the no that it's less a fact about perception than a bet about transferable training?
