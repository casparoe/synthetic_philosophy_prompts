In my physics class we discussed Newton's bucket experiment as part of a unit on rotating reference frames, and I have been trying to connect it to a philosophy debate I read about between substantivalists and relationists. As I understand Newton, he suspends a bucket of water by a twisted rope and releases it. At first the bucket spins while the water remains flat; later the water spins with the bucket and its surface becomes concave. Newton argues that the concavity cannot be explained by the water's motion relative to the bucket, since the surface is flat when the water and bucket are moving relative to each other and concave when they are not. He concludes that the water is really rotating with respect to absolute space. Relationists answer that the water's rotation relative to the fixed stars, or to the rest of the matter in the universe, is enough to explain the concavity. But then I am puzzled about how relationism handles an extreme counterfactual. If the entire universe were removed except for the bucket and water, and the bucket were somehow made to spin, would the water's surface become concave? It seems to me that a relationist must say either yes or no. If yes, then relative rotation to other matter cannot be doing the explanatory work, and relationism seems to collapse into substantivalism. If no, then relationism is making an untestable prediction and begins to look like a strange empirical claim rather than a philosophical reinterpretation. Is this a genuine dilemma for relationism, or am I misconstruing what relationism is supposed to do? In particular, I have been assuming that a theory about the nature of motion should settle what happens in the empty-universe case. Is that assumption mistaken?
