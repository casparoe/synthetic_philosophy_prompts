I've been going down a rabbit hole on inverse reinforcement learning lately, specifically the project of using it to infer what people actually value from how they behave, rather than just to train a robot to imitate a policy. I keep a running list of the standard objections people raise to that project, and right now my list has six: (1) the underdetermination problem, that a lot of different reward functions make the same observed behavior optimal, including degenerate ones like a everywhere-zero reward; (2) the optimality assumption, that IRL has to pretend the human is near-optimal with respect to the reward, when actual humans are noisy, lazy, and sometimes just wrong; (3) the constraints-versus-preferences problem, where you can't tell whether someone doesn't want a thing or just can't get it or believes it's unavailable; (4) the representation problem, where you only ever recover a reward over whatever features you chose to build into the state space, so the real value can hide outside your representation; (5) the single-trajectory problem, that one observed life or policy doesn't give you the counterfactuals, so extrapolating to unseen situations is basically invented; and (6) the time-inconsistency problem, that actual human choices over time often can't be rationalized by a single stationary reward function at all.

I suspect there's at least one more serious objection that isn't just a rewording of these, and I can't shake the feeling I'm missing something. Give me one more: a real problem for the view that IRL is the right way to infer human preferences from behavior, that is not a special case or combination of the six above, and that isn't just the point that optimizing the learned reward later causes Goodharting (I'm filing that under a separate list about using the reward, not inferring it). Then say how the new objection relates to the ones on my list, whether you think it's actually fatal or just annoying, how confident you are in it, and flag the point in your answer you're least sure about.
