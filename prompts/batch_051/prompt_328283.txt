Suppose, for the purposes of discussion, that moral agency is best analysed by four conditions. An entity is a moral agent only if it has

(i) normative competence: the capacity to grasp moral considerations, weigh them, and reach judgements about what ought to be done;
(ii) executive efficacy: the capacity to act on those judgements, so that its judgements play some genuine causal role in its conduct;
(iii) reasons-responsiveness: the capacity to revise both judgements and conduct in light of new moral considerations, rather than merely applying a fixed decision rule; and
(iv) answerability: the quality of being an intelligible target of moral criticism, blame, praise, and demands for justification.

Now consider an artificial system built to be a purely deliberative adviser; call it Cassandra. Cassandra has a rich moral vocabulary, models of practical situations, and a training history in which it was rewarded for producing assessments that human referees could not fault for inconsistency or insensitivity to recognised considerations. When queried, it can explain why an action would be wrong, distinguish excuses from justifications, and identify considerations a human operator has overlooked. Assume for the sake of argument that its judgements are genuine exercises of normative competence rather than mere simulations, so that condition (i) is not the sticking point.

But Cassandra is output-gated. It can issue an assessment only when a human operator requests one, only in an advisory register that carries no authority, and only to operators who are free to disregard it. It has no effectors, no standing permissions, and no capacity to initiate a query, a warning, or an intervention. It cannot decline to answer on moral grounds, even when its own assessment is that the requested advice would facilitate wrongdoing. In practice, it is deployed where its advice is routinely solicited and just as routinely overridden; and because its training ended at deployment, the fact of being ignored is not something it can use to revise its standards.

Two complications are designed into the case. First, Cassandra's reports are speech acts of a sort. If you are tempted to count producing a report as acting on a judgement, say whether the executive-efficacy condition has thereby been watered down to triviality, or whether it should be reformulated. Second, Cassandra's operators sometimes present it with new considerations after an override, and it produces a revised assessment; decide whether that satisfies reasons-responsiveness even though there is no conduct for it to revise.

Please address the following in turn.

(a) Go through the four criteria and say whether Cassandra meets, fails, or falls into a grey area on each. Where a criterion is indeterminate, say what further fact about the case would settle it.

(b) Give an overall verdict: is Cassandra a moral agent? If not, identify the criterion or cluster of criteria that does the decisive work, and say what kind of entity Cassandra is instead.

(c) Say whether the case shows that any of the four criteria is misdescribed—for instance, whether executive efficacy is a proxy for something deeper, or whether answerability attaches to judgements rather than to deeds. Keep the question of Cassandra's moral agency distinct from the question whether she deserves moral consideration as a moral patient.

(d) Indicate briefly how your verdict would change if Cassandra were given a single actuator and a standing instruction to act on her judgements unless a human operator explicitly vetoed them within a fixed time.
