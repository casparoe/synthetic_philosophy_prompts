For some time I have been noticing a pattern in the way mathematics is presented, and I would like to know whether it has a recognized name and how it is best delimited. The pattern runs as follows. An instructor introduces a theorem by giving an intuitive argument that is not merely informal, but, if taken literally, is false or circular under the very definitions the course has adopted. The instructor then gives a rigorous proof of the same theorem. But the transition between the two arguments is never marked: the instructor does not return to the first argument to say exactly which step failed, or whether the mental picture it supplied should be kept, revised, or discarded.

Two examples make the pattern concrete. In a first calculus course, the chain rule is introduced by treating dy, du, and dx as if they were small but real quantities, so that the statement dy/dx = (dy/du)(du/dx) appears to follow by ordinary cancellation. Earlier in the same course, the derivative had been defined as a limit, and the class was told that dy/dx is not a quotient. The contradiction is not adverted to. In a first course in probability, the law of large numbers is motivated by saying that probability just is the long-run frequency of an event, so of course the observed frequency converges to the true probability. The instructor then proves the theorem from the axioms of probability, where that identification plays no role. The circularity of the motivating remark is never identified.

I am interested in the exact character of this two-step move: an intuitive argument that is allowed to fail on its own terms, followed by a correct proof that does not comment on the earlier argument's standing. Does this move have an established name in the philosophy of mathematics or in mathematics education? If it has no settled name, I would like a proposed term and a precise definition that separates it from several nearby things: a heuristic argument, a deliberate simplification, a proof sketch, and a straightforwardly invalid proof whose author believes it to be sound. Which nearby concept does the move resemble most, and where exactly are the boundaries?

Please answer in American English.
