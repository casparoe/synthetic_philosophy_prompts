I'm giving a talk at a democratic-theory workshop in a few weeks and I already know the Q&A is going to be rough. My thesis is that in a large electorate the standard lesser-evil argument for voting rests on a mistake: it treats casting a ballot like a high-stakes causal intervention, when actually a single vote is almost never pivotal, so nearly everything left worth caring about is expressive. From there I argue that we should stop talking as if there is a strong moral duty to vote for the lesser evil, and that sometimes refusing to do so — voting third party, or even protesting by abstaining — can be perfectly respctable. The audience is mostly normative political theorists who work on collective action and democratic obligation. They are sympathetic to the idea that voting is a duty, they think your-vote-doesn't-matter arguments are overated, and they are suspicious of expressive theories of voting because they think those theories reduce to moral self-indulgence or virtue signalling. My argument, roughly: pivotal-vote probabilities are tiny; expected-value reasoning doesn't get the duty back because the stakes don't overcome that probability; so the live values are expressive and character-based; and once we admit that, the lesser-evil case loses most of its force.

Draft me a Q&A prep sheet for this crowd. I want the eight or so objections I'll actually get from them — not the ones I'd get from decision theorists or campaign professionals — ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each put the way this audience would phrase it. For each one, tell me whether it has real force and what I should concede up front, give a tight two-minute reply, and note where that reply is soft. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
