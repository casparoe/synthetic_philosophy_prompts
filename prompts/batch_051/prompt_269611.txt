I'm a software engineer, and I've been going round in circles about whether quantum randomness actually helps the free will debate. The coding analogy keeps pulling me in two directions: a deterministic function cannot "choose" its output, but adding /dev/urandom doesn't give the function any more responsibility either. A friend recommended William James's The Dilemma of Determinism, and I'm reading it now. There's a passage where he says, from meory and roughly, that if determinism is true, then judgments of regret become sad, absurd things, because the act was the only possible one. I can't quote it exactly, but his thought seems to be that indeterminism is what makes the feeling that something should not have happened intelligible.

I tried to formalise the quantum version as an argument, and I can't tell if it's valid or where it breaks:

(1) A decision is free only if, at the moment it is made, more than one outcome is physically possible.
(2) If determinism is true, exactly one outcome is physically possible at every moment.
(3) Therefore, if determinism is true, no decision is free.
(4) Quantum mechanics tells us that some physical events, including some neural events, are not fully determined: there is objective chance in them.
(5) At least some decisions involve such undetermined neural events.
(6) Therefore, at those decision points, more than one outcome is physically possible.
(7) Therefore, at least some decisions are free.

I half-suspect step 4 or 7 is doing something sneaky, because "random" and "free" feel completely different. But maybe step 1 is too strong? Please go through it step by step and say whether the argument is valid, and if not, exactly which step fails and under what reading it would need to be restated. Also, if James's point about regret is doing different work than my quantum argument, flag that. If I've got the physics wrong or the setup is confused, tell me plainly instead of just pretending the argument is well-formed.
