I'm a science and ideas journalist, and I'm trying to get past the pop version of Nick Bostrom's simulation argument for a piece I'm working on. In his 2003 paper "Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?" he leans on an assumption that the whole trilemma sort of hangs from:

"The idea is that mental states can supervene on any of a broad class of physical substrates. Provided a system implements the right sort of computational structures and processes, it can be associated with conscious experiences."

He calls this substrate-independence, says it's common in philosophy of mind, and mostly takes it as a given. But the famous third horn — that we're almost certainly in a simulation — only goes through if minds like ours really can run on some other substrate. So I don't want to know whether the argument is valid; I want to know whether this quoted claim is the kind of thing you could actually bring evidence to bear on, and how much a test would buy for the simulation argument. Design the strongest test you can of that claim (or some operationalizeable chunk of it), say what result would count for it and what result would count against it, and then be honest about what even a positive result wouldn't settle. In particular, is the leftover an engineering gap, a conceptual gap about consciousness, or a hole in the simulation argument itself?
