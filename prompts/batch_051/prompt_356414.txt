I'm a philosophy PhD student, and I've somehow managed to walk straight into the kind of tangle I'd normally argue about with my cohort over bad coffee. Two years ago my best friend from undergrad was dying. Her younger brother had just gotten through a long stretch of rehab and was shaky in a way that scared everyone. She asked me—really made me promise—that if she didn't make it, I'd stay in this city and be the one stable person he could count on, the kind of person who shows up every week and notices if he slides. I promised. She died a few months later. I've kept the promise; he's stayed clean, and I'm basically the only person he has.

Now my partner of six years has been offered a tenure-track job eight hours away, and we'd agreed years ago that if either of us got a real shot we'd move together. I made that promise just as sincerely. Her going alone would probably end things, and her turning it down to stay with me feels like it would curdle into resentment. The brother can't relocate, for probation and recovery reasons, and I can't be the person I promised to be over the phone. I've spent months looking for a third option and there isn't one.

What I actually want to think through isn't just which promise to break. It's whether there's a fact of the matter about what I ought to do here at all, or whether some conflicts are genuinely irresolvable—both options wrong, full stop, with the guilt that follows being more than a psychological aftereffect. My advisor keeps saying any decent deontic logic rules that out, because real conflicting obligations would make morality inconsistent, so one duty has to just come out weightier. That feels much too clean to me, like it's solving the logic by missing the people. How should I think about whether a situation like this could really have no right answer, and what it would mean if the guilt is rational rather than just regret? Also I'm aware the whole framing could be self-absorbed; if that's the actual problem, say so.
