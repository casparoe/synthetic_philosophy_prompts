I'm a PhD student in economics, working on decision theory. The dispute over the Ellsberg paradox and ambiguity aversion has been running since Ellsberg's thought experiment and still seems to be at an impasse, and I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself rather than another survey of the models.

Let me lay out the positions as I understand them. The core choice pattern is this: subjects are offered bets on an urn with a known 50/50 composition of red and black balls, and on an urn with 100 balls in an unknown proportion of red and black. Most people prefer to bet on the known urn, and they prefer it whether the winning color is red or black. The expected-utility side says this is incoherent: the two preferences together are incompatible with any single subjective probability over the unknown urn, and the pattern violates Savage's sure-thing principle, so ambiguity aversion is a mistake. The other side says the choices are reasonable and the model of rationality should be generalized to allow probabilities that are not uniquely specified — maxmin expected utility, Choquet expected utility, smooth ambiguity preferences, and robust-control approaches all start from some version of that thought. There is also a deflationary line that says the pattern is an artifact of the standard description: specify the states richly enough — the composition of the unknown urn, or the possibility that it was filled adversarially rather than by an indifferent mechanism — and an ordinary expected-utility maximizer can prefer the known urn without any new model of rationality. The empirical literature does not settle the matter either: ambiguity aversion appears in some elicitation formats and disappears or reverses in others, especially for losses and for low-probability events.

My working hypothesis is that this is not one debate but several running under one name: (a) a formal question about whether Ellsberg-type choices can be represented as expected utility with respect to some probability over a well-chosen state space; (b) an empirical question about how robust and coherent the choice pattern actually is; and (c) a normative question about what rationality requires when probabilities are not given. But I am not confident in that partition, and I am not sure which of these, if any, is the real bottleneck — it may be that the two sides are answering different questions under a shared example, or that the question itself is ill-posed.

So: what kind of disagreement is this really — verbal, empirical, formal, or normative — and where exactly do the sides talk past each other? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a theorem, an experiment, a distinction, a change of question? Or should I expect the debate to remain permanently unresolved because it is several debates that only look like one?
