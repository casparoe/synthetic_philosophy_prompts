In the familiar ticking-time-bomb argument, the case for interrogational torture is made to rest on the interrogators' knowledge: they know an attack is coming, they know the detainee has the information needed to prevent it, and they know the interrogation would yield that information in time. Consider a variant that keeps the underlying facts the same — an attack really is planned, and the interrogation would be effective if the detainee has the information — but changes the interrogators' epistemic position. The authorities now have only a calibrated machine-learning risk-flagging system. It classifies the detainee as high-risk, and the system is known to be right about 80 percent of such flags; in the other 20 percent, the flagged person knows nothing. The interrogators cannot tell which kind of case this is. Does the ticking-time-bomb argument survive this change, does it survive only in a repaired or weaker form, or does it lose its force? Isolate the premise that the change puts under pressure, and say what the argument needs to be true in order to deliver its conclusion. I am interested in what happens to the argument, not in whether any particular policy should be adopted.
