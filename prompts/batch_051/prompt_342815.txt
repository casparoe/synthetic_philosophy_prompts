I'm taking a philosophy of language course and we spent two weeks on metaphor. We read Davidson arguing that metaphors dont have a special "metaphorical meaning" — they mean only what the words literally mean, and the rest is an effect they have on us — and then we read the conceptual metaphor people (Lakoff and Johnson, roughly) who say ordinary metaphors like TIME IS MONEY structure how we think. Heres the thing that buggs me. In everyday life people treat fresh poetic metaphors as if they carry real insight that a paraphrase would lose: if a poet writes "the moon is a sickle reaping the dark," people say the line sees something true that "the moon is a thin crescent at night" misses. But those same people shrug at perfectly systematic everyday metaphors — "I'm at a crossroads in my life," "we sank a lot of time into that project," "what's the next step for us" — as just dead idioms with nothing much behind them. So we credit the novel ones with real content and deny it to the conventional ones, even though both are literally false and both get used to do real cognitive work. Is there a defensible principle that justifies this asymmetry (novelty? richness? context? something about poetry as a practice?), or is it just a habit we should give up? If you had to pick the single strongest justification and test it against a nearby case, what would it be?
