I keep a running note of positions I haven't fully thought through, and right now the entry is on epistemic justification and the regress problem. I'd like someone to audit it and tell me whether the whole set hangs together. Here's the list as it stands:

1. I think the regress argument basically works: if every belief had to be justified by another belief, nothing would ever get justified, so justification has to bottom out somewhere — in something that isn't itself just another belief waiting for support.

2. But I'm not a classical foundationalist. I don't think there are infallible, self-evident basic beliefs immune to revision; even a perceptual belief like "there's a cup" could be overturned in sufficiently weird circumstances.

3. I'm also attracted to coherentism, because in real life whether someone's belief is justified seems to turn on how well it hangs together with the rest of what they except.

4. Still, coherence can't create justification from nothing. A closed, perfectly coherent web of beliefs that's cut off from the world would be no more justified than a well-plotted fairy tale.

5. So I think perceptual experience has to play a special role: my seeing the cup justifies my belief that the cup is there, even though the experience itself isn't the sort of thing that's justified or unjustified.

6. Then again, I don't see how a raw experience — which isn't true or false and dosen't say anything — can rationally support a belief. Reasons seem to be the kind of thing that stands in inferential relations, and sensations don't.

7. And I'm tempted by the deflationary thought that the regress is a pseudo-problem: it comes from insisting on reasons all the way down, but in ordinary life justification works fine without the chain ever being completed.

Treat these as fixed only until they bump into each other. I want an audit, not a survey of the regress literature. Tell me which pairs or clusters are really in tension and which only look that way, order the real tensions by how severe they are, and for each say what the cheapest fix is and what it costs. Then identify the single revision that would clean up the most at once. Last thing: tell me where your own read is most likely to fail, and what argument or consideration would make you reverse a verdict.
