Ok, I keep running into a move in social philosophy and in the stuff my students are reading online: envy and resentment are treated as the moral analogue of pain. Pain isn't a character flaw; it's the body's way of announcing damage, and the sane response is to locate the injury and fix it, not to tell the sufferer to stop hurting. Swap in the social body and the argument is that when workers resent the boss or the poor envy the rich, that's not a vice to be managed — it's the body politic wincing, and the right response is to go find the injustice, not moralize at the sufferer.

I want an actual audit of this analogy, not the usual hand-waving. Give me the correspondence table: what in the social case is supposed to play the pain, the injury, the nervous system, the body, the doctor, and what would phantom-limb pain correspond to. Then walk through where the mapping breaks, and — more importantly — which disanalogies matter for the work the analogy is being made to do and which are just puns. Pay especialy close attention to the fact that the analogy is meant to cover envy and resentment together: does it fit one better than the other? Rawls's treatment of excusable envy has to come in here. He takes ordinary envy to be a vice, but allows that envy can be excusable when inequalities are so great they wound self-respect. Does the pain model capture that line between ordinary and excusable envy, or does it flatten it? And the bottom-line question: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion survive — that we should treat these emotions as injustice signals rather than vices — or was the analogy doing all the work? If a better analogy would do the job without the distorting parts, give me one; if the whole signal picture should be dropped, say so.

Oh and: lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restating the question.
