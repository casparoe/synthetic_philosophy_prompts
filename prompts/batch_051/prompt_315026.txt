I've gotten mildy obsessed with those letter-string analogy puzzles people use to study analogy-making — the ones where you're told abc goes to abd and then asked what iijjkk goes to. The interesting thing is that the rule won't stay put. If you say the rule is replace the rightmost letter by its successor, that gets you abd for abc, but on iijjkk the same rule would give iijjkl, while the answer that feels right is iijjll — the 'rightmost thing' slid from a letter to a group of letters, and 'successor' slid along with it. And then there are the unbounded cases: if abc goes to abd, what does xyz go to? Does successor wrap around the end of the alphabet, is z a dead end, or does the whole operation get renegotiated against the new string? The concept that looked crisp in the source string turns out to be a little fleet of slightly different operations that get selected partly by the target string.

I'm starting a cognitive modeling project on exactly this and want the spec before I pick a framework. What would a theory of concepts-as-they-slip need to get right to handle this microdomain? Give me a requirements list: the cases it has to handle, the distinctions it can't afford to collapse (role vs occupant, a concept vs the pressure that settles one reading of it), the domain facts it can't ignore (alphabet adjacency, the boundary at z, grouping structure), and the pressures it has to balance (keeping the rule 'the same' vs making the whole target string come out well, bottom-up vs top-down influence). For each requirement, give the motivating case and why it's a requirement; mark which are non-negotiable for any account and which are just modeling taste; and if some requirements pull against each other, say which and whether there's a clean resolution or a built-in tradeoff. Write the answer in American English, please.
