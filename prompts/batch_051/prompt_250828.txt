In Science and Method, Henri Poincaré treats the appearance of chance as the product of a small cause: when a cause too small to be noticed determines an effect too large to be missed, we call the effect due to chance. I keep seeing that one observation used as though it already settles what a theory of predictability must do—either to dismiss deterministic but chaotic systems as hopelessly unpredictable, or to claim that determinism and predictability are simply independent questions. Before I take a side, I want the full specification first.

Write the desiderata that a theory of predictability under deterministic dynamics must meet, assuming the purpose is practical: deciding when we are entitled to trust a simulation, forecast, or control policy in a system with imperfect measurements and imperfect models. The list should include: the cases it has to handle (short-range weather forecasting, iterated maps with sensitive dependence, planetary orbits over historical timescales, mixing fluids, population models with shocks, markets with feedback); the distinctions it cannot collapse (initial-condition uncertainty vs. model-form error, finite-horizon local divergence vs. long-run statistical regularity, point forecasts vs. ensemble or probabilistic forecasts, qualitative stable behavior vs. quantitative trajectory); and the results it cannot contradict (continuous dependence on initial data for finite times, sensitivity to finite precision, deterministic systems that nonetheless exhibit stable macrolevel distributions). For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and say which requirements are negotiable and which are not. Then tell me which single requirement does the most to undermine treating Poincaré's observation as a complete account, and score briefly how broad positions like Laplacean determinism, effective unpredictability, and statistical determinism fare against it. A table is fine.
