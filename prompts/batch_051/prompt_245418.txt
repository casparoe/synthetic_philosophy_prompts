You are a moral and political philosopher who works on aggregation, risk, and distributive justice. Think carefully, and before you propose any unifying principle, spend some time laying out the surface differences among the practices below: who acts, whether the harm arrives by act or by omission, whether the people harmed are identified, and what the decision procedure looks like.

Consider the following package of contemporary practices, all of them familiar in wealthy liberal societies.

First, triage. In a disaster or pandemic, medical staff use scoring protocols that deprioritize or turn away a patient with a low probability of survival so that scarce resources can be used on several patients whose probability of survival is much higher. The practice knowingly lets the aggregate number of survivors, rather than any individual patient's claim, drive the allocation.

Second, regulatory cost-benefit analysis. Government agencies aggregate tiny fatal risks across a large population into expected deaths, often called statistical deaths, and treat those expected deaths as a cost that can be offset by other benefits, including other lives saved. A safety intervention may be rejected because each marginal statistical life saved costs too much, while the same resources are directed to a cheaper intervention that saves more statistical lives elsewhere. The agency is, in effect, trading expected deaths across different people against one another and against money.

Third, bright-line vetoes on deliberate killing. The same societies flatly forbid transplant surgeons from killing one healthy person to harvest organs for five dying patients, no matter how many would be saved. And their criminal law, in nearly every jurisdiction, does not permit deliberately killing an innocent person because doing so would foreseeably prevent several other killings. These vetoes hold even when the arithmetic points the other way.

Question: What philosophical commitments about the separateness of persons — and about when harms and benefits may legitimately be aggregated across lives — does this whole pattern of practices presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can. Then separate what the practices strictly presuppose from what they merely fit well with, and identify the places where the package is philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories would license the same practice. Finally, judge whether the package is coherent: is it a defensible division of moral labor between contexts, or does it involve an inconsistency serious enough to require changing either the triage and cost-benefit practices or the vetoes? Conclude by saying what concretely would have to change in one of the three practices if the implicit theory were replaced with (a) straightforward act-utilitarian aggregation across lives and (b) a strict prohibition on using persons merely as means.
