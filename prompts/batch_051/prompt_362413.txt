Me and a couple friends have been stuck on this argument for like two years now, and I can't even tell what kind of disagreement we're having anymore. The question is whether a company itself can be blamed for the harm it causes, as opposed to only ever blaming the actual people inside it.

The setup we keep coming back to is something like a chemical plant where a spill happens because three different departments each made a defensible call on their own, a safety warning got buried in a routine filing, and no single person ever chose the bad outcome. We all agree on the facts: no one wanted the spill, the decision was spread out over a bunch of people and procedures, the company has no nervous system and can't feel guilty, and the law happens to treat the company as a unit for some purposes. Still doesn't settle it.

One side says the company itself can be blamed. Their line is that a company has goals, plans, records, procedures for making decisions, and a kind of memory; when the disaster is produced by that whole apparatus, it's not reducible to any one employee's choice, so blaming only the individuals misses the actual thing that did the damage. On this view, saying "the company is to blame" doesn't mean it feels bad inside; it means the organization is the right thing to hold answerable, make pay, and force to change.

The other side says no, blaming a company is like blaming a spreadsheet. Blame needs somebody home: a single mind that could have known, could have chosen otherwise, and can feel the weight of what it did. A company is a legal fiction, so you blame the engineers who signed off, the manager who buried the report, the executives who set the cost-cutting targets. They also point out that fines just get absorbed as a cost of doing business, so what we call "blaming the company" is often really just pricing bad behavior, not blame at all.

I've got a few guesses about why we can't move. Maybe we're using "blame" for two different jobs: one side means it backwards-looking (who did it and deserves it) and the other side means it forward-looking (what lever do we pull to get repair and prevention). Or maybe it's not that sophisticated, and its just a bedrock difference about whether a bunch of people plus rules can amount to a "thing" that acts, the kind of intuition you can't argue somebody into or out of. Or maybe there's a hidden factual question underneath, about whether corporate decisions ever really run through the kind of single plan that would make a company count as a chooser.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict: what kind of disagreement is this actually? Is it verbal, is it about the point of blame, about starting intuitions, or about some fact we haven't looked at? And given your diagnosis, is there anything that would actually move it, or is this the kind of impasse where nobody should expect the other side to budge?
