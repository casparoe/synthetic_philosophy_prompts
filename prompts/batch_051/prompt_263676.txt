I am a retired physical chemist, and I have spent the past year reading philosophy of science seriously for the first time—Kuhn, Popper, Lakatos, and most recently Feyerabend—with a small discussion group of fellow retired scientists. What keeps surprising me is how differently each of us understands the idea of a scientific method once we are forced to adjudicate concrete historical episodes instead of reciting the textbook account. I would like to make a handout for our group: a decision tree that sorts a reader into a position on whether there is a scientific method, based entirely on what they say about cases and commitments rather than on which label they would choose for themselves.

Please write that decision tree. Each internal node should be a plain-language yes-or-no question that a working or retired scientist could answer without already knowing the philosophical vocabulary—for example: whether a theory must be abandoned as soon as it faces an apparent falsification, or may instead be retained by adjusting an auxiliary hypothesis (draw on the anomalous orbit of Uranus and the proposal of Neptune, not on the abandonment of Newtonian mechanics); whether the same criteria should govern theory choice across every field and every historical period; whether a statement of method is meant to describe how scientists in fact reason or to prescribe how they ought to reason; whether a rule that a successful historical episode legitimately ignored is thereby shown not to be a rule at all; and whether the norms of scientific inference can themselves be tested empirically and revised. Do not use items that amount to asking readers whether they are Popperians, Kuhnians, or the like.

The leaves should include at least the familiar textbook method—a single universal procedure centered on hypothesis formation, prediction, and experiment—falsificationism, Lakatos's methodology of research programmes, a Kuhnian values-without-rules position, Feyerabend's anything-goes position (carefully distinguished from the straw man that all standards whatsoever are abolished), a Bayesian position treating scientific inference as probability updating rather than as a fixed rulebook, a pluralist position, and Laudan's normative naturalism if the tree can separate it from the others. For each leaf, add a single sentence stating its most awkward commitment. Please also state explicitly which pairs of positions the tree cannot cleanly separate without adding a further and quite specific question, and flag any combination of branch answers that no known position occupies, noting whether it is unoccupied because the resulting view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Aim for roughly eight to twelve nodes and no more than ten leaves; the audience is retired scientists and interested lay readers, so define any technical term briefly as you use it.
