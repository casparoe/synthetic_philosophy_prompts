In a metaphysics seminar on identity and individuation, we have been working through Max Black's two-sphere universe: a world containing nothing but two iron spheres exactly alike in size, shape, temperature, and composition, arranged symmetrically in otherwise empty space. The standard conclusion in the room is that Black's example refutes the identity of indiscernibles, at least in its qualitative form, since the two spheres share every qualitative property yet seem plainly to be two. I find that conclusion too quick, and I would like to defend the claim that Black's example does not refute the version of the principle that a careful defender of the identity of indiscernibles should actually hold.

Please make the argument for me. Develop it by distinguishing the strong qualitative principle (roughly, that no two things can share all their qualitative properties) from a formulation that permits primitive, non-qualitative individuators such as haecceities, and argue that the intuition that there are two spheres tacitly relies on the very non-qualitative individuation the example is supposed to overturn. Then face the strongest objection squarely: that this reply saves the principle only by trivializing it, and that the whole interest of the identity of indiscernibles lay in excluding bare non-qualitative distinctions. Begin the response with the thesis you are defending as its first sentence, then give the reasoning. Do not preface the response or restate the question.
