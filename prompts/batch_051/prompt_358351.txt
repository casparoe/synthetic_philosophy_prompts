While studying the debate over moral testimony for a class, I developed a distinction that I would like an honest assessment of. I call it the difference between treating another person as a moral calculator and treating them as a moral sounding board. If I use someone as a moral calculator, I ask for their verdict about what I should do and adopt that verdict because I trust them; my own engagement with the underlying reasons remains idle. If I use them as a moral sounding board, I also ask for their view, but I treat that view as material to think with: I ask why they see things that way, test their reasons against my own reactions, and hold myself responsible for the verdict I eventually reach even when it matches theirs.

A case might make the distinction clearer. A friend tells me that a comment I was about to make would be cruel. If I simply keep the comment to myself on her authority, I have used her as a moral calculator. If I ask what would make the comment cruel, work through her answer on my own, and conclude that she is right because I can now see the cruelty for myself, I have used her as a sounding board. The outward behavior may be identical; the kind of moral competence I am left with is not.

My central claim is that many pessimists about moral testimony are really objecting only to the first practice and have accidentally wound up criticizing the second. The optimists, meanwhile, often defend something like the second practice while calling it testimony. If that is right, some of the dispute over moral testimony is not a disagreement about one practice but a misalignment between two different practices that share the same surface grammar.

I would like to know whether this distinction already appears in the literature under another name, or whether it collapses under pressure. Could a pessimist consistently object that even the sounding-board practice is illegitimate, because moral understanding cannot be acquired from another person in any form? Could an optimist reply that the calculator practice is justified more often than I have admitted? In short, does the distinction do the philosophical work I am assigning to it, or is it cleaner in my head than it is in reality? Please write your response in American English.
