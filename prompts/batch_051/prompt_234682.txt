i've been working on a novel about a woman in her mid-thirties who gets assessed for adult ADHD after months of seeing symptom videos online, and I'm stuck on how to render her relationship to the diagnosis without either endorsing it as pure self-discovery or treating it as just a social contagion. I read Ian Hacking on looping effects a while back, and the idea that classifications of people can alter the people classified — because they become aware of the label and react to it — feels like it might give me the vocabulary I need. But I want to check whether I'm actually dealing with a looping effect or just a regular case of people learning about their own minds.

Here's my working criteria for a Hacking-style looping effect: there's a classification aimed at people; the classified people know they fall under it and care about that fact; their self-understanding or behavior shifts in response; the experts then revise the classification in light of those shifts; and the revised classification loops back to the classified people again. My borderline case is the recent wave of adult ADHD diagnoses, especially in women, where people often arrive at the clinic already fluent in the diagnostic language from online communities, describe their lives in terms of symptoms, and are assessed in a context where the clinician knows this is happening. The official model says ADHD is a stable neurodevelopmental condition with childhood roots, but the referral population seems to be shaping itself around the criteria. My question is: does this qualify as a looping effect under my criteria, or does the neurodevelopmental claim block that? Go criterion by criterion, and if the answer is no for some of them, tell me whether the criteria are too strict or the case is genuinely different. And if it does qualify, should that make me as a novelist treat the diagnosis as less real, or is that a false inference? I'm less interested in the clinical debate than in whether the concept of looping effects can handle a case where the condition is supposed to be brain-based but the presentation is culturally amplified alot.
