I am a retired physical chemist, and after four decades of working with irreversibility in the laboratory I have begun reading the philosophy of physics seriously. A colleague who teaches an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the direction of time has asked me to help design the final take-home essay, and I would like you to prepare the complete assessment package.

The essay is a three-hour take-home exercise worth 25 marks. It must be answerable without research, but it must not be answerable by reciting the standard narrative that the Second Law makes entropy increase and that this is why we remember the past rather than the future. The question should force students to engage the possibility that the thermodynamic arrow is partly an artifact of our epistemic and interventionist posture toward evidence.

The crux I want the question built around is this. Microphysical dynamics are time-reversal invariant, so a low-entropy boundary condition at one time does not by itself single out the future as the direction of increasing entropy; some package such as the Past Hypothesis plus a statistical postulate is introduced to close the gap. But a persistent line of criticism holds that the gap is not entirely in the world. Our acquisition and use of evidence is itself temporally asymmetric: we treat records and memories as reliable traces of an already settled past while treating the future as something to be predicted and manipulated, and our judgment that the past had lower entropy may depend partly on those asymmetric practices rather than being independently read off nature. A student who has only memorized the labels will not be able to distinguish the claim that the thermodynamic asymmetry is a robust feature of the universe from the claim that it is partly constituted by the way we infer and act. The question should separate those two kinds of student.

Please prepare the following.

(1) A single essay question asking whether the Past Hypothesis together with a statistical postulate can account for the full set of temporal asymmetries — thermodynamic, epistemic, causal, and interventionist — or whether some of those asymmetries are better understood as conditions of inquiry that no low-entropy boundary condition can fully ground. The question should name the interventionist challenge without stating its conclusion in advance.

(2) A model answer of roughly 900–1,200 words. It must do each of the following with argument rather than assertion:
- formulate the reversibility objection precisely, including why a low-entropy state would, under naive retrodiction, seem to make entropy higher toward the past as well as toward the future;
- distinguish the entropic arrow from the epistemic and interventionist arrows, and explain why records, memories, and prediction resist reduction to a bare entropy gradient;
- state the interventionist challenge fairly: our asymmetric practices of inference and intervention may partly constitute, rather than merely detect, the thermodynamic asymmetry;
- assess how much the Past Hypothesis and a statistical or typicality postulate can answer that challenge, and where the package implicitly leans on causal or epistemic priorities;
- address the charge of circularity or self-support, namely that the past is counted as low-entropy partly because records are treated as reliable while records are treated as reliable partly because the past is counted as low-entropy, and say whether this is a benign coherence of mutually supporting inferences or a genuine explanatory circle;
- reach and justify a definite conclusion about whether the thermodynamic arrow is a physical feature of the world, partly a projection of our epistemic posture, or some principled mixture.

(3) A marking scheme with six rubric points totalling 25 marks, aligned to the six tasks above. For each rubric point, give the number of marks and one line specifying the concrete error made by candidates who possess the right vocabulary but have not understood the logic — for example, conflating the time-reversibility of the laws with the time-reversal invariance of particular trajectories, or treating the claim that the past has lower entropy as a direct observation rather than as an inference from records whose reliability is part of what is at issue.

Write in a formal register and do not ask for citations, footnotes, or reading lists.
