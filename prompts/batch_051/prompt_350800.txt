Last night, a friend and I spent a long evening arguing about whether the obligation to avoid animal products can be stated as a single rule everywhere, or whether its demands properly vary with what else is available. She calls herself broadly sympathetic to veganism, but she rejects the claim that everyone, everywhere, is required to avoid every animal product irrespective of circumstances. When I pressed her on particular cases, she settled on the following judgments, each after some back-and-forth:

1. An affluent professional in a large Western European city has cheap and varied plant foods readily available, but routinely buys industrially produced pork and eggs. She said this is not permissible.
2. A family in a remote Arctic village, where imported plant foods are scarce, extremely expensive, and nutritionally inadequate, relies on locally hunted seal and fish as its staple foods. She said this is permissible.
3. A pastoralist family in the Sahel keeps cattle whose milk and occasional meat are their main buffer against crop failure and hunger; in bad years the animals are the difference between eating and not. She said this is permissible.
4. A middle-income family in a South Asian city has cheap, nutritionally adequate lentils, chickpeas, rice, and vegetables available year-round, but buys industrially farmed chicken because it is inexpensive and they prefer the taste. She said this is not permissible.
5. A poor fishing family on the coast of Bangladesh relies on small fish as its cheapest adequate source of complete protein; rice and legumes are available, but at the family's income a fully plant-based diet would leave the children short on several nutrients. She said this is permissible.
6. A middle-class family lives where eating goat at an annual religious festival is an important cultural practice. They have adequate plant foods all year and no nutritional need for the meat, but abstaining would cause real social friction within their extended family. She hesitated, then said this is not permissible.

The case we ended on, and never settled, is this. A rural household in a lower-middle-income country keeps a small flock of heritage-breed laying hens that roam freely, are fed kitchen scraps and some purchased grain, receive basic veterinary care, and are not killed when they stop laying. The family eats the eggs and occasionally sells a few. Good rice, beans, and greens are available and affordable, and the household could be adequately nourished without the eggs; the eggs provide extra protein and a little income but are not needed to prevent malnutrition.

I am less interested in the bare verdict than in the rule behind it. Identify the two or three most plausible rules that fit her six stated judgments, and say explicitly whether each rule would permit or forbid the eggs. Note any of her six judgments that each rule handles awkwardly. Then give your best prediction of her actual verdict on the egg case, and the rule—or weighted combination of rules—you think best captures her, with your reasons for preferring it over the alternatives. Finally, specify one further hypothetical case, as concretely as possible, that would most sharply distinguish the leading candidate rules.
