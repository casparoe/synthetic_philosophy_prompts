Something from lunch last week keeps going back and forth in my head, and I'd love to see it argued out here because I know some of you actually know the speech-act literature cold. Two of my coworkers, Ravi and Dana, got into it about something our manager did. Earlier that day, the manager had made a cutting remark about a junior coworker's presentation in front of maybe six people — something like "I see someone didn't do the reading" — and after an awkward silence he said "I take that back." No apology, just that. Dana said later that the retraction did basically nothing and the remark still stung; Ravi said she was underselling it. It went roughly like this:

(1) Dana: "You can't unsay an insult. Everyone already heard it, the sting already landed. 'I take it back' doesn't reach into the past. So whatever he was doing, he didn't un-insult her."

(2) Ravi: "That proves too much. No speech act can undo its own effects — an apology doesn't erase the offense either. Retraction is a real act with a real institutional function: retract an objection in a meeting and the objection is off the table and off the record. The test isn't whether everyone feels better, it's whether the speaker withdraws the thing he did."

(3) Dana: "But the main thing an insult does isn't a commitment you can withdraw. It's a demotion in front of witnesses — it tells the room the target is fair game. Retraction only addresses the least important part of the insult. It's like taking a photo down after everyone at the party has already seen it."

(4) Ravi: "The manager's line wasn't really a factual assertion, so there wasn't much of a claim to retract. What he actually did was downgrade the illocutionary force after the fact: he went on record, with witnesses, as not standing behind the jab. That changes which messages the group is licensed to take away. If he'd said 'I was clearly joking,' you'd give that at least some weight too."

I didnt jump in, and I wont say which side I was leaning toward. What I want is a move-by-move verdict: rate each of (1)-(4) from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and say who's right about which specific point and what, if anything, both of them are missing. While you're at it, please seperate what's settled about how these acts work from what's genuinely contested among people who actually know the topic, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus. American English, please.
