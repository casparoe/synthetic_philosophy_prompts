I'm on the committee writing this year's comprehensive exam for our PhD program in philosophy of cognitive science, and I've been asked to draft one essay question on whether reinforcement learning agents can suffer. Many of our students came up through the alignment and rationality literature, so they know the RL formalism (states, actions, rewards, policies, value functions), the standard point that the reward channel is not the objective, and the dopamine reward-prediction-error findings. I want a question that cannot be answered by reciting that standard point. The crux should be: what would have to be true of an agent's architecture for its reward signal to count as felt valence, and which features of a reward channel license or block the inference from low reward to suffering?

Please write the question, a model answer of about 800 words, and a marking scheme for a 20-mark essay answerable in 90 minutes. The marking scheme should name five must-hit moves with the marks each is worth, plus a one-line note on how candidates typically get each one wrong. A top answer should: (i) steelman the strongest version of the reward-as-valence thesis rather than attacking the version that treats reward as a mere scalar; (ii) distinguish reward as an optimization target from reward as a channel whose dynamics could carry hedonic tone; (iii) say why the possibility of directly manipulating the reward channel, i.e., wireheading, cuts both ways as evidence for the valence reading; (iv) specify at least one additional architectural condition that would have to be present before low reward licenses an attribution of suffering, such as integration into a self-model, global availability, or counterfactual sensitivity; and (v) answer the objection that avoidance behavior plus a negative error signal is no more evidence of suffering than a thermostat's behavior is. The one-line notes on typical errors should name the specific confusion each move forestalls rather than just saying that candidates are vague.
