I'm on my hospital's scarce-resource allocation committee, and we're stuck on a decision that has to go out by Wednesday. We have one vial of an antivenom and six patients from the same incident. One patient will die unless he gets the whole vial; the other five will die unless they each get one-fifth of it. With the right dose everyone is expected to recover fully, and without it everyone dies. There's no other relevant difference among them — same prognosis, same time of arrival, nobody's a staff member or anything. The draft decision in front of us says split the vial and save the five, on the grounds that five lives are better than one.

One committee member keeps objecting that this is an arithmetic mistake. Her line is: the loss each of the five would suffer is no worse than the loss the one would suffer, so you can't add the five losses together to get a bigger loss that outweighs his. Five people dying isn't a greater harm, she says; it's just one person dying, five times over. How much of the vial you happen to need is a fact about your body, not about how strong a claim you have on being saved. So she wants a coin toss: heads, the one gets the entire vial; tails, we split it among the five.

I find the coin toss genuinely hard to shake, but I also can't see how to defend knowingly leaving four extra people dead when we could have saved them. The chair asked me to draft the short decision memo to circulate before the meeting, with a definite recommendation and the reasoning. Could you write it? Make it a document the committee could actually adopt: recommend split, coin toss, weighted lottery, or something else, and give the strongest version of the case against your recommendation before you answer it. We're all tired of the back and forth, so the memo should actually settle it.
