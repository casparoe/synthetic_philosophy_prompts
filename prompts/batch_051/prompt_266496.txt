I have been sitting in on a metaphysics reading group devoted to the nature of laws, and one of the senior participants has a habit I have started to record: whenever someone describes a small toy universe and a candidate generalization, she announces whether she would count that generalization as a law of the universe in question. Her rulings so far are these. In a universe containing many massive bodies whose motions are exactly captured by an inverse-square gravitational principle, with no exceptions anywhere in its history and with the principle sustaining the relevant counterfactuals, she calls the principle a law. In a universe that includes billions of gold spheres all smaller than one mile across, where the dynamics fully permit larger gold spheres and the absence of any larger sphere is traceable to the initial distribution of matter rather than to the dynamics, she declines to call the generalization that all gold spheres are smaller than one mile across a law. In a universe where the generalization that every F is G holds without exception but F and G are highly disjunctive, gerrymandered classes with no natural unity, she likewise declines to call it a law. In a universe where a stable decay frequency recurs across every sufficiently large sample and every sufficiently long interval, she is willing to call the probabilistic decay rule a law rather than a mere summary of outcomes.

Next week she will consider a universe containing many X-particles and many Y-particles, where the structure of the universe confines the two populations to permanent and mutually exclusive regions, so that no X-particle ever meets a Y-particle at any point in the history; the two kinds of particle are nonetheless abundant. The candidate generalization is that whenever an X-particle meets a Y-particle, both are annihilated. The generalization is exceptionless but empty, since it has no actual instances. I am trying to predict, before the session, whether she will count this uninstantiated generalization as a law of that universe. I am less interested in the bare yes or no than in the diagnosis: which accounts of lawhood her four earlier rulings leave open, which accounts they already rule out, how the surviving accounts divide on this uninstantiated case, and which verdict she should give if she wants to remain consistent with her track record. Then say what she would be committing herself to under either verdict, and specify one further toy case that would most cleanly settle which account of lawhood she is actually operating with.
