I cover behavioural science and public policy for a general-interest magazine, and I am writing a long piece revisiting the United Kingdom's automatic enrolment in workplace pensions as a test of nudge-style policymaking. Please analyse automatic enrolment through the lens of libertarian paternalism, and then give an honest account of where that framework breaks down.

For background, I know that under the Pensions Act 2008 employers have been required since 2012 to enrol eligible workers automatically into a qualifying workplace pension, with minimum contributions phased in over several years, and that employees may opt out. What I want is a careful mapping: who the choice architect actually is here, what the default and the opt-out look like, and which assumptions about human judgement and motivation the policy imports from behavioural economics. Then assess what the nudge framework explains well about the policy's design and its high participation rates, and what it misdescribes or cannot address, such as the employer's role in setting the default, the difference between steering someone toward a preference they already hold and steering someone who has no determinate preference about retirement saving, and the blurring of the line between a nudge and a mandate once the default is sticky and financial literacy is low. Close by saying what the case shows about libertarian paternalism itself: whether automatic enrolment confirms it, exposes a flaw in it, or reveals that the framework was never really built for cases like this. Please write the answer in British English.
