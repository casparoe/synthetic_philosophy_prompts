The exam question was: "Explain the structure of the central argument in Part 1 of Hume's 'Of Miracles.' What, on this argument, can testimony reasonably establish about whether a miracle occurred, and why? Then give one standard objection to the argument and one possible Humean reply."

The student wrote:

Hume defines a miracle as an event that violates a law of nature. Laws of nature are built up from uniform past experience, so the evidence for a law of nature is the strongest kind of empirical evidence there can be. Testimony is also evidence grounded in experience, but only in our experience that witnesses generally tell the truth. When someone reports a miracle, two bodies of evidence conflict: the evidence for the law, and the evidence for the witness's reliability. Hume thinks the testimony could win only if it would be more miraculous for the witness to be lying or mistaken than for the reported event to have happened; but since a miracle is by definition maximally improbable, this condition can never be met. So Hume concludes that a rational person can never believe on the basis of testimony that a miracle occurred, and therefore miracles cannot happen. The standard objection is that the argument is circular, because Hume builds the falsity of miracles into the definition of a law of nature. A Humean could reply that the argument doesn't prove miracles impossible; it only says the evidence against them is overwhelming, given how uniformly our experience runs, and that uniformity is an empirical fact rather than an assumption.
