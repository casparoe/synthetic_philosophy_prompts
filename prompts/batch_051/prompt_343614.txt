I keep returning to a few pages in *Computing Machinery and Intelligence* where Turing treats extrasensory perception, especially telepathy, as a live threat to the design of the imitation game. His position, as I understand it, is roughly this: the statistical evidence for telepathy is strong enough that a serious test of machine intelligence must guard against it, and so the game would need to be run under conditions that block telepathic leakage between the human player and the interrogator. I find myself wanting to defend this as methodologically sound, even though it strikes many readers as an embarrassing lapse in an otherwise sober paper. I would like you to play it straight and tell me what would count against this position.

I do not mean the cheap objection that ESP simply does not exist. I mean the more pointed defeaters: what empirical findings, replication failures, or better explanations of the parapsychology literature would genuinely undermine Turing's treatment? Which assumptions in his argument carry the weight? For example, would his position be weakened if the apparent effects turned out to be sensory leakage or fraud, or if telepathy existed but could not transmit the kind of discriminating cue the imitation game exploits, or if later accounts of scientific evidence showed he was wrong to treat parapsychological statistics as ordinary empirical data? What would a committed defender of Turing have to concede counts against him, and what can they reasonably dismiss? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say whether the position is well insulated and whether that insulation should trouble a defender.
