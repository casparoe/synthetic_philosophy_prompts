I have been reading Epictetus' first chapter of the Discourses, and what strikes me is that he does not introduce the distinction between what is and is not in our power as a piece of self-help advice, but as the conclusion of an argument about what a rational creature embedded in a body could have been given. Here is the passage I mean (Epictetus, Discourses 1.1, trans. George Long):

> But what says Zeus? "Epictetus, had it been possible I should have made both this paltry body and this small estate of thine free and unhampered. But as it is—let it not escape thee—this body is not thine own, but only clay cunningly compounded. Yet since I could not give thee this, we have given thee a certain portion of ourself, this faculty of choice and refusal, of desire and aversion, or, in a word, the faculty which makes use of external impressions; if thou care for this and place all that thou hast therein, thou shalt never be thwarted, never hampered, shalt not groan, shalt not blame, shalt not flatter any man."

Before I read any further in the modern Stoic literature, I want the specification that an adequate account of the dichotomy of control must meet, not a survey of who says what. Modern writers use the distinction in incompatible ways: as a strict two-way split, as a three-way split that adds a category of partial control, or as a continuum of influence rather than a difference of kind; and they want it to do different jobs, from reducing anxiety to assigning responsibility. Set the existing contenders aside and give me the desiderata: the cases a defensible account must handle, the distinctions it cannot afford to collapse, the claims in the passage above it must preserve or knowingly reject, and the constraints from ordinary life—compulsion, grief, illness, structural disadvantage, practices of holding people responsible—that it must not trip over. For each requirement, say what consideration motivates it and whether it is non-negotiable or merely the preference of one camp. Then flag any pairs in your list that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied.

Keep the weight on the requirements and their motivating cases rather than on any scorecard of contenders; the criteria are where the thinking is. The part I most want, though, is the pruning: state which single requirement does most to rule out (a) the strict internalist reading, for which only judgements and acts of assent are up to us and everything else is mere externals, (b) the therapeutic three-way reading, which softens the doctrine into complete/partial/no control and abandons the metaphysical claim that the body is alien to us, and (c) the degrees-of-influence reading, which dissolves the dichotomy into a scalar measure of what one can bring about.
