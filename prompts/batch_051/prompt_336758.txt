I teach an undergrad environmental humanities course, about 18 students, and I've got a 75-minute session coming up on the Anthropocene. I want to run a structured activity where the students themselves feel why the question "when did the Anthropocene start?" is so hard to answer, and why so much of the answer depends on who's asking and for what purpose. No lecture up front.

My working design: split the class into four groups, each defending a candidate boundary between the Holocene and the Anthropocene. The candidates I have in mind are: around 1610 (the Orbis spike idea, a CO2 dip tied to the depopulation of the Americas), the late 1700s to early 1800s (coal and the industrial revolution), the mid-1940s (nuclear test fallout, especially plutonium), and the mid-20th century Great Acceleration (the sharp upturn in CO2, methane, plastics, concrete). Each group gets a card I'll make, describing the physical signal, where it shows up, and the main objections.

The twist: the class makes the decision twice under different rules. Round one is a geological comission — they can only weigh whether the signal is global, roughly synchronous, durable, and detectable far in the future. Round two is a negotiation with assigned roles: a representative of a low-lying island state, a historian of industrialization, a mining company person, an ecologist, an indigenous community representative whose territory is being dated, and a future geologist trying to read the rock record. Same candidates, same facts, but now any kind of reason is allowed. My hunch is the winning date will move between rounds, and that's the whole point.

Design this properly for me: exact rounds with time boxes, what the candidate cards and role cards should contain (describe them, I'll write them out), the decision procedure for each round, and the debrief. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the real issue: stratigraphic criteria themselves encode values, so the role round isn't the only politics in the room. Tell me what will predictably go wrong running this — my fear is students deciding that round one is "the science" and round two is "just politics", and landing on a lazy relativism about there being no fact of the matter. Also be honest about what the activity can't reproduce: the actual committee process is decades long, and the official geological timescale is itself an institution with its own history. Keep it to one session, no pre-reading.
