One recurring analogy in the philosophy of mind is that introspection is a form of inner perception. On this picture, just as vision discloses the layout of a room, introspection discloses one's own beliefs, desires, emotions, and sensations; the mind is conceived as containing states to be observed, and the self as something like an internal observer. The analogy is frequently put to argumentative work: because a person's access to her own mental life is said to be observational, her sincere self-ascriptions are commonly treated as carrying something like the authority of a careful witness's perceptual reports.

Please audit this analogy in detail. First, produce a correspondence table setting out what is supposed to play the role of the sense organ, the sensory field, the object perceived, the observing subject, and the conditions of good and poor observation. Second, identify where the analogy breaks. In doing so, give particular attention to the following: whether introspection supports an appearance/reality distinction of the kind perception presupposes; whether the act of introspecting can alter or even constitute the very state being inspected; and whether the analogy quietly imports a private mental theatre in which an inner spectator watches the passing show, thereby creating further puzzles rather than explaining anything. Third, among the disanalogies you identify, separate those that matter for the analogy's use in debates about self-knowledge from those that are merely cosmetic. Finally, assess whether the conclusion the analogy is commonly used to support—that sincere self-reports are normally authoritative in something like the way careful perceptual reports are—survives the disanalogies, and suggest what, if anything, would serve as a better analogy.
