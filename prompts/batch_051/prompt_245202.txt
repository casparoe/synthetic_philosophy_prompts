You are an expert on inverse reinforcement learning, revealed-preference theory, and the philosophy of action. Please think slowly and carefully, and begin with a short brainstorming pass before you commit to an analysis.

I am considering building a personal AI assistant on the following principle: behavior is the only honest signal of what a person wants. During a one-year observation phase, the system passively records every choice I make — purchases, clicks, meals, how I spend my time, which requests I agree to, which messages I ignore. It never asks me what I want, and it deliberately excludes anything I say about my goals, on the grounds that self-report can be strategic. At the end of the year it locks in a single model of my true preferences inferred solely from that behavioral record, deletes the raw data, and begins acting on my behalf: scheduling my days, making purchases, allocating savings, and drafting routine communications, always choosing whatever the locked-in model says best serves my preferences. The model is never updated after lock-in, because the designers believe updating would let me game it once I know it is watching.

Analyze this as a mechanism, not as a matter of whether it is a good idea in the abstract. Start by distinguishing the different reasons behavior can diverge from preference. Then work through what the system would actually infer about me, how my behavior would change during the observation year once I understand the setup, what the system would do after lock-in, and where it would fail first. What second-order incentives does it create — for me, for advertisers and other third parties, and for the system's designers? Could the main failure modes be fixed without abandoning the core idea that behavior should outweigh self-report?
