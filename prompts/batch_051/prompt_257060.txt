I'm a philosophy PhD student working on animal ethics, and I keep noticing an asymmetry among the strict vegans and freegans in my reading group that I can't make come out consistent. Most of them think buying battery-cage eggs is wrong because it pays for the harm, but they'll happily eat eggs from a friend's rescued hens, since the hens aren't exploited and the eggs would just go to waste. Fine. But the same people will eat leftover turkey sandwiches from a catered lunch headed for the bin — the bird is already dead and their eating them didn't drive the demand — while they refuse roadkill venison, and they're unsure about taking a free leather coat from a curb pile. So the rough rule seems to be "animal products you didn't create demand for are okay, but only sometimes," and I can't tell whether the "sometimes" tracks a real principle or just disgust and vibes. Give me the strongest principle you can find that generates as many of these verdicts as possible, then test it against: (1) finishing a steak a friend ordered but couldn't eat, (2) eggs from rescued hens, (3) dumpster-dived deli meat, (4) a free leather couch left on the curb, (5) roadkill you watched get hit. If no single principle survives, say which of the original judgments should be given up and which stick, and why. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning — no preamble and no restating of the question.
