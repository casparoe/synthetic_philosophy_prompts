I'm a neurologist and I just got put on my hospital's research ethics board, so I've been trying to actually understand the equipoise principle instead of just nodding along. I went back and reread Freedman's 1987 piece "Equipoise and the Ethics of Clinical Research" in NEJM. I don't have it in front of me, but the part that keeps rattling around in my head is, roughly, that the ethically relevant question is not whether the individual investigator is genuinely uncertain about which arm is better, but whether there is genuine uncertainty in the expert medical community about the preferred treatment. He seems to want to say that an individual investigator's hunch or anecdotal impression shouldn't count as disturbing equipoise, because those are exactly the things the trial is meant to overcome. Or at least thats how I've been reading him, maybe I'm flattening it.

Our board has a messy track record, and I'm trying to figure out what principle we're actually committed to. A few past decisions, stripped of identifying details:

- We approved a multicenter randomized trial comparing two already-approved blood pressure drugs, even though the local PI told us she personally believed Drug A was better. There was no head-to-head data and the expert community was genuinely split.
- We rejected a trial comparing surgery to radiation for early prostate cancer when the proposer was basically the only one in the relevant specialty who still thought surgery was better, and recent registry data had pushed most experts toward radiation. We said there was no genuine community uncertainty.
- We approved, with conditions, a placebo-controlled trial of a new antidepressant for moderate depression. Some experts clearly thought placebo controls were no longer acceptable for that severity level, others thought they were still necessary; our conditions included rescue medication and tight monitoring.
- We rejected a trial comparing two dosing regimens for a serious infection where the investigators were honestly uncertain and the community was split, but the consent form read like the patient was just picking between two standard options and never mentioned randomization. We sent it back for a rewrite, but the underlying equipoise issue wasn't the problem there.

Now the new proposal. It's a randomized trial of a new seizure drug against an established first-line drug for focal epilepsy. The expert community is genuinely divided about whether the new drug is likely to be better, and this exact question is being actively debated at conferences. The PI says he is genuinely uncertain. But here's the wrinkle: he also has an unpublished open-label pilot study, small, n=30, that he describes as "strongly favoring" the new drug. He's told the board about it because he thinks it's material, but he isn't unblinding the trial. Half our members say the pilot doesn't matter for equipoise because the community still doesn't know about it and hasn't accepted it; the other half say the PI now has a private evidentiary basis that makes randomizing patients feel wrong.

If we want to be consistent with ourselves, what should we decide? Which past decision is the closest analog, and what rule would we be adopting under each option? If our precedents genuinely underdetermine this, say what further case would settle which rule we're actually committed to. And if I'm misusing Freedman's distinction or my framing of equipoise is off, tell me that plainly instead of building an answer on it.
