I am a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, and I have been asked to circulate a short position statement on multiple realizability ahead of a workshop. Before I send it out, I would like an audit of whether the commitments I list below actually hang together.

Here are the commitments, each of which I would endorse on reflection:

(1) Token physicalism. Every particular mental occurrence is a particular physical occurrence; I reject substance dualism.

(2) Functionalism about mental types. A mental state type such as being in pain or believing that the door is locked is individuated by its causal-functional profile—its typical causes, typical effects, and its relations to other mental states.

(3) Multiple realizability. One and the same mental state type can be realized in systems whose physical makeups are wildly different. A human brain, an octopus nervous system, and a suitably organized computing system can all literally be in the same mental state type.

(4) The force of multiple realizability. The multiple realizability of mental states is a decisive reason to reject type identity theory, understood as the claim that each mental state type is identical to some single type of physical state.

(5) Determinate cross-system sameness. There is a determinate fact of the matter about whether two very different systems are in the same mental state type. It is not merely a pragmatic or interest-relative matter of how we choose to carve up the mental.

(6) Mechanistic explanation. Good explanations in cognitive science are ultimately mechanistic: they explain a cognitive phenomenon by identifying the components, operations, and organization that produce it.

(7) Autonomy of the psychological level. We should not defer to neuroscience, or wait for it to mature, to tell us what beliefs, desires, and pains are.

What I want is an audit of this set rather than a survey of the literature. Please be specific about which commitments pull against one another and which merely appear to.

Please organize your answer into the following sections, in this order:

- "The stable core" (about one paragraph): which commitments cooperate and form the most defensible part of the position.
- "Genuine tensions, in order of severity" (about three or four paragraphs): for each tension, identify the commitments involved, explain exactly what pulls against what, and say how severe the tension is.
- "Costs of the fixes" (about two or three paragraphs): for each genuine tension, state the cheapest revision of the belief set and what is lost by making it.
- "An apparent tension that dissolves" (one or two paragraphs): identify at least one pair of commitments that look inconsistent but can in fact be held together, and explain why.
- "The single cleanest revision" (one paragraph): name the one change to the belief set that would remove the most tension at the lowest cost, and describe what the resulting position would look like.
