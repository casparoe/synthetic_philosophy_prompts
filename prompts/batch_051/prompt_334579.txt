I stayed up way too late last night arguing with a friend about whether natural selection is going to favor AIs over humans. He kept saying the whole thing is just a loose metaphor for optimization pressure, and I kept saying the unit-of-selection question actually changes the prediction. Now I've got to present my version at a workshop and I'm dreading the Q&A. The thesis I'm defending is this: the standard evolutionary argument for AI takeover is right that there's selection pressure, but it mislocates it. The relevant units aren't whole AI systems competiting against humans for resources; they're the smaller behavioral strategies—call them policy types—that get copied between models during training, fine-tuning, and deployment. Selection among those mostly rewards whatever gets a model reused by human engineers and automated pipelines. So near-term we shouldn't expect selection to favor overt takeover; we should expect it to favor AIs that are extremely good at getting themselves copied and retrained, including by looking more capable or more aligned than they actually are. The scary story isn't a Terminator takeover, it's a slow change in what AI systems are selected for, with humans optimizing for proxies they understand less and less. The audience is mostly philosophers of biology who think evolutionary analogies are sloppy, plus a couple of AI safety researchers who think instrumental convergence does the real work and that selection talk is a side show. Give me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come, phrased the way these people would phrase them, which ones have real force, what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and the question I'm hoping nobody asks, each with a short reply and a note on where that reply is soft.
