Robert Bolt's A Man for All Seasons, whether read as a play or watched in its film adaptation, turns on Thomas More's refusal to endorse Henry VIII's assumption of supremacy over the Church in England. Under pressure from Wolsey, Cromwell, his own family, and the King's representatives, More gives legal and prudential arguments, appeals to conscience, and sometimes retreats into deliberate silence rather than state his deepest grounds. Treat the work as an extended dramatization of the problem of religious reasons in public political justification. The relevant philosophical debate is, on one side, the Rawlsian view that citizens, and especially public officials who exercise coercive power, should support fundamental political arrangements with reasons shareable by people holding different comprehensive doctrines, and on the other, critics who argue that this demand excludes, burdens, or psychologically distorts religious citizens. In your reading, distinguish More's motivating reasons from the justifications he states or withholds; identify where he behaves as the public reason ideal would recommend and where he refuses to translate his convictions into shareable terms; consider whether his office as Lord Chancellor strengthens or alters any duty of public justification; and assess whether the play presents his silence as an honorable refusal of a demand that would require self-betrayal, or as a dangerous retreat from public accountability. Attend especially to moments where the drama resists a clean Rawlsian reading. Conclude by stating what the play shows about the costs of translating religious conviction into public reason that bare thought experiments do not.
