A friend and I went down a rabbit hole over the weekend trying to reconstruct Skolem's paradox from a seminar, and the more I write it out the more it looks like the argument proves to much. As best I can piece it together, it runs like this. (1) Standard first-order set theory, if it's consistent, has a countable model — that's the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem, since the language of set theory is countable. (2) In every model of set theory the usual theorems hold, so in particular 'there exists an uncountable set' is true in the model, because set theory proves the powerset of the natural numbers is uncountable. (3) But in that countable model, the whole universe of sets is countable from the outside: you can enumerate, one by one, every set the model says exists. (4) So the thing the model identifies as the powerset of the naturals is countable from outside but uncountable from inside the model. (5) Therefore 'uncountable' is not a real, absolute property of sets; it's relative to a model, and talk of 'the' powerset of the naturals doesn't pick out anything definite. (6) And if that's right, then the countable/uncountable distinction that real analysis and set theory rely on is not a fact about mathematical reality, and mathematics is basically a perspective-dependent game. My friend says the only exits are to give up on first-order logic for foundations, or to accept that math is model-relative all the way down. I can't accept the conclusion in (6), but I can't say exactly where the argument goes wrong, and every time I try I go in circles. Which step do you reject — or, if the argument is valid, which premise is the least costly to give up — and what does rejecting it cost? I'd most like to keep ordinary mathematics being about something objective; I'm much less attached to first-order logic being the right setting for foundations.
