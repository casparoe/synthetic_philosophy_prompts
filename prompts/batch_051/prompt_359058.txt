I have recently started playing the piano again after my father's death, and I keep returning to pieces I have always called sad: slow, minor, descending, quiet. The question of how a mere sequence of sounds can be sad has stopped being an idle one for me. A friend who teaches music theory has a swift answer. He says instrumental music is sad the way a sigh or a sob is sad. A falling chromatic line, a dragging tempo, a breathy tone, and a thinning texture are, in his words, the audible shape of human grief: posture, voice, and gesture translated into sound. That resemblance, he thinks, is the whole story. There is no feeling hidden in the work, and no need to posit an imaginary person inside the music who is doing the suffering; the sadness is a perceptual property of the sound contours themselves. I am half persuaded, and half worried the analogy is doing more work than he admits, especially for someone now living with exactly the grief the music is supposed to resemble.

Act as a philosopher of music who works on the expression debate. Begin by brainstorming for yourself: identify the strongest things the resemblance view explains and the cases it handles worst, then use that to organize your final answer. Then produce a rigorous audit of how far the "sad music resembles sad human behavior" analogy holds. I want, in this order: (1) a correspondence table showing what in the music maps onto what in a grieving person — voice, gesture, posture, movement, and anything else that seems to carry weight; (2) the places where the mapping breaks, separating the disanalogies that matter for his conclusion from the ones that are merely incidental — and in particular, explain why not every falling line or slow tempo strikes us as sad; (3) a catalogue of what the source domain quietly imports — for example, that perceiving a resemblance is sufficient for expression, that no mind needs to be imagined, that the listener's own felt emotion is irrelevant — with a verdict on whether each import is licensed; (4) a judgment on whether his conclusion survives the disanalogies — no imagined persona, no real feeling in the work, sadness as a perceivable contour property — and, if it does not fully survive, what better analogy or emendation would replace it. Be specific rather than slogan-level, and do not hand me a bibliography; I want the argument itself.
