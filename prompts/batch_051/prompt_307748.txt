I am a novelist, not a philosopher, and I am drafting a novel whose protagonist, a retired foreign correspondent, is trying to write her memoirs of the humanitarian-intervention era — Kosovo, Iraq, Libya — and keeps stalling on a pattern in the arguments she used to have with her colleagues. Whenever an intervention went badly, the corpses, the collapsed institutions, and the refugee columns were spoken of as things the intervening powers had done; the interveners owned them. But when a government chose to stay out while a regime killed its own people, the dead were spoken of as the dictator's doing, or as a tragedy, or as the way that region simply is, and almost never as something the bystander governments had any moral ownership over. She notices that her circle treats a death traceable to a decision to act as belonging to a different moral ledger from a death traceable to a decision to stand aside.

I would like the strongest principle that could justify treating deaths caused by an intervention as different in kind from deaths an intervention might have prevented, and an honest verdict on whether that principle survives the hardest case she can construct: a decision to intervene made on a sober ex ante judgment that it would very probably prevent a considerably larger massacre, which then fails and produces deaths of its own. Should the retrospective moral arithmetic treat that failure the same way it treats standing aside? If not, exactly what is doing the moral work? The novel's final act depends on whether she can answer this without special pleading.
