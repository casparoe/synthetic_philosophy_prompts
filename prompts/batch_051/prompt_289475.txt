I've been trying to pin down what my departmental colleague Marta, a political theorist who works on populism, actually means when she says something is 'the will of the people', and I'd like a prediction about a case she's about to give a verdict on. Her track record, as best I can reconstruct it: she said a 58–42 referendum in favour of a new land tax was the people's will and the government should just get on with it. She said a newly elected government's central manifesto pledge on school funding was the people's will even though only 44 per cent said they supported the specific bill just before it was enacted. She said a minister extending police surveillance through secondary legislation, never mentioned in any manifesto and never put to a vote, was not the people's will, especially with 70 per cent opposed. And after a referendum on a high-speed rail line failed 61–39, she said the transport minister couldn't press on anyway by claiming the result had missed the public's 'considered' will.

The new case: a referendum has passed 57–43 to remove a regional minority language from use in local courts and schools, and the government wants to know whether implementing that is giving effect to the will of the people. I can see at least two rules that fit her record. On a straight authorisation view, referendums and salient manifesto commitments just are the people's will, so the language referendum would count. On a more constitutional view, authorised outcomes count only if they also clear basic rights-based limits, and this one pretty plainly doesn't. I can also see a crude majoritarian reading, but her second case seems to kill that.

Give me a committed read, not a menu of options: what would Marta likely say, which rule is she actually running (or is there a third I've missed), what does each live rule predict about the language case, and if her record genuinely underdetermines it, what further case would settle it?
