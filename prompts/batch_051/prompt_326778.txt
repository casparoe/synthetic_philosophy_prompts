I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether petitionary prayer is coherent on classical theism, and we keep hitting the same wall. If God is omniscient, He already knows exactly what you'll ask and what He'll do about it; if He's immutable, His will can't be moved by anything you say. So asking God for something looks either pointless, or like it's secretly doing something else—self-therapy, acceptance, praise—while dressed up as requesting. My friend thinks it's straightforwardly pointless; I suspect that's too quick, but I can't quite say why.

Here's how I'd like to approach it. Take the standard criteria for a petitionary prayer: (1) it's addressed to a personal God in the second person; (2) it asks for some particular outcome, not just praise or general devotion; (3) it's made on the assumption that the outcome isn't guaranteed, so that things might go differently absent the request; (4) the person intends the prayer to to influence whether God brings the outcome about. Maybe you'll also want a fifth criterion about what attitude the person has to have toward "being answered"—something about really expecting or hoping for a response.

Now apply that to a case I half-invented to stress-test the criteria. Imagine a community that affirms full divine immutability and omniscience, and explicitly denies that prayer gives God new information or moves His will. But they also hold that God's eternal decree has a conditional structure: He has willed from eternity to grant certain goods only if asked, so the prayer is one of the antecedents baked into the plan—like a key that turns a lock God already installed. They pray for specifics: a sick friend, a harvest, a job. They insist their asking changes nothing in God, while also insisting that without the asking the good would not come, because the decree says so. One of their teachers describes praying as taking hold of what God's will has already attached to your asking, rather than changing that will. So: is this petitionary prayer, or is it something else—submission, therapy, or a ritualized way of coming to accept outcomes—dressed up as requesting? Go through the criteria one by one, flag where they underdetermine the answer, and then commit: does the case qualify? If you think the criteria themselves are the problem—for example, that they smuggle in "changing God's mind" or "giving God new information"—say which criterion you'd drop or revise and what the replacement does. I'd rather you take a definite position and defend it than lay out all the sides.
