I keep running into a conflict in how I think about integrity. I'm inclined to accept a picture where integrity is a matter of one's higher-order commitments reliably outranking first-order temptations; on this picture incorruptibility is the limit case where the temptation doesn't even get a grip -- the bribe has no motivational purchase. That fits the ideal of the judge or clerk who simply cannot be suborned. But I also can't shake the intuition that someone who is badly tempted by a bribe and refuses anyway is more admirable, and in some sense *more* incorruptible, precisely because they had something to be corrupted by. If incorruptibility is just the absence of a corrupting desire, then a drug that made money motivationally inert would turn the sleaziest politician into a paradigm of incorruptibility, and that seems obviously wrong. But if incorruptibility requires actually feeling the pull and winning, then the ideal depends on its possessor being corruptible, which feels incoherent. I don't want a winner declared. I want to know what would have to give in each direction: what it costs to debunk the intuition, what it costs to amend or abandon the theory, and whether there's a reading on which both survive -- maybe a standing capacity that leaves occurent temptation intact, or a distinction between being incorruptible and merely not coveting. And is it always a virtue? Someone could be unbribable because they're indifferent to everyone's welfare, not just to money; I'd like to know whether that's still incorruptibility or a different, bleaker defect.
