I have been unable to settle whether a resemblance between a moral puzzle and the theory of ordered magnitudes is deep or merely formal. Consider the dust-speck problem: a choice between one person's severe, prolonged torture and an astronomically large number of people each suffering a momentary dust speck in the eye. Aggregative consequentialism with ordinary real-valued harms seems committed to saying that for large enough N the speck harms outweigh the torture, yet many people find that conclusion unacceptable. One way to block the conclusion is to treat harms as lexically ordered, so that no finite number of dust specks, however large, reaches the badness of a single torture. Structurally that looks like the move from Archimedean to non-Archimedean ordered structures: in an Archimedean order, every positive element can be repeated finitely many times to exceed any fixed positive element, whereas a non-Archimedean order contains positive elements so small that no finite number of their copies attains a given positive finite quantity. Is the parallel genuine — is the anti-aggregation intuition simply the claim that the harm order is non-Archimedean — or does it confuse the ordering of harms with the assignment of magnitudes to those harms? State the exact mapping, say what would have to be true on both sides for results to transfer from the mathematics to the ethics, and name one prediction the parallel generates that looks false.
