I have been trying to steelman the institutional theory of art — the line of thinking associated with Arthur Danto's notion of the artworld and with George Dickie's later, more explicit definitions — and I want to stress-test it against a case it was not originally built for. As I understand it, the theory's central move is to shift attention away from the perceptible properties of an object and toward a social practice: a thing is a work of art, roughly, when a body of people who together constitute the artworld confers on it the status of candidate for appreciation. The paradigm case is Duchamp's readymades, where a urinal becomes art not because of how it looks but because of an institutional act. The standard objection is that this makes the theory circular and overpermissive: the artworld has to be picked out in terms of art, and the theory seems unable to distinguish genuine conferral from any sufficiently confident institution labeling whatever it likes as art.

The development I want to bring to bear is generative image systems. Suppose someone writes a prompt, a diffusion model produces an image, and that image is subsequently exhibited and wins a juried prize — images of this kind are already moving through exactly these institutional channels. In the theory's own terms, the artworld has now conferred status on the image. Yet behind the output there is no single agent who composed the picture in the ordinary sense: the prompter supplied a high-level description, while the model resolved the compositional and stylistic details, presumably without intentions at all. The status of candidate for appreciation now attaches to a thing whose making is distributed between a human specification and a trained network, with the network contributing much of what would traditionally have carried the artistry.

My question is whether the institutional argument survives this development. Does it go through unchanged, because it never required authorship or intention and only ever needed actual conferral by the artworld? Or does the AI case expose a hidden premise — perhaps the artifact condition, perhaps an assumption that somewhere upstream there is an intending maker — that Duchamp never forced the theory to state? If the argument needs repair, what is the minimal repair, and does the repaired version still handle readymades, or does it collapse into the vacuous claim that art is whatever the right people decide to call art? I am interested in where the argument stands after the change, not in whether AI images are good or bad. Reply as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
