Next month we're hosting a small workshop and I got drafted to give the opening ten-minute talk. The room will be split down the middle: some people basically accept the astronomical-stakes reasoning and think the only serious question is how to steer it, and another group thinks the whole thing is a Pascal's mugging with extra steps. I'd like the talk to do one specific job: take the simulation argument seriously and ask whether it should dampen that far-future fanaticism — and I want you to lean toward a real answer, not a both-sides shrug. My hunch is that if simulation-style reasoning is right it doesn't just lower our credence in a long human future; it changes what the far future even means for us, since the action might belong to the simulating civilization rather than our own descendents. But I keep getting tied in knots about whether that actually reduces the expected-value stakes or just relocates them.

I need you to write the speech, acting as a philosopher who knows both the simulation argument and the decision-theoretic fanaticism debate. The idea has to do real structural work: the speech should show which claims about the far future are licensed or undermined once you condition on us probably living in a simulation, and how that changes what a longtermist should actually do. Keep it around 900–1200 words. No citations or namedropping — this is a talk, not a reading list. And make it land for both halves of the room; I don't want the committed longtermists to feel ambushed and I don't want the skeptics to feel like they won by default. Think carefully before you draft, maybe outline the argumentative beats first.
