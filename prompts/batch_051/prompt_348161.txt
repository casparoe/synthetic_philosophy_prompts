I'm a graduate student TAing an upper-level undergraduate moral philosophy course, and next week I have to run a single eighty-minute discussion section on hypocrisy and the standing to blame. The professor's brief is that by the end students should be able to hold two apparently conflicting thoughts together: that a hypocrite's criticism can be true or well-grounded even when the critic herself violates the norm she is invoking, and that the hypocrisy can nevertheless do something to the normative standing of the criticism. Don't explain this material to me; I need a teaching plan, not the explanation itself.

The topic has become personal for me: I spent most of a weekend arguing with my brother about our father, who smoked heavily for thirty years and now lectures my brother about vaping. My brother's line was that Dad has no right to say any of it, and I kept wanting to say that the criticism could still be correct even though something about Dad's making it felt off. I couldn't articulate the structure. I am considering building the session around that case, but I worry it is too easy for students to treat it as a mere question of health facts rather than as a question of blame, so I am also considering either an academic-integrity panel member who cheated as a student and now judges another student for cheating, or a manager who padded her own expenses and now reprimands a subordinate for a smaller false claim. Tell me which example should carry the session, what it has to have, and what it must avoid in order to keep truth and standing from collapsing into each other.

The plan should specify: the idea that must come first and what it is doing; the order of the subsequent moves and what each depends on; the misconceptions students will predictably bring or form — I already expect "the hypocrisy shows the criticism is false" and its mirror "tu quoque is a fallacy, so the hypocrisy is completely irrelevant" — and exactly at which point in the sequence each should be headed off; what I should openly refuse to cover at this level; and one quick end-of-session check that would tell me whether they have the distinction rather than merely the phrase "standing to blame." Keep the plan to at most 800 words, and begin with a one-sentence statement of the session's overall arc.
