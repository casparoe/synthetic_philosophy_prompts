Question: Benacerraf's multiple reductions argument is often taken to show that questions like whether 2 is an element of 4 have no determinate answer. Explain the argument, and critically discuss whether it supports structuralism.

Student's answer: Benacerraf's point is that the natural numbers can be identified with sets in lots of different ways, and arithmetic itself gives us no reason to prefer one. On von Neumann's version, 0 is ∅, 1 is {∅}, and each number is the set of all smaller numbers, so 4 = {0,1,2,3}, which makes 2 an element of 4. On Zermelo's version each number is the singleton of its predecessor, so 4 = {{{{∅}}}} and 2 = {{∅}}, so 2 is not an element of 4. Since the two reductions are isomorphic, any proof carried out in one can be mirrored in the other, so they must agree on every meaningful mathematical statement. That means whether 2 is an element of 4 is not a meaningful mathematical question at all; it is just an artefact of a particular coding. Benacerraf's conclusion is that numbers are not objects in their own right, but positions in any structure satisfying the Peano axioms. Structuralism then says the only facts about the number 2 are those that hold of the second position in every such structure: being even, being prime, being the successor of 1, and so on. Membership questions like whether 2 belongs to 4 are category mistakes, rather like asking whether the number 2 is blue.
