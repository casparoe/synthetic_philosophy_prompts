I work as product counsel at a midsized consumer software company, and I keep watching the same kind of decision get made without any shared framework. A team ships a flow in which a user must check a box or tap "I agree" before using a feature: a new arbitration clause, a changed privacy policy, a waiver in a fitness app, the terms for a paid subscription. Design treats the checkbox as a conversion step, product treats it as a formality, and legal treats it as contract formation. I think the honest answer is that these flows sit on a spectrum between a signature on a negotiated contract and a decorative click, and that the underlying question is philosophical: when does an adhesive clickwrap amount to agreement at all, rather than an imitation of agreement that users and courts end up treating as the real thing? For my own purposes I keep two failures distinct. In one, no agreement is formed, because the user never reasonably manifested assent to being bound by anything. In the other, an agreement is formed in some thin sense, but a particular term should not be treated as binding, because it falls outside what a reasonable user would expect or because the interface made meaningful assent to that term impossible. The line between those failures is one of the things I need the procedure to be honest about rather than blur.

Please turn this into a reusable procedure. It should be runnable in about twenty minutes by a non-lawyer on a product or compliance team, several times per sprint; I, as product counsel, will make the final call on anything the procedure flags, but I want the routine decisions made consistently without me. The stakes are that this governs whether arbitration clauses and liability limits are enforceable, whether privacy and data consents survive scrutiny, and whether our flows are genuinely honest rather than merely well-disclosure-optimized. The procedure should give the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with "do not treat this as agreement" or "flag for counsel," and where a question is one of values rather than fact and must be handed to a person. Keep the two failures above distinct, and do not collapse everything into "make the disclosure more prominent." Then run the procedure on three flows: a mobile game that asks users to accept an arbitration clause mid-tutorial, with the terms visible only behind a scroll box and the "I agree" box pre-ticked; a two-hour furniture delivery booking whose terms contain nothing surprising but are shown as a dense wall of text on a phone above a required "I have read and understood" checkbox; and a free newsletter signup whose only term, disclosed in one plain sentence beside the button, is that the publisher may sell the subscriber's email address. At least one of these should produce an answer the team will not like; say which and why you stand by it. I want a committed procedure, not a menu of possible frameworks: make the decisions, identify which steps you are treating as settled and which as judgment calls, and give your reasons. Close by saying what the procedure cannot do, because at some point the residual disagreement is genuinely about what agreement is.
