I am a PhD student in philosophy, and I sit as the graduate representative on my university's Risk Communication Review Panel, the body that decides whether the university issues campus-wide health advisories. The chair has asked me to draft the memo for the following case.

A single preliminary screen of tap water in one of our older research buildings has detected a chlorinated solvent at a concentration just above the university's action level for short-term exposure. The same screen three months ago detected nothing; the pipes were flushed after that test; and two independent contract laboratories are running confirmation tests that will not come back for ten days. The panel must recommend whether the university should immediately advise pregnant staff and students not to drink the water in that building, or wait for the confirmation results.

The panel is split. One group holds a strict evidentialist position, in the spirit of W. K. Clifford: a university is under the same duty as an individual never to communicate or act on a claim that outruns the available evidence, because acting on unconfirmed findings—even out of caution—teaches everyone that assertions about risk need not wait for evidence and spreads a scare whose harms are real even if the test is wrong. The other group holds a permissive, pragmatic position, in the spirit of William James: where the decision is forced on us by the arrival of a concerning result, momentous for the people who would be exposed, and a live option that we cannot defer, we are entitled to let the practical stakes decide; here the cost of ten days of unnecessary caution looks small compared with the cost of ten days of avoidable exposure if the result is real.

Please draft a short policy memo for the panel. It should state a clear recommendation—issue the advisory now, withhold it, or adopt some intermediate measure—give the reasoning in terms the panel can actually apply, and treat the strongest consideration on the losing side honestly. Include criteria we can reuse when a single preliminary result suggests a serious but unconfirmed risk. Keep it self-contained rather than a literature review, and do not cite sources. Finally, say how confident you are in the recommendation, and single out the point in your reasoning you are least sure of.
