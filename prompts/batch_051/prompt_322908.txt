I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and my beat runs through economics, philosophy, and public policy. I have been asked to lead a ninety-minute workshop at my publication's annual training week for early-career science and policy reporters, and I would like a plan for the session rather than the explanation itself. The reporters are sharp but not mathematically trained, and a payoff table will initially read to them as a finance lecture. The announced topic is how to cover policy arguments made under ignorance: cases in which the experts say they cannot responsibly assign probabilities. My narrower goal is to stop the reporters from treating all such arguments as interchangeable, because three decision rules get run together in coverage: maximin (choose the option whose worst possible outcome is best), minimax regret (choose the option that minimizes the largest gap between the outcome achieved and the outcome that would have been best in hindsight), and expected utility (choose the option with the best probability-weighted result, available only once probabilities can be assigned). The deeper point I want to land is that the familiar disagreement between Rawls and Harsanyi over the veil of ignorance is not a fight between fairness and selfishness; it is a fight about what impartiality commits a chooser to — Rawls-style reasoning treating the choice as a species of worst-case thinking, Harsanyi-style reasoning treating the chooser as equally likely to be anyone affected and then maximizing expected utility. Please give me the teaching sequence: the order in which the components must be introduced and why each depends on the one before it, so that the differences among the rules begin to seem forced rather than terminological; the single concrete case I should carry through the whole session and what it must have and lack; the misconceptions the reporters will bring or form and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off (I already expect four: uncertainty is just risk with worse data, maximin is formalized pessimism, minimax regret is about how the decision maker will feel afterward, and the veil of ignorance is a claim about what actual people would choose); the check for understanding that would show they can use the distinction rather than merely define it; and what I should openly decline to cover at this level and say so.
