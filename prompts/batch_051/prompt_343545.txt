I've been reading the rationalist blogs and adjacent forums for years, and I keep noticing that every time someone argues against tranquilism — the view that the only thing that matters in a life is the absence of craving and suffering, so positive experiences like pleasure, love, or achievement have no intrinsic value — they reach for the same straw-mannish cases where the happy person is actually full of unnoticed desire or anxiety. I want you to build me a thought experiment that cant be dismissed that way.

Spec: two ordinary, non-science-fictional lives that are equal in every respect except positive content. Both have the same amount of craving, fear, disappointment, and background unease, and the positive content in the richer life does not reduce any of that. It genuinely is not relief, not distraction, not the quieting of an itch. In the richer life the person falls in love, does meaningful work, laughs with friends, and enjoys all of it; in the other life none of that happens. Crucially, the positive content has to be causally clean: it removes no pre-existing craving or suffering, and it leaves no new cravings in its wake.

Then do the payoff. Tell me what tranquilism says about the two lives, what a garden-variety hedonist says about them, which single feature of the scenario is doing the work to force the split, how a smart tranquilist could respond without just biting the bullet, and where they would be most tempted to attack the setup. Finish by saying what would happen to the case if you removed the feature that does the work.

Dont make it about meditation or renunciation, and please write the answer in British English.
