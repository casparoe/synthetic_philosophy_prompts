Two of my coworkers went back and forth at a department mixer last week, and I'd like you to adjudicate the argument. Sam is a molecular biologist, and Dana is a philosopher of science who studies how biologists talk about their objects. The argument was over whether it is literally true that DNA is a code, or whether that is a metaphor that has gotten out of hand. I paraphrased the back-and-forth as faithfully as I can.

(1) Sam said that DNA is a code in a perfectly literal sense. The genetic code is a mapping from the sixty-four nucleotide triplets to the twenty standard amino acids, plus stop signals; that mapping is fixed and reliably executed by the translation machinery — the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, transfer RNA, and the ribosome; and it could in principle have been different, which is exactly what makes it a code rather than a physical necessity. Calling a codon a unit of code, she said, is no more metaphorical than calling a dot-dash pattern a unit of Morse code.

(2) Dana replied that Morse code is a code only because someone designed it and people use it to communicate, and neither of those things is true of DNA. Words like "transcription," "translation," "message," and "reading," he said, are borrowed from intentional agents, and the cell does not read or interpret anything in that sense. What the cell does is carry out a causal sequence; code-talk smuggles in a sender and a receiver that biology has not found.

(3) Sam answered that Dana was helping himself to a needlessly mentalistic definition of "code." A code, she said, can be defined purely structurally: two finite sets of elements and a mapping between them, where the correspondence is arbitrary but stable and one domain reliably determines the other. Under that definition, DNA literally instantiates a code, whatever its origin; the burden is on Dana to say why the intentional extras belong in the definition rather than in the metaphors people layer on top of it.

(4) Dana said that even the structural sense overstates the case. For one thing, the codon assignments may not be as arbitrary as the frozen-accident picture implies; some origins-of-life work suggests stereochemical affinities between codons or anticodons and their amino acids. For another, a DNA sequence does not determine a single protein the way a Morse string determines a fixed message, because alternative splicing, RNA editing, and gene regulation all help determine what, if anything, is made. If the product depends on cellular context and machinery as much as on sequence, he said, then "DNA encodes the protein" is too strong; sequence is one necessary resource among several, not a set of instructions.

(5) Sam replied that context-dependence does not disqualify coding; it just makes the code context-sensitive. A word can contribute different meanings in different sentences, and no one concludes that words are not part of a language. Nor do chemical biases in the symbol table make a code not a code; they would only mean the arrangement was not unconstrained. She said Dana's argument works only against a strong blueprint view — the genome alone specifying the organism — and she was defending the minimal claim that DNA instantiates a code.

(6) Dana came back that the minimal claim, taken on its own, is so thin it would make a thermometer's mercury column a code for the temperature, because any stable mapping would count. The reason biologists keep saying "code," he said, is precisely to borrow the thick informational sense, which makes the genome sound like a set of instructions; that is what drives research and funding toward sequence annotation and away from developmental context. You cannot, he said, keep the rhetorical benefits of the thick sense while paying for only the thin sense.

That was the last move before someone changed the subject. I couldn't tell who was right about what, and I'd like a point-by-point adjudication. Please go through the six moves in order. For each move, rate it on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating in a sentence or two. Then say what, if anything, both of them are missing or conflating, and give an overall verdict about which specific claims end up justified. Aim for around 800 words. In your response, please write in continuous prose, as in an email or a letter, and avoid headings, bullet points, numbered lists, bold text, and tables; refer to the moves as "Sam's opening move" or "Dana's second move" rather than using a list format.
