I have been rereading the passage in Smith where he says that I divide myself, as it were, into two persons — the examiner and judge on one side, and the person whose conduct is examined and judged on the other — and that the first is the supposed impartial spectator, while the second is the agent. I find myself unable to pin down exactly what kind of self-division he is describing. On one reading, this is just a vivid metaphor for a psychological process: I take up a perspective on my own conduct, and that perspective has a distinctive emotional signature. On another reading, Smith is making a stronger claim about moral identity or about the structure of conscience, as if there really are two centers of judgment within a single self, one of which is in some sense not fully me because it is the perspective of a spectator. I want help understanding what the passage actually commits him to.

Here is the passage, from Part III, Chapter 1 of The Theory of Moral Sentiments:

When I endeavour to examine my own conduct, when I endeavour to pass sentence upon it, and either to approve or condemn it, it is evident that, in all such cases, I divide myself, as it were, into two persons; and that I, the examiner and judge, represent a different character from that other I, the person whose conduct is examined into and judged of. The first is the spectator, whose sentiments with regard to my own conduct I endeavour to enter into, by placing myself in his situation, and by considering how it would appear to me, when seen from that particular point of view. The second is the agent, the person whom I properly call myself, and of whose conduct, under the character of a spectator, I was endeavouring to form some opinion. The first is the judge; the second the person judged of. But that the judge should, in every respect, be the same with the person judged of, is as impossible as that the cause should, in every respect, be the same with the effect.

I have three connected questions. First, is Smith here offering a psychological account of how self-assessment works, or a metaphysical account of what the self is, or both? I want to know whether the phrase divide myself, as it were, into two persons is meant to soften the claim, and whether as it were signals that he is speaking figuratively throughout. Second, what does the final sentence do for the argument? It seems to say that the judging self cannot be identical in every respect with the judged self, but I cannot tell whether Smith means identity over time, identity of perspective, identity of character, or something else. Third, does the impartial spectator, on this account, have to be understood as a real perspective — one that I can genuinely occupy — or is it enough that I imagine how a spectator would judge me, even if no such spectator exists? I want a close reading of the passage that answers those questions, but I also want you to flag where the passage underdetermines an answer and where later parts of The Theory of Moral Sentiments would need to be brought in to settle the question.
