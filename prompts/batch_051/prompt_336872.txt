I've been trying to work out why I find epistemicism about vagueness both unavoidable and repellent, and Peirce keeps coming up. In his 1905 Monist article Issues of Pragmaticism, Peirce suggested roughly this: a proposition is vague when there are possible states of things such that it is intrinsically uncertain whether the speaker would have meant the proposition to exclude or to permit those states. What I like about this is that it makes vagueness a property of the proposition's content, not merely our ignorance about some hidden fact.

I tried to turn this into a short argument against epistemicism, and I want to know whether it goes through. Let B be the sentence 'The man with 5,000 hairs on his head is bald,' evaluated for a borderline case.

1. If Peirce's criterion is satisfied by B, then there are possible states of things — for instance, the man's being exactly borderline as he is, with our concept of baldness exactly as it is — concerning which it is intrinsically uncertain whether the speaker would have counted the state as excluded or allowed by B.
2. If epistemicism is true, then B has a sharp, unknowable cutoff somewhere along the sequence from 0 to 50,000 hairs, so for every possible state there is a determinate fact about whether B excludes or allows it.
3. For any determinate fact about whether B excludes or allows a state, the uncertainty about that exclusion or allowance is not intrinsic to B; it is just uncertainty about an unknowable fact.
4. Therefore, if epistemicism is true, the uncertainty in Peirce's criterion is not intrinsic uncertainty.
5. So epistemicism is incompatible with B's being vague in Peirce's sense.
6. But B is vague in Peirce's sense, since the criterion is satisfied.
7. Therefore epistemicism is false.

I'm suspicious of step 3, but I can't quite articulate the objection. On one reading, 'intrinsic uncertainty' is uncertainty that cannot be resolved by any amount of empirical information, and an unknowable cutoff would indeed make the uncertainty unresolvable. On another reading, it is uncertainty that arises from the content of the proposition rather than from our epistemic position, and a determinate-but-unknowable fact could still make the uncertainty external rather than intrinsic. Does the argument equivocate here? Or is there a weaker reading of epistemicism on which the argument goes through? Please go step by step and say which premise is false or which inference fails, where exactly the failure lies, and what the strongest epistemicist reply to Peirce should be.
