I've been reading Berkeley's economic writings, where he argues roughly that gold and silver are just counters or tickets, and that a nation's real wealth is its people's industry and credit, so paper money is as good as metal if people will take it in payment. That got me to a view I've been calling the "rolling IOU" theory of money. Here it is.

The central claim is that a unit of money is not, at bottom, a store of value or a medium of exchange. It is a blank IOU issued by the community to itself. If I hold twenty dollars, nobody in particular owes me twenty dollars. What I hold is a floating claim on the community's future product, and what that claim can actually be settled for is renegotiated continuously by prices, wages, taxes, and inflation. So inflation isn't a breakdown in the value of money; it is the community adjusting the terms of all those IOUs at once, instead of defaulting on them one by one. Devaluation is the same move at the level of exchange rates, and even some sovereign default is just a more honest version of it.

I think this also explains why a gold standard feels more honest but really just fixes the terms of the IOUs in a way that benefits whoever already holds them, and why people under a gold standard still manage to renegotiate through deflation and unemployment rather than through the price level.

I'm aware this sounds like it could be either just restating chartalism or confusing a metaphor with a mechanism. So my question is: give me the three strongest objections to this way of looking at money, and tell me whether any of them is fatal, or whether they just show it's an unusual redescription of standard stuff rather than a real theory. Also, please write the answer in American English.
