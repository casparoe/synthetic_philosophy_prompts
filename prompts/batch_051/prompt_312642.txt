In mechanistic interpretability there's a recurring problem—anomaly detection pushes you straight into it—of deciding whether a model is using the same mechanism on two inputs or in two training runs. The usual move is to patch or swap activations from a normal example into a weird one and check wether the usual circuit lights up and drives the answer, or whether some other path happens to produce the same output. In comparative biology the standard way to ask whether two traits are the same is homology versus analogy: a bat wing and a bird wing are analogous (same function, no shared ancestral structure), while a bat wing and a whale flipper are homologous (same structure by descent, different function). I keep feeling the interpretability question—same circuit, or a different circuit that found the same function?—just is the homology/analogy distinction, with shared training history playing the role of shared ancestry. But maybe it breaks because network components can be relearned from scratch with no common ancestor, and because structure and function are nowhere near as cleanly separable in a trained network as they are in a limb.
