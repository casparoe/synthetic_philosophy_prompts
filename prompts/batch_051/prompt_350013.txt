Right, I'm putting together a handout on the old debate about whether artefacts have politics, and I want a thought experiment that isn't just the usual cases with the serial numbers filed off — the parkway bridges built too low for buses, the mechanical tomato harvester that helped shift farming towards big growers, that sort of thing. You can lean on those examples as contrasts, but the case itself has to be new. The point of the handout is to test a specific split. Some people say the politics can be built into the artefact itself, so that the object shifts power even when nobody intends it and the law stays neutral. Others say the politics is never in the thing; it's all in how people take the thing up, the institutions around it, who owns it, who sets the rules. I want a case that comes out in the strong direction, but through a route where the use-contextualist would normally feel safest.

So here's the spec.

1. Ordinary present-day artefact. No sci-fi, no speculative tech, and avoid recommendation systems, scoring algorithms and similar, since they let both sides fall back on examples they've already rehearsed.
2. It has to be political in the strong sense: the design of the thing itself systematically advantages one group and disadvantages another, and it would keep doing so if every user was well-meaning and the surrounding rules were neutral.
3. But none of the easy mechanisms: not expense, not legal compulsion, not a designer who openly meant to exclude, and no obvious physical exclusion like a too-low bridge or a hostile bench. The effect should come from some structural mismatch between the artefact and a taken-for-granted fact about ordinary bodies, routines, or whose needs count as the default when the thing gets specced.
4. The skew should be robust. Drop the same artefact into another city, another firm, a couple of decades later, and the skew should show up again unless somebody actively counteracts it.

Now do the verification, because the whole point is that the case can't just stipulate it's verdict.

Say exactly what the artefact is and which group it quietly advantages, and state, in the strong view's own terms, why the artefact itself qualifies. Then trace the causal chain from one specific design feature to the unequal outcome. Then put on the use-contextualist's hat and give the best re-description of the case as 'just how people happen to use it,' showing the exact spot where that re-description stretches or gives out. Identify the single design feature doing the real work, and say what you get if you swap only that feature out: does the case turn back into a neutral tool, or into one of the stock examples? Finish with the strongest remaining objection the contextualist could make, and the reply that keeps the strong reading standing.
