Friend of mine is doing a psych masters and we spent most of an evening arguing about whether the DSM is discovering disorders or partly creating them. His line was that psychiatric classifications are different from botanical ones because classifying a person changes the person: call someone 'depressed' and she starts narrating her lows in the language of the label, clinicians start hearing her differently, people form identities around the category, and down the line the category itself gets revised partly because people changed in response to it. I kept pushing back that every science redraws its categories, and nobody thinks the redrawing alters the things themselves. When botanists split a fern species the ferns don't reorganise; when astronomers reclassify a body as a dwarf planet they don't worry about the object's feelings or future behavior. Yet when the DSM folds one diagnosis into another, everyone treats the reclassification as almost an intervention—we worry it will reshape the people it names, not just our map of them.

So we'd argued ourselves into this asymmetry: in natural science we treat a taxonomy as a passive description of a target that doesn't care, while in psychiatry we treat the taxonomy as part of a feedback loop between the label and the labeled. Is there anything that actually justifies that asymmetry? The obvious candidate is that people are self-interpreting and rocks and ferns aren't. But then what do you do with human classifications that hardly seem to loop—blood type, say, or being left-handed—or with borderline cases where a nonhuman classification loops a little, like a shelter dog labeled 'aggressive' starting to act the part? I want to know whether the asymmetry is principled or just a comforting piece of folk philosophy. And please pretty explicitly separate what is actually settled from what people who study this genuinely dispute, rather than presenting a contested position as the consensus.
