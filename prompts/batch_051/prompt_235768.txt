Here's the sort of thing I keep chewing over. The standard blue bus problem says you shouldn't find a bus company liable merely because it runs 80% of the buses on a given route; naked statistical evidence, everyone agrees, feels different from an eyewitness who says, 'I saw the blue bus.' The usual explanation is that purely statistical evidence fails to individuate—it applies just as much to every member of the reference class. Fine. But now suppose the 'witness' is an automated number-plate recognition camera trained on millions of examples, and it outputs '86% probability this was the bus that hit the cyclist.' Is that individualised evidence, or is it just the blue bus problem with a neural net wrapped round it? The number is still computed from base rates in the training data, chosen priors, and correaltions, not from a direct encounter with the event. But the system did look at this particular plate. So my question: does the old argument against naked statistical evidence survive the arrival of algorithmic classifiers, or does it need a new line? I want you to say precisely what 'individualised' was doing in the original argument, and then say whether the classifier's output clears that bar—and if it doesn't, what the real remaining distinction is.
