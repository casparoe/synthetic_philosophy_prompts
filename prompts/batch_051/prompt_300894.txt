Build me a thought experiment for a development-econ seminar: two neighboring countries that split from the same population a couple generations back, so culture is basically identical, arranged so that an institutionalist, a geography-first person, and a culture-first person give three different predictions about the gap fifty years later. Give the countries names. The institutionalist has country A pulling clearly ahead because A got stuck with better courts, cleaner property rights, and a ruler who can't expropriate; the geography-first person has country B pulling clearly ahead because B got the good river, the deepwater port, and the malaria-free highland; and the culture-first person says the gap stays small because the two share the same norms and the differences in institutions and geography are second-order. The countries have to feel like places you could read about in an area-studies journal, not a toy model. The culture in both has to be specified as concrete practices — inheritance rules, trust networks, labor norms — rather than 'values' or 'work ethic,' and you can't let A's better institutions be an obvious product of A's geography, or make B's geographic advantage something A could just build its way out of. The point of the exercise is to see whether the three causal stories can be pulled apart in a single realistic case, so it counts as cheating if the scenario stipulates the rankings instead of letting each theory's own mechanisms generate them. Walk through the mechanism each theory uses, show why each gives its verdict, then say which single feature of the setup is doing the real work of separating the three predictions and what happens if you remove it. End with a defender of whichever theory comes off worst attacking the case, and your call on whether the attack kills the case or just changes its lesson.
