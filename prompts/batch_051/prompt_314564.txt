A friend and I recently had a long argument about whether our county should adopt a risk-scoring tool to decide who gets offered pretrial diversion instead of prosecution. My friend's position was that when people object that such tools are biased, they are really just demanding more careful engineering: he insisted that a single score can be made fair in every important sense at once — equally accurate across racial groups, equal false positive and false negative rates across groups, and well calibrated so that a given score means the same thing regardless of group membership. I remembered that algorithmic fairness includes impossibility results suggesting one cannot in general satisfy all these criteria simultaneously unless special conditions hold, such as equal base rates or a near-perfect predictor. But when it came to explaining what follows from those results, I floundered. What are the most important implications of these impossibility results for a public agency deciding whether and how to use such a tool? In particular, should the results make us conclude that algorithmic fairness is hopeless, or is the correct conclusion narrower — for example, that the choice among partially conflicting fairness criteria is an inescapably normative, political decision that no optimization can settle, and that the right comparison is between the tool and the actually available alternative rather than between the tool and an unreachable ideal? I want the implications reasoned through, not a literature survey.
