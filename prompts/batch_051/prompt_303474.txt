Act as a philosophical theologian who can move comfortably between evolutionary biology and theodicy, and start by steelmanning each side before you try to mediate.

I've been stuck between two things and I want a negotiated settlement, not a winner. The first is an argument I find increasingly hard to resist. Death, predation, parasitism, disease, and slow starvation don't enter the picture after humans do; they are baked into the fossil record for hundreds of millions of years, and pain and fear look like selected adaptations, not like a later corruption of an originally peaceful creation. So the usual story that suffering only enters creation as a consequence of human sin can't carry the whole weight. Any honest account has to say either that God's good creation natively includes creatures eating each other alive, or that something went badly wrong before we arrived, and the second option starts to look like special pleading once you see how much of the biological world is built on harm. The argument's virtue is its near-total honesty: it takes the science as given and refuses to let words mean whatever they need to mean.

The second thing is a conviction I can't shake: the goodness of creation has to be morally substantial, not just fiat or functionality. A God who calls a world good while its ordinary operation depends on conscious creatures being terrified, injured, and killed doesn't sound like the God I worship, and I don't want to solve that by quietly redefining good to mean fitting for its purpose. I'm not saying every animal death is a murder. I'm saying that much built-in harm makes the word good ring hollow unless it points at something real.

What I'd like from you is not choose one side but tell me what each kind of resolution would actually cost. What would have to be true for me to be justified in dropping the conviction as mere sentiment or anthropocentrism, and what would dropping it honestly look like? What would have to be true for me to instead amend the argument, for instance by saying nonhuman suffering doesn't count the way human suffering does, or biological death isn't intrinsically evil, or the fall was a primordial cosmic event before humanity? And is there a reading on which both genuinely survive, where the goodness of creation names something real that doesn't require a pre-human world without pain? Where you take a position, distinguish what is settled among philosophers of religion and theologians from what is genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
