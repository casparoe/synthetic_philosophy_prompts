I teach an upper-level seminar in practical rationality, eighteen students, and we meet for seventy-five minutes. We have just read Parfit's hitchhiker, and my students have the standard reaction: they call it a desert puzzle and then argue that in real life reputation or decency would solve it. I would like a classroom game for next week that makes them feel the underlying structure before we return to the text — the way a person who is known to be rational can be left worse off than someone known to be irrational, because certain threats and promises cannot be made credible to an audience that predicts the person will maximize at the moment of choice. I want a game built around the core two-stage interaction: one person creates value at some cost, the other later decides whether to repay or fulfill a promise, and both know in advance what the self-interested later choice is. It should have rules, simple materials, and a points-based payoff scheme that needs no real money, with roles rotating so everyone is on both sides at least once. I do not want to explain any of this in advance; the trap should emerge from the rules themselves.

The debrief should map exactly what happened in the game onto the formal structure of the hitchhiker and onto the idea that it can be rational to arrange to be irrational later. It should also state clearly what the game cannot reproduce — I suspect it has something to do with the difference between Parfit's one-shot interaction and the reputation effects that creep into any repeated classroom exercise — and how to keep students from treating the game's repetition as a way around the problem rather than as a clue to it. Tell me also what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to fix it on the spot. Please keep the entire answer under 900 words.
