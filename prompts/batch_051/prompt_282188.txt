I've been going back and forth with someone on a forum about the bootstrapping and easy knowledge stuff in epistemology, and I want somebody to actually work through a variant instead of giving the stock replay about Roxanne's fuel gauge. The usual setup, for contrast: a subject looks at a red table and comes to know it's red without first having independent evidence that her color vision is reliable, and then the bootstrapping worry is that she could track a bunch of those color judgments and conclude that her vision works after all.

Now change the case. The subject has real calibration data rather than a vague my-faculties-are-probably-fine prior. She knows that 2 percent of people with her profile have an undiagnosed red-green defect, that normal people classify red/green chips correctly 98 percent of the time, that defective people get them right only 60 percent of the time, and that the defect feels exactly like normal vision from the inside. She is shown a long randomized sequence of chips and told that 70 percent are red and 30 percent green, independently sampled. She looks at forty of them. Each chip simply looks red or green to her, nothing appears marginal or strange, and she forms the corresponding color belief. She never gets to check any chip independently. Afterwards she tries to infer from the run of seemings that her own color vision is reliable.

What I actually want: (1) Does the run of forty seemings give her genuine probabilistic evidence about her own reliability, or is it still the bootstrapping move because every observation is produced by the very faculty under test? (2) Does knowing the 2 percent defect rate already defeat the original easy-knowledge verdict that she knows the first chip is red, and if not, at what defect rate or at what accuracy gap between normal and defective does the intuitive verdict flip? Work the update out in one clean model and then say which part of the original puzzle that model does and doesnt capture.
