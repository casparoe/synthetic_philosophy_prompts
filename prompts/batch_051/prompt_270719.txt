You are an expert in the philosophy of risk, decision theory, and the epistemic norms of regulatory science. Take your time, and begin by brainstorming several candidate answers before settling on the one you find best supported.

Consider a stylized but familiar asymmetry in how many industrialized regulatory systems treat new products. When a firm wants to bring a new pharmaceutical, pesticide, or, in some regimes, a novel industrial chemical or genetically modified crop to market, the default is restrictive: the product may not be sold until the proponent has produced evidence satisfying regulators of its safety under the intended conditions, and the burden of showing safety rests on the proponent. But for a great many other innovations — a new material embedded in consumer goods, a new household product, or a novel software service — the default is permissive: the innovation may be introduced immediately, and regulators may intervene only after they have accumulated evidence of harm. The slogan precautionary principle is often attached to the first sort of rule, but I am interested in something the slogan papers over: what implicit theory of risk, ignorance, and responsibility would make this asymmetry principled rather than arbitrary?

Work through the following. First, spell out what the restrictive default appears to presuppose: about the relative seriousness of false positives and false negatives, about unconsented exposure to uncertain harms, about treating the status quo as a baseline, and about whether uncertainty itself can count as a reason against acting. Second, distinguish which of those presuppositions the practice strictly requires from which it merely fits well with; several rival theories may license the very same rule. Third, address the apparent inconsistency that systems which apply precaution to novel interventions rarely apply it symmetrically to incumbent technologies or to the decision to delay an innovation, even though incumbency and delay can carry risks of their own. Does the practice need the assumption that the status quo is a safe default, or can it survive without that assumption? Finally, explain what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced — for example, by a strict expected-utility framework, or by a symmetry principle holding innovations and incumbents, and acting and refraining, to the same standard of evidence.
