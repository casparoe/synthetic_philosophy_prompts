I cover local politics and ideas for a magazine, and I'm working on a piece about a midsized city that wants to let non-citizen permanent residents — and maybe 16-year-olds — vote in municipal elections. The political theorists I talk to keep calling this the boundary problem: before you can ask what the people decide, you first have to ask who counts as the people. I want to be able to push back when someone on city council waives around a vague principle.

Here are the principles I keep hearing. Can you sort them into three tiers — keep, maybe with conditions, reject — and say in a sentence or two why each one lands where it does?

(a) All-affected interests: everyone whose interests are affected by a decision should get a say.
(b) All-subjected: everyone who is subject to the city's laws should get a vote.
(c) Citizenship: only formal citizens get the franchise.
(d) Residency: everyone who lives there, full stop.
(e) A weighted version of (a): you need some minimum stake to count, but above that threshold your vote is weighted by how much a decision touches your interests.

Judge them on how well they handle the city's proposal, plus these edge cases: a non-citizen who has lived and paid taxes in the city for twenty years; a citizen who moved abroad but still owns property in the city; and someone who commutes in every day for work but lives in a neighboring town.

Hard limit: 250 words for the whole response, no citations needed.
