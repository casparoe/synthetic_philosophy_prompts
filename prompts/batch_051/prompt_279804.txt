In the literature on social generations there is a persistent split between those who treat categories like the Baby Boomers, Generation X, Millennials, and Generation Z as real social kinds and those who regard them as arbitrary slices of age and calendar time that researchers and the public have reified into pseudo-explanations. The dispute shows up in familiar symptoms: boundary years are fought over without any obvious fact to settle them, membership in a cohort often adds little predictive power once age and period are controlled for, and yet defenders of generations argue that shared historical events shape a cohort in ways that a pure age-period decomposition misses. I would like to know whether the claim that such generations are real kinds is testable, and if so what the strongest test would look like. How might one operationalize the claim — through age-period-cohort decompositions, tests for discontinuities at generational boundaries, or cross-national comparisons of supposedly formative events — and what would a positive or negative result actually establish? Be precise about which part of the "real kind" claim an empirical test could reach and which part remains a conceptual or definitional choice, and about what the boundary-year disputes reveal once they are themselves treated as data.
