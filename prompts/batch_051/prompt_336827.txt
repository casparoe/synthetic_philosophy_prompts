You are grading a midterm in an upper-level philosophy of language course on speech act theory. Below are the exam question and one student's answer. Assign a grade out of 20 and write the comments an instructor would write to justify that grade: a point-by-point assessment noting what the student gets right, where the student goes wrong or under-argues, and what a stronger answer would say. Begin your response with the numeric grade and a single sentence stating the overall verdict in the very first sentence; then give the point-by-point reasoning. Do not restate the question or the student's answer, and do not include any preamble.

Question (20 points):

Consider the following case. During a department meeting, the chair says, "I now declare this meeting adjourned." A visiting undergraduate bystander then says the same words from the back of the room. That evening, a faculty member sends the chair an email saying, "I hereby resign from the curriculum committee." Separately, the same faculty member, intending only to draft a message, accidentally sends the chair another email saying, "I hereby nominate myself for department chair."

For each of the four utterances, explain whether the relevant act—adjourning, resigning, or nominating—has been successfully performed, and why. In your answer, distinguish (i) locutionary, illocutionary, and perlocutionary acts; (ii) the felicity conditions and how they are satisfied or violated in each case; and (iii) the role of uptake. Draw on Austin's and Searle's accounts where they help, but do not merely list terminology; apply it to the cases.

Student answer:

All four utterances are performatives: they do not describe an inner act but constitute or purport to constitute the act named. The chair's adjournment is successful because she has the institutional authority, the meeting is the right setting, and she executes the conventional formula completely. The bystander's utterance is an infelicity of a specific kind—a misapplication—because the procedure for adjourning a meeting exists for the chair, so the bystander's act is void, not merely inappropriate. The resignation also succeeds: the faculty member is a member of the committee, the chair is the right addressee, and the 'hereby' formula removes any doubt that this is a performative; Searle's preparatory and propositional content conditions are met, and the utterance commits the speaker in the way resigning requires. The accidental nomination likewise succeeds, since performatives are assessed as felicitous or infelicitous rather than true or false, and here the words were sent to the right person in the right form; once the utterance has been made, the act is done regardless of what the speaker intended, because intention is a matter for the sincerity condition at best. In each case the perlocutionary effect—whether people actually leave, whether the chair updates the committee roster, whether anyone takes the nomination seriously—is separate from the illocutionary act, and an act can succeed even if the perlocutionary effect does not follow. Uptake is the hearer's recognizing the force of the utterance, but Austin treats uptake as subsequent to the act rather than as one of the felicity conditions; so failure of uptake does not undo any of the four acts.
