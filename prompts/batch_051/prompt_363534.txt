I've been reading about philosophical questions raised by photography, and one essay I came across summarized the so-called transparency thesis in a way that struck me as both fascinating and possibly overblown. The summary runs roughly as follows: "The transparency thesis says that a photograph is not just a depiction of a scene but a way of seeing — literally seeing — the object that stood before the lens: a window onto the thing itself. The reason is that the photographic image is produced mechanically. Light from the scene fixes the image on the film or sensor directly, with no beliefs or intentions having to get between the world and the picture. On this view, that is why you can see a long-dead relative in an old snapshot, much as you can see a star that burned out millennia ago. Paintings and drawings are different: there the artist's beliefs about how things look guide every mark, so the result is merely a representation of its subject. It follows that photography, unlike painting, is not really a representational art at all."

I would like to know how fair this is as a summary of the view. Could you go through it sentence by sentence and say which parts a defender of the transparency thesis would accept, which parts they would correct as overstatements or subtle conflations, and which parts are simply false? I am especially suspicious of the final inference — that because photographs are produced mechanically, photography is not a representational art — but I cannot tell whether the earlier sentences actually support that conclusion or whether it trades on a slide between two different senses of "mechanical."
