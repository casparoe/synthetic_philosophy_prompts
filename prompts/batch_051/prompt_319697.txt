Read John Steinbeck's The Grapes of Wrath through Fernand Braudel's distinction between the longue durée and the event, but press the novel against the distinction rather than using it as a label. The novel alternates between wide-angle chapters about drought, soil, tractors, banks, and highways and close-up chapters following the Joad family's choices; on its face this looks like Braudel's hierarchical picture, in which geography, climate, and slow economic structures set the frame while events and individuals are surface movement on deeper currents. I want a committed thesis about whether the novel ultimately endorses that picture or subverts it. Does the chapter structure teach that the Joads' decisions are mostly downstream effects of structures they never chose, or do the close-up chapters make a competing case that individual agency—Ma holding the family together, Tom's final conversation with Ma, Rose of Sharon at the end—remains morally real and not reducible to those structures? Take one side and defend it with the book's structure and specific scenes, and say honestly where the novel complicates or escapes the categories instead of illustrating them. I'm writing an essay that wants to use Steinbeck to test Braudel, and I need a thesis I can defend rather than a survey of available readings.
