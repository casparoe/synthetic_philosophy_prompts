I'm a novelist, and this started as a craft problem rather than a philosophy problem. In my current manuscript the characters live under a fairly rigid honor code, and I wanted the code to feel elegant — the kind of thing where readers sense that a dozen rules are somehow all one idea wearing different costumes. So I started reading about why philosophers treat simplicity as a virtue in ethical theory, and now I'm stuck on what looks to me like a hole in the usual discussion.

The standard story is that simpler theories — less principles, fewer moving parts — are more virtuous, but they also have to fit our case-by-case intuitions, and whenever they don't, people bolt on an exception or a qualification, so the "simple" theory stops looking simple. I've been toying with a distinction I haven't seen put quite this way, which I'm calling **compiled simplicity**. The claim is that a moral theory's simplicity should be measured not by the number of principles it states, but by the length of the total decision procedure once you include all the interpretation, the exception-handling, the borderline tie-breaking, and the unstated auxiliary machinery — roughly, how compressible the complete mapping from situations to verdicts is. My rule of thumb: if a theory takes one sentence to state but a thousand pages of commentary to say what counts as happiness, whose happiness matters, and what happens when two verdicts clash, it isn't simple; if a theory has a dozen principles that keep recombining two or three core patterns, it might be.

To be clear, I don't know weather this is original or just muddled. It sounds like it should already exist under some name — systematicity, or algorithmic elegance, or reflective equilibrium gone computational — and I'd rather not go around claiming I invented Kolmogorov complexity for ethicists. So here's what I want: act as a professional moral philosopher and think carefully about my construct. Start by brainstorming the obvious objections (for instance, that moral theories can't really be "compiled," or that I'm smuggling in a questionable picture of what a principle is), then give me an honest assessment. What's the strongest version of compiled simplicity? Is the core claim — that one-principle theories often hide complexity rather than eliminate it — actually true, or does it rest on a confusion? And where does the idea break down, or stop being distinctive?
