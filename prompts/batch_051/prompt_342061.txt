A friend in the philosophy-of-physics world talked me into an argument and now I cant find the exit, because it seems to prove too much.

(1) Take two electrons in a helium atom. Quantum mechanics only countenances states that are antisymmetric under particle exchange: swapping the two electrons changes the state vector by at most an overall sign, and that sign is not physically detectable.
(2) So the configuration electron-1-spin-up/electron-2-spin-down is not a genuinely different physical situation from electron-1-spin-down/electron-2-spin-up. There is no fact of the matter about which electron is which.
(3) If there is no fact of the matter about which one is which, then the electrons are not distinct individuals in anything like the ordinary sense. They lack the this-one-not-that-one identity that chairs and people have.
(4) Every ordinary object is composed of electrons and other quantum particles like quarks.
(5) If the fundamental constituents of an object are not individuals, then the object they compose is not an individual either, or at least its individuality must be inherited from its parts, so the problem carries upward.
(6) So ordinary objects, including persons, are not individuals in the ordinary sense.

I want to say step 5 is the overreach, but I cant explain why without either calling the physics just a compact way of writing things down (which sounds like instrumentalism) or positing some unexplained emergent individuality at the macro level. And maybe the real culprit is step 3, but denying 3 feels like it guts the whole metaphysical significance of quantum statistics. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost?
