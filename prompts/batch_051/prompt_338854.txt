So a friend of mine has started saying "fortune favors the bold" every time someone brings up downside risk, like that one line is the entire teaching of Machiavelli's chapter on Fortune. He uses it to justify skipping the boring prep work and just moving fast and improvising, because audacity is what Fortuna rewards. But when I actually read the chapter, what stuck with me was the river image: you can't stop the flood while it's happening, so the men who get ruined are the ones who didn't build dikes and canals beforehand — which sounds closer to "fortune favors the people who already did their maintenance." And then there's the other bit where he seems to say Fortune is like a woman who responds better to the bold than to the cautious, which is presumably where my friend's whole thing comes from. What I want is a menu: what are the distinct claims that slogan 'fortune favors the bold' could actually be making, from the trivial to the genuinely radical? Then tell me which of those readings Machiavelli is actually committed to in that chapter — where does the river image fit, where does the impetuous-versus-cautious argument fit, and does virtù have to cover both? And the practical question: does my friend's "ignore the downside and improvise" conclusion survive on any reading of the slogan that Machiavelli would actually endorse, or is it being carried by the slogan alone?
