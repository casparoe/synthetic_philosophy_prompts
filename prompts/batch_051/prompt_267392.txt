I'm a novelist, not a mathematician, but my husband is, and after years of dinner-table arguments about whether following a proof line by line is the same as understanding it, I've started keeping a notebook of his verdicts on his students. All of these are about one proof: the standard proof that the square root of 2 is irrational—assume √2 = a/b in lowest terms, square both sides, and chase the evenness back and forth until it says both a and b are even, which is the contradiction.

His verdicts so far:

- Minh wrote the whole proof out from scratch and justified every line, including proving that if a² is even then a is even. He located exactly where the lowest-terms assumption enters, and when I asked what the proof is about he said, without pausing, that it's an evenness loop: evenness travels backward from square to root, so a supposedly reduced fraction would have to keep being reducible. Verdict: he understands it.

- Ruth reproduced it word for word from the textbook, but she couldn't prove the a²-even-implies-a-even step, couldn't say where the lowest-terms assumption was doing work, and had no one-sentence version of the proof. Verdict: does not understand it.

- Ben couldn't get the proof going from memory in symbols, but he said the idea out loud—reduce the fraction, square, parity propagates backward, now both top and bottom are even—and then reconstructed every line from that idea, justifying each step as he went. He also adapted the whole thing on the spot to show √3 is irrational, swapping evenness for divisibility by 3. Verdict: understands it.

- Priya gave the clean line-by-line proof, justified every step, and also explained why the same template breaks for √4: in that case the analogous move leaves you with b² = k², which doesn't force any parity on b, so the descent never gets going. She gave a one-sentence version of the mechanism too. Verdict: understands it.

- Tom could reproduce the proof and said "it's a proof by contradiction," but under questioning he couldn't prove the even-square step, couldn't say where lowest terms mattered, and when asked to summarize the proof just read back a shorter version of the steps. Verdict: does not understand it.

Now the new case: Callie, a student this semester. She can write the proof out perfectly and justify every single line, including giving her own proof that a² even forces a even. She can tell you exactly where the lowest-terms assumption is used. But when I asked her what the proof is about in one sentence, she just started reciting the proof again. When a visiting mathematician asked her to run the same argument for √3, she said she didn't see the connection and tried to rebuild everything line by line from scratch, then got tangled. She also wouldn't guess why the template fails for √4, except to say that √4 is rational so there has to be a flaw somewhere.

So: would my husband say Callie understands the proof or not? I'm less interested in the verdict itself than in what rule he seems to be running about what it is to understand a proof. Lay out the candidate rules that fit his track record so far—line-level justification, extracting and re-deploying the core idea, something else—say which of them diverge on Callie, give me your best guess about his verdict and why, and name the single further case you'd want to see to settle which rule he's really running.
