I am a PhD student in the philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and I have been trying to get clear about what Gödel's incompleteness theorems actually license, if anything, in debates about minds and machines. I recently read a long essay in a popular science magazine whose author summarizes the technical results and then draws very broad philosophical conclusions from them. The central passage reads as follows:

"Gödel's first incompleteness theorem states that any consistent formal system capable of expressing elementary arithmetic is incomplete: there are true arithmetical sentences that it cannot prove. This shows that mathematical truth outruns proof in any fixed formal system, and therefore truth cannot be identified with provability. His second theorem states that no such consistent system can prove its own consistency, so the consistency of mathematics cannot be demonstrated from within mathematics itself. The author then turns to the mind. A computer running a program, he says, is just a formal system in physical form. Since any formal system rich enough for arithmetic must leave out true arithmetical sentences, no AI can ever match human mathematical understanding. The decisive step is this: when a human mathematician is presented with a consistent formal system, she can see that its Gödel sentence is true even though the system itself cannot prove it. Because the human mind can reliably perform this feat for any formal system proposed as a model of it, the human mind cannot itself be a formal system. Gödel thus refutes, not just this or that AI architecture, but the computational theory of mind as such, and with it the idea that human reasoning is the execution of a finite, specifiable set of rules."

I find the first two sentences broadly plausible as a popularization, but the philosophical payoff strikes me as much stronger than what the theorems establish, and I cannot pinpoint exactly where the slide occurs. Could you audit the summary sentence by sentence: which claims about the theorems themselves are accurate, which popularizations cut corners, and which of the further conclusions follow once the technical results are stated carefully? I would be especially grateful for an account of how a defender of the computational theory of mind would respond to the "the human can see that the Gödel sentence is true" move, and what auxiliary assumptions that move has to import beyond Gödel's actual results.
