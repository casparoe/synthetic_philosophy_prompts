You are a professional philosopher of mind and action, with a deep knowledge of the free will debate and of the Sphex wasp example as used by Daniel Dennett and Douglas Hofstadter. You are also a deft satirist whose humor is built on conceptual accuracy rather than exaggeration for its own sake.

I want you to write a parody of a corporate training handout titled something like Procedural Agility: Identifying and Mitigating Sphexish Behavior in the Modern Workplace. The piece should run about 800 words and should stay deadpan throughout, formatted with numbered sections, a case study, and a sign-off box.

The target of the satire is the view that free will just is behavioral flexibility — that if a system has enough context-sensitive rules, branching routines, and overrides, it has agency in whatever sense matters for moral responsibility. The humor must come from the actual philosophical content. That includes: the difference between first-order behavioral plasticity and second-order caring about or wanting one's routines; the regress that appears when you handle a rigid routine by adding a higher-order rule that is itself rigid; the idea that adding randomness or a menu of scripts is not the same as acting for reasons; and the suspicion that a sufficiently elaborate branching program may still be, in Hofstadter's sense, sphexish. Get the wasp right: it re-inspects the nest after prey is moved, without altering the sequence, and the example's power is that the behavior looks intelligent but is fixed.

The handout should include sections such as Learning Objectives, Definitions (e.g., Fixed Action Pattern, Nest-Checking Loop, Higher-Order Volition), Recognizing Sphexish Micro-Routines, Approved Flexibility Strategies, and a Case Study of an employee named W. Sphex whose supervisor keeps moving a file on his desk to test his procedural agility. The case study should end with discussion questions that quietly expose the problem with using flexibility as a criterion of free will. The prose should sound like HR compliance material, but a philosopher of mind who knows Dennett, Hofstadter, and the free-will literature should find it genuinely funny rather than wince. A reader who doesn't know the literature should still find it amusing and legible, without any wink.

Before drafting, think for a minute about which moves in the free-will debate the satire can fairly exploit and which moves would confuse the target with a strawman. Then write the piece.
