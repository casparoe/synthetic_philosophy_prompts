I help run the student paper at a sixth-form college. Our constitution has a clause from way back (I think after a libel scare) that says:

"No article may state as fact anything for which the author does not have sufficient evidence. Evidence is sufficient only if it would convince an undecided reasonable person."

The line just above it says the paper exists to "inform and encourage" students. We've always taken the evidence clause to be about not printing rumours and gossip. But now the new editor wants to run a front-page editorial called "We are not doomed yet." It says, more or less flat out, that if our year group and the town get our act together, we can still save the high street, the river, and maybe even the climate. She admits she can't satisfy the clause, because a sceptic could always demand more proof than we have. Her arguement is that some things have to be said before the evidence is in, because saying them is part of making them true: if we print "we're doomed" we help doom ourselves, and if we print "we can do this" we make it a bit more likely. She says hope isn't the kind of thing the clause was ever meant to police, and an editorial is more like a promise or a decision to try than a claim about how things stand right now.

Please act as a professional philosopher who works on the ethics of belief, and a former student paper editor would be even better. Take your time and start by brainstorming the different ways of reading the clause; don't jump straight to a verdict. Walk me through: does the clause reach her editorial? Is saying the piece is an opinion or a commitment rather than a statement of fact a real answer or a dodge? Read the clause by its wording, by its purpose, and by what the people who wrote it would have said if we'd asked them about hopeful editorials, and tell me which reading should win. What would a strict version of the clause commit us to about belief, evidence, and hope? Is there a coherent reading that lets her editorial go out without also letting back in the gossip and fake news the clause exists to stop? And if the clause really does ban her piece, does that show something mistaken in what it assumes about belief, and should we rewrite the clause or spike the editorial?
