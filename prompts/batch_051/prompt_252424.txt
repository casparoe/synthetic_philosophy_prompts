I'm a philosophy professor, and I've been roped in as external examiner for a small theological college that trains priests and deacons. Every exam season their marking team makes a mess of the compulsory question on the Trinity. They know the formula — one God in three persons — but they can't operationalise it, so they fail candidates who are just saying the orthodox thing with slightly unusual phrasing, and they pass candidates who have quietly gone full modalism or tritheism. Some markers think any sentence that says the Father and the Son are the same is fine, and others think any sentence that treats the persons as distinct must be heretical. Their not trained in metaphysics, they've got about ten minutes per script, and the results feed into ordination decisions, so the stakes are real. I'll also have to defend whatever you produce to the college's theology faculty, who are a bit suspicious of a philosophy professor meddling in doctrine.

I'd like you to act as an expert on the logic of identity and philosophical theology, and write me a reusable marking procedure — a decision procedure, basically — for deciding whether a candidate's sentences about the Trinity are within the orthodox claim that there is one God in three persons. The people using it will have no training in the underlying theory, so you'll have to keep the distinctions that actually do the work and drop the ones that don't.

Before you draft the procedure, take a moment to think carefully, in your own notes, about the difference between the 'is' of predication and the 'is' of identity, and about counting substances as opposed to counting persons, since that's where nearly every balls-up happens. Then turn that into an ordered set of tests a marker can run on a sentence: what to ask first, what counts as a sufficient answer, when the procedure stops with a fail, and when it should stop and hand the case to a theologian because the logic of identity has run out.

Then apply the procedure to three sample candidate sentences: (a) 'The Father and the Son are the same God but not the same person'; (b) 'The Trinity is like water being ice, liquid, and steam'; (c) 'The Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit are three distinct divine beings, each fully God.' Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer the college's markers won't like, and be explicit about what the procedure cannot settle.

Oh, and write the answer in British English. No reading lists, citations, or bibliography please — I need something the marking team can actually use on a wet Tuesday.
