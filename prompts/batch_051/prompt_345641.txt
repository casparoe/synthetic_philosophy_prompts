I run trust and safety for a medium-sized marketplace where buyers and sellers both have accounts, and we suspend or ban people pretty often. Right now our enforcement is basically strict liability: if the system or a reviewer finds a listed item or a message that violates a policy, the account gets a strike, and enough strikes means a suspension or a ban. We almost never look at whether the person meant to break the rule, because at our volume we cant. But we're getting more and more appeals where the person clearly didn't intend the violation — a seller listed what turned out to be counterfeit cosmetics she bought from a wholesaler, a buyer used a phrase that reads as a slur in a review but was quoting the seller's own product title back at them, things like that. Our reviewers are not lawyers and each decision has maybe five minutes.

I want you to write me a reusable enforcement procedure, grounded in what criminal law actually knows about strict liability and mens rea, that tells a reviewer: which mental states to distinguish (and which of those distinctions can be collapsed in practice without losing the point), when its okay to penalize without proving any culpable mental state at all and when it isn't, what counts as enough evidence for each level, where the procedure stops with a "no," and where it has to hand the decision to a human because its genuinely a judgement call about values rather than facts. Also be honest about where this will misfire.

Then run the procedure on three cases: the counterfeit cosmetics seller above, a user who posts a slur claiming they were reporting/quotation, and a seller with a nine-month history of late or missing shipments who says its all been bad luck with suppliers. The procedure should give an answer I'm not going to like in at least one of these, and you should say which one and why.
