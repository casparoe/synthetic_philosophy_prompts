I ended up here because I'm trying to write a term paper on how a rational person should defer to objective probabilities, and my roommate keeps asking me why we should trust a weather model's 30% chance of rain over our own judgment. That argument somehow led me to reconstruct the following as a foil for the paper, and now I can't tell where it breaks. It seems to show that the Principal Principle — the idea that, conditional on the objective chance of A being x and on no "inadmissible" information, your credence in A should be x — is inconsistent with almost any interesting chance law. That can't be right, but the premises all look innocent.

The argument goes like this. (1) Chance facts are settled by the overall pattern of particular outcomes: a chance law is true just in case it's the best systematization of the total history of the world, so a different enough pattern of outcomes would have made a different chance law true. (2) Let L be a candidate chance law saying that a certain coin has an independent chance of 1/2 of landing heads on each toss. (3) Under L, the proposition F — that the next 10,000 tosses all come up heads — has positive chance, thought tiny. (4) Suppose F is an undermining future for L: if F were to occur, the total pattern of outcomes, past plus future, would be best systematized by a law saying the coin is biased, not by L. So F and L, together with the past, are incompatible. (5) By the Principal Principle, conditional on the true chance law and the admissible history, my credence in F should equal the chance L assigns to F — a positive number. (6) But if (4) is right, then conditional on L I should be certain that F doesn't occur, so my credence in F should be zero. (7) So the Principal Principle tells me to assign both positive credence and zero credence to the same proposition. (8) There was nothing special about L or F; any probabilistic chance law that gives positive probability to an undermining future lands in the same contradiction. (9) So either the Principal Principle has to go, or the pattern-summary picture of chance has to go, or we must insist that no chance law ever gives positive probability to a future that would undermine it — and that last option looks crazy, since a fair coin obviously can land heads many times in a row.

I'd rather keep both the Principal Principle and the pattern-summary picture of chance, since my paper is built on exactly that combination, but the argument makes it look like that's the unstable pair. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost the rest of the view? And could you flag which parts of this have a settled answer and which parts are still genuinely contested among people who work on this? I dont want some contested fix presented to me as if it were the obvious consensus.
