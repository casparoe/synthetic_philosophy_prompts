After a long argument with a friend about whether there would have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler, I went back to a passage in Leo Tolstoy’s War and Peace that keeps haunting the question. The argument was over whether individual leaders or underlying structures do the real explanatory work; my friend took the structural side, and I defended something closer to Tolstoy’s suspicion. The passage is from the Second Epilogue, in the Maude translation:

“Modern history has rejected the beliefs of the ancients without replacing them by a new conception, and the logic of the situation has obliged the historians, after they had apparently rejected the divine authority of the kings and the ‘fate’ of the ancients, to reach the same conclusion by another road, that is, to recognize (1) nations guided by individual men, and (2) the existence of a known aim to which these nations and humanity at large are tending.”

I read Tolstoy here as claiming that modern historians reject divine agency but still organize their explanations around individual figures and assumed historical ends. If that is right, structural explanation might be a newer expression of the same old habit: history remains a story about someone, or something, acting toward some end.

My friend replied that this is a dated argument. He said modern structural history no longer needs either thing Tolstoy describes. It can appeal to demographic pressure, institutional constraints, economic geography, communication networks, or formal models, and it can test hypotheses with comparisons and counterfactuals rather than narrating the careers of great men. On his view, Tolstoy diagnosed a weakness of nineteenth-century narrative history, not a permanent problem.

Does Tolstoy’s argument survive that development? What exactly would a structural explanation have to do to avoid turning “the nation,” “the system,” or “the revolution” into a disguised agent with an implicit goal? If the argument does survive, what is the remaining limit on structural or counterfactual explanations of large historical events? If it does not, what did Tolstoy get wrong about explanation that the newer methods have fixed?
