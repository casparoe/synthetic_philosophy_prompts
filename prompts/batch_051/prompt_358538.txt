You are an expert in the philosophy and formal theory of cooperation, especially the prisoner's dilemma, iterated games, and the evolution of reciprocity. I teach an upper-level undergraduate philosophy seminar on cooperation and social norms: 18 students, one 75-minute session. I want an activity that makes them feel, in sequence, three shifts: that defection is locally rational in a one-shot anonymous encounter; that a repeated relationship changes this by giving conditional strategies such as tit-for-tat a grip through the shadow of the future; and that extending cooperation to strangers requires reputation or indirect reciprocity, which is fragile when information is noisy or incomplete. Design a runnable sequence of games with clear rules, a scoring scheme that needs no real money, and a debrief. The teaching should happen through the rules, not through what I say afterward: build the design around minimal rule changes, probably a one-shot phase against rotating strangers, then a repeated-partner phase, then a phase with some public record of past choices, though you may improve on that structure. In the debrief, map what happens in each phase to the underlying mechanism, explain why the repeated phase should favor forgiving, non-envious conditional strategies over always-cooperate, always-defect, or grim strategies when mistakes are possible, and say what breaks when reputations are incomplete, inflated, or mistaken. Before the final design, work through the incentives of every rule and briefly brainstorm one or two alternative mechanisms you considered and rejected. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—for example, students coordinating choices, misreading the payoff matrix, or treating the reputation phase as a test of character rather than incentives—and how to handle each failure. Be explicit about which part of real-world cooperation the activity cannot reproduce, such as partner choice, punishment, or evolutionary selection across a population, and what the debrief must therefore supply.
