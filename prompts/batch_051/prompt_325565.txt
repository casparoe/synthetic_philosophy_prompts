In Part III of The Theory of Moral Sentiments, Adam Smith claims that someone who grew up in solitude would have no idea of the beauty or deformity of his own mind, and then writes: "Bring him into society, and he is immediately provided with the mirror which he wanted before. It is placed in the countenance and behaviour of those he lives with, which always mark when they enter into, and when they disapprove of his sentiments; and it is here that he first views the propriety and impropriety of his own passions, the beauty and deformity of his own mind." Please compare the social mirror Smith describes in this passage with the impartial spectator he introduces earlier in the same chapter. Is the impartial spectator best understood as the internalized judgment of the actual people around us, or does it correct and transform what the social mirror shows? Which of the two should govern self-judgment when they conflict, and why?
