I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. I'm building a feature where millions of users get a small chance of something bad happening to them without being asked — think a 1-in-50,000 chance of a double charge or a privacy leak — and I want to know wether we're justified. A friend of mine put the standard view like this: "The ethics of risk without consent is basically expected-value reasoning: if the probability-weighted benefit is bigger than the probability-weighted harm, it's permitted. Consent is nice to get but it's not a free-standing requirement, and if the bad outcome never materializes, nobody was actually harmed, so theres nothing for the person to complain about. People who object to tiny risks like secondhand smoke are really just bad at thinking about small probabilities. The only serious problem is that a tiny risk spread over a huge population seems to add up to someone being hurt, but since each individual faces only a tiny chance, that's just an artifact of aggregation and you're allowed to ignore it." I suspect this is too convenient for my case, but I can't tell where it goes wrong. Which parts would a careful philosopher accept, which parts are misrepresentations, and does his conclusion about the aggregation problem survive fixing the wrong parts?
