In a philosophy of law seminar last week a student kept leaning on "the king can do no wrong" as though it settled whether the state can wrong its citizens. His line was that a tort suit against the government is a category mistake, since whatever the state does in its sovereign capacity is the law's own act. When I pushed back, he said I was overcomplicating a simple maxim. I think that one-liner is doing alot of hidden work, but I want to make sure I'm not strawmanning him.

Can you lay out the distinct precise claims the maxim could be making, from the trivially true to the wildly implausible? I'm especially interested in how the "lawful by definition" reading (the sovereign makes the law, so it can't violate it) differs from readings about moral infallibility, about whether the state can be sued at all, and about whether official misconduct gets blamed on the official rather than the state. For each reading, say whether it's a platitude, a substantive claim, or just false. Then tell me which reading actually supports what my student wanted to argue, and which reading he's most likely tacitly relying on. I want to hand him a menu of readings and see which one he'll bite.
