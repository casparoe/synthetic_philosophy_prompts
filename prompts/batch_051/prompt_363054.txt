I'm a retired analytical chemist who fell into epistemology a few months back, and I've been turning over Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge. I started with the closure stuff, the claim that you can know p, and know that p entails q, and still not know q, but before I trust that result I want to pressure-test one of the less discussed parts of the machinery. Just so we're on the same page: Nozick says S knows p if and only if p is true, S believes p, if p were false S wouldn't believe p (sensitivity), and if p were true S would believe p (adherence). People fight endlessly about the third condition, but the fourth mostly gets a free pass, and I suspect thats a mistake.

So here's the order I'd like filled. Build me a thought experiment, one ordinary-life paragraph, where a person has a true belief that satisfies the sensitivity condition but fails the adherence condition. No demons, no brains in vats, no fake barns, no disguised animals, no skeptical set-pieces. A kitchen, a workshop, a lab bench, a parking lot, that's the register I want. The belief should be the kind a reasonable observer would call knowledge, but Nozick's four conditions, as stated, should say the person does not know. Then walk me through the case slowly: state exactly what p is, state the method by which the person forms the belief, describe the relevant nearby possible worlds in each direction, and show why sensitivity holds while adherence fails. After that, tell me which feature of the scenario you had to engineer to make the two conditions pull apart, and what a defender of Nozick could say to stop the case from counting. If you think the case can't be built without cheating, I want that verdict too, with the reason, not just a shrug.
