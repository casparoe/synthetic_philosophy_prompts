I'm a philosophy professor, which is why its awkward to admit I have a habit that feels more dogmatic than philosophical. When someone floats a doubt that would undercut the very practices I'd need in order to evaluate it—like that our memories of the syllabus were implanted five minutes ago, or that each of us is trapped in a private dream and our words never really make contact—I don't treat it as a point that needs refuting. I change the subject or make a joke and get on with the class. I honestly can't refute these things; when I've tried, the arguments just assume the very things being doubted. A colleague says this is exactly the move I'd mock in an undergraduate: refusing to engage a challenge because engaging it is uncomfortable. But I also suspect some of these so-called doubts aren't doubts at all, just requests to stop doing what we're doing dressed up as objections. Is my policy defensible? If so, what distinguishes the doubts I'm allowed to bracket from ordinary doubts about the syllabus, a date, or a premise of an argument, which I take completely seriously? And if it isn't defensible, what would genuinely taking those doubts seriously look like?
