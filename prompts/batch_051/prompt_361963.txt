I cover science and ideas for a living, and I've gotten a little obsessed with a pattern that shows up in experimental philosophy: people's judgments about whether something was done intentionally change depending on whether the side effect was good or bad. Here's the version I keep giving friends. A VP is told a new project will make money and also wreck a local river. He says he doesn't care about the river, he just wants profit, and greenlights it. Almost everyone says he intentionally damaged the river. Now keep everything the same—same VP, same profit motive, same "I don't care about the environment"—but the project will clean up a local river. Now almost everyone says he did not intentionally help the river. Logically, the side effect is equally foreseen and equally ignored in both cases; the only thing that flips is whether the outcome is bad or good.

I want to know if anything justifies that asymmetry, or whether it's just a glitch in how moral feelings bleed into factual-sounding judgments. Take the strongest position you can: maybe intentionality is partly a normative notion, so a bad ignored outcome counts as intentional while a good ignored outcome doesn't. Or maybe our judgments are just contaminated by blame and we should correct them. Give me the best principle you can construct for treating the harmful and helpful cases differently, test it against a pair of cases that are exact mirror images, and then say whether you'd keep the asymmetry or get rid of it.
