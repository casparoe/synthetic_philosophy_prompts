I'm a novelist, and for the past year I've been reading nineteenth-century Arctic expedition histories for a book I'm writing. A pattern keeps jumping out. When a commander pushes on through late-season ice and the ship is crushed, historians describe the decision as suicidal, reckless, the product of hubris. When another commander makes the same push with the same information and slips through, the same authors call it brilliant seamanship and nerves of steel. The decision gets its entire character from how it turned out, not from anything the person could have known at the time. I catch myself doing it in my own drafts too: once I know how the plot ends, I go back and shade an early choice so it reads as foreshadowing or as hubris, depending on the ending.

So here's what's actually blocking me. Is there any defensible principle behind treating the same decision, made under the same uncertainty, as blameworthy when it fails and admirable when it succeeds? Or is this just a narrrative tic—hindsight reading the ending back into the choice—that we should admit is sloppy in fiction and corrupting in historical explanation and in how we judge real people? If you think there is a real justification, give me the strongest version of it and test it against two decisions that are as close as possible except for outcome. If you think there isn't, tell me what principle should replace the asymmetry, and whether a novelist has any business resisting it.
