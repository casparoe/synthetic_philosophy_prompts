I teach an upper-level seminar on the moral limits of the criminal law, and last week's session ended in genuine puzzlement over the paradox of blackmail. The students could see that a person may generally disclose a true but embarrassing fact about someone, and may generally ask another person for money; yet they could also see that combining these into a conditional demand — money in exchange for silence — is clearly wrongful, and rightly criminalised. The difficulty, we agreed, is not in the judgement but in saying where the wrongness enters, since neither ingredient seems wrong on its own.

I would like a poem I can take into next week's session: a villanelle about this paradox. Please write it as a careful poet who is also an expert moral philosopher. Use the strict form — five tercets and a closing quatrain, with the first refrain returning at lines 6, 12, and 18 and the second at lines 9, 15, and 19. The two refrains should correspond to the two innocent ingredients, the act of disclosing and the act of asking, and each refrain should sound fully permissible, perhaps even principled, whenever it appears alone in a tercet. The structural work should happen in the closing quatrain: as the form finally places the two refrains side by side in the last two lines, the reader should feel that their innocent conjunction has tipped over into the threat. Before drafting, take some time to brainstorm two or three candidate pairs of refrains, and choose the pair whose innocent readings are most convincing apart and whose juxtaposition is most quietly coercive together. Do not quote from any source; the refrains should be your own. The poem should not moralise or explain itself, and it should be accurate enough that a philosopher working on coercion or the moral limits of the law would recognise the problem at once, while a reader who has never encountered it would still find it a good poem.
