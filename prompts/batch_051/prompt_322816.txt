In reporting a piece on what information is worth, I have been trying to pin down an argument that seems to prove more than anyone should believe. The reasoning runs as follows. (1) A decision maker must choose among several actions, and the desirability of each action depends only on an unknown state of the world — not on what she believes or feels about it. (2) Before acting, she is offered a costless signal that is correlated with the state; the signal changes only her beliefs about the state. (3) For any particular realization of the signal, she could ignore it and choose the action she would have chosen without seeing it. Since that action remains available, the action she actually chooses after seeing the signal cannot be worse in expectation conditional on that realization. (4) Therefore, averaging over all possible realizations of the signal, the policy of observing the signal and then choosing must have expected payoff at least as high as the policy of choosing without observing. (5) Hence a rational payoff-maximizing agent should never refuse free information.

This conclusion seems to prove too much. It would make it irrational not to open a letter because it might bring bad news, irrational to decline a genetic test for a disease with no treatment, irrational to prefer not to know how a precarious work situation will resolve. These are familiar and arguably quite sensible human responses, not mere mistakes. And yet each step looks nearly self-evident: the reasoning appears to say only that information cannot force one to choose worse, because one retains the option of acting as one would have acted anyway.

What I want is a diagnosis of the proof rather than a longer list of examples. Which premise or step, if any, should be rejected, and what does rejecting it commit one to? If the argument is sound, what exactly does it establish about the rationality of real information avoidance? If the right response is instead that the framework is too narrow — that desirability is not fixed by actions and states alone because it depends on hope, dread, or the act of coming to know — what must an adequate model of information avoidance add, and what becomes of the argument once that is added?
