Suppose the following mechanism were proposed for the international system. Before any state or coalition may use armed force in another state on humanitarian grounds without Security Council authorisation, it must lodge a substantial sum with a standing international escrow body. Within five years, a panel drawn from outside the intervening states reviews the operation against criteria fixed in advance: whether the emergency was genuine and imminent, whether force was the least harmful feasible option available at the time, whether the protected population's security was on balance improved, and whether regime change was pursued only so far as protection required rather than as an independent war aim. An intervention judged to meet the criteria recovers its deposit with interest together with a formal certificate of justification; one judged to fail forfeits the deposit, which is paid directly to the affected state's population through channels insulated from its government. Using Kosovo (1999), Iraq (2003), and Libya (2011) as the primary test cases, analyse how this scheme would actually operate. What would it incentivise for would-be interveners, for the regimes presiding over vulnerable populations, for rebel groups and neighbouring states, and for the escrow body itself? Where would it be gamed, and what second-order effects would it have on the speed of future interventions, the conduct of intervening forces, and the development of humanitarian law? Are its likely failure modes fixable without undermining its original purpose?
