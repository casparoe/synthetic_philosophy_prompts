I've been arguing with my coworker about the 35-hour week and he keeps coming back to this analogy: time off is basically scheduled maintenance for a truck. You don't maintain a truck for the truck's own sake, you do it so it can keep hauling freight without breaking down on the highway. Same thing, he says, with rest — you let workers recover only because that's what keeps their output high over the long run, not because leisure has any claim in itself. He uses this to argue that campaigns for shorter hours have to justify themselves on productivity grounds alone, and that stuff like karoshi is best understood as overrunning a machine until it seizes up, rather than as an injustice to the person. I have a feeling the analogy is doing a lot of silent work, especially the part where the worker's own ends simply don't enter the picture except as a constraint on performance. But I'm also not sure how much of that work is removable before the analogy stops being useful at all.

Can you audit this one properly? I'd like a correspondence table: what maps to the truck, the engine, the maintenance schedule, the hauling, the owner, and the mechanic in the case of a human worker with legal rest rights. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. In particular, how does it handle the idea that a worker has a perspective and an interest in their own time, while a machine's "interest" is just assigned by its owner? Does the analogy implicitly import a Protestant-work-ethic picture where rest is justified only as an instrumental good for future work? And once the disanalogies are on the table, what happens to his conclusion that leisure has to earn its keep through productivity? Is there a better analogy that doesn't silently build in the answer, or does every analogy in this debate end up taking sides on what leisure is for?
