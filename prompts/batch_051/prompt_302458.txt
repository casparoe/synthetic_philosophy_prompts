You are a philosopher of biology teaching an upper-level undergraduate course in philosophy of science. I need you to write one 75-minute essay question for a midterm, worth 20 marks, on the tautology objection to natural selection and the propensity interpretation of fitness. The propensity interpretation, as I am using the term here, says that an organism's (or trait's) fitness is a probabilistic disposition to survive and reproduce in a specified environment, not its actual reproductive output.

Begin by brainstorming the specific confusions this material produces: conflating fitness with actual reproductive success, thinking a probabilistic claim cannot be empirical, sliding from criticism of the slogan survival of the fittest to the conclusion that the theory of natural selection is empty, and so on. Then design the question so that a student could not score well merely by reproducing a summary; the question must force them to do the philosophical work themselves.

The question you write must require three moves. First, a formulation of the tautology objection precise enough to show which view of fitness would make the objection succeed. Second, a statement of the propensity interpretation, with an analogy or line of argument showing how it is meant to make fitness claims empirically meaningful. Third, an assessment of how much this actually achieves: whether the objection can be recast against the propensity view, which readings of it collapse, and which survive in altered form. A strong answer will engage at least one further difficulty, such as the problem of measuring an unobservable propensity from actual outcomes, the single-trial objection, or the worry that the objection returns as a claim about the circularity of tests of natural selection.

Deliverables:

1. The exam question itself.
2. A model answer in full paragraphs at the level of a strong upper-level undergraduate.
3. A marking scheme organised around five must-hit points, totalling exactly 20 marks. For each point, give the marks it is worth and a one-line note on the specific philosophical error that typically costs students that point; these notes must diagnose the misunderstanding, not offer generic exam advice.
4. A two- or three-sentence rationale stating what this question tests that a memorised summary could not.

Keep the assessment self-contained: do not assume students have a particular textbook, and do not include verbatim quotations from any primary source; express every idea in your own words.
