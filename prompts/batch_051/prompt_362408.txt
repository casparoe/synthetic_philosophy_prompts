I'm a backend engineer, not a philosophy person, but I've ended up in a long argument about the World Happiness Report because at work we basically live and die by dashboards and KPI's, and I keep saying the ranking is just another metric that will get gamed once it's a target. We got stuck on a concrete what-if and I'd rather have a careful prediction than the usual "all metrics are bad" line.

Suppose that in 2026 a mid-sized, fairly rich parliamentary democracy — call it Valmont, 25 million people, decent welfare state, mediocre scores on perceived corruption — passes a law creating a Ministry of National Happiness. Its only legal mandate is to move Valmont from roughly 20th into the top 10 of the World Happiness Report within 15 years. The law requires every major cabinet proposal to include a "happiness impact statement" based on the same Cantril ladder survey the report uses, and it gives the ministry a modest budget but no veto power over other departments; it also forbids the ministry from cutting healthcare, education, or the safety net to stop it from gaming the ranking through tradeoffs.

Walk me through what actually happens over the next 25 years. I want the mechanisms and incentives made explicit: which levers does the ministry pull first (the six factors the report publishes — GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, perceived corruption — plus survey administration, sampling, timing, public messaging, and anything else), which effects are robust, which are guesses, and what would have to be true for the whole project to backfire instead. Also please seperate what people who seriously study this mostly agree on from what's genuinely contested — for instance, whether the ranking actually measures happiness or wellbeing, and whether a government should try to top it at all. I'm fine with a long answer if it's structured.
