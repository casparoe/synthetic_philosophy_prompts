In a recent seminar on democratic theory, I found myself improvising a distinction that I have since been unable to shake off, and I would like a candid assessment of it before I build anything further on it. I have been calling it the difference between statistical and modal representation. A body statistically represents a public when its composition matches the public's salient characteristics — gender, class, region, occupation — within some reasonable tolerance; it is a property of the body's actual profile. A body modally represents a public, by contrast, when each citizen can say of the people who deliberate and vote: they are there by a procedure that might equally have selected me. It is not a property of the resulting profile but of the selection procedure, viewed ex post from the standpoint of any particular citizen.

My emerging view is that the distinctive legitimating work done by sortition is modal rather than statistical, and — this is the part that troubles me — that the modern habit of stratifying citizens' assemblies, with quotas for gender and other categories and purposive attempts to capture particular life experiences, is not a natural extension of the lottery's logic but a quiet substitution of a statistical ideal for a modal one, and it may even weaken the very feature that made the lottery attractive in the first place. Does this distinction mark a real difference, or have I merely relabeled the familiar contrast between descriptive representation and the ex ante fairness of a lottery? If it is real, does it carry the normative weight I want it to carry, and where precisely does it overreach or break down?
