my daughter got diagnosed with a peanut allergy last year and we carry an epipen everywhere now. a philosophy friend of mine made a joke about how we've "turned her allergy into a mask case," and instead of laughing I went down a rabbit hole. the more I read about finks and masks the more I think the whole distinction is doing less work than people claim. i wrote up some notes for a short essay and I'd love comments. I'm not a professional philosopher so leave aside the bibliography stuff, i just want to know if the argument is any good.

working title: The EpiPen Problem: masks, finks, and where the bearer stops

1. Standard set-up: a mask stops a disposition from manifesting while the disposition stays in place. Fragile glass packed in foam is still fragile, even though it won't break if dropped while packed. A fink removes the disposition when the stimulus condition arrives: a sorcerer stands ready to make the glass shatterproof the moment it's struck, so it wouldn't break if struck, but we still want to call it fragile? Roughly.

2. My claim is that the phrase "stays in place" in the definition of a mask is carrying a hidden load. To decide if a factor masks a disposition or is just part of the bearer, you already need a boundary between the system and its surroundings. The glass case feels easy because the foam is obviously outside the glass. Biological cases aren't easy. If someone takes a beta blocker and stops having rage episodes, is the drug a mask for their irascibility, or is the medicated person just no longer disposed to rage? Same with an insulin pump: at some point do we say the person-with-pump simply has different dispositions, rather than the old one hanging around masked?

3. Speaking of my daughter: I realize the EpiPen might not even be a mask, since it doesn't stop the reaction from starting, it reverses it after onset. Maybe that's its own category, a "counteractor." Should I flag that and move on, or is it a problem for the broader point?

4. If my boundary claim is right, then mask and fink aren't two deep kinds of dispositionality. They're downstream of a prior, often practical, decision about where the system boundary goes. The conditional analysis tried to handle this by gesturing at "normal conditions," and the mask/fink literature sort of smuggles in a normal body or a normal environment.

5. The part I can't figure out: why are our intuitions so confident in the toy cases and so shaky in the medical ones? I suspect it's because in the toy cases the mask is spatially external and temporary, while medical interventions are functionally integrated and permanent. But spatial externality can't be doing the conceptual work—a pacemaker is inside the body and isn't a mere mask for the heart's failure to pace itself, or is it?

6. Upshot: dispositional talk like "is she still allergic" or "he still has that temper, it's just the meds talking" is doing boundary work. We settle the boundary by deciding what counts as the person and what counts as treatment, and the metaphysics doesn't decide it for us.

I'd welcome brutal comments, specifically: (i) does point 2 actually land, or is "system boundary" just relocating the vagueness I'm accusing everyone else of; (ii) is the EpiPen complication in 3 right, and should I keep it; (iii) am I strawmanning the standard conditional analysis, and would someone in the dispositions literature find this old news; (iv) does the pacemaker thought in 5 work or is it muddled. Honestly if the whole thing is a known point, tell me, I just want to know before I sink more time into it.
