I'm a PhD student in philosophy of biology, and next month I'm giving a paper at a small workshop on mathematical explanation in the life sciences. The paper defends a deliberately asymmetrical version of the explanatory indispensability argument. My line is: the periodical cicada case is a genuine mathematical explanation, but the honeycomb case is not. The difference isn't a quirk about bees — it's that in the cicada case the mathematics does contrastive modal work: it explains why the life cycle is 13 or 17 years rather than any of the nearby alternatives, where no purely physical or selection-based story pins down a unique answer. In the honeycomb case, by contrast, a physical optimality story (minimizing wax for a given cell volume under the bees' construction constraints) does the explanatory work, and the hexagonal tiling theorem just certifies an optimum the physics already delivers. Then I take the further step of claiming that even this one carefully narrowed mathematical explanation forces a modest realism about the natural numbers — though not about sets or the rest of mathematics.
