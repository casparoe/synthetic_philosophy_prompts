In a course I am teaching on personal identity, we have been working through Locke's account in An Essay concerning Human Understanding, Book II, chapter xxvii, "Of Identity and Diversity." As I read him, Locke's view is that a later person is the same person as an earlier one just insofar as the later consciousness can reach back and appropriate the earlier person's thoughts and actions as its own; personal identity extends no farther than consciousness can extend. A colleague and I fell into a long disagreement about how to apply this criterion to a case we invented, and I would like you to adjudicate it. Here is the case. A woman, Ana, in her early thirties, is diagnosed with a degenerative condition that will gradually destroy her episodic memory. She enrolls in an experimental program in which, each week, memories that are about to be lost are encoded into a neural implant and then deleted from her brain. The transfer is seamless: immediately afterward, she can call up the encoded episodes just as she could before, and she reports that accessing them feels like ordinary remembering rather than like reading a transcript, even though she knows the memory is being delivered by the device. After fifteen years, none of her original biological episodic memories survive in her brain; every memory she has of her life before the implant is stored externally and accessed on demand. Meanwhile, her skills, language, temperament, relationships, and values persist with the usual continuity. Is Ana at the end of this process the same person as the Ana who began it, on Locke's criterion? Work through the criterion piece by piece. In particular, say whether a consciousness that can reach back only through an external device is still "one consciousness" in Locke's sense, or whether the case reveals that Locke's criterion needs to be amended or supplemented. Please answer in American English and keep your response to no more than 600 words.
