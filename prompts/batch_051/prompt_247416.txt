In a seminar on the moral uses of biography, we have been reading Plutarch's treatment of the lives of statesmen alongside late antique and Byzantine saints' lives, and a standing puzzle is how these texts could be prized as moral instruction when their authors were evidently not bothered by factual inaccuracy in the modern sense. When someone pressed this reliability question, a classmate settled the matter, to his own satisfaction, with the proverb that example is better than precept. His move was roughly this: a life teaches virtue by showing it embodied in a whole person rather than by stating rules; its work is to produce imitation, not reportage; so asking whether every episode actually occurred is to mistake the kind of thing the text is. I doubt one sentence can carry that much weight, and I want to know exactly what it would have to mean.

Disambiguate the proverb 'example is better than precept' into the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, and lay them out as a menu. Separate, at least, readings that treat it as an empirical claim about what is pedagogically or motivationally effective, as an epistemological claim about what kind of knowledge moral knowledge is, as a claim about the order of justification in ethics (whether rules inherit their authority from exemplary lives rather than the reverse), and as a claim about the function of biography or hagiography as a genre. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false. Then identify which reading or readings the classmate's dismissal actually needs, and say whether any defensible reading of the proverb would genuinely license setting aside the factual-reliability question, or whether the move trades on an unnoticed slide from an ethical priority of exemplars to a literary license for the exemplary life to be freely invented.
