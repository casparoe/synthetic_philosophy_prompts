So in my AI alignment reading group we've been going around and around on "should an AI reason as a utilitarian or a deontologist" for months, and I want someone to diagnose the disagreement rather than relitigate it. We all roughly agree a paperclipper is bad and a CEV-ish system is fine, and nobody is confused about expected utility or about the difference between a hard constraint and a giant penalty term. The utilitarian side keeps saying deontological rules just are lexicographic utility weights, so deontology collapses, and the deontologist side keeps saying the utilitarian framing can't distinguish following a rule because it's right from violating it whenever the expected value gets high enough, and around we go. My hypothesis is that part of this is verbal: "reasoning as a moral theory" could mean the theory lives in the object-level decision procedure or in the meta-level training/maintenance story (like a constitutional constraint the model treats as un-cancellable), and those are different claims. But I suspect theres also a substantive disagreement about whether corrigibility actually requires treating at least some constraints as non-negotiable in a way that can't be represented as a tradeoff. Does that diagnosis hold up, what kind of disagreement is this actually, and what would count as progress?
