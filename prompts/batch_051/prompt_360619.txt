I recently joined the board of a family foundation that has always funded local direct-service organizations — after-school programs, a food bank, a free clinic — and I am about to propose that we move roughly a third of our annual grantmaking into an effective-altruism-style portfolio: global health and development interventions chosen primarily by impartial cost-effectiveness estimates, with a smaller allocation for harder-to-measure structural work. I plan to argue that our obligation runs to the people we can actually help, whether or not they are near or visible to us, and that explicit cost-effectiveness reasoning is the least bad way to honor that obligation. The other trustees are not hostile to evidence, but they are steeped in community philanthropy, and two of them have already raised the familiar criticisms of effective altruism: that it neglects political and structural causes in favor of measurable symptoms, that its metrics quietly redefine impact as whatever is easiest to count, and that earning to give rationalizes complicity in harmful institutions. Please prepare me for the question period. I need the objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased in the words these trustees would use; for each, what I should concede, what I should hold, and a concise reply in their vocabulary; which point in my own case is the softest and must be defended rather than left unmentioned; and the one question I am quietly hoping no one raises. Throughout, separate what is genuinely settled among people who have studied these debates from what remains contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
