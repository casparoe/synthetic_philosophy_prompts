I teach an upper-level undergraduate unit on historical moral judgment, and I would like a classification instrument — preferably a decision tree, but a "which position do you actually hold?" quiz with a scoring key is also acceptable — that sorts readers by how they respond to concrete cases involving Thomas Jefferson, Winston Churchill, and Christopher Columbus, rather than by which label they prefer. The instrument should focus on the "man of his time" defence: the claim that it is unfair to condemn past figures by present standards because their conduct was normal or widespread in their own day.

Please build the instrument from the logical space first, and only then locate named positions within it. Choose branch questions that are concrete case judgments or basic claims on which the positions genuinely come apart, and phrase every node so an undergraduate can answer it without already knowing the names of the positions or any metaethical vocabulary. Where possible, ask about the standard, widely known charges rather than abstract principles: Jefferson's slaveholding set against the Declaration's language of universal liberty; Churchill's stated racial hierarchy and imperial record set against his wartime leadership; and Columbus's treatment of the Indigenous people he encountered. Each of the three figures should appear at least once, and no question should depend on obscure biographical detail.

The tree might branch on questions such as: whether moral standards apply across time or are indexed to their context; whether the prevalence of a practice among contemporaries excuses it, merely explains it, or fails even to explain it; whether the relevant comparison class is the agent's whole society or the best-informed moral opinion then available; whether wrongdoing in one domain can be offset by achievements in another; whether blame attaches to whole lives or only to specific acts and omissions; and whether the question is about the person's blameworthiness or about how we should commemorate them.

The leaves must include at least these positions: presentism; a naive historicism that treats "man of his time" as blanket exculpation; a contextualism that explains without excusing; a qualified universalism grounded in the idea of moral progress; an aspect-sensitive view that condemns the act while withholding a verdict on the whole life; a "best available lights" view that judges against what the agent could reasonably have known, including dissenting contemporaries; and a skeptical view that regards cross-temporal moral judgment as systematically unreliable. After working out the combinations of answers the branches allow, note any combinations that no defended position occupies, and say briefly whether each is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely underexplored. Flag any pairs of positions the tree cannot really separate — for instance, if contextual explanation slides too easily into exculpation once applied case by case — and state where the instrument's resolution fails. Aim for no more than ten branch nodes and eight to ten leaves, with one sentence under each leaf naming its most awkward commitment.
