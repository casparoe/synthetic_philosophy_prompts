In my school's ethics class we have been debating whether moral status is binary or comes in degrees, all the way from insects down to thermostats. I am a high school student preparing a paper on this, and I want to work through a concrete counterfactual in detail before I take a position.

Suppose that starting next year a validated instrument — call it a sentience index — becomes cheap and widely available. For any physical system it returns a continuous score from 0 to 1 that measures the degree to which the system has a morally relevant subjective point of view: the capacity to feel pain, form preferences, and have experiences that can go better or worse for it. The instrument is replicated by independent laboratories and is accepted as evidence in courts. In clear cases its scores match ordinary judgment: an adult human scores near 1, a dog roughly 0.6, a mouse roughly 0.4, a bee roughly 0.05, a plant below 0.01, and a thermostat or a rock effectively 0. But in disputed cases it contradicts common intuition: it scores a lobster (0.08) above a human fetus at mid-gestation (0.06), scores a housefly above a starfish, and assigns some artificial systems, including large language models of the kind available today, a score only slightly below a honeybee's.

Predict, with your reasoning visible at each step, how the following areas look thirty years later: animal welfare and food law; abortion, embryo research, and neonatal policy; liability for damaging or destroying low-scoring systems; and the treatment of artificial systems that score above zero. For each prediction, distinguish what follows robustly from the existence of the instrument from what depends on further assumptions about how people actually use the number — for example, whether voters and judges treat the score as continuously demanding or collapse it into rough bands, treating everything below some threshold as of no concern. What would need to be true about human psychology and institutions for your least confident predictions to be wrong?
