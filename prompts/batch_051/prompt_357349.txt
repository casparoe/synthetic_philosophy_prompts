Build me a two-envelope case to the following spec. Envelopes come in pairs (N, 2N), one envelope gets N and the other gets 2N, and you pick yours at random. There should be a well-defined prior over which pair was placed in front of you: the probabilities of the possible pairs sum to 1, so no flat prior over all positive integers or anything like that. But I want it to be true that, for every amount you could actually see when you open your envelope, the conditional expected value of the other envelope is strictly bigger than the amount you saw. So always switching is right by ordinary conditional expectation, not by the usual bad argumant that treats the other envelope as equally likely to be half or double. This is for a write-up about why the two-envelope paradox isn't just a story about improper priors, so don't sneak one in. Then do the check: write down the prior and show it sums to 1, compute the conditional expectation of switching for at least two observed amounts including the smallest possible one, and explain why this doesn't contradict the symmetry fact that before you open anything the two envelopes are indistinguishable. Finally tell me what feature of your case makes it work (my hunch is that it forces the unconditional expected amount to be infinite, but check me on that), and what happens to the always-switch conclusion if you cap the amounts at some large finite maximum.
