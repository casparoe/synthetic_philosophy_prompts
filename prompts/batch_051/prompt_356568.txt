I keep going back to this passage from Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles", because it seems to me to show that deductive inference has an infinite regress built into it:

"'If A and B and C are true, Z must be true,' the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. 'That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?'"

The way I understand Carroll, the Tortoise is showing that a conclusion never actually follows from any finite bunch of premises, since the rule connecting the premises to the conclusion can always be demoted to just one more premise, and then the same move can be made against the new rule, endlessly. So inference never bottoms out in something that licences the move; it just bottoms out in us giving up and agreeing to infer. My question is: if that's right, why do logicians still talk about being forced to a conclusion, and why is modus ponens treated as something we're rationally obliged to obey rather than a convention we've adopted because its useful?
