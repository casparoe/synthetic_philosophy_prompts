I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I keep seeing the same argument about whether a finite mind could ever run out of new expriences, and it never seems to move. One side says: the brain is finite (finitely many neurons, finitely many possible states, bounded precision), so there's a finite cap on the number of distinct phenomenological episodes you could ever have; an immortal creature with a finite brain eventually has to repeat itself, and the only question is whether it loops or just wanders around a finite state space. The other side says that's confusing implementation with experience. Experience, they say, isn't an output of counting states; there's the continuum of color and sound, so in principle infinitely many shades and qualities, and also combinatorial structure — finitely many primitives can yield unboundedly many gestalts, like sentences from a finite vocabulary. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is really about how to individuate an experience: whether two barely different experiences (a slightly brighter gray, say) count as distinct, and whether "distinct" should be tracked by discriminability, by what-it's-like, or by neural state identity. But I don't trust my own diagnosis, since both sides seem to agree on the basic facts. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is: is there a hidden empirical question about neural grain, a formal question about whether finite primitives can generate infinitely many composed states, or are both sides just using "experience" for different things? And if you're right, what would actually count as progress — a theorem, an experiment, a distinction, or giving up the question?
