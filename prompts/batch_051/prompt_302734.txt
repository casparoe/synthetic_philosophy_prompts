A friend and I recently argued well into the night about a variant of the Twin Earth case, and neither of us could work out whether the standard externalist conclusion survives the modification. I would like the variant analysed from scratch rather than answered by appeal to familiarity with the original.

Start from the canonical set-up. Earth and Twin Earth are identical to ordinary inspection, except that the clear liquid in the rivers, lakes and taps on Earth is H2O, whereas the corresponding liquid on Twin Earth is XYZ: a different compound with the same superficial appearance, taste and function. Speakers on both planets call the local liquid 'water', no ordinary speaker can tell the two substances apart, and before chemistry nobody could. The usual externalist lesson is that Earthlings' word 'water' refers to H2O and Twin Earthlings' word refers to XYZ, even though the two populations are internally indistinguishable; meaning, on this view, does not supervene on what is inside the individual's head.

Now change one thing: remove the causal isolation. Suppose the two planets establish regular transport before either develops chemistry, and over several generations the two populations fuse into a single community scattered across both worlds. They speak one language with one word, 'water', for the clear drinkable liquid found in the rivers, lakes and taps of whichever planet a speaker currently occupies. People move back and forth, intermarry, and spend portions of their lives on both planets; they keep no records sufficient to say which liquid they encountered on any past occasion. At any moment, a speaker cannot tell from the inside whether the glass in front of her contains H2O or XYZ. Assume the word was already in use on Earth before contact, and was simply applied to the Twin Earth liquid once settlement began, nobody noticing the difference.

What does the word 'water' refer to in this fused community — H2O, XYZ, the disjunction H2O-or-XYZ, or something less tidy? Is the correct answer the same for an Earth-born speaker who has in fact drunk only H2O her whole life, a Twin-Earth-born speaker who has in fact drunk only XYZ, and a speaker who has shuttled between the planets and drunk both? Does it matter whether we evaluate a particular utterance made on Earth as against one made on Twin Earth? Would the answer differ if the word had been coined only after contact, for both liquids at once, rather than having been carried from Earth? And when chemistry is eventually discovered and the community learns that two substances have been travelling under one name, does the reference of their earlier utterances change retroactively, or does the word simply split?

Finally, say which strand of semantic externalism — the one that ties reference to the hidden nature of the stuff in the speaker's environment, or the one that ties it to the community's linguistic practice and deference — handles the fused case more cleanly, and locate the exact point at which the other strand begins to look ad hoc.

Please answer in British English.
