I'm a novelist, and the book I'm drafting has a theologian character who's chewing on an argument someone put to her, and I want to get the theology right enough that the argument feels live rather than straw-manned. Here it is, roughly as she'd lay it out:

(1) If God exists, God is absolutely simple: no parts, no composition, no real distinction between God and his attributes.

(2) So God is identical with his existence, his essence, and each of his perfections — omniscience, omnipotence, goodness, and the rest.

(3) But an attribute or a perfection is a property, and a property is a kind of abstract object.

(4) Abstract objects are causally inert: they can't create worlds, answer prayers, command, or love.

(5) Therefore the God who creates, answers, commands, and loves — the living God of classical theism — turns out to be an inert abstraction, which is just a polite way of saying he's not God at all.

She feels the conclusion can't be right, because the same tradition that asserts (1) and (2) also insists that God acts; it would be very odd if the tradition's central doctrine secretly entailed that there is no such God. But she also can't find the exit. If she gives up (1) or (2), she's no longer a classical theist. If she gives up (3), she needs an account of what an attribute is if it isn't a property. If she gives up (4), she has to explain how an abstract object could do anything. She most wants to keep (1) and (2) — they're the heart of the character's formation — but she can't see how to keep them and avoid the conclusion.

I'd like a committed answer, not a survey of the options: which step should she reject, what exactly is wrong with it (or why isn't it wrong), and what does rejecting it commit her to? And could you write it in British English? My editor is in London and I need the terminology to line up.
