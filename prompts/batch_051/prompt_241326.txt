I'm building a session for a reading group on the philosophy of measurement and large-scale histories, and our last meeting stalled on a single question: when we call a whole civilization "more advanced" than another, what exactly are we measuring? The discussion kept returning to Nikolai Kardashev's familiar ladder, on which Type I commands the energy of a planet, Type II the energy of a star, and Type III the energy of a galaxy, as well as John Barrow's less familiar inversion of it, which ranks civilizations by how small a structure they can deliberately manipulate, descending from metre-scale engineering toward the atomic and subatomic. To those two we added a pair of candidates of our own: an information-processing yardstick, on which advancement tracks how much information a civilization can store, transmit, and transform, and how sophisticated the computations it can run; and a resilience yardstick, on which advancement is not peak power or miniaturization but the capacity to persist, self-repair, and absorb shocks over long timescales.

Here is what I would like you to do. Rank these four yardsticks — total energy command, fine-grained manipulation, information processing, and resilience — by how well they capture what we should centrally mean by calling a civilization more advanced. Then commit further: if a defensible notion of advancement had to be expressed as a weighted sum of these four components, what percentage would you assign to each, summing to 100? The part I most want you to address is the trade-offs rather than the list itself: which of the four would most mislead if used as the sole criterion, and whether at least one of them deserves nonzero weight even if it performs poorly on its own. Please take a definite position and defend the exact ranking and the exact weights, flagging where you think your own weighting is most contestable.
