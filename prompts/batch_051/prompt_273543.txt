Question: Below is a passage from Garrett Hardin's "Lifeboat Ethics: The Case Against Helping the Poor" (1974):

"Suppose we decide to preserve our small safety factor and admit no more to the lifeboat. Our survival is then possible although we shall have to be constantly on guard against boarding parties. While this last solution clearly offers the only means of our survival, it is morally abhorrent to many people. Some say they feel guilty about their good luck. My reply is simple: 'Get out and yield your place to others.' This may solve the problem of the guilt-ridden person's conscience, but it does not change the ethics of the lifeboat. The needy person to whom the guilt-ridden person yields his place will not himself feel guilty about his good luck. If he did, he would not climb aboard. The net result of conscience-stricken people giving up their unjustly held seats is the elimination of that sort of conscience from the lifeboat."

Explain the exact argument of the final sentence, and assess what work it does for Hardin's overall case for the lifeboat policy. Distinguish any descriptive claims from any normative claims in the passage.

Student answer: The passage answers the objection that the no-admissions policy is selfish even if necessary. Hardin separates the personal question — what a guilty passenger should do with a seat — from the policy question — how the boat should be governed. Giving up a seat only transfers it to someone who does not share the qualm, so the policy problem remains. The final sentence is a selection argument: passengers who act on guilt leave the boat, while those who remain or enter lack that guilt, so guilt is progressively eliminated from the boat. Hardin's underlying point is that the objection is self-defeating, since the only people who take it fully seriously are precisely those whose exit removes it. This also shows that the guilt is irrational: a trait that systematically removes itself from a population cannot be tracking a genuine moral requirement, so the no-admissions policy is justified.
