For a graduate seminar on epistemic logic and bounded rationality, write a decision tree that sorts a reader into a position on the problem of logical omniscience — the fact that standard possible-worlds models of knowledge make an agent know every logical truth, every logical consequence of what she knows, and all logically equivalent sentences to the same extent. The tree should function as a diagnostic instrument: it should sort people by their answers to concrete cases, not by which label they came in preferring.

The tree's purpose is to sort views about how to repair or replace the standard possible-worlds model, not views about ordinary-language knowledge attributions. The leaves must be these eight positions, though you may flag any that split further:

- the classical idealization (closure is kept as a regulative ideal or an acceptable idealization);
- impossible-worlds semantics (the worlds themselves are allowed to be logically non-classical, so closure fails at the lowest level);
- awareness semantics (the underlying information states remain classical, but an awareness function filters what the agent explicitly knows);
- an explicit/implicit two-component view (a classical, closed store of implicit knowledge is paired with an explicit store that is not closed);
- an algorithmic or bounded-inference model (knowledge is a syntactic store plus inference rules that must actually be applied);
- an evidence- or justification-based account (knowledge is relativized to pieces of evidence or justifications that do not combine automatically);
- a relevant-logic or paraconsistent background account (the background logic is weakened so the offending consequences are not valid);
- an unawareness account (lacking a concept is represented as a distinct kind of state rather than ordinary uncertainty).

Build the tree from the logical space, not from the list of names. First choose five or six yes/no diagnostic questions on which these positions genuinely come apart, and say which combination of answers each listed position corresponds to. Then, before presenting the tree itself, list the combinations of answers that no listed position occupies, and say for each whether you think it is empty because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or simply underexplored.

Every branch node must be a concrete case or claim, phrased so that someone can answer it without already knowing the position names. The following cases must appear as branch nodes, though you may add others:

- An agent has written P on one slip and 'if P then Q' on another, but has never put the two together. Does she know Q?
- An agent has never entertained the sentence 'P or not P.' Does she nevertheless know it?
- An agent believes P and believes not-P, and has never noticed the conflict. Should the model be allowed to represent her as believing both at once?
- An agent lacks the concept of a collateralized debt obligation. Should the model treat her as uncertain about CDO-propositions, or as being in some other kind of state?
- If an agent knows P but, when asked whether she knows P, cannot produce an answer, may the model still count her as knowing that she knows P?

Each leaf should give the position's name, a one-sentence statement of its core idea, and a one-sentence statement of the commitment its defenders find most embarrassing or costly. End by flagging any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate — in particular, say whether awareness semantics and the explicit/implicit two-component view come apart on your diagnostic questions or only in their implementation. Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words.
