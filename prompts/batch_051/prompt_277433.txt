A friend has been pressing me with a reconstruction of Galen Strawson's Basic Argument from his 1994 paper "The Impossibility of Moral Responsibility," and I cannot find the step that overreaches. The core claim he keeps invoking, paraphrased, is that to be truly morally responsible for what you do you would have to be ultimately responsible for the way you are, including the mental states that produce the action, but nothing can be ultimately responsible for the way it is, because that would require being causa sui. His numbered version goes like this:

(1) Ultimate moral responsibility for an action requires ultimate responsibility for the mental states that produce the action.
(2) Ultimate responsibility for those mental states requires ultimate responsibility for the earlier states that produced them, and so on backward.
(3) The regress ends only in the agent's unchosen initial constitution, for which the agent cannot be responsible.
(4) Since no point in the regress supplies ultimate self-creation, no agent is ultimately morally responsible for any action.
(5) Ordinary praise and blame routinely presuppose such ultimate responsibility, so that practice is systematically in error.

The twist that bothers me: if this argument is valid for moral responsibility, it seems equally valid for epistemic responsibility. Whether you check a source carefully or carelessly is also a function of cognitive dispositions you did not choose, tracing back to your initial constitution. So careful inquirers and reckless ones are no more epistemically praiseworthy or blameworthy than the moral agent is. But rejecting that conclusion seems to gut ordinary epistemic criticism, such as saying someone should have checked the source. I want to know which step is actually doing the work and what rejecting it costs. In particular, can one resist the extension to epistemic responsibility without giving up the argument against moral responsibility, or does Strawson's argument force both?

Please give exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Distinguish what is established from what is contested among philosophers working on this, and do not present a contested claim as the consensus.
