I'm a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, and a debate I keep circling back to with my reading group concerns the semantics and pragmatics of slurs. The picture I've absorbed from the formal side is that a slur and its neutral counterpart are truth-conditionally equivalent: both pick out the same group, and the slur differs only in some non-at-issue dimension, such as a conventional implicature or an expressive attitude. But reclaimed in-group uses seem to behave nothing like that. When a member of the targeted group uses the slur among other group members, the same word can function as a marker of solidarity or even affection; when an out-group speaker produces that word in an otherwise identical sentence, it counts as a serious violation, and no visible sign that the speaker "meant the reclaimed sense" seems to repair it. That asymmetry has pushed me toward the conclusion that the slur must simply be ambiguous between two full meanings, one derogatory and one affiliative, and that the interesting question is which contextual mechanism selects between the two readings.

You are a philosopher of language who works on slurs, expressives, and the distinction between at-issue and not-at-issue content. Think carefully about the argument I've just sketched rather than answering the question as posed. If the ambiguity diagnosis is right, tell me what determines the reading in a given case, and whether an out-group speaker could ever deliberately trigger the affiliative reading. If it is wrong, tell me precisely where the inference from "truth-conditionally equivalent" to "social effect should be uniform across speakers" breaks down, and what the standard analyses of derogatory force would say about reclaimed uses instead.
