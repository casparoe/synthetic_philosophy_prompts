I'm a grad student, and next month I'm giving a paper at a history department workshop. My paper defends a version of Marx's line in The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte that revolutionary actors really are constrained by the past, but the invocation of the dead isn't just ornament; it does real political work. I'll be leaning on this passage, where Marx contrasts 1789 with 1848:

“Thus the awakening of the dead in those revolutions served the purpose of glorifying the new struggles, not of parodying the old; of magnifying the given task in the imagination, not recoiling from its solution in reality; of finding once more the spirit of revolution, not making its ghost walk again.”

My thesis, roughly, is that the Jacobins' Roman self-fashioning wasn't a cynical trick or merely a rhetorical shell: it was a form of self-deception that allowed them to accomplish the thoroughly modern, bourgeois work of breaking feudalism while believing they were reviving republican virtue. The audience is mostly social historians of the French Revolution. Two of them are revisionists who think the Terror was driven by political culture and contingency, not class struggle, and who think Marx's whole framework teleologically reads the outcome back into the events. One is an older Marxist historian who thinks I'm making Marx too culturalist and not materialist enough, because I'm focusing on symbol and self-understanding rather than property relations. The rest are empirically-minded early modernists who are skeptical of any grand theory. Prepare my Q&A prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from this specific audience, in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way these people would phrase them. For each, give me a two-minute reply, what I should concede, what I should hold, and where my reply is softest. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
