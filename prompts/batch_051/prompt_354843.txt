You are a philosopher of mind who works on phenomenological accounts of skilled action and has enough clinical familiarity with neurorehabilitation to know what an outpatient therapist can realistically observe in a session. I am an occupational therapist working mostly with stroke survivors and new upper-limb prosthesis users. I see patients once or twice a week for 30–45 minutes, on a fixed course of sessions, and I have to justify my approach in documentation that insurers and referring physicians will review, so I cannot afford to try every instructional style and see what happens.

In my practice I keep hitting a fork. For some patients, breaking a movement into components and having them attend to each part — elbow angle, wrist position, sequence — improves performance. For others, the more they focus on the body parts doing the moving, the more the movement falls apart; they do better when I set a concrete goal, such as grasping the glass or finding the curb with a cane, and let the movement organize itself. A colleague told me this divide maps onto Merleau-Ponty's distinction between the lived body and the body treated as an object, and onto his account of motor intentionality: the body's pre-reflective, goal-directed grip on the world, which explicit attention can disrupt rather than support. I have not read the primary texts and could not hand them to patients.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure for choosing how to instruct a given patient on a given task: the questions to ask in order, what observations or quick test movements should count as evidence for each answer, where the procedure instructs me not to decompose a task, where it instructs me to decompose and later fade the explicit cues, and where it must hand the decision to clinical judgment because the phenomenology does not settle it. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a stroke patient whose reach is functional but slow and disorganized; (2) a new prosthetic user who says the device feels like an instrument held by the hand rather than part of the arm; (3) a patient who has plateaued under implicit, task-based training and is asking me to explain exactly what to do. Make at least one of the applications produce an answer I will find uncomfortable. For each step, say how much follows from the theory itself, how much is your own extrapolation, and where a reasonable colleague could do the opposite. If the request rests on a confused reading of Merleau-Ponty, or if this distinction cannot be operationalized in a clinic this way, tell me that plainly rather than forcing a procedure. Start by brainstorming candidate criteria before settling on the final version, and be explicit about what the procedure cannot do.
