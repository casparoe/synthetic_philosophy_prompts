I produce and edit a weekly interview segment for a small local public-radio station. I have no formal background in philosophy or linguistics; I got here through community journalism, and the habit I'm describing is just something I've been thinking about on my own for years. I've become convinced that most of the real damage in an interview happens before anyone says anything flatly false. A question can smuggle in an unstated assumption — "What did you tell staff about the cost overruns before the vote?" quietly treats the cost overruns as established fact — and if I don't notice, the listener absorbs it as background. I've started to think of a conversation as keeping a running score of what everyone is now treating as settled: every time an assumption goes unchallenged, it enters that shared record, and the interviewee is playing the same game back at me. An official will answer, "The delay was necessary to protect public safety," and only later do I notice that the station has effectively handed listeners the claim that the delay was necessary, simply because nobody on air flagged it.

I want a reusable procedure, not one-off advice: a set of tests to run, in order, while I prep for a fifteen-minute interview and then again quickly while cutting the segment down. It should take about fifteen minutes in prep and five in editing, should be workable by someone who has never read any theory and will be holding a notecard, and should tell me how to list the quiet assumptions carried by my planned questions and by an interviewee's answers, then sort each one into one of three moves: leave it in place because both sides genuinely share it; challenge it on air; or flag it for the listener with a short corrective like "that's the developer's account, not established fact." I don't want it simplified so far that it misses the hard cases — when a shared assumption is itself what's in dispute, when challenging one loaded question would derail the interview I came to do, when an assumption is carried not by words but by what a question or answer omits. The stakes are fairness, accuracy, the trust listeners put in the station, and defamation exposure, and I answer to a managing editor. So mark plainly which steps are judgment calls rather than mechanical rules, be honest about what the procedure can't do, and say where it will push me toward an answer I won't like.

To test whether it actually works, apply the procedure to three cases from this line of work: (1) I'm about to ask a city councilor, "What did you tell staff about the cost overruns before the vote?"; (2) after an interview, a school board candidate's answer includes, "The real problem is that these curriculum changes were rushed through without parent input," and I'm deciding whether to keep that sentence as-is in the final clip; and (3) my own prepared question reads, "How will you reassure residents who are worried the new shelter will make the neighborhood unsafe?" Please write the answer in American English.
