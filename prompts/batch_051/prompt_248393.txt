In *The Will to Believe*, William James argues that when a person confronts what he calls a genuine option—one that is living, forced, and momentous—and the option cannot by its nature be settled on intellectual grounds, it is not only permissible but necessary for the person’s passional nature to decide it. I have adopted a version of this as a standing rule in my own life: when I face an important practical choice in a close relationship where the evidence underdetermines both alternatives and refusing to choose is itself effectively a choice, I let my hopes and affections settle which belief I act on rather than remaining in suspense. Last month I applied this rule after an ambiguous breach of confidence by a close friend. The evidence could support either distrust or continued trust, and suspending judgment was not practically available because it would have slowly dismantled the friendship anyway. I chose to believe her account. Is my rule defensible, or have I turned James’s argument into a license for wishful thinking? How would I need to restrict the rule for it to survive the Clifford-style objection that belief without sufficient evidence is always wrong?
