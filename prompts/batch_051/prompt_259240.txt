I sit on a seven-member panel that may waive an otherwise-applicable limitation period when the purposes of a time bar would not be served. The panel treats its own decisions as precedents, and today we are expected to decide in a way that coheres with them. Five of our prior decisions matter.

In the first case, a worker filed a claim thirty years after workplace exposure to a solvent. The disease was diagnosed six years before filing, and the causal link between the solvent and the disease became established only four years before filing. Exposure logs and safety memoranda survived, as did several coworkers. The employer had been notified as soon as the diagnosis was made and had maintained reserves for possible claims. We allowed the claim.

In the second, a claimant filed a defamation claim thirteen years after publication. He knew of the statement from the day it appeared and offered no reason for the delay beyond reluctance to sue. The reporter had died, and the audio archive had been destroyed under a retention schedule. We refused to waive the bar.

In the third, a woman filed nineteen years after a childhood assault, four years after reaching adulthood. Two eyewitnesses and the medical records survived, and the defendant had received notice of the injury at the time it occurred. We allowed the claim.

In the fourth, a business owner filed twenty-seven years after a forged deed. She discovered the forgery six years before filing. The counterparty had repeatedly promised to make the matter right and had signed a written acknowledgment six years before the filing. The original deed and the relevant bank records were intact. We allowed the claim.

In the fifth, a company filed a breach-of-contract claim seven years after the breach. It had known of the breach from the first day and had delayed in order to use the threat of litigation in negotiations. A key witness had died, and the remaining witness conceded that his memory had faded. We refused to waive the bar.

Today's application is this. A patient underwent surgery seventeen years ago, and a surgical sponge was left in her abdomen. For fifteen years she had intermittent pain; five years ago a physician told her it was probably adhesions and not to worry. Two years ago a CT scan found the sponge, and she filed promptly. The sponge and the hospital records make the negligence conclusive; three members of the surgical team have since died, but liability does not depend on their recollection. The hospital merged into a larger system six years ago, closed the old surgical practice, and archived the records. It had no notice of any claim until two years ago, and it argues that it reasonably ordered its affairs on the assumption that this episode was behind it. The claimant argues that she could not have sued any sooner and that the evidence here is stronger than in some cases we have allowed.

The panel is divided. I have been asked to draft the opinion. What should we decide if we want to be consistent with our earlier rulings? Identify which of our prior decisions is the closest analog. Set out the strongest rival reading of our precedents, and state which principle we would be adopting under each reading if the case is close. Finally, describe a future case that would settle whether that principle is really our rule.
