I have adopted a policy toward large language models that I want assessed. I never treat a factual assertion produced by an LLM as testimony: I do not update my beliefs on its say-so, I treat its truth-value as irrelevant until the claim is checked against a source I independently trust, and I decline to engage with arguments whose only support is that a model produced them. The situation that made me formalise the policy: a colleague cited a model's summary of a technical paper to settle a dispute, and I refused to treat that as evidence until they consulted the paper itself. My rationale is Frankfurt's distinction between lying and bullshit. The liar knows the truth and deliberately misrepresents it, whereas the bullshitter simply lacks any concern for truth; since a language model cannot care whether its outputs are true, its claims are bullshit, and the proper stance toward bullshit is to refuse to treat it as truth-apt for one's own reasoning. My colleague objects that this overapplies the distinction: much model output is neither lying nor bullshit but ordinary false output from a next-token predictor that is no agent at all, and sensible users may treat some outputs as assertoric working drafts. Is my policy defensible? Does the classification of model output as lying, bullshit, or mere error make any practical difference to how I should treat it, and if not, what rule should replace my policy?
