Next month I am presenting to the acquisitions committee of a contemporary art museum, arguing that the museum should acquire an abstract strategy game about managing a failing irrigation network under drought conditions. My position is that the game's aesthetic value resides chiefly in the quality of agency it designs — the way its rules shape deliberation under uncertainty and force choices among competing losses — rather than in its spare visuals or minimal narrative. My planned argument is that the work rewards sustained attention only through its decision-structure, that a rule-system can itself be the primary object of aesthetic appreciation, and that the fact every playthrough differs should count as a feature of the work rather than a defect. The committee includes painting and video curators who grant that a game can contain beautiful or moving parts but doubt a set of rules can be an object of aesthetic attention; a new-media curator who treats interactivity as merely a delivery mechanism for authored image and story; and the director, who is wary of technical obsolescence and of collecting something realized differently each time it is played. Prepare me for the discussion period: the objections these people would actually raise, phrased the way they would put them, ordered by how likely and how damaging each is, with what I should concede, what I should hold, a reply I can give in about two minutes, and a frank note on where that reply is weakest. Include the challenge I should most hope does not get asked. At the end, say how confident you are in your overall assessment and single out the point you are least sure of.
