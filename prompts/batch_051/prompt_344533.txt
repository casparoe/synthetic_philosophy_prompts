In my graduate seminar on the interpretations of probability, a student offered the following argument, and Ive been turning it over since. It is aimed at anyone who thinks probabilities are objective, but it ends up saying something about the whole frequentism-versus-propensity-versus-subjectivism debate.

(1) A probability statement has determinate content only relative to a chance setup or a reference class: "the probability of heads is one half" says nothing until you fix the tossing procedure, and "the probability this patient responds is 0.6" says nothing until you fix the class of patients you are treating her as belonging to.
(2) The setup or reference class is not simply read off the world; specifying it requires a judgment about which features of the situation are relevant.
(3) Judgments of relevance are neither true nor false, because different choices pick out different but equally real setups or classes of cases.
(4) If the content of a statement depends on a judgment that is neither true nor false, the statement has no fully objective content.
(5) Therefore no probability statement has fully objective content.
(6) Therefore the dispute among frequentism, propensity, and subjectivism is at bottom a dispute about how we classify, not about anything in the world.

The conclusion struck me as too strong, but my attempts to block it keep collapsing into either the claim that nature itself privileges some classifications over others, or a merely pragmatic dig at step (3). I cant get further than that. What I need is an exact diagnosis. Is the argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails, and what is the correct version of it? If it is valid through (5), does (6) actually follow? Please answer in British English.
