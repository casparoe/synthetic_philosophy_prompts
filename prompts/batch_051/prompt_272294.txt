I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, so I come at this from the design side and may have the argument wrong; correct me where I do. The reading I keep returning to is the case for sortition — legislative bodies filled by random selection from the citizenry rather than by election — built on a diagnosis of career politicians. As I understand it, the argument runs like this. Elections do not select for the ability to govern; they select for the ability to campaign, raise money, and remain visible, so they produce a class of career politicians whose real constituency becomes donors, party machinery, and the next election rather than the decision in front of them. Term limits look like the intuitive patch but merely rotate the same type of person through the same seat, leaving the selection mechanism untouched. Sortition is proposed as the structural fix: a random sample is descriptively representative, hard to lobby across a career, and supplies ordinary judgment on problems that experts and incumbents routinely frame too narrowly.

That argument was built when random selection implied putting a few hundred citizens in a room, giving them time and expert testimony, and letting them deliberate face to face. A development in the last fifteen years or so seems to change that assumption. Digital deliberation platforms such as vTaiwan, Pol.is, and Decidim run open consultation at very large scale, with clustering and consensus tools to aggregate contributions; anyone can participate and no one has to be selected. At the same time, organizers have begun convening randomly selected online mini-publics, which removes much of the cost that used to make sortition logistically heavy.

Does the classic argument for sortition survive this, need repair, or collapse? Superficially the development cuts against it: if the whole population can be consulted at low cost, selecting a random sample stops looking necessary. But I can see the opposite case as well. An unselected online crowd is self-selected along precisely the dimensions that matter, and it spawns its own career participants — power users, moderators, serial posters, people good at being visible in a thread — which looks like the same pathology the argument diagnosed in electoral politics, reappearing in a new medium. I want a careful mapping of which premise the development actually bears on, which features of the argument are unscathed, and what weaker but still valuable claim could be restated if the strong form dies.
