Last night a friend and I spent hours arguing about whether the race to build frontier AI should be analyzed with the same machinery political scientists use to explain why wars occur. He thought the comparison was a category mistake: wars are fought between states with armies, borders, and declared enemies, while the AI race is mostly firms and a few state-backed programs competing over benchmarks, talent, and compute. I think the structural analogy is more serious than he granted. The rationalist puzzle Fearon and others start from is that war is costly, so there should always be some ex ante bargain both sides would prefer to fighting, and yet wars happen anyway. The AI race has a parallel shape: nearly everyone involved would prefer a world in which frontier systems were developed more slowly and safety problems were solved before deployment, but they race because falling behind looks intolerable. The three standard rationalist mechanisms also seem to map over. Labs and national programs hold private information about their capabilities, timelines, and safety practices, and they have incentives to misrepresent that information to attract investment, reassure the public, or deter rivals. The key players cannot credibly commit to slowing down or sharing the benefits of a lead, so a kind of preventive logic sets in. And the ultimate prize, if there is one, may be effectively indivisible: influence over the most capable system cannot be divided among competing powers the way territory or money can.

What I cannot decide is whether this is a genuine transfer of structure or a loose analogy that breaks exactly where it matters. My friend kept returning to the disanalogies: the harms of unsafe racing are diffuse, speculative, and partly in the future rather than immediate battlefield costs; the actors are often corporations rather than sovereign states; the competition is economic and technical rather than violent; and there may be positive-sum ways to share the benefits of safe systems that have no wartime equivalent. So I would like someone to adjudicate the parallel as precisely as possible. Please lay out the mapping explicitly, identify the load-bearing joints where the two cases must match for the rationalist explanations of war to carry over, and then assess whether each of the three mechanisms transfers intact, transfers only partially, or fails. Also name at least one thing the analogy seems to predict that is probably false, and say what that false prediction reveals about where the analogy gives out. Please write the answer in American English, and do not include a bibliography or reading list.
