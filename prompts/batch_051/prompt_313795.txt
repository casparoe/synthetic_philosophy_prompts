I would be grateful for a consistency audit of a set of views I find myself holding in decision theory, particularly about ambiguity. I have not worked out how they interact, and I would rather be shown where the strains are than given a survey of options.

(i) I accept the Sure-Thing Principle and the Savage framework: a rational agent's preferences over acts can be represented as expected utility maximization relative to a single, finitely additive subjective probability and a cardinal utility function.
(ii) I accept statewise dominance: if one act yields an outcome at least as good in every state and strictly better in at least one state, it should be preferred.
(iii) In the Ellsberg single-urn problem — 30 red balls and 60 balls that are black or yellow in unknown proportion — I strictly prefer a bet on red to a bet on black, and strictly prefer a bet on black-or-yellow to a bet on red-or-yellow, all at the same stakes.
(iv) I regard those Ellsberg preferences as reasonable: ambiguity aversion is a defensible response to unknown proportions, not a bias to be corrected.
(v) I hold that every payoff-relevant event in a well-posed decision problem has a sharp, real-valued subjective probability, and that these probabilities are revealed by betting preferences.
(vi) I hold that costless information about a payoff-relevant event can never make a rational agent less willing to choose after receiving it, even when it does not expand the available acts.

Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, and which only appear to be? Please rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say which commitment or commitments would have to be revised, and what the least costly revision would be.
