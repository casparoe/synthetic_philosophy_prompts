I'm putting in for a one-semester research leave, and the dean's office wants a two-page proposal; its due next week. The paper I keep half-starting is on the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis. My half-formed idea is that the usual debate treats "what kind of claim is the thesis?" as one question, when it should split it into at least two: (a) what a precise version of the thesis actually commits you to, and (b) what could in principle count as evidence against that version. I also want to distinguish two ways of being "empirical" that keep getting run together: the thesis as a lawlike claim about what can be computed by any physical process, and the thesis as something we accept partly on the historical failure of attempts to find a procedure that outruns a Turing machine. My suspicion is that some versions are definitional but still revisable, and some are empirical but not falsifiable by a single clever algorithm; and that the interesting action is in how the formal and physical versions constrain each other. A good paper should also say how to keep the target stable, because in arguments about whether some exotic physical setup could compute something uncomputable, the thesis often seems to get silently redefined until no counterexample can touch it.

So: turn that into a tight proposal. Give me a precise statement of the question, a quick account of why the usual positions (roughly, "it's a definition" versus "it's an empirical hypothesis") leave something out, the approach I'd actually take month by month across the leave, what the possible conclusions are and which considerations would push toward each, and an honest account of what would count as the project failing. No bibliography—I'll add citations myself, so it's fine to name positions and arguments but don't spend time on citation details. And when you reply, start with the proposal itself: no preface, no "here is your proposal."
