At a political-theory seminar I sat in on, two participants got stuck in an argument neither would concede. The first argued that a ward-level official who directs public jobs, permits, and emergency relief toward voters in her own area in exchange for their electoral loyalty is corrupt: she is converting impartial public goods into particularized patronage and keeping her dependents dependent. The second replied that, in a setting where formal institutions have long failed to deliver anything to that area, these favors are not a distortion of a functioning system but the only channel of distribution that actually works; judging them against an external ideal of bureaucratic neutrality imposes a foreign standard on a society organized around reciprocal gift-and-favor obligations. The first came back that the practice is still wrong from the inside: supporters are made dependent, rivals are excluded, and “that is just how things work here” is the argument every local strongman uses. The second answered that a charge of corruption ought to be tied to identifiable harms, and that the relevant comparison is clientelist networks versus official neutrality that leaves the poorest with no access at all; in that comparison, dismissing the local favor economy as corrupt is itself a kind of condescension. Who is right about which specific points, and what are both parties missing? Adjudicate the claims as actually made, distinguish what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on the ethics and politics of corruption, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
