I've been in a reading group that's slowly going through the Shōbōgenzō, and we just spent an evening on the Uji fascicle without getting much futher than realizing how little the English word "time" does for us. I'm not a Buddhist, but I read a lot of the usual rationalist-blog stuff about timeless decision theory, A- and B-series, and whether the passage of time is an illusion, so my first instinct was to slot Dōgen into one of those boxes. The thing that broke that instinct was the firewood passage: it's apperently not firewood at one time and ash at a later one, with the firewood doing the becoming; firewood-time is wholly firewood and ash-time is wholly ash, and neither goes back or carries over. Time isn't a medium things pass through; each dharma-position is itself time, and a self isn't a spectator standing outside it watching the moments file past. I keep trying to get this into a slogan and it keeps sliding back out, which is why I want a poem.

Write a sonnet about Dōgen's being-time (uji), with the octave building the naive picture — firewood turning to ash, spring sliding into summer, a persisting self that has a past and future while time flows by — and the volta as the refusal of that picture: not a transition between states but complete positions, nothing resumed, nothing available twice. The sestet should work out what this does to the idea of a self that owns its memories as a past, and the closing couplet should state what being-time amounts to: you are not inside time, you are time. On the form side, I want the poem to stop doing what the idea denies. Let the octave have a regular rhyme scheme that sets up an expectation of recurrence, and after the volta let the rhymes go slant or break or refuse to echo the octave, so the sound itself stops returning. It should be accurate enough that someone who has actually read the fascicle would wince less than nod, and good enough that someone who hasn't would want to go read it.
