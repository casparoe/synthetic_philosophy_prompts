I keep coming back to something like Lewis's Principal Principle, even though I couldn't defend every formulation of it: if you know the objective chance of an event, your rational credence should just equal that chance. It's the tidiest thing in the philosophy of probability. Chance is the one expert your never allowed to second-guess, and it gives a definite number whenever you have a chance theory to hand. I like that it stops me from overriding the physics whenever I feel lucky: a fair die is 1/6 no matter what I tell myself on the drive over.

But there's a case that won't leave me alone. Take a genuinely chancy setup—say a nucleus with an objective 50% chance of decaying in the next hour according to the right physical theory. Now suppose I own a gadget that has called, in advance, 90% of the last few hundred such decays correctly. It's telling me this nucleus will decay. My instinct is that my credence should now be around 0.9, not 0.5. The chance hasn't changed; the principle, read strictly, seems to say the gadget's track record is irrelevant information I should refuse to be moved by. That feels like willfully ignoring evidence. But if I let the gadget move me off the chance, have I abandoned the idea that objective chance has any special authority, or only added an admissibility patch that I can widen until the principle is empty? I don't want a verdict for one side. I want to know what each option costs: what would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what would it take for the principle to be the thing that goes, and whether there's a reading—chance as the ur-prior, admissible evidence, or something else—on which both survive.
