I belong to a small intentional community that has lived under the same written covenant since 1983. The community was founded by people who believed that needing less is a kind of freedom, and the clause in question has never been amended. It reads: "Each member may keep for his or her own use only what is needful for bodily health, for the day's work, and for plain recreation. All else shall be held in common." For as long as anyone remembers, this has been applied to furniture, tools, clothing, books, food, and the like, and disputes have usually turned on whether a physical object was truly needful.

The present dispute concerns my digital possessions. I pay a monthly fee for a music service that gives me nearly unlimited listening and for storage of several thousand family photographs; I also keep several hundred e-books, which I can read but not sell, and a small balance in digital currency as savings against illness. None of this takes up space in the house, and the community could not use any of it even if I handed it over, since the photographs are private, the licenses are tied to my account, and the currency is nothing but an access key. Some members say I am plainly in breach, because the music and e-books are not needful for health, work, or plain recreation in any frugal sense, and they draw on my money and attention every month. Others say the covenant cannot reach these things at all, because I do not "keep" them in the way the founders meant: they are not objects that could be held in common, but only permissions, files, and entries in a ledger.

My interest is less in winning the dispute than in understanding whether the ideal behind the covenant still draws a usable line. Take the music subscription, the photographs, the e-books, and the digital savings one by one, and work out what the plain words, the purpose of the clause, and a reasoned guess at what the founders would have said if asked each imply about them. Then say which of these approaches should govern, and whether a community built on the conviction that needing less is freedom can draw a coherent boundary between what a member genuinely keeps and what is held in common once property has become intangible.
