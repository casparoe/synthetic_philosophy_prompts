Here's what the student wrote:

The superiority theory (Hobbes) says laughter comes from a sudden feeling of being above someone else in respect of their weakness or misfortune. Here we laugh because the waiter basically admits his restaurant is bad, so we feel above him and above anyone who actually eats there. Incongruity theory says humour is the perception of a mismatch between what we expect and what actually happens. The setup makes us expect the waiter to be confused, offended, or apologetic, and the punchline replaces that with a knowing agreement, so it's a textbook incongruity. Relief theory, which began with Freud, says jokes discharge psychic energy that would otherwise go into keeping forbidden impulses — mostly hostile or sexual ones — repressed. Here the customer's annoyance at bad service is normally something he'd have to suppress, so the joke lets us vent that aggression without consequences, and that release is the laugh. Benign violation theory (McGraw and Warren) says something is funny when it's a violation of the way we think the world should be, while also being appraised as safe or harmless. Here the violation is the rude, impossible order, and it's benign because it's only a verbal scenario and nobody really suffers. In my view incongruity does all the real work: the funniness just is the expectation being reversed. Superiority is implausible because nobody is actually harmed, relief is a story about built-up tension that this joke obviously lacks, and benign violation is just incongruity with the word benign tacked on to cover the awkward cases.
