I am a PhD student working on the boundary between grammatical and lexical morphology, and a friend in my program has walked me into the following argument, which now feels like an argument I cannot find my way out of. It seems to show that the English plural is derivational, and from there that the whole inflection–derivation distinction collapses along with the notion of a morphological paradigm. I would like to know where the argument goes wrong, if it does, and what it would cost me to reject the offending step.

Here is the argument as he presents it.

(1) A morphological operation counts as inflectional only in virtue of being semantically regular and compositional: the meaning of an output word form should be recoverable from the meaning of the base plus the morphosyntactic feature the operation realizes.

(2) The English plural does not meet this condition. Alongside the compositional cases, such as cats meaning more than one cat, there are plural word forms whose meanings are not recoverable from the base plus a plural feature: arms can mean weapons, waters can mean a body or sheet of water, papers can mean documents or identity credentials, and customs can mean the machinery of border control.

(3) By (1), then, the English plural is not inflectional; it is derivational.

(4) But the plural is supposed to be a central case of inflection. It is paradigmatically organized, fully productive in form for count nouns, available to new coinages, and it enters into subject–verb agreement: we say the cats are, not the cats is.

(5) If a central, paradigm instance of inflection fails the best criterion we have for being inflectional, then the criterion does not track the distinction; and if the semantic criterion fails while the other familiar criteria are themselves hedged with counterexamples, then no criterion draws a principled line.

(6) Therefore there is no principled inflection–derivation distinction. A plural such as cats is no more a form of the lexeme cat than a derivational formation such as cat-like or catliness is.

(7) But that conclusion cannot be right. If cats is a different lexeme from cat in the way cat-like is, then morphological paradigms, declensions and conjugations, are not real objects of linguistic theory, and agreement is not agreement with a single noun lexeme. That seems to throw out the core of inflectional morphology.

I would most like to keep (4) and (7); I am less attached to the criteria as stated than to the existence of a real distinction. Please tell me which step to reject, explain why it looked fine, and price the alternatives. I am not asking for citations; I want the reasoning worked through in continuous prose, as in a letter, with no headings, bullets, bold text, or tables.
