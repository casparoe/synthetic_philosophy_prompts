I'm in an early Christian ethics class and we just did Augustine on lying, and I came out more confused than before. Everyone — the prof, the textbook, the secondary lit — describes him as the hard-line absolutist: lying is never allowed, not even to save someone's life. Fine. But then in the treatise itself (De mendacio, I think) he lays out eight kinds of lies and ranks them from the most serious, like lying about the faith, down to small lies that help somebody and harm no one, and he clearly thinks some are much worse than others. Thats the part I don't get. If the prohibition is really absolute, why would he bother with a whole ranking of seriousness? Once you've said 'this lie is much worse than that lie,' haven't you already admitted that the lesser one is at most a venial sin — the kind of thing you'd rather avoid but which isn't ruled out in every case? It feels like the ranking only makes sense if Augustine actually tolerates the minor lies in practice, and the 'never lie, not even to save a life' thing is a later simplification. So which is it: is Augustine quietly a moderate about the minor lies, or is there a reason an absolutist would still care about ranking them? And if the ranking is real, what work is it doing for him?
