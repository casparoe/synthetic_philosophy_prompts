So I'm teaching du Châtelet's *Discours sur le bonheur* in my early modern seminar again, and a student asked me something I haven't been able to stop thinking about sense. Her whole line, as I read her, is that happiness isn't tranquility: it's desiring things and being carried away, and the people who tell you to weed out your passions and make yourself independent of fortune are selling you a pretty thin substitute for happiness. She carves out one exception, though—study, which she thinks is the passion you can cultivate without making your happiness hostage to other people, and she's explicit that this matters more for women, who in her world were barred from most of the other routes to glory and pleasure.

So here's what I want your view on. Suppose she came back and got shown the modern mood-tracking app industry: apps that ping you four times a day to rate your affect on a one-to-ten scale, plot your happiness as a time series, and then push "evidence-based" micro-interventions—gratitude prompts, cognitive reappraisal reminders, that sort of thing. The premise underneath it all is that happiness is mostly a measurement problem: we misremember how we actually felt, so the fix is better data, and once you can see the numbers you can manage them. What would she make of this? Would she read it as a version of the self-sufficient study she endorsed, or as an attempt to turn the passions from something you throw yourself into into something you administer from the outside, or something else? Try to be specific about where she'd object, where she might be half-sympathetic, and whether she'd think the quantified-self picture of happiness is even aimed at the same thing she was talking about.
