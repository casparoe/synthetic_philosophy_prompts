I'm trying to merge two things we read in my philosophy of science class: Goodman's grue puzzle and Solomonoff induction. In the original puzzle, all observed emeralds have been green, and two hypotheses fit the evidence: all emeralds are green, or all emeralds are grue (green up to some future time, blue afterward). Green seems simpler, but what counts as simple seems to depend on which predicates we treat as basic. Solomonoff induction is often advertised as a way to make Occam's razor precise: fix a universal Turing machine U, assign each prefix-free program p prior probability 2^{-length(p)}, and predict by a Bayesian mixture over all programs. Kolmogorov complexity is the length of the shortest program that emits a given finite or infinite sequence.

Now vary the puzzle. Instead of emeralds, the evidence is a binary sequence observed one bit at a time: n zeros in a row. Consider two infinite continuations that both fit what has been observed. H1: the sequence is all zeros forever. H2: the sequence is exactly n zeros and then ones forever. H1 has a very short program. H2 has a program that stores the number n as compactly as possible, prints n zeros in a loop, and then prints ones forever. On Solomonoff's rule, how does the posterior probability of H2 relative to H1 behave as n grows? Give the rough form of the ratio (constant, polynomial in 1/n, exponential in 1/n, or something else) and say what it depends on in U.

Then change one more thing. Suppose U is chosen by a hostile party who wants H2 to win for a specific observed length n, subject only to U being a universal Turing machine. Can such a U always make H2 at least as probable a posteriori as H1 for that n? Can it make H2 beat H1 for every n beyond some threshold, or is the advantage bought only at the price of constants that grow with the observed n? I am not sure whether the answer is a clean yes for each finite n but only with growing constants, or a flat no.

Work through the analogy: which hypothesis is the binary version of grue and which of green, and does measuring simplicity by program length dissolve Goodman's problem or relocate it into the choice of universal machine? I want not just a formula but a statement of what the Solomonoff solution actually buys you in a version of grue where the grue-like alternative must pay explicitly for n.
