I keep flip-flopping on a parallel I noticed, and I can't tell whether I'm spotting a real structure or just pattern-matching on the word 'examples'.

Plutarch's method is to write a life, not a history: he says a little thing like a quip, a gesture, or a passing remark can reveal character better than a whole campaign, and he picks his anecdotes accordingly. Hagiography pushes this further — the saint's life is deliberately edited, some episodes kept, some polished, some left out — so the text can work as a model for the reader. The reader isn't handed a rule like 'be humble'; they're given a curated set of concrete lives and left to acquire the virtue through them.

That is, as far as I can see, structurally almost identical to imitation learning. Instead of specifying a reward function or a policy directly, you give a learner a dataset of expert demonstrations and let it infer the behaviour. And imitation learning has the same two species that moral biography seems to. Behaviour cloning just copies the demonstrated actions, rather like imitating the outward details of a saint's life — the asceticisms, the formulas — and hoping the inner character comes along for free. Inverse reinforcement learning tries to infer the expert's underlying reward function first and then optimise it in new circumstances, which looks, maybe, like what you're actually doing when you read a saint's life for its principles rather than for a script to copy.

So my question: is this identity of structure deep, or is 'learning from examples' doing all the work and everything else comes apart? I'd like the mapping drawn precisely, and then I'd like you to be brutal about where it fails. Does anything from the ML side — distribution shift, the way behaviour-cloning errors compound, reward hacking when the inferred reward is slightly wrong, demonstrations that are only locally optimal — actually transfer to the old debate about whether reading lives can make someone good? And in the other direction: is there something in the Plutarch/hagiography side, like the fact that a life is retroactively narrated rather than a logged trajectory, that shows the analogy is already smuggling in conclusions it hasn't earned? If the parallel is real, name at least one thing it predicts about moral learning that turns out false; if it's superficial, say what the nearest genuine structural similarity would be instead.
