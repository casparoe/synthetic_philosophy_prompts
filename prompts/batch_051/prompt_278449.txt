You are a professional philosopher with expertise in both medieval Islamic philosophy and contemporary philosophy of mind. Please think carefully, and before you commit to a verdict, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming the distinct ways the challenge below could land.

Avicenna's "flying man" (sometimes called the "floating man") thought experiment asks us to imagine a person created all at once, fully formed and of sound intelligence, but suspended in empty air with his limbs spread apart so they do not touch one another, his eyes veiled, and no information reaching him from outside or from his own body—no sight, sound, touch, pressure, or sense of position. Avicenna claims this person would nevertheless be immediately aware of his own existence, and would not affirm that he is any limb, organ, or part of his body. The argument is standardly read as showing that the soul's awareness of itself does not depend on the senses, and on stronger readings that the self or soul is really distinct from the body. Scholars disagree about how much Avicenna meant to establish; keep that disagreement in view.

Here is the development. Over the last several decades, embodied and enactive approaches to cognition, together with the neuroscience of self-consciousness and research on interoception, have assembled a rival picture. On this picture, even minimal self-awareness—the basic pre-reflective sense of being a subject, located in a body and distinct from the world—is generated and continuously maintained by the integration of bodily signals: heartbeat, respiration, visceral state, touch, balance, and the sense of one's own movements. When these processes are experimentally disrupted, people's sense of what and where they are can shift; and a creature that had never received any such signals might never develop a self to be aware of at all. If this picture is right in its essentials, Avicenna's imagined person may not be a clean case of self-awareness in the absence of bodily experience, but a case whose coherence the argument silently presupposes.

Your task is to say whether Avicenna's argument survives this development, requires repair, or is dead. Do the following:

Pick out, as precisely as you can, which premise or step of the argument the development bears on—is it the thought experiment's background assumptions about how the self comes to be, the step from "he is aware of himself without sensory awareness of the body" to "self-awareness is non-sensory," or the further step to a real distinction between self and body?

Weigh the strongest reply available to Avicenna's side: that the thought experiment is a first-person conceivability argument rather than an empirical account of development or neural maintenance, and that the content of self-awareness need not include the body even where its causes are bodily. Say exactly how much of the argument that reply can hold up.

Consider whether a repaired version remains: for instance, that self-awareness is non-sensory in its content even if bodily in its causes, or that the self is the subject of bodily processes rather than a pure substance. Say whether that version still deserves the name "Avicennian," and whether it collapses into the embodied view or stays distinct.

Then give a clear verdict: does the argument survive intact, survive only in modified form, or not survive?

End by telling me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would most plausibly move you to a different verdict, and where in your own reasoning you think failure is most likely—for example, in how you interpret Avicenna's intended conclusion, how much weight you grant the empirical literature, or how you draw the line between causal dependence and conceptual independence.
