Two people in my group had a long argument the other day about a language model we've all been using for a side project, and I ended up genuinley unable to call it. The model writes out explanations for its answers and routinely says things like "I believe that..." or "My best guess is..." Mara said that at this point we should just take the intentional stance toward it: if the way you predict and make sense of a system's outputs is to attribute beliefs, goals, and a rough kind of reasoning to it, and that stance keeps proving reliable, then the system literally has beliefs. For her, nothing more is needed -- demanding some extra inner fact behind the pattern is demanding more than we have for other people. Dev said that move is far too cheap. A chess engine's moves are also best predicted by saying "it wants to control the center", but nobody concludes Stockfish has beliefs. The model was trained to produce statistically likely text; the "beliefs" are our projection onto a sequence predictor, not something in the system.

Then it got deeper. Dev pointed out that if you ask the model the same question with slightly different wording, it sometimes gives a different answer, and if you challenge it, it walks itself in a circle. A real belief, he said, has to be a reasonably stable state that survives paraphrase and gets used across contexts. Mara answered that humans flunk that test constantly -- you can flip a person's answer with framing effects or ask them something they've never considered and watch them wobble -- so if stability is the test, ordinary people don't have beliefs either. Dev came back: humans have a whole life of acting on their beliefs; the model only emits tokens while the prompt lasts, and it doesn't update across conversations. Mara said that no one expects a belief to show itself without some triggering context, and the model's behavior is exactly the kind of robust, content-shaped pattern the stance tracks; refusing to call it belief just sneaks a private inner glow back in.

Dev's last point was that maps also carry content -- a map of Chicago encodes where the streets are -- but the map doesn't believe anything. Representing that P is not the same as believing that P, and a model that can be made to output "I believe P" by prompting it politely is just doing very fancy map-making. Mara said the map comparison fails because we don't need the intentional stance to predict a map; with the model, the stance is doing real predictive work, and on her view that's the entire criterion there is.

I'd like a point-by-point adjudication of the exchange as it actually happened: which specific moves by Mara and Dev are right, which ones overreach or smuggle in an assumption, what both of them are missing, and who is more right overall.
