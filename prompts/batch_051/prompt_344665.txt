Most governments that do cost-benefit analysis for new regulations discount future benefits at a steady annual rate: at the old 3% rate a life saved 50 years out counts as only about a quarter of a life saved today, and at the 7% sensitivity rate it barely registers at all. Nobody in that agency would say a person born in 2075 is worth less than a person alive now, but the arithmetic seems to say exactly that. So what theory of time and value does the practice actually presuppose, if any? Does discounting future lives force the agency into pure time preference — the view that later wellbeing matters less merely because it comes later — or can all of it be explained by economic growth and the opportunity cost of capital, leaving pure time preference at zero? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, and say where the practice merely fits several different theories rather than requiring one: if an agency set pure time preference to zero but kept the growth-based peice, what would actually change in the numbers and would its decisions look any different? Also, climate analyses often use a much lower rate for damages centuries out than the rate used for a routine road project. Is that a sign of a different implicit view about time, or just diffrent beliefs about growth and risk?
