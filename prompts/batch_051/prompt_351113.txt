In a recent discussion about political leadership, someone invoked Machiavelli's remark comparing fortune to a woman—the passage near the end of The Prince where he suggests that fortune responds better to boldness than to caution. That person used it to argue that a leader who approaches power through tentativeness or consensus-building is making a conceptual error about how luck works: fortune, he claimed, is not a neutral background condition but something that answers to character, so the right stance is aggressive self-assertion. I have read just enough Machiavelli to think this cannot be the whole story, since he also compares fortune to a flood-prone river and insists that success depends on matching one's style to the times. I would like you to disambiguate this slogan. What are the distinct precise claims that the comparison of fortune to a woman, or the more generic thought that fortune favors the bold, could be making? Please separate possible readings concerning whether luck is genuinely random or has a stable, quasi-psychological character; whether audacity is always superior to caution; whether fortune is something to be conquered rather than accommodated; whether the metaphor is doing explanatory work or is merely rhetorical; and any further readings you identify. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false or overextended. Then say which of these readings the person in that discussion actually needed for his argument, and whether The Prince, read together with Machiavelli's concept of virtù, supports that reading.
