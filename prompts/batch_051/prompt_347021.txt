I'm in an odd spot right now because I've been trying to decide whether a general principle I've been defending should be allowed to overturn a very strong intuition about a particular case. This came to a head last week when two people in a discussion group I lurk in got into a fight about a passage from William James, in the preface to Pragmatism. The passage is:

"The philosophy which is so important in each of us is not a technical matter; it is our more or less dumb sense of what life honestly and deeply means. It is only partly got from books; it is our individual way of just seeing and feeling the total push and pressure of the cosmos. I have no right to assume that many of you are students of the cosmos in the class-room sense, yet here I stand desirous of interesting you in a philosophy which to no small extent has to be technically treated."

Dana read it as saying our deepest philosophical commitments are pre-theoretical and felt, so a theory can't simply override a firm intuition. She said: James is saying the system is only partly built from books, and the rest is a "dumb sense" that's prior to argument. So when a clever theory says to abandon an intuition like that, the theory is treating as disposable the very material it is supposed to account for. Lewis said she was reading a descriptive remark as a methodological rule. James is saying where a person's philosophy lives psychologically, not that the felt sense is authoritative. People's felt senses conflict, and a good theory can show that a particular intuition is the outlier. Dana came back that any method for deciding which theory is "good" will itself rely on pre-theoretical judgments, so theory can't be the final court of appeal. Lewis replied that public evidence and deductive consistency aren't just more feelings, and that treating intuitions as fixed fails to explain how science makes people reject powerful intuitive beliefs. They asked me to referee, and I honestly couldn't, partly because it maps onto my own situation. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right about which specific point, not just who wins overall? Also say which argument would most likely change your verdict, or where your reasoning is most fragile.
