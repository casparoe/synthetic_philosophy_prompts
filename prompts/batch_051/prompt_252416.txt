People keep saying that some choices in math are "just arbitrary labels" and other choices "encode real structure," but I've never seen a decent account of where the line is. Give me a working definition of *merely conventional* (or "canonical," or whatever you think the right term is) that gets these cases right:

- calling the two square roots of 2 "a" and "b," then swapping the names;
- deciding which square root of -1 gets to be "i";
- picking a basis for R³ before doing a linear algebra computation;
- choosing local coordinates on a sphere before writing down a geodesic equation;
- labeling the three real roots of x³ − 3x + 1 as r1, r2, r3 in some order, given that the Galois group cycles them and the rational numbers cant tell them apart;
- calling the electron's charge negative instead of the proton's;
- redoing the whole calculation in feet instead of meters.

I'm pretty sure the first and the last are mere bookkeeping, and I'd say the charge one is too. The basis and coordinates ones feel like they matter only relative to a question you aren't asking invariantly. The i vs −i one and the cubic roots one are where I waver: sometimes they seem like exactly the relabeling case, sometimes they seem to smuggle in an orientation or a choice of which root you're "standing on." Propose the definition, run it through all seven, and say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer. Then either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. If some of my cases collapse into each other, tell me.
