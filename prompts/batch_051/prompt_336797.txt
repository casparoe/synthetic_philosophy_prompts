I write historical fiction and sit on the program council of a private foundation that funds public programming in history and policy. We are deciding on a grant proposal for a year-long public program, tentatively titled 'Then and Now,' that pairs three historical episodes with three present-day situations: appeasement at Munich in 1938 beside a current negotiation widely called appeasement; American escalation in Vietnam beside a current debate about incremental intervention; and the Peloponnesian War beside a present great-power rivalry, via the 'Thucydides trap' idea that a rising power and an established power tend toward war. The applicants argue that such analogies already shape public debate, and that the program would make them explicit and subject to scrutiny, teaching audiences to compare cases rather than be carried by comparisons. A substantial minority of the council dissents: for them, a historical analogy is a narrative instrument that looks like evidence but works by plot, selecting a past case that already tips the moral and strategic verdict, and it is only weakly disconfirmable because any mismatch can be absorbed by claiming the analogy holds at a higher level of abstraction. The council has asked me to draft the decision memo to circulate before the vote: approve, reject, or approve with conditions, with any conditions specified concretely enough that the grantees could meet or fail them. I should register one bias of mine in the memo: as a novelist, I trust my sense of narrative fit more than I ought to, and I am unsure whether the proposal appeals to me as pedagogy or as plot.

Please write the memo. It should state a clear recommendation and the reasoning for it, give the strongest version of the losing side its due rather than a caricature, and close with a short section on what would change my mind: which consideration, or what evidence about how the program actually works, would move me to the opposite recommendation, and where my reasoning is most likely to fail.
