I am a PhD student in the philosophy of technology, writing a dissertation on how responsibility should be assigned for harms caused by increasingly autonomous systems. A colleague in law and I have spent the past year arguing past one another about the responsibility-gap problem, and our dispute crystallized around a stock example we both use: an adaptive system—imagine a driving or clinical decision system—causes serious harm after generalizing beyond any behavior its designers explicitly specified or tested. The worry is that no designer, operator, or institution could specifically have anticipated or controlled the harmful action, so none of the usual addressees of blame satisfies the conditions we ordinarily take to license moral responsibility. I have come to regard the situation as structurally novel, one that cannot be absorbed by existing moral and legal machinery without distortion. My colleague regards it as a largely continuous extension of familiar cases—corporate liability, vicarious liability, strict liability for dangerous activities, teams, and other forms of distributed agency—in which our practices already assign answerability and liability without attributing full-blown individual blameworthiness.

We have agreed to write up the disagreement as an adversarial collaboration rather than two opposed papers, and I would like you to draft the joint statement we should produce. It should contain: a numbered section of claims about autonomous systems, control, foreseeability, and responsibility that both camps can sign without reservation; a disagreements table stating, in wording each side would accept as fair to its own view, exactly where the discontinuity camp and the continuity camp part ways; and a set of cruxes—sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one of us—each paired with a concrete case or kind of evidence both sides agree bears on it and a prediction from each side about how that evidence will come out.

Please be more careful than we have been to keep three things apart: backward-looking blameworthiness, forward-looking answerability, and legal liability; conflation of these has kept several of our arguments alive longer than they deserved. I would also ask you to flag any residual disagreement that on your analysis is merely verbal, especially anything turning on different senses of control or responsibility. I suspect the central cruxes will concern whether blameworthiness requires a unified reasons-responsive agent, whether the relevant notion of control is actual, counterfactual, or prospective, and whether the point of responsibility practices is fundamentally retributive or regulatory, but I would defer to your judgment about how to frame them.
