After my daughter asked why there's a street in our town named for a guy who ran a eugenics society in the 1930s, I ended up on the city's Public Commemoration Review Committee. We meet once a month for about two hours in a smallish city of around two hundred thousand people, and we usually get two to four requests per meeting: a statue someone wants removed or moved or kept with a new plaque, a street residents want renamed, a school name, that sort of thing. We're just ordinary people — a librarian, two teachers, a realtor, an engineer — and none of us has any training in this. City council has final say but they mostly rubber-stamp us, unless we look unprincipled, so the bar is basically: be consistent and be able to defend it in a page. Problem is, right now we make it up case by case and the same stuck arguments repeat: keeping something "erases history," taking it down "celebrates bad people," then we vote on vibes. I want something we can reuse every month instead of reinventing the argument each time.

A workshop I went to last spring gave me a distinction that cut through some of this. It's between honorific commemoration, which holds someone up as worthy of admiration (a pedestal statue, a school named to honor someone), and memorializing or marking, which mostly records that something happened or that people are buried here. The idea I took away is that honorific commemoration is an ongoing speech act: while the statue stands, the city is still saying "this person deserves your esteem," whereas a marker can function more like a historical record. The same speaker also argued that a name's meaning can drift, so a street named after someone in 1912 might now be, for most residents, just a street name and not a message about the namesake at all. These distinctions aren't clean, and I don't know how to weight them, but they seem like they could break the deadlock we keep having.

Could you build me a decision proceedure — a checklist or a flowchart in words, with tests to run in order — that uses those distinctions (or better ones you think we should use) and hands us a recommendation: remove, rename, retain, or retain with reinterpretation such as a counter-plaque or new signage? It has to work within our constraints: two hours a month, no budget to hire historians, a one-page written decision for city council, and volunteers who will not read jargon. I want you to flag the steps that look mechanical but are actually judgment calls, and be honest about what the procedure cannot do for us. It should handle hard cases like a scientist whose work was genuinely important but who pushed forced sterilization, a street where nobody remembers who the namesake even was, and a monument a neighborhood strongly wants to keep after the namesake's wrongs are documented. Then run the procedure on two or three of those hard cases, and make sure at least one of your worked examples gives an answer my committee won't like, so I can see whether we would really follow it.
