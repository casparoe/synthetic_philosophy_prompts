I am a graduate student in an ancient history seminar on the problem of when a legendary tradition should count as evidence for a historical core, focused on the Trojan War, the Arthurian material, and the Exodus narrative. One recurring move worries me. When someone observes that there is no contemporary Greek account of a ten-year siege of Ilios, no mention of Arthur in sources close to the period he is supposed to have lived, and no Egyptian administrative record of a mass Israelite departure, the instructor replies that absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, as if this settled the matter and allowed the class to move on with a historical kernel intact. I suspect that sentence lumps together several quite different claims: a platitude about inductive fallibility, a substantive claim about whether missing records should shift our confidence at all, and a further claim about burdens of proof and default positions.

I would like a document I can bring to the seminar that disambiguates the slogan. Lay out the distinct precise principles it could be expressing—for example, the difference between evidence we merely happen not to have and evidence a record-generating process would likely have left; the conditions under which a search that would probably find something makes the absence itself evidence of absence; the difference between suspension of judgment and positive belief in a historical kernel; and the question of who should bear the burden of producing evidence in a debate over a legendary tradition’s historical core. For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, defensible, or false, and then identify which reading or readings the instructor would need for the slogan to actually block the inference from missing records to doubt about historicity, and which readings would not get him there. Be precise enough that the seminar can stop using the sentence as a conversation-ender.
