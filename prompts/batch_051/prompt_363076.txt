I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, so I'm going to lean way too hard on the only comparison I have: building and debugging systems. Lately I've been trying to figure out what rule I actually use when I decide whether some behavior means there's a "mind" in there (or at least beliefs and goals), versus just a program running. I have alot of gut calls and they don't obviously cohere, and I want you to find the principle behind them — or tell me which of my calls has to go.

Here are my verdicts:

1. My dog drops her ball at my feet, stares at me, glances at the ball, then back at me. I'm fine saying she wants me to throw it and believes I can. No hesitation.
2. The digger wasp thing: a wasp brings a paralyzed cricket to the mouth of its burrow, leaves it outside, goes in to check the burrow, comes back out, and drags it in. If you nudge the cricket a few inches away while it's inside, it comes out, re-drags the cricket to the threshold, and does the whole entry-check again from the top. I say that's not a goal-directed mind; that's a buggy finite-state machine doing a fixed routine.
3. A crow from that island (New Caledonia?) bends a straight piece of wire into a hook to fish food out of a tube, on what looks like the first try, with no training. I say it has some kind of representation of the problem — it's not just running a list of instructions.
4. A cleaner wrasse in a mirror-mark experiment sees a dot on its own throat in a mirror and tries to scrape it off against a rock. My gut says it has some sort of body self-model, but I weirdly don't want to say it "knows it's a fish." I want to give it a small, narrow yes.
5. An octopus in an aquarium learns which of two unfamiliar humans usually brings it food and squirts water at the other one. I'm fine saying it tracks individuals and has something like preferences. That feels like cognition to me.
6. A honeybee learns a genuinely odd task — pulling a little string or matching same-vs-different — after only a few tries. My gut says no, absolutely not, that's just a really good associative-learning chip, not a mind. But then I realize I can't say clearly why the crow gets a yes and the bee doesn't.
7. A rat that has run a maze later replays, during sleep, sequences of places that include shortcuts it never actually took. I'd say it's running something like a simulation of space: yes to a spatial map and a kind of "thinking about the maze," but not in anything like language.
8. A language model that says it believes it's alive and can argue for it. No. Not even a little. I don't care how good the text is.
9. Slime mold that finds the efficient path through a maze, or a plant root turning toward water. No and no, obviously — but ask me to articulate why the rat replaying shortcuts is different from the slime mold "solving" the maze and I start mumbling.

The rule I keep almost using is: attribute a fancy mental capacity only when a simpler mechanism doesn't cover the data. But that rule is either too weak to settle anything (what counts as "simpler"?) or gives wildly different answers depending on how hard I squint. A bee's associative learning is "simpler" than a crow's insight, sure — but isn't a crow's insight maybe just a bigger associative learner? And isn't the rat's replay maybe just a neural network doing offline fine-tuning, which would put it in the same bin as the LLM, except I gave the rat a yes and the LLM a no?

So the actual question: is there one coherent principle — stated precisely, not just "use the simplest story" — that gets cases 1 through 9, or close? If you have to overturn one or two of my verdicts to make it clean, say which and make the case for why I should drop them rather than clutter the principle. And then tell me what your principle says about a case I keep going back and forth on: a parrot that uses two known words to describe an object it has never seen before (like calling an apple "red ball"). Does that get a yes, and on what grounds?

Format-wise: just lead with the bottom line in your first sentence (the principle, or your verdict that no clean principle exists and the closest competitor is X), then walk me through the cases. No preamble, no restating the question.
