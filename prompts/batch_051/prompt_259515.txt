I am grading a final examination for a graduate seminar in the philosophy of mind, and I would like a second set of eyes on one answer before I assign a mark. The exam question read: "State Hempel's dilemma as a challenge to the formulation of physicalism—the thesis that everything is physical. Explain why the dilemma arises, and assess whether it is avoided by defining 'physical' in terms of current physics or, alternatively, in terms of an ideal completed physics."

One student wrote the following:

"The dilemma is that a physicalist must say what 'physical' means, and there appear to be only two ways to do it. On the first option, 'physical' refers to the entities and properties recognized by our current physics. The difficulty is that physicalism would then be hostage to a historically dated theory: current physics is incomplete and contains idealizations, so a physicalism defined this way would be committed to excluding anything not yet recognized—for example, the entities a future theory of quantum gravity might posit. On the second option, 'physical' refers to whatever an ideal, completed physics would posit. The difficulty is that we cannot say in advance what such a physics will include, so the thesis that everything is physical becomes vacuous or trivially true, since an ideal physics would describe everything there is. Hempel's point is that neither way of spelling out the thesis succeeds, and therefore physicalism is not formulable in a non-trivial way. This is essentially the explanatory gap under another name: just as we cannot see how conscious experience could be accounted for in physical terms, we cannot say what 'physical' means without either pinning physics to its current stage or emptying the term of content. The best resolution is to define the physical as what current physics says exists, amended as future physics discovers new entities, since this keeps the thesis empirical while allowing for revision."
