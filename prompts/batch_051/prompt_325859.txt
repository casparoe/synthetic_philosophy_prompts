Consider the following argument, which is designed to show that computational irreducibility does not really limit scientific explanation. I suspect it is trading on two different senses of explanation, but I cannot locate the equivocation precisely.

(1) To explain a system's behavior is to derive that behavior from a description shorter than the system's own step-by-step evolution; a derivation at least as long as the evolution is not an explanation but merely a simulation.

(2) A system is computationally irreducible when there is no shorter description of its behavior from which that behavior can be derived: the only way to determine what it will do is to run the evolution itself, or something equivalent to it.

(3) Therefore, for a computationally irreducible system, its complete behavior cannot be explained.

(4) Yet scientists do explain computationally irreducible systems. A physicist can explain why turbulence transfers energy from large eddies to smaller ones, and a computational scientist can explain why certain cellular automata settle into recurring structures, even when neither can derive the complete evolution from a compressed description.

(5) Hence computational irreducibility is not a limit on explanation; it is only a limit on computing exact trajectories by short methods.

Is this argument valid? If it is valid, are the premises true on any single consistent reading? If it is not valid, identify the exact step at which the failure occurs and what shifts or fails between that step and the next. In particular, does step (4) contradict step (3), or does it explain something different from what steps (1) through (3) concern? Give the sense of explanation under which the conclusion of step (5) is true, and state whether it is the same sense that was fixed in step (1).
