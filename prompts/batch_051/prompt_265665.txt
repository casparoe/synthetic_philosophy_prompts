Okay, here’s something I’ve been turning over. In my seminar I keep using Mill’s The Subjection of Women, and there’s a passage in the first chapter where he’s setting up the burden-of-proof rules for any defense of the subordination of women. He says:

The à priori presumption is in favour of freedom and impartiality. It is held that there should be no restraint not required by the general good, and that the law should be no respecter of persons, but should treat all alike, save where dissimilarity of treatment is required by positive reasons, either of justice or of policy.

Now, Mill’s whole point is that this should bite against appeals to “nature” when people use them to justify women’s legal subordination. If you’re the one defending an inequality, you don’t get to just gesture at nature and call it a day; you have to actually produce positive reasons that the dissimilarity of treatment is required. I’m sympathetic to that as a procedural move, but I’m not sure how much work it can actually do.

So here’s what I want you to do. Treat the position as this: appeals to nature in debates over sex inequality are structurally suspect, because they are attempts to justify an unequal arrangement, and therefore under Mill’s rule the burden of proof falls on the person making the appeal. Now tell me what would count against that position. What would have to be true for a natural-difference argument to legitimately override the presumption in favor of freedom and impartiality? Are there specifiable conditions under which the burden should shift back onto the egalitarian? And does Mill’s methodological rule just settle the matter before the naturalist ever gets a substantive hearing, or can the two levels—procedural and substantive—be kept apart?

Don’t just list the standard objections. I want you to rank the defeaters, from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about which ones a committed Millian would actually concede as counting against the position. Also, if you think the position is well insulated against the appeal to nature and that this is itself a problem, say so. And write the whole answer in American English.
