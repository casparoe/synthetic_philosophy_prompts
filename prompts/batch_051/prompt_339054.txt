I chair the ethics and mortality-review committee at a 200-bed community hospital. Each month we review every death that followed a decision to withhold or withdraw treatment, and we must classify each case as a permissible letting die, an impermissible killing, or one we cannot classify and must refer to legal counsel and an outside ethicist. The committee includes physicians, nurses, a respiratory therapist, and two community members, none of whom has formal philosophical training, and each case must be closed in under 30 minutes. Each classification is signed off by the attending physician and by me as chair, and becomes part of a permanent mortality-review record that can be requested by the state licensing board, so the procedure has to make plain which steps were mechanical and which were judgments.

The clinicians currently work with a simple rule: switching off a machine, or never starting a treatment, counts as letting die, while giving a drug or otherwise acting with the goal of hastening death counts as killing. The rule is breaking down under use. Deactivating an implanted heart pump is regarded by the physicians as switching off a machine, yet it is as deliberate a physical act as many things they call killing; and when a clinician simply omits an ordered treatment, the rule compels us to call it letting die even when the omission looks like a deliberate killing by omission.

Please write a decision procedure my committee can actually run, grounded as closely as possible in what the doing/allowing distinction and the surrounding debate about killing and letting die actually establish. The procedure should be an ordered set of questions with criteria for yes, no, and insufficient evidence; it should say where it stops with not classifiable and hands the case to a person; and it should mark each step that is a judgment call dressed up as a test. It should also state, explicitly, where the doing/allowing distinction cannot bear the moral weight the hospital wants to place on it.

Then run the procedure on these three cases:

1. A cardiologist deactivates an implanted ventricular assist device, a pump that takes over much of the heart's work, at the explicit and repeated request of a conscious, competent patient who is not a transplant candidate. The patient dies within minutes. The working rule would classify this as letting die, but the physical act is as direct as an injection.

2. A nurse on a medical floor deliberately omits an entire night's prescribed insulin for a patient with type 1 diabetes because, by her own later account, she believed the patient's life was no longer worth living. The patient dies of diabetic ketoacidosis. The working rule would classify the omission as letting die.

3. A physician escalates a morphine infusion for a patient with end-stage lung disease and intractable air hunger, accepting that the escalating doses may suppress breathing. The patient dies 40 minutes later. The physician states that the sole intent was relief of air hunger, and the chart documents the escalation under the hospital's double-effect policy.

For each case, show the classification the procedure returns, which questions did the decisive work, and where the procedure misfires or hands the decision back to a human being. Flag whatever answer the committee will least want to hear, rather than softening it.
