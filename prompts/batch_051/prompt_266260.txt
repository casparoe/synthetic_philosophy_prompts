I am a university student taking a course in moral philosophy this term, and I also chair the allocations committee of a campus service coalition. The coalition pools roughly forty volunteers' weekly hours and a small semester budget, and each Sunday evening in about forty-five minutes my committee has to decide how to divide those resources among three standing projects: shifts at a campus food pantry, canvassing for a climate divestment campaign, and weekend work at a city animal shelter. Our members genuinely disagree about which project is most important, and the disagreement has become corrosive: some argue that the pantry alone addresses urgent, preventable human suffering; others argue that the climate campaign dominates because its consequences are so much larger over time; others argue that the shelter is where our small group can make the most tractable difference. We currently settle allocations by debate followed by a majority vote, and nearly every semester the losing side reduces its hours and the coalition comes close to splitting. None of us on the committee has a background in philosophy, and whatever we decide has to be set down in public minutes that the project coordinators can read.

In my course we read about the idea, discussed under the name moral trade, that people with incompatible moral views can sometimes make each other better off by their own lights without agreeing on any common scale of value: each side agrees to direct somewhat more effort toward what another side cares about, in exchange for that side doing the same for them, so the arrangement is a Pareto improvement in terms of each side's own values. I suspect this could replace our winner-take-all voting, but I do not know how to run it as a reusable procedure rather than an ad hoc negotiation. Please write a decision procedure my committee could actually follow each week: a checklist of questions or steps in order, with criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, explicit points where the procedure ends in a refusal, and the points where a person must make a value judgment rather than apply a rule. Then run the procedure on two proposals for next week: one that shifts six volunteer-hours from the climate canvass to the shelter in exchange for the shelter volunteers' help with a pantry fundraiser, and one that would drop the climate project entirely on the grounds that it scores poorly on your procedure. Include at least one worked example in which the procedure recommends something I, as chair, would rather not do, and say explicitly what the procedure cannot settle. Please give the whole answer as exactly six numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
