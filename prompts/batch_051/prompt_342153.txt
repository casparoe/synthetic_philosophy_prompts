I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I want a check on a parallel I keep noticing. In decision theory the Ellsberg paradox is standardly read as showing that people prefer known probabilities to unknown ones: if one urn is 50/50 red and black and another has an unknown mix, people tend to prefer betting on red from the known urn and also prefer betting on black from the known urn, a pair of preferences no single subjective probability distribution can represent. At work I see what looks like the same preference. Engineers routinely pick the component with a long, measured failure record, even a mediocre one, over a component whose failure profile is uncharacterized; and they treat a flaky test whose trigger nobody has identified as worse for the project than a failure that occurs at a known, stable rate, even when the flake's observed rate is lower. My question is whether this is genuinely the same structure as Ellsberg-style ambiguity aversion, or whether the resemblance is only the shared word uncertainty. Where do the two cases match and where do they come apart, and what would have to be true of the engineering preference for the decision-theoretic machinery about ambiguity to transfer? Is there a prediction that such a transfer would make that is plainly false in practice?
