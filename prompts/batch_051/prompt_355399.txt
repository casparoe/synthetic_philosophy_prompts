I am a PhD student in the philosophy of law, and a dispute has stalled in our workshop that I suspect is not really about what it appears to be. The question is whether the law's long-standing treatment of corporations as legal persons gives any real support to proposals for legal personhood for AI systems. One side treats corporate personhood as decisive in principle: it shows that legal personality is at bottom a bundle of rights, duties, and liabilities that a legal system can assign to whatever entity it suits, so the only serious questions about AI are questions of design and policy, not of whether an AI really is a person. The other side insists the analogy does no work: a corporation's personality is derivative, a way of organizing the rights and liabilities of the human beings behind it, whereas an AI system would be a direct subject of legal norms, so nothing follows from corporate personhood except that law can create convenient fictions. Both sides accept the same doctrinal history, and neither has moved. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is really a disagreement about what legal personhood is: a term of art whose extension is set by the legal system for its own purposes, or a placeholder for a kind of agency and answerability that the law recognizes rather than invents. I would like a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement rather than a survey of positions: is it verbal or substantive, what would count as evidence to each side, and is there a distinction or test case that could actually move it?
