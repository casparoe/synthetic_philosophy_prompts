I'm a philosophy PhD student, and for the past semester I've been the de facto referee in a running argument between two people in my department about Buridan's ass — the donkey that starves halfway between two identical bales of hay because it has no reason to prefer either one. One of them says the whole puzzle dissolves once you distinguish picking from choosing: a rational agent with exactly tied options may simply pick one, and the ass's starvation is an artifact of the story's not giving the animal any actual psychology; brute selection among tied alternatives isn't a failure of rationality, and complete preferences are just a modeling idealization. The other says that's a dodge: intentional action is done for a reason, and if two options are truly and exactly tied there is no reason that favors one over the other, so 'just picking' is either a hidden appeal to some tie-breaking consideration or a refusal to do decision theory in the first place. They've been argueing past each other for months, and I've been asked to write up the document they could both sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration.

Give me that document. I want a numbered section of claims both sides can accept about the setup, about what a reason is, about idealization in decision theory, and about what the paradox does and doesn't show; then a disagreements table where each row names the exact point at issue, states each side's position in terms that side would accept as its own (not a caricature), and flags whether that residual disagreement looks substantive or partly verbal; then the cruxes — the sub-questions that, if answered, would actually move at least one side — and for each crux, a concrete variant of the ass case or a small formal model both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Use neutral labels for the two views rather than names you'd use to win the argument. Don't rule on who wins. And throughout, seperate what's basically settled among people who work on practical rationality and decision theory from what's genuinely contested, and don't present one side's contested view as the consensus.
