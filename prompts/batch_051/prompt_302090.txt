As a retired research physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy, I find myself drawn to a broadly Humean view of the laws of nature. Let me state the position plainly. I do not believe there is any nomic necessity or governing relation over and above the totality of particular matters of fact—the positions, velocities, charges, and other local properties that obtain across the whole of space and time. I take the laws to be the strongest true generalisations about that vast mosaic, or, more precisely, the generalisations that achieve the best balance of simplicity and informativeness in systematising it. Laws, on this view, describe the world; they do not produce or constrain it.

I want to know what would count against this position. Please tell me which observations, arguments, formal results, or developments in physics or in metaphysics should move me, and sort them by how decisive they would be rather than merely listing them. In particular: what would the universe have to look like for us to conclude that laws genuinely govern or generate the mosaic rather than summarise it? Are there actual features of scientific practice—the role of conservation laws, the apparent centrality of symmetries, or the privileged status some quantities seem to enjoy—that already put pressure on the view? Which objections would a committed Humean concede count as genuine costs, and which would they dismiss as question-begging or as mere re-descriptions of the same phenomena? For my part, I suspect the most serious pressure comes from the fact that the best system account seems to require a prior distinction between natural and gerrymandered properties before it can even define simplicity and strength, and I would like to know whether that is a real concession to the anti-Humean or a price the view can pay. Finally, I want an honest assessment of whether the position, as I have stated it, has been qualified into unfalsifiability—and, if so, whether that is a serious defect in a view of this kind or an acceptable feature of it.
