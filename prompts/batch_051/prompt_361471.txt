Philosophers of punishment standardly distinguish five candidate justifications for state punishment: deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation, retribution, and the expression of social condemnation. Consider deplatforming, coordinated public shaming, and similar informal sanctions as a form of punishment imposed by publics, employers, and platforms rather than by courts. For each of the five justifications, assess how much justificatory work it can do once it is transferred to this informal setting, given the disanalogies with state punishment: there is no trial or due process, the sanction is often indefinite and difficult to appeal, the sanctioning public is diffuse and shifting, and there is little or no rehabilitative apparatus. Rank the five justifications according to their capacity to genuinely legitimise or explain informal social sanction, and state the criteria behind your ranking. I am writing an essay on this topic and want to concentrate on the two or three justifications whose reassessment would most change the broader debate, and to say briefly which are overrated. Defend the ranking, identify which disanalogy does the most justificatory work for each verdict, and say what evidence or cases would most change your ordering. You may also consider whether some of these sanctions are better classified as ostracism or third-party norm enforcement than as punishment, and whether that reclassification strengthens or weakens particular justifications.
