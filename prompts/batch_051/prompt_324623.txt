I've been trying to get clear on whether I actually think truth, tradition, and purity are worth something in themselves, or whether I only treat them that way when they're doing useful work for me. I have a bunch of case-by-case verdicts, and I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, captures them—and which ones I should probably abandon.

On truth:
- If a physicist tweaks data to make a result publishable, that's about as bad as it gets for me. If a physicist runs a sloppy analysis and publishes a wrong result, it's bad but nowhere near the same, even though the literature ends up with a false claim either way.
- I'd want to know if my partner had a one-nighter ten years ago even if nothing followed from it and knowing would make me unhappy. But I don't think I have any obligation to count the windows on my block or memorize the serial numbers on my appliances. So its not that true beliefs matter one by one.
- I'd rather learn a useless fact I already wonder about (say, what the Cambrian sea floor would have looked like) than a useful fact I've never been curious about (the details of my pension). But if you offered me true beliefs that don't satisfy any curiosity, have no use, and can't be shared, I don't think I'd care to have them.
- I don't think you're always required to say the truth. Telling a friend their short story is good when it isn't is fine, as long as they just wanted encouragement.

On tradition:
- I think a fishing town that keeps up an annual blessing of the boats is doing something worthwhile even if nobody believes in it and it saves zero lives.
- But foot binding and child genital cutting feel wrong no matter how old the practice is; the fact that something is the tradition does no real work for me once it hurts someone who didn't choose it.
- A family that invented a tradition last year and treasures it, well, I don't think their tradition matters less for being new. That makes me wonder whether age is doing any of the work in the first case, or whether it's really about continuity and shared identity.
- If a place replaced its centuries-old planting calendar with a strictly better agronomic calendar, I'd feel like something real was lost, but I couldn't say the farmers are worse off. And I'm not sure feeling a loss is the same as there being a loss.

On purity:
- I'm disgusted by the idea of eating a sterilized, deep-fried cockroach, and I wouldn't eat it, but I don't think my disgust gives anyone else a reason.
- I think lab-grown meat that's molecule-for-molecule beef is fine to eat if it's safe, even though part of me feels it's not right. I'd eat it, but I'd probably wince the first time.
- Purity talk in sexual ethics mostly looks to me like a way of dressing up status and control, and I don't want to give that any weight.

The case I keep going back and forth on: someone offers my charity $100,000 that came from a payday-loan fortune that wrecked a lot of people's lives. Taking it and buying malaria nets would do a lot of good, but taking the money feels contaminating. I can't tell if that's the same kind of thing as the boat blessing—a felt-value I don't endorse on reflection—or whether it's a reason I actually stand behind.

Is there one principle that predicts when I treat these things as intrinsically valuable merely because they feel that way, and which of my verdicts does it force me to abandon? What does it say about the payday-loan money?
