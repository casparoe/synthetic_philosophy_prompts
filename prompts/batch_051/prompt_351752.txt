I am working through a dispute about the terms of a long-established online journaling and note-taking service. Its terms contain the following clause:

"Your Data belongs to you. You retain all right, title, and interest in the text, images, and other material you enter into the Service. You grant us a limited, revocable, and non-transferable licence to store, process, and display Your Data solely in order to provide the Service and the features you enable, and for no other purpose. If you close your account or ask us to erase Your Data, we will permanently delete Your Data from our systems and cease using Your Data."

The clause was drafted years ago, when the service did little but store entries, keep backups, and let users export their files. Everyone agrees the drafters had ordinary storage and retrieval in mind, not machine learning.

Since then, the company has trained a large language model on its users' entries. The model powers a search and auto-tagging feature inside the product: a user types a fragment of a memory, and the model surfaces related notes and suggests tags. One user has now deleted her account and revoked the licence. The company says it will delete her raw entries and any retrieval index containing them, but it will not delete or retrain the model. Its position is: the model is not "Your Data" and does not "contain" her entries; training fell within providing the features she enabled while her account was active; and once the raw data is gone, nothing remains over which her retained ownership could reach.

I want to know how far the clause itself reaches, read as a matter of interpretation rather than as a prediction about any particular regulator. Please work through at least the following approaches and say what result each gives: the ordinary meaning of the quoted words; the purpose of the clause, read against its property-like framing and the rest of the terms; what the original drafters would most likely have said if the question had been put to them; and what a reasonable user, reading the clause at sign-up, would have understood it to promise. Then say which approach should control for a case the drafters did not foresee, and why. In particular, I want to know whether the deletion and cessation language reaches a trained model, and what principle decides where property in someone's raw entries ends and where aggregate, derived capability begins.
