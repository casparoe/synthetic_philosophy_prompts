At a philosophy discussion group, two friends quarreled over our mutual friend Gabriel, who had recently rejoined a religious community after saying that the evidential case for its core commitments was, in his words, about half convincing. Marcus invoked Clifford’s principle that it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone to believe anything on insufficient evidence. He argued that belief is not a private matter because it disposes us to act, and that Gabriel’s decision sets a bad precedent: if one allows passional needs to tip the scale here, any momentous hope could be allowed to override evidence. He added that Gabriel could participate in the community, adopt its practices, and see what happens while suspending full assent, so no practical good is lost.

Elena replied that James’s essay argues precisely that there are forced, momentous, and live options in which the evidence cannot decide, and that refusing to believe is not a neutral escape but a passional choice in the other direction. She said Gabriel’s case has the structure James cares about: if the commitment is ever to prove itself, it can only do so after someone first takes the risk of trusting it, and permanent suspension would guarantee losing whatever truth might be available only from the inside. Marcus answered that this makes faith self-fulfilling rather than truth-tracking, since the experiment begins by suppressing doubt. Elena answered that the distinction between acting as if and believing is less clean than Marcus thinks; wholehearted trust can change what one notices and makes possible, while detached acting may screen off exactly the evidence that would settle the matter. They both turned to me and asked me to referee; I could not give a point-by-point judgment.

Please adjudicate this disagreement in American English. Specifically, identify which of Marcus’s claims are sound, which of Elena’s claims are sound, where each overstates or misses something, and then say who is more right overall about Gabriel’s particular situation. Do not frame the answer as advice to Gabriel, but as an evaluation of the arguments as actually made.
