I've just joined the program committee at a K-12 school that decides which competitive activities the school will sponsor. The committee's standing question is always whether a proposed activity is likely to build character or corrode it. The senior members treat their past decisions as precedents, but no one has ever written down the principle behind them. Below are the rulings I inherited, followed by the two proposals I have to vote on next week.

This year's rulings:

1. A voluntary robotics league, in which students form their own teams, prepare together for a regional tournament, and receive a single shared result. No grades, prizes, or varsity selection are attached. Ruled character-building and approved.

2. A required math-drill ranking, in which every sixth grader's individual percentile on a weekly fluency test would be posted on a hallway chart for the whole grade to see, and students could not opt out because the drills replaced an existing required classroom activity. Ruled corrosive and rejected.

3. An opt-in intramural basketball program, with self-formed teams, no cuts, no connection to grades or varsity selection, and a short end-of-season playoff. Ruled character-building and approved.

4. A mandatory junior-year college-readiness index, in which each student would be assigned an individual rank against classmates and the number would be sent home to parents each semester. No group or collaborative component, and no opt-out. Ruled corrosive and rejected.

5. A voluntary chamber-music festival, in which students choose to enter in ensembles they form themselves, and each ensemble receives one shared critique and one shared result. Ruled character-building and approved.

The two proposals before us:

A. A house league. Every student in grades nine through twelve would be assigned to one of four school houses for all four years. The houses would compete throughout the year in sports, debate, and an academic quiz format, with points accumulating toward a house cup displayed in the lobby. Results would have no effect on grades, recommendations, or anything outside the league, and no student could opt out or switch houses.

B. An open proof duel. An after-school, one-on-one mathematics competition. Any student could choose to enter; entrants would be paired against a single opponent in a timed proof-writing match and advance by single elimination. No coaching or collaboration would be permitted during matches. At the end, a bracket showing every entrant's name and how far they advanced would be posted outside the math office.

I need to be able to defend my votes, and I want to reconstruct the underlying principle before I cast them. So rather than just telling me yes or no, please do the following: identify the principle the committee is implicitly applying, along with the rival principles that fit the track record just as well; state how each candidate principle would handle the house league and the proof duel; for each proposal, name the past ruling that is its closest analog and say which way it cuts; and give a best guess about how the committee should rule on each if it is being consistent with its own precedents, making explicit what principle it would be committing itself to either way. Finally, tell me which of the two proposals is the more decisive test of the committee's actual principle, and, if neither vote settles things, describe the further case that would cleanly separate whichever principles remain live.
