At a reading group a few of us were going through the opening of Peter Singer's "Famine, Affluence, and Morality," and someone read this passage aloud:

“For comparative purposes, Britain's share of the nonrecoverable development costs of the Anglo-French Concorde project is already in excess of £275,000,000, and on present estimates will reach £440,000,000. The implication is that the British government values a supersonic transport more than thirty times as highly as it values the lives of the 9 million refugees.”

The disagreement started when Miriam said this passage makes it impossible to treat foreign aid as optional charity. She said if a government can find £440m for Concorde while refusing the roughly £300m the World Bank said India needed, then the moral position is straightforward: that government has decided to spend on a luxury while people who could be kept alive are left to die, and the distinction between “aid” and “duty” is doing no real work.

Dev pushed back. He said the comparison is rhetorically powerful but practically empty. The Concorde money wasn't sitting in a single pot that could be redirected to Bengal; it was tied up in contracts, jobs, technology policy, and a decision made years earlier. He also said a government has primary obligations to its own citizens, and foreign aid is exactly the kind of thing that is supererogatory — good if you do it, not wrong if you don't, or at least not wrong in the same way.

Miriam answered that Singer is not offering a policy logistics handbook; he's showing what our actual priorities reveal, and the fact that redirection is politically hard is part of the indictment, not an excuse. Dev said that making it an “indictment” just collapses all large public spending into a moral failure unless it's spent on the worst-off globally, which would make almost every government action wrong and leave no room for legitimate national projects. Miriam replied that maybe that is uncomfortable but not obviously false, and if it seems implausible, the burden is on Dev to say where the line between duty and charity actually is.

At that point they asked me to referee, and I honestly couldn't. Please adjudicate: who is right about which specific points? Are there distinctions Singer's actual argument would support that neither of them drew? And who is more right overall, given the passage as printed? Keep the whole response under 1,000 words.
