I am a PhD student in philosophy of language, and I teach a 75-minute weekly discussion section for an upper-level undergraduate course on pragmatics. The section has 16 students, and we have just finished reading the Lewisian scorekeeping picture, the Stalnakerian idea of common ground, and some work on presupposition accommodation. I want a single-session activity in which the students feel the force of these ideas rather than being told about them after the fact.

The target phenomenon is this. A conversation has a public record — a scoreboard or common ground — that is updated by conversational moves and against which later moves are evaluated. An assertion is normally felicitous only if its presuppositions are already on that record. But speakers very often utter sentences whose presuppositions are not yet established, and hearers typically do not object; they quietly add the presupposed content to the record so that the utterance counts as acceptable. That is accommodation. Accommodation is different from accepting what is asserted: a hearer can accept the presupposition while denying the asserted content, or can challenge the presupposition itself and refuse to let it onto the scoreboard. Speakers can also exploit accommodation to introduce controversial material into the common ground without ever asserting it. I want the students to come away seeing that presuppositions are not merely private background beliefs but public, norm-governed items on a conversational record, and that accommodation is a routine repair mechanism with a distinctive cost.

Please design a runnable classroom game or structured activity for this group. The students should encounter the phenomenon themselves before it is named. The activity should involve a visible scoreboard that the group must maintain; roles or rules that force students to choose between challenging a presupposition and accommodating it; and an incentive or payoff structure that does not require real money. It should draw on standard presupposition triggers — definite descriptions, factive verbs, clefts, additive particles such as too and again — but the students should not be lectured on these in advance. Please include: a complete list of materials; a timeline for the 75 minutes; the rules exactly as they would be explained to students before the activity begins; a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the distinctions above, including the difference between denying the assertion and challenging the presupposition and the difference between a false move and an infelicitous move; a note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure; and an honest statement of which feature of the scorekeeping or accommodation picture the activity cannot reproduce and that I will need to supply myself afterwards.
