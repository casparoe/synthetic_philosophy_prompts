I am a novelist. During a recent month at a writers' residency on the Maine coast, I was drafting a novel whose central character — a mid-century illustrator — keeps finding that work she made for one purpose is being put to quite different political uses. That preoccupation made me listen closely when two other residents, an essayist and a playwright, fell into a long argument at dinner about whether fiction should be answerable to political ends.

The discussion turned on W. E. B. Du Bois's view that all art is propaganda and that artists — Black artists above all — should direct their work toward racial justice. The argument unfolded in five recognizable steps. (1) The playwright argued that Du Bois formulated this position inside a specific historical emergency, and that a writer today cannot adopt it as a timeless rule without smuggling in that emergency's context. (2) The essayist replied that the descriptive version of Du Bois's claim does generalize: every narrative choice — whom the story centers, what it treats as inevitable, what it finally rewards — encodes a political picture of the world, so art is already propaganda whether the artist admits it or not. (3) The playwright granted the value-encoding point but argued that it empties the term: if "propaganda" means only that stories carry values, Du Bois's claim is trivial; if it means deliberate advocacy, the claim is false as a description of all art and harmful as a prescription, since aiming at advocacy flattens exactly the ambiguities that make fiction good. (4) The essayist called this a false dilemma, insisting Du Bois was making a moral claim rather than an empirical or stylistic one: a writer is answerable to the needs of a community, and pretending to stand outside politics is a way of defaulting on that responsibility. (5) Finally, the playwright asked what the essayist would say about a formally inventive, humane novel whose politics were quietist or conservative; the essayist answered that it might be excellent in execution and still fail in the one respect that mattered most. The playwright called that a reduction of literature to a loyalty test.

I kept out of it, partly because I have not settled the question for myself. I would like a step-by-step adjudication of the five moves: for each, who is right, who overreaches, and why. And beneath the local moves, which of the two is offering the more defensible reading of Du Bois — or does the distinction between the descriptive, prescriptive, and moral readings of his claim allow both to be partly right while still missing something important?
