At my department's weekly drinks my labmate Elena, who does ecology, and her partner Josh, who's in philosophy of science, got into an argument about whether natural selection is vacuous, and I've been turning it over all week. I'll give you the gist of what they each said, because I want someone to referee it.

Elena kicked it off by saying she's tired of hearing the slogan survival of the fittest, since it's either false or empty. If "fittest" just means the ones that actually leave the most offspring, then saying the fittest leave the most offspring is trivially true by definition and doesn't explain anything. But if "fittest" means something independent, like strongest or fastest, it's straight-up false, since the strongest often don't leave the most descendants.

Josh said that's exactly why people moved to what he called the propensity interpretation. On that view, fitness isn't actual reproductive success; it's a probabilistic disposition to produce offspring in a given environment, more like a biased die than a scoreboard. An organism's fitness is a real feature it has before any particular outcome, and actual survival and reproduction are fallible evidence of that feature.

Elena shot back that a propensity you can't observe directly is doing no real work. If the only way to know a die is biased is to roll it a bunch, then the bias is just a summary of the throws, not an explanation of them. You see the moth genotype out-reproducing the other one and then claim to "explain" it by saying it has higher fitness, which is just relabeling the fact — like explaining why a sleeping pill works by saying it has a sleep-inducing power.

Josh said she was ignoring how a die's bias is grounded in its physical construction: you can weigh it, find the off-center weight, and predict the throw frequencies before you've rolled it much. Same for organisms. You can decompose fitness into measurable components like camouflage, foraging efficiency, metabolic rate, and juvenile viability — stuff specified independently of the reproductive outcomes — and then make risky predictions about future frequencies. If the predictions fail, the view is in trouble, so it's not unfalsifiable.

Elena came back with: sure, but a fitness propensity is always relative to an environment. The minute conditions shift, the propensity gets re-specified, and in practice you can save any failed prediction by saying you misdescribed the environment or it changed. So the concept is flexible enough to absorb whatever happens. And she pressed further: even if you ground fitness in components, those components are themselves defined partly by their contribution to survival and reproduction. So he hasn't solved the circularity, he's just pushed it down into camouflage and metabolic rate.

Josh replied that every science has to tolerate some level of practical underdetermination and hard-to-specify boundary conditions; a concept isn't useless just because it's prickly to test. And on her last point, he said it was a fair worry but not fatal: some of those components can be measured entirely independently of reproduction, so the circle gets broken at the points that matter. If it turned out that none of them could, he'd admit the problem, but that's not where things stand.

They went around like this for a while, and I honestly cant sort out who's right about what. Can you adjudicate? I want to know, point by point, who's right about which specific claims, where each of them overreaches or concedes too much, and overall who comes out closer to right. Also, if both of them are missing something that would reframe the whole dispute, I'd want that too.
