There's a strange asymmetry in how historians treat the past. We're constantly warned against moral presentism: don't judge Aristotle for accepting slavery, or the American founders for not freeing their slaves, because people can only operate with the standards of their own time. Okay. But those same historians have no problem judging the past epistemically — they'll say that Victorian race science was bad science, not just evil by our lights; that a medieval chronicler was simply wrong to blame a plague on divine wrath; that phrenology was pseudoscience even at its peak. So moral standards get treated as belonging to their time, while epistemic standards get treated as if they're timeless. Is there a principle that justifies that difference, or is it just a line we draw so we can feel superior about past knowledge without feeling superior about past morality? I want a verdict — keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or give it up — and I want the answer to say explicitly which argument would most likely change its mind, or where its reasoning is most likely to fail.
