I am a student in a course on long-run decision theory and the governance of emerging technologies, and I am working through a counterfactual for a paper. Suppose that in 2030 a treaty-backed protocol becomes available that allows any person, firm, or state to place assets—money, compute, data, or other property—into a conditional trust that is released, or transferred to named beneficiaries, after the first verified development of artificial general intelligence, defined for this purpose as a system that can perform essentially all economically and scientifically significant cognitive work at or above human level. The trust is enforceable in ordinary courts against the original parties and their legal successors, and an independent verification body determines whether the trigger condition has been met. But the protocol binds only pre-AGI agents and their successors: nothing compels the AGI itself, or any post-AGI regime, to honor the arrangement or respect pre-AGI property claims. Predict how the availability of this mechanism changes behavior in the years before the transition. Who is most likely to use it, what bargains will they try to lock in, and why? Does it mostly enable efficient pre-commitment or mostly set off a destructive race to tie up resources? I want the chain of reasoning made explicit: which predictions follow fairly directly from the incentives the protocol creates, which are guesses that depend on assumptions about coordination, risk attitudes, or the speed and character of the transition, and what would have to be true for the mechanism to end up irrelevant.
