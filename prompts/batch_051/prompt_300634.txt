I work as a software engineer, mostly on machine learning systems that make predictions from visual data, so I have a practical interest in how much human perception is shaped by what a person already believes, though I have no formal training in philosophy. I recently read a general-audience article about something it called the cognitive penetration of perception, and one paragraph in it has been bothering me. The paragraph, lightly compressed, runs as follows:

"Until fairly recently, many psychologists treated perception as a one-way, encapsulated process: the world impinges on the senses, and the visual system reconstructs a faithful representation of what is out there. The New Look movement of the 1940s pushed back against that picture, and philosophers of science such as Paul Churchland later used those ideas to argue that perception is itself theory-laden. The Müller-Lyer illusion is often treated as the main complication: even after you have been told that the two lines are equal, you continue to see one as longer, which suggests that at least some early visual processing is insulated from what you believe. Recent work, however, is said to resolve the tension in the other direction. In one well-known experiment, people carrying a heavy backpack see a hill as steeper than people who are not carrying one; in another, desirable objects are seen as closer than undesirable ones. These results have been widely interpreted as showing that goals and desires reach all the way down and alter perceptual experience itself. Taken together, these findings show that cognition reaches into visual experience, so the modular view of perception is now obsolete. This matters beyond the lab: if two observers with different background theories can literally see the same scene differently, then perception cannot serve as a neutral court of appeal between rival scientific theories."

I do not feel equipped to judge how fair this summary is. My own inclination is to find the final claim plausible, partly because it resembles things I have seen argued in discussions of machine learning, where what a model "sees" depends heavily on how it was trained. But I do not want my priors to do the work here. Could you give me a precise accuracy audit: go through the summary claim by claim and tell me which parts a philosopher or cognitive scientist working on this topic would accept, which parts they would qualify or wince at, and which parts are outright wrong or overstated? Then say whether the conclusion about perception failing to adjudicate between rival scientific theories actually follows from the claims in the summary that survive correction. I would rather have sharp distinctions than a general essay.
