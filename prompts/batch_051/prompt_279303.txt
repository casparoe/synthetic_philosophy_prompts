I recently read a popular introduction to free will that summarises Galen Strawson's Basic Argument roughly like this. To be truly free or morally responsible for an action, one would have to be ultimately responsible for the way one is, in the relevant mental respects; but one could be responsible for the way one is only by having caused oneself to be that way, and nobody can be the cause of themselves. So, on Strawson's view, nobody is ever truly free or morally responsible. The book then says the argument stands or falls with determinism: our actions are not ours in the relevant sense only because they are fixed by our genes, upbringing, and prior causes, and if the world were indeterministic — so that at least some choices were uncaused or self-caused — responsibility would be possible after all. That final step strikes me as too quick, but I do not know the Basic Argument well enough to say why. Is this a fair summary? Please go through it point by point, separating what is accurate, what is a common simplification, and what Strawson's actual line of reasoning rules out; in particular, assess whether the argument really depends on determinism, and explain how the summary would need to be corrected before I rely on it. Please answer in British English.
