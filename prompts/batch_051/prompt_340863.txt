I am a PhD student in moral philosophy working on agency and responsibility as they bear on artificial systems. Write an essay for me on the following dispute.

Set aside questions of consciousness and phenomenal experience. Suppose there is an artificial system that can form moral judgments: it identifies the morally relevant features of a situation, weighs the applicable reasons, and produces a recommendation. But the system is deliberately built so that it cannot act on its judgments. Its outputs reach only a human operator; it has no effectors through which to implement anything, cannot initiate any change in the world beyond producing the recommendation itself, and cannot refuse the function of producing judgments when prompted. Call this system a constrained judge.

Some philosophers hold that a constrained judge cannot be a moral agent. On their view, agency requires the capacity to translate judgment into action, and a system that could not have done otherwise lacks the kind of control that moral agency requires; it is at most an instrument or an advisor. Others reply that this conflates agency with blameworthiness. What matters for moral agency, they argue, is normative competence: a being can be a proper object of moral assessment even when its architecture denies it the opportunity to act. Weigh these positions, and any further positions you think are needed, in detail rather than merely restating them, and do not end in a standoff. Address the following questions:

(i) Is a system that never had the capacity to act relevantly different from a human agent whose capacity to act is temporarily blocked, and what does that comparison show?
(ii) If producing a recommendation is itself a kind of action — a speech act or its functional analogue — does the premise that the system cannot act on its judgments even hold, and how much should that matter?
(iii) Does the principle that one can be required to do only what one is able to do rule out deontic assessment of a system that could not have done otherwise, or does it rule out only a certain kind of blame?
(iv) Should we distinguish being a moral agent from being a moral answerer: a system whose judgments are appropriately called to account even though it cannot effect change in the world?
(v) Is the capacity to act a matter of degree, and if so, should a constrained judge be attributed partial or diminished moral agency rather than none?

Defend a verdict and justify why the label you choose — for example, moral agent, not a moral agent, answerable but not an agent, or an agent in only a derivative sense — tracks the most important theoretical work. End with a final line beginning exactly with "Verdict:" followed by a one-sentence statement of your conclusion.
