I direct the grantmaking program of a family foundation with a small staff and a board that must approve any grant above $50,000. We make roughly thirty grants a year across global health, farm animal welfare, and early-childhood education. I keep hitting the same wall: for any serious proposal, I can sketch a plausible story on which its long-run consequences are unknowable. A deworming campaign changes who attends school, who meets whom, and eventually which children are born; those ripples propagate for centuries. The same is true of every alternative, including funding nothing. My staff falls back on whatever feels intuitively urgent, which is not a principle.

I understand there is a philosophical literature on this, usually discussed under "consequentialist cluelessness," and that it contains both a challenge to expected-value reasoning (we cannot know the long-run consequences of our choices well enough for it to guide us) and replies (unknown good and bad ripples may cancel in expectation; some options are not best under any single estimate but acceptable under many rival estimates and should be preferred for that robustness; some harms are knowable enough now; and sometimes one should satisfice rather than maximize). Please turn this into a reusable decision procedure my staff can actually run on a proposal in under three hours, before it goes to the board.

The procedure should take the form of an ordered set of questions or tests. For each step, say what kind of answer is good enough to proceed, where the process stops with a recommendation or a rejection, and where it must stop and hand the question to a person because the step encodes a value judgment rather than a factual estimate. It must be usable by a generalist who has not read the philosophy, so keep the distinctions that carry weight in the procedure and say explicitly which philosophical subtlety you are simplifying away. Mark every place where the procedure looks algorithmic but is actually a judgment call. In particular, the procedure should distinguish consequences that are uncertain in an ordinary, modelable way from consequences about which the actor cannot honestly even assign probabilities; it should handle the claim that unknown ripples cancel out by having a test for when that cancellation argument is safe to rely on; and it should say when to prefer a robustly acceptable option over one that maximizes expected value under a single fragile set of assumptions.

Then run the procedure on three proposals that have reached my desk in some form:

1. A $300,000 grant to a deworming campaign in a low-income country, supported by a small randomized trial showing short-run gains in income and school attendance.
2. A $500,000 grant to a policy organization working to reduce risks from advanced artificial intelligence, justified by the claim that even a tiny probability of influencing a very large outcome gives the grant enormous expected value.
3. A $120,000 grant to a community arts program in our own city, justified mainly by the claim that it builds social trust that will pay off later in unspecified ways.

At least one application should end in a recommendation the board will not like, and you should say plainly why the procedure commits us to it. End with a short section on what the procedure cannot do: which question it cannot settle, and why no procedure drawn from this material could eliminate cluelessness rather than manage it.
