For the past year I have chaired the residents' association of a small block of flats with a communal garden, and a slow-burning dispute has forced me to interrogate a pattern in my own judgements that I now suspect may be indefensible. One resident, Mr. Alder, pays nothing into the garden fund and does none of the work, yet each week he helps himself to vegetables, herbs and cut flowers from the beds. The association, myself included, condemns this without hesitation: he is taking something that others produced and maintained while giving nothing back. A second resident, Ms. Bell, also pays nothing and does no work. She has never once set foot in the garden, but she values it, keeps her rear-facing curtains open for the view, and benefits from the cleaner air and the increase in the building's value that the garden brings. Nobody, myself included, treats Ms. Bell with anything close to the disapproval directed at Mr. Alder.

I can see that the cases differ: one person removes something, while the other merely enjoys what is already there. What I cannot yet see is whether that difference carries the moral weight I have been giving it. The garden produces no less for anyone else because Ms. Bell enjoys the view, but much of what Mr. Alder takes — a handful of herbs, a few flowers — is equally trivial in quantity and would often go unused. Both have received a real benefit generated by other people's money and labour; both decline to contribute; both would be the first to complain if the garden were dug up. Nor is it clear that the asymmetry survives stress-testing: I doubt I would still rank Mr. Alder as worse if his taking were never noticed and never missed, while Ms. Bell's passive enjoyment raised her flat's value by a large sum, and all the more so if she campaigned to keep the garden precisely because it benefited her.

My question, therefore, is whether there is a defensible principle that justifies treating the taking free rider differently from the merely benefiting free rider, or whether the asymmetry is a piece of ordinary moral superstition — a bias toward visible appropriation — that we ought to give up. I would like you to construct and defend the strongest candidate principle you can, then test it hard against the nearby cases I have mentioned: trivial or surplus taking, large passive benefit, and a passive beneficiary who fights to preserve the good while still refusing to fund it. State whether the asymmetry should be kept as it stands, revised into something narrower, or abandoned.

Please write in British English. End with a final line reading exactly one of the following: Verdict: keep / Verdict: revise / Verdict: abandon.
