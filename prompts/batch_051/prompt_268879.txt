I am to teach a single evening session to a mixed adult education group—a local museum director, two city councillors, a few secondary-school history teachers, and several retired clergy—on the question whether a community can ever have a duty to forget. The group will arrive thinking that remembering past atrocities is always the safer moral and political course, and that forgetting is merely evasion. I want a plan for the session, not an explanation of the topic. Please give me the order in which the conceptual pieces must come and what depends on what; the one carrying example or case I should build the session around, with what it must have and what it must not have; the misconceptions this audience will predictably bring or form, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off; where I should openly say what I am not going to settle; and a final question that would show me whether they understand the live tension between remembering as a moral demand and forgetting as a condition of political possibility, rather than merely having switched sides. I am working with the thought that Avishai Margalit’s distinction between ethics and morality might help, and that Philip Rieff’s worry about a culture that cannot leave its past alone might help too, but I do not want the session to become a discussion of two authors. Do not include headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; write the plan in continuous prose, as in a letter.
