I spent forty years as a bench scientist, and I have only recently begun reading philosophy of mind with any seriousness. The argument that has stayed with me longest is Daniel Dennett's case against qualia built on the two coffee tasters, Chase and Sanborn. Both begin from an equally firm report that something in their gustatory world changed — the coffee they once liked now disappoints them — yet they locate the change differently. Chase says the coffee tastes exactly as it always did and that his standards have changed. Sanborn says his standards are unchanged and that the taste itself is different. Dennett's conclusion is that no evidence, behavioral, neurophysiological, or introspective, could decide between them, and that the very idea of an intrinsic, private, directly apprehensible qualitative state — a quale — is an incoherent posit, an artifact of a bad theory rather than a datum to be explained.

I find the eliminativist picture admirable for the reasons a scientist would find it admirable. It declines to posit an entity for which there is no possible measurement. It cleans the explanatory books. It treats first-person reports as things to be explained, not as introspective evidence for a special class of facts. And yet I cannot give up the conviction that when I taste coffee — or when, after an illness, I taste it differently — something in the experience itself has changed, and that there is a fact of the matter about it even if no third-person procedure could recover it. I do not want you to tell me which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that yields, what it would take for the theory to be the thing that yields, and whether there is a deflationary reading of qualia on which both survive.

You are a professional philosopher of mind who works at the intersection of philosophy and cognitive science and who knows Dennett's arguments and the qualia literature well. 3. **The case for the intuition at its strongest** (roughly 350 words). Give the best defense of the thought that there is something it is like to taste coffee, and of the claim that Chase and Sanborn differ over a real subjective fact even if no experiment can show it.
