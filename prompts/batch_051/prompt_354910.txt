Write out a full specification of the desiderata that an adequate account of the relationship between deliberation and self-prediction must satisfy. The target question is whether, and under what conditions, an agent who is presently deciding what to do can coherently assign probabilities to her own possible actions, and which principles those self-assignments must obey. I want the list of requirements first, with the consideration behind each; then a brief evaluation of candidate accounts; and, most important, a resolution of any conflicts among the requirements.

For each requirement, state it precisely, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and say whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp. Cover at least the following, and add any further requirements you think are genuine:

1. First-person prediction. The account must capture why there is a felt tension between, on the one hand, seeing one's own options as open during deliberation and, on the other, assigning each option a probability in the ordinary way. It must say whether the tension is epistemic, practical, or merely psychological.

2. The instability phenomenon. In cases where the outcome of an act depends on how likely the agent is to choose it, assigning a probability to an option can change that option's expected value and thereby undermine the assignment. The Death in Damascus scenario is the standard illustration: the agent will meet Death wherever she goes, so each option's desirability is a function of the very probability being assigned to it. The account must say whether this yields incoherence, imprecision, a fixed-point requirement, or none of these.

3. The predictor cases. The account must handle Newcomb's problem and variants in which a predictor acts before the agent deliberates and may condition the payoff structure on the predicted choice, as well as cases in which the forecast is made on the basis of the agent's own present deliberation. It must not collapse the distinction between the predictor's forecast and the agent's self-assignment.

4. The asymmetry between self and other. The account must explain why an agent can straightforwardly assign probabilities to another agent's choices, and to her own future choices when she will not then be deliberating, even while the parallel assignment during present deliberation appears problematic.

5. Coherence with standard rules. The account must state its relationship to probabilism, to conditionalization, and to the Reflection principle. It must say which of these constraints apply to self-ascribed action probabilities during deliberation, and whether the act of assigning a probability is itself an act whose consequences must be included.

Then address two structural questions. First, which of these desiderata reflect genuine requirements on any adequate account, and which are artifacts of particular commitments, such as a causal rather than an evidential formulation of decision theory, or a metaphysics on which the openness of deliberation is a real indeterminacy rather than an epistemic limitation? Second, determine whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable. If it is, identify exactly which requirements conflict and why, since that would be the most important result of the list.

End with a brief scorecard, one short paragraph per view, of how the following three families of account fare: (a) the view that deliberation precludes assigning precise probabilities to one's own options; (b) the view that self-assigned probabilities are ordinary probabilities subject to all the standard principles; and (c) the view that such assignments are coherent but self-referential, so that the very assignment alters the probability that is assigned. Keep the scorecard subordinate to the requirements themselves.
