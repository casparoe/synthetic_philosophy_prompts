For much of the past term I have been arguing with a colleague in musicology about whether a passage of instrumental music can be sad in itself. He holds what I will call the objectivist view: a slow descending line in the low register, with minor-mode harmony and falling semitone motion, is sad in something like the way a flag is red — the sadness belongs to the heard sound-structure and does not reduce to what the music does to listeners. I am a philosopher by training, and I am inclined to say this is a conceptual claim wearing an empirical costume: that the sentence "the music is sad" is best paraphrased as "suitably enculturated listeners are reliably made sad, or reliably put in mind of sadness, by certain sound-features."

I would like you to take my colleague's view seriously enough to try to test it. You are an expert in the philosophy of music and in experimental design; think carefully, and begin by brainstorming several candidate operationalizations of the claim before settling on the strongest. Then describe, concretely and in order, the empirical program you would run: the stimuli and controls (including how to factor out lyrics, programmatic titles, and cultural training), the subject populations and comparison conditions, and exactly which pattern of results would count for the objectivist claim and which would count against it. Be explicit about every point at which your design quietly presupposes the listener-response picture and thereby begs the question against my colleague. Finally, offer a residue report: what part of the claim that the music itself is sad no possible observation can settle, and whether that residue is the part of the claim doing the philosophical work or only a vivid way of speaking.
