You are a philosopher of mathematics who specialises in the epistemology of proof. I serve on the standards committee of my university's mathematics department, and I want a clear statement of what the discipline's current practices already presuppose before we propose any change. The practice that puzzles me is this. Mathematicians treat the four-colour theorem and the Kepler conjecture as settled theorems, citing them as premises and teaching them as facts; yet both proofs depend essentially on computer calculations that no individual mathematician has verified step by step, and in the four-colour case a celebrated later verification is itself a machine-checked formal proof rather than a humanly readable derivation. The same community nevertheless expects referees to verify proofs rather than merely trust an author's software, and continues to prize short, surveyable, explanatory arguments. Reconstruct the implicit theory of proof, and of mathematical knowledge, that this practice presupposes. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say whether its parts presuppose consistent things. Then explain what would have to change in practice under each of two rival standards: that a proof must be surveyable by a single human being, and that a proof need only be a formally verified derivation, however long and unreadable. Before you draft the statement, begin by brainstorming at least three candidate reconstructions so that you do not settle on a caricature.
