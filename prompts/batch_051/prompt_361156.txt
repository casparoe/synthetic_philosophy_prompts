I sit on the Algorithmic Decision Review Committee of a medium-sized university, which must certify every machine-learning system used in student-facing decisions before it goes live. We have a short but binding record of our own precedents, and I want to reason from those precedents rather than from general slogans about fairness.

Our first relevant precedent concerned a model that shortlisted students for optional peer-tutoring places by predicting who would fail a core module. The model was well calibrated: for students in each of the university's two main admissions streams, a predicted failure risk of 40 per cent corresponded to a near-identical actual failure rate. But its false-positive rate was several points higher for students from one stream. We approved it, holding that a score should mean the same thing for every student, and that it would be perverse to discard predictive signal for the lower-base-rate stream merely to equalise a benign error — namely, a student receiving extra help they did not strictly need. In a second precedent we rejected a coursework-flagging model for mandatory academic-integrity review. That model was also well calibrated, but its false-positive rate fell far more heavily on one admissions stream; we held that a burdensome consequence — an investigation and a possible misconduct finding — should not fall unevenly across groups, and required instead a model with balanced false-positive and false-negative rates, even though the data made it impossible for a single model to have both properties. A third precedent, concerning an algorithm that allocated extra-time examination adjustments whose use was optional, inclined the first way: we approved a model satisfying calibration but not balanced error rates, remarking that a withheld adjustment is not the same kind of burden as an investigation.

We must now decide a new case. The Student Success Office wants to deploy a model that predicts first-year failure and automatically enrols every flagged student in a mandatory, non-credit-bearing summer bridging programme before the autumn term; the flag is recorded on a list visible to academic advisers. The Office describes the scheme as a support, while some committee members regard it as a burden in all but name. On current data the two admissions streams have different base failure rates, so — as the developers' own fairness audit confirms — the model can satisfy calibration or balanced error rates, but not both. I am less interested in the committee's instinct than in what our own precedents commit us to. Please recommend the decision that best coheres with our record: identify the candidate rules that fit the earlier verdicts, say whether they diverge on this new case, identify the closest precedent or precedents, and state plainly what principle we would be adopting with each available choice. Where the record genuinely underdetermines the answer, say so, and specify a further case that would separate the competing rules. Throughout, please distinguish what is mathematically settled about the trade-off between calibration and balanced error rates from what is a genuinely contested normative choice among people who work in this area, and do not present one contested view as the consensus. Kindly write in British English and keep the response to no more than 900 words.
