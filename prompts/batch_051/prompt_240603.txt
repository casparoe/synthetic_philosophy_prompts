I retired a few years ago from a career in developmental biology, and since turning to philosophy I keep meeting a comparison in conversations with former colleagues about machine intelligence — a comparison that strikes me as doing more argumentative work than it is given credit for. The claim, in its informal version, is that human creativity is at bottom only variation on a theme: one bends or "twiddles" parameters on concepts one already possesses, much as one twiddles the knobs of an amplifier, and what we call originality is merely an unusually distant or felicitous setting of those knobs. In Hofstadter's hands this is a subtle idea — the theme is a mental structure whose dimensions can be slid, and the crux of creativity lies in recognizing which slidings are interesting. But in the discussions I have in mind, the comparison is pressed toward a deflationary conclusion: that a generative model adjusting parameters in a high-dimensional space is doing essentially what a human artist or scientist does, and that there is no further ingredient — no genuinely new theme — for us to explain or for a machine to lack.

I would like you to audit this use of the knob-twiddling comparison. Lay out what maps onto what as explicitly as you can — the knobs, the positions of the knobs, the pre-existing circuit, the hand doing the adjusting, the ear that listens to the result, the experienced quality of the fresh variation; a table of correspondences is welcome. Then identify where the mapping breaks, separating the disanalogies that matter for the deflationary conclusion from those that are merely incidental. I am especially interested in whether the dimensions along which a theme can be varied are given in advance, the way an amplifier's controls are, or whether noticing a new dimension is itself a central part of the creative act; whether the comparison quietly imports the passivity of a fixed device, already wired and labeled by someone else; and what becomes of the deflationary conclusion once the disanalogies are on the table. If the conclusion survives only in a weakened form, say exactly what that form is; if you think a better analogy is available, name it and say what it gets right that the amplifier gets wrong. Please begin with your bottom line in the first sentence — a direct statement of how far the analogy holds and whether the deflationary conclusion survives — and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
