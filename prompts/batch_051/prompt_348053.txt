As a novelist working on a book about a negotiator who begins to suspect that the peace she brokers is really only an armed pause, I have been reading Kant’s Perpetual Peace, and one of its early moves has unsettled me. The essay seems to insist, in its first Preliminary Article, that a treaty of peace that tacitly reserves some matter for a future war is not a genuine peace treaty at all but only a suspension of hostilities. Yet contemporary peacemaking often seems built on exactly the opposite practice: agreements routinely include denunciation clauses, renewable mandates for outside forces, side letters defining what would count as a material breach, and exit mechanisms that allow one party to resume force under specified conditions. I want to know what philosophical theory of treaties, promising, and international right this contemporary practice presupposes. Does it strictly presuppose that no treaty can bind beyond a sovereign’s continuing interest, or is it compatible with several different views? Does it quietly rule out the Kantian idea that peace must be permanent, or can Kant’s view accommodate withdrawal clauses as conditions rather than hidden reservations? Reconstruct the implicit theory, separate what the practice requires from what it merely fits, and tell me what a peace-process design would have to change if it genuinely took the Kantian principle as authoritative. Please write this as continuous prose, as in a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
