The modern state often claims to be secular, but four Western countries institutionalize that claim in sharply different ways. In England, there is an established church: the monarch is the Supreme Governor of the Church of England, and twenty-six bishops of that church sit by right in the House of Lords. Germany has no established church, but the state collects a supplementary church tax through the income-tax system from taxpayers who are registered as members of eligible religious bodies and transfers the proceeds to those bodies; it also offers denominational religious instruction in public schools. In France, the 1905 separation ends public funding of organized worship and excludes confessional instruction from public schools, yet the state owns and maintains nearly all church buildings erected before 1905 and funds chaplaincies in the armed forces, prisons, and hospitals. In the United States, the constitution forbids an establishment of religion, but houses of worship and religious charities receive broad tax exemptions, and legislative chambers routinely open with prayers led by publicly paid chaplains.

I want a disciplined reconstruction of the implicit philosophy of secularism in each of these four arrangements. For each country, state what conception of church–state separation, neutrality toward religion, or religious freedom the institutional pattern strictly presupposes — as opposed to what it is merely historically associated with — and identify any place where one country's institutions presuppose inconsistent things. Then assess whether the four arrangements are best understood as four rival philosophies of secularism, as four local ways of realizing one underlying principle, or as underdetermined by any interesting principle at all. Be exact about explanatory work: if a principle is supposed to explain why a country funds military chaplains but not denominational schooling, or why one country taxes churches while another exempts them, the principle must actually distinguish those cases rather than merely be compatible with them.
