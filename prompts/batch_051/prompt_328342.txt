I am assembling a graduate seminar on research ethics and want to open the unit on randomized controlled trials with something the literature keeps failing to state in full: the specification that any defensible principle of randomization must meet. The field has candidate principles — clinical equipoise, the uncertainty principle, the requirement that no subject be knowingly assigned to care worse than what they would receive outside the trial, and the view that adequately informed consent by itself discharges the investigators' obligations — but the candidates are usually defended case by case rather than against a fixed list of criteria.

Write the desiderata for a theory of when it is permissible to randomize a patient among the arms of a trial. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, state it precisely enough that a candidate principle can be tested against it, and mark it non-negotiable or parochial to a camp (Bayesian versus frequentist, individual versus community equipoise, research ethics versus bedside clinical ethics). The list must at minimum address these pressure points:

- A patient with a settled, informed preference for one arm who asks to enroll only if she can be assigned to it.
- A clinician-investigator with a private leaning toward one arm even though the relevant expert community is genuinely divided.
- A comparative-effectiveness trial of two treatments each accepted as standard practice, where the trial exists because no one knows which is better; throughout the trial every subject receives an accepted therapy, yet by the end one arm may turn out to have been receiving the worse of the two standards.
- A placebo-controlled trial conducted where the active treatment is not locally available, so the control condition is what subjects would have received anyway, while subjects elsewhere could have received the active drug.
- An adaptive trial in which allocation ratios shift as outcome data accrue, so the evidential condition that justified randomization changes while the trial is running.
- The tendency of many subjects to believe that their own assignment is being chosen for their individual benefit rather than by chance for the benefit of future patients — what the literature calls the therapeutic misconception.

For each pressure point, say what it shows: whether it forces a genuine desideratum, exposes a distinction a theory must respect, or should be set aside as a problem for consent practice rather than for the randomization principle. In doing so, decide which of these distinctions a correct theory must respect, which it may collapse, and which are parochial: individual versus community uncertainty; the therapeutic versus the research context; ex ante justification of a trial versus justification at each subsequent assignment as evidence accumulates; and local versus global standard of care.

Then assess the list itself. Which pairs of desiderata do you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied? In particular, can the demand that each enrolled patient's care remain governed by the physician's individualized judgment and the demand that treatment be assigned by a randomizing device be jointly satisfied, or must one give way? Conclude by stating which single desideratum most threatens the standard positions — clinical equipoise, the uncertainty principle, and the consent-suffices view — and why. Do not write a literature review; the specification and its internal tensions are the deliverable.
