I'm on a state arts council panel that funds literature for the public good, and one member — an economist, otherwise sensible — keeps pushing a line I've now heard three times: reading a novel is basically a flight simulator for empathy and social judgment. His idea is that fiction gives you low-cost, low-stakes practice at modeling other minds, so when real messy social life comes at you, you've already logged the hours. He uses it to argue we should fund realistic literary fiction preferentially because it has the highest transfer, and cut support for things like poetry or weird experimental work. I'm supposed to write the comittee's response.

Can you actually audit the analogy? I want the correspondence table: what plays the role of the simulator cockpit, the instrument panel, the student pilot, the visual system, the crash, the instructor feedback, the logged hours? Then where does it break, and which of those breaks actually matter for the funding question, versus the ones that are just nitpicks? What does the analogy quietly smuggle in about what fiction is for — that it's training for something else, rather than something worth doing for itself — and does that smuggling do any work in his argument? Does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or is the whole case for funding riding on an analogy that's carrying it? And if it is a bad analogy, is there a better one that captures whatever real point is hiding in it?
