I've been going back and forth with a friend about life extension, specifically about whether the "deathism" crowd has a real argument or is just rationalizing. He always lands on Bernard Williams's old point that an endless life would eventually go flat: even with good health and company, the things you care about would either run out or repeat until they stopped mattering, and you'd end up without the desires that make life worth living, so death would come as a relief.

Here's the part I can't get past. Suppose a plausible version of life extension shows up that doesn't just extend lifespan but also gives people a reliable way to periodically refresh or reweight their memories and desires. Not wiping identity, just making old interests feel new again, or letting you shift to a genuinely new passion when the old one wears out. Something like memory reconsolidation combined with a guided desire update. It seems like Williams's argument assumed your interests can't be reset indefinitely without cost, or that a reset would just make you into someone else. But maybe the technology only patches the boredom, and the deeper point about meaning or the narrative shape of a life survives. Does Williams's tedium argument survive this? Which premise does the development actually bear on, and can the argument be repaired? If it's dead, what should the remaining objection to an endlessly refreshed life be called? I don't need reading recommendations, just the argumentative anatomy.
