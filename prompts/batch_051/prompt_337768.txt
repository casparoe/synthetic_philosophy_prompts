I've found myself defending a view I've started calling ratificationism about literary meaning, and I'd like it checked before I try to write it up. The usual debate, as I understand it, pits writing-time intentions against what well-placed readers would take the words to mean. I think both sides put the author in the wrong temporal position. My claim is that the meaning of a text is fixed by the author's retrospective ratification, not by what she had in mind while composing. An interpretation is correct if, and only if, the author would — or, when available, actually does — ratify it after the fact under what I call conditions of sober ratification. By that I mean: she is shown the proposed interpretation in full, she has real time with it, she is competent (no dementia, intoxication, or performative contrarianism), and she is not under pressure from reputation, scandal, or the need to flatter an audience. If the author in that state says yes, that is what the work means, then the interpretation counts as the meaning even if the thought never crossed her mind while writing. If she disavows it, it is not the meaning, however elegantly the text supports it. The appeal for me is that it lets authors learn what they made: it explains why we keep caring about decades-later prefaces and interviews, and it respects the author without pretending that a work's meaning is whatever her writing-day psychology happened to contain.

The worry I cannot shake is that this seems to let meaning change retroactively. A fixed string of words could mean one thing in 1970 and another in 2020 solely because the author changed her mind, with not a word of the text altered. I am prepared to bite that bullet: I would say meaning is a property of a work-plus-author-history, not of the text alone. But I want to know whether the view collapses into some already-named position in this debate, whether my sober-ratification condition is doing too much unearned work or can be gamed by a well-timed authorial pronouncement, and where the view breaks in a way I have not yet seen. The test case I keep returning to is this: a novelist writes a comic set piece, and then a death in her family leads her to reread it as grief-laden and to disown it as flippant. On my account, the same sentences have changed their meaning without being altered. I can't tell whether that is the view's clearest consequence or its reductio.
