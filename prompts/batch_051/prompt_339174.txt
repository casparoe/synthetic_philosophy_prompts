Please propose a working definition of being in pain, in the ordinary sense in which we ask whether a person, an animal, or some other creature is in pain. The definition should be precise enough to guide a clinical, scientific, or moral judgment about difficult cases. Then run your definition against each of the following cases, saying explicitly where it delivers the correct verdict, where it delivers the wrong verdict or no verdict at all, and whether you would revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict:

1. A person who stubs a toe and experiences an immediate, vivid, localized, unpleasant sensation.
2. A patient under general anesthesia whose nociceptors respond to a surgical incision and whose spinal cord shows nociceptive activity, though the patient has no conscious experience of anything.
3. A person with pain asymbolia: asked about a sharp or hot stimulus, the person can identify it, locate it, and may even say "it hurts," but reports no unpleasantness, does not withdraw or want the stimulus to stop, and shows no distress.
4. A person grieving the death of a spouse who says the grief is "painful," that it hurts in the chest, and that the state is unbearable.
5. A person who reports pain located in a limb that was amputated years earlier.
6. A person who seeks out and enjoys being cut or burned during sex, describing the sensation as painful, with tissue damage occurring and the sensory quality being much like that in case 1.
7. A person born with congenital insensitivity to pain who walks on a fractured leg without noticing it, even as the tissue is being damaged.
8. A fish that, after being hooked through the mouth, struggles, swims away, and then guards and rubs the injured region.
9. A person who reports "feeling hurt" after being excluded by a group, with no obvious tissue damage.

I suspect that the ordinary word "pain" does at least two jobs — tracking a sensory signal with location and intensity, and tracking something like aversiveness or badness — and that this is most visible in pain asymbolia, where the two seem to come apart. If you agree, consider giving the two components separate names and saying which of the cases satisfy both, which satisfy one but not the other, and which satisfy neither.
