I'm on the compliance committee for a regional pest-control outfit, and we're genuinely stuck on how to read one clause in our state association's code of conduct. I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher who works on applied ethics and the interpretation of professional rules, and to think carefully about this instead of just firing off a verdict. Maybe start by laying out how each interpretive approach handles it, then tell me what the rule actually requires.

Here's the clause (section 4.2):

"In selecting a method of control, a member shall use the least hazardous product that is effective against the target pest. A member shall not knowingly use any method that causes a lingering death or unnecessary suffering."

The situation: a flour mill we service has Indianmeal moth throughout its stored flour and processing floor, and two treatment programs are on the table.

A) A grain protectant containing methoprene, an insect growth regulator. It's about as low-hazard as anything we have — practically non-toxic to mammals and birds — and because it only disrupts insect development, it leaves alone the small parasitic wasps already keeping a secondary beetle population down. But it works by mimicking juvenile hormone, so the moth larvae never manage to pupate into adults. They keep feeding and crawling for a week or more, then die as larvae.

B) A whole-mill fumigation with sulfuryl fluoride. It kills every life stage — larvae included, not just the flying adults — within about a day, which is as close to a prompt kill as this job allows. But it's a highly toxic gas: the mill has to be sealed and vacated, every insect in the structure dies (the parasitoid wasps included), and sulfuryl fluoride is a serious greenhouse gas with its own set of regulatory headaches.

Our field manager says the plain words require B, because the second sentence is a flat ban on a lingering death and methoprene plainly makes the larvae linger. Our sales lead (not exactly a philosopher, I'll grant you) says the whole point of the clause was to push people toward low-hazard, targeted products like the IGR, and that the no-lingering-death rule was written with rodents and raccoons in mind, not moth larvae.

So what does section 4.2 require or permit here? Please work through (1) the plain words, (2) the apparent purpose of the clause, (3) what the people who drafted it would most likely say if asked about a product that's slow but probably not painful, and (4) what a reasonable reader of a professional code would take the words to mean. Then say which approach you think should win and why, rather than mudging them together.

Oh, and please write the answer in American English.
