Consider the following position, prominent in comparative politics and in the official self-understanding of some governments: authoritarian or semi-authoritarian rule can earn genuine political legitimacy — understood not as grudging compliance, resignation, or rule by fear, but as the population's acceptance that the regime has the right to rule — through sustained delivery of economic growth, rising living standards, and public order, even in the absence of free and competitive elections. Call this the performance legitimacy view. Its standard illustrative cases are Singapore under the People's Action Party and China during the reform era: cases where, on this view, prosperity and stability have produced durable and widely held support that deserves the name legitimacy rather than something weaker.

I would like you to reason adversarially about this position and map its exposure. What would have to be true, or what would have to occur, for the performance legitimacy view to be false, either as a general thesis about what can legitimate non-democratic rule or as an account of Singapore and China? Distinguish throughout between evidence that would be merely awkward for the view and evidence that would be decisive against it. In particular, address the following:

- The observability problem. In settings without free elections, a free press, or reliably independent polling, how could one tell whether people accept a regime as rightful rather than merely complying out of fear, habit, or the absence of any visible alternative? If a defeat for performance legitimacy would be systematically hidden by preference falsification, does that make the view unfalsifiable, and if so, should that insulation worry its defenders?

- The distinction between approval of incumbents and legitimacy of the regime itself. Surveys in such cases may measure satisfaction with current material conditions or the present leadership rather than acceptance of the regime as such. What evidence could separate these, and why does the distinction matter for how exposed the view is?

- What the view predicts about performance failures. If legitimacy rests on continued delivery, what happens to the view's predictions when growth stalls, unemployment rises, housing becomes unaffordable, or corruption is exposed on a large scale? What pattern of persistence, protest, or replacement would confirm the view rather than count against it?

- The transition problem. A common rival claim is that sustained prosperity eventually creates a middle class whose demands shift from material welfare to procedural rights, accountability, and participation. What exactly would have to occur in Singapore or China for that shift to count as evidence against performance legitimacy, rather than being absorbed into it?

- Concessions from the view's own defenders. Which developments would thoughtful defenders of performance legitimacy themselves regard as counting against it — a sharp and sustained economic contraction alongside visible elite enrichment? Large protests in prosperous cities rather than impoverished ones? A successful transfer of power after a crisis to an electorally accountable alternative?

- The strongest opposing consideration. What is the single strongest argument or piece of evidence against performance legitimacy, and why? After weighing it, are we left with a falsifiable empirical thesis, or with a near-tautology in which any durable non-democratic regime is retroactively redescribed as legitimate by definition?
