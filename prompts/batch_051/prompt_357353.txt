I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher. I work on the motion-planning component of an autonomous delivery vehicle, and I have been turning over an argument about blame for a crash in our fleet. I keep failing to find the bug in the argument, and I would like you to treat it like a code review: go through it line by line, identify the exact point where it fails if it does, and say what the corrected version would look like.

The case is this. The vehicle's driving policy is learned rather than hand-authored. In the crash I have in mind, the vehicle met a road configuration and a pattern of sensor inputs that no engineer had reviewed in advance, and the policy produced a sequence of maneuvers that ended with a pedestrian seriously injured. No one on the team could point to a specific design decision that produced that sequence.

Here is the argument I keep coming back to.

(1) A person is blameworthy for a harmful event only if there was some earlier point at which they could have acted to prevent it, and at that point they foresaw, or should have foreseen, that a harm of roughly this kind was a real risk of what they were doing.

(2) In this crash, no individual person satisfied that condition. The particular sequence of maneuvers was not designed, reviewed, or predicted by any individual; no individual was in the loop steering the vehicle as it happened; and no one could have foreseen this particular behavior under those particular inputs.

(3) Therefore no individual person is blameworthy for the harm.

(4) The vehicle itself cannot be blameworthy in the sense at issue: being blameworthy means being able to answer for what one did, recognize the wrong, and make amends, and a trained model can do none of those things.

(5) The only remaining candidate is the company as a whole. A company can be fined or sued, but that is not blame in the sense at issue, because a company does not deliberate or act over and above the individuals who compose it.

(6) Since no individual is blameworthy, the vehicle is not blameworthy, and the company is not a further target of blame, nobody is blameworthy for the harm.

(7) If nobody is blameworthy for a serious harm caused by a system we chose to design, test, and release, then there is a responsibility gap; and I find it hard to accept that such gaps are simply the price of putting these systems on public streets.

My question is whether this argument is valid. If it is not, exactly which step fails, what is wrong with it, and what would the corrected step say? I half-suspect one of the steps is quietly doing more work than it appears to, but I cannot make the diagnosis sharp. I want a precise verdict about each step, not just a dislike of the conclusion.

After your verdict, say what would change your mind: identify the premise or inference you are least confident about, and say which argument or consideration would push you to the opposite answer.
