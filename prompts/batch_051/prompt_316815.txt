Two friends of mine, both longtime vegans, got into an argument after dinner about the ethics of using animal products that would otherwise go to waste — dumpstered food, roadkill, and the eggs of rescued hens. I mostly listened, and then they asked me to referee. I couldn't do it on the spot, so I'm writing it up as I remember it.

(1) The first said consuming such products is obviously permissible, perhaps even better than letting them rot, because using them creates no additional demand: the store has already discarded the dairy, the deer is already dead, the hen will lay the egg whether or not anyone eats it. In none of these cases does the act harm, confine, or breed any animal that would not have been harmed, confined, or bred anyway. To throw away edible nutrition for the sake of a rule whose violation has no victim is to fetishize one's own purity.

(2) The second replied that this is exactly the frame that keeps animal exploitation going: once the damage is done, the animal's body becomes free resources. Even if a particular act sends no market signal, it keeps the appetite and the habit pointed in the direction of treating animal bodies as food. A veganism built on exceptions like "it was free," "it was already dead," or "it would have been wasted" is a veganism whose line blurs until it disappears, and whose visible exceptions tell other people there is no coherent principle here worth imitating. The rule isn't only about marginal harm; it's also about refusing a practice — treating animals as things whose remains and secretions are available to us.

(3) The first answered that this trades a clear rule — don't cause death and suffering, don't create demand — for a much vaguer one about what counts as treating a creature as a thing, and it does so at a real cost. A rescued hen's unfertilized egg is not a victim. A deer killed by a car is not a victim. A carton of yogurt pulled from a dumpster is not a victim. If a rule forbids all three, it has stopped being about the animals and started being about the moral display of the human. And the worry about visible exceptions is an argument for discretion — don't eat a dumpstered steak at lunch in front of coworkers — not for a private ban.

(4) The second replied that the search for a victim in each specific case is the error, because some reasons concern the kind of life one builds and the kind of relationship one has with animals. Most of us would not eat the body of a dog who died of old age, even though the dog is not a victim and eating it would waste nothing — because how we handle the bodies of creatures expresses how we regard them. If the first would flinch at eating a companion dog but not at eating a road-killed deer, the difference is not a principle about harm; it is comfort, familiarity, and species habit — the very double standard veganism was supposed to get rid of.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the four moves, say which claims are on solid ground, which overreach, and which parts are empirical rather than conceptual. Then say which position is stronger overall, and whether the right answer plausibly varies across the cases they lump together — dumpstered dairy, roadkill, the eggs of rescued hens, and the companion animal who died of old age — and why.
