The standard view is that Bertrand's paradox is decisive against the principle of indifference: because equally natural ways of parameterizing the space of possible chords assign different probabilities to the same event, the instruction to treat equal possibilities as equally probable is too underdetermined to generate objective priors, and indifference-style reasoning gets demoted to a rough heuristic at best. I am inclined to accept that conclusion, but I want to know how exposed it really is. What would have to be true for the principle of indifference to survive Bertrand-type objections as a principled constraint on prior probabilities rather than a source of contradictions? In your answer, distinguish what the paradox settles from what remains genuinely open. In particular: can a defensible version of the principle specify how the relevant possibilities are individuated, or how the outcome space is parameterized, without presupposing the very probability distribution it is supposed to yield? Can an appeal to symmetry or invariance select a uniquely correct parameterization, and what assumptions would such an argument need beyond the principle itself? Which of the standard replies should I treat as live options rather than merely logical possibilities, and are there domains where the paradox undercuts indifference-style reasoning less completely than is usually claimed? Rank the potential defeaters of the standard dismissal from decisive to merely awkward, and say whether the dismissal has been insulated against criticism—and whether, if it has, that insulation is itself a reason to distrust it.
