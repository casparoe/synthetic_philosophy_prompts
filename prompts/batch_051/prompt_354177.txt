I work for a regional transit authority that logs anomalies across a rail fleet — cracked welds, brake faults, door-sensor failures, traction-power trips — and we need a reusable rule for deciding when two field reports identify the same underlying mechanism and should be merged into one root-cause investigation, versus when they are distinct mechanisms that happen to trigger the same alarm. Start from the following account unless you can improve it: a mechanism is a configuration of components whose organized interactions transmit the effect of an upstream intervention downstream in a regular way, and two anomaly events share a mechanism when the same upstream intervention would have prevented both. Do not conflate sharing a mechanism with sharing a fault code, a part, or a location. The procedure must also settle the grain of comparison: fatigue cracking under cyclic loading is one mechanism at a coarse grain, while growth from a casting void and growth from a surface gouge are two mechanisms at a finer grain, and the merge/no-merge answer may flip with the grain. State which grain the procedure uses by default and when an investigator may change grain. It should be usable by staff without philosophy training, specify what evidence to request at each step (service histories, design drawings, fracture surfaces, software logs), give merge/no-merge answers where possible, and mark the exact points where a person must exercise judgment rather than follow a rule. My working hunch is that grouping should follow the damaged subsystem — brakes with brakes, doors with doors, welds with welds — but I want a procedure strong enough to override that hunch when it should. Apply the procedure to three logged cases: (a) two door faults on the same train series traced to the same undersized relay; (b) one brake fault and one door fault both traced to a single software bug in the braking controller, via different output channels; (c) two fatigue cracks on the same welded bogie joint, one initiated at a casting void at the weld root and one at a surface gouge on the weld toe. Make sure at least one case returns an answer that overrides my hunch, and explain why I should accept the procedure's answer there. End with a short statement of what the procedure cannot do: the look-alike cases it will misclassify, the additional information that would change its answers, and the residual decisions that are policy calls rather than mechanism judgments.
