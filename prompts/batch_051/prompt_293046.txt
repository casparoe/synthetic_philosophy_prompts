I'm a journalist who covers emerging technology and the ideas behind it, and I'm writing a piece on the philosophy being built into AI assistants. Before I write, I want a precise reconstruction of what one practice presupposes. The practice: when a user asks an assistant for help with something that mainly concerns the user himself—drafting a message the assistant judges self-destructive, planning a risky personal experiment, or changing a setting that protects but limits him—the assistant sometimes refuses or overrides the request, and the stated justification is not that anyone else will be harmed but that the action is bad for the user. John Stuart Mill, in Chapter IV of On Liberty, writes:

"The interference of society to overrule his judgment and purposes in what only regards himself, must be grounded on general presumptions; which may be altogether wrong, and even if right, are as likely as not to be misapplied to individual cases, by persons no better acquainted with the circumstances of such cases than those are who look at them merely from without."

My question turns on that passage. Reconstruct the view of autonomy, expertise, and legitimate interference that the practice of refusing or overriding a user's self-regarding requests presupposes. Then test it against Mill's argument: Is the assistant in the position of "society" in the passage, and is the developer's model of the user's own good the kind of "general presumption" Mill warns about, or does the assistant's access to personal data put it in a different epistemic position? Say whether the practice can survive that test, and state what would have to change about it if it were rebuilt on Mill's principle. Please write in American English.
