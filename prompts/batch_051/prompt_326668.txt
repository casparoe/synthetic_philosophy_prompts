I have been rereading Orwell's "Politics and the English Language" with a reading group, and the group has mostly been treating its central argument as intact and portable: that vague, cliché-ridden political prose and bureaucratic euphemism are not merely symptoms of dishonest or lazy thought, but actively enable it, because ready-made phrases let people defend policies without ever picturing what the words mean. On Orwell's account this is a two-way cycle — bad thought produces bad language, and inherited bad language makes further bad thought easier — and his proposed remedy is a discipline of individual word choice: prefer the concrete word, cut the dead metaphor, attend to what the words would mean if one looked at them squarely. The development that seems to me to break something in this picture is that an increasing share of political and institutional language is now drafted, filled out, or rephrased by language models and predictive text. A civil servant, a press officer, or a legislator can begin with a fairly clear and even candid intention, while the sentences that reach the public are assembled statistically from exactly the stock of prefabricated phrases Orwell was diagnosing. That seems to move the laziness, insincerity, and euphemism out of the individual writer's moment-by-moment choices and into the system and its training data. I am trying to work out whether Orwell's central argument survives this change. Could you treat it as a question about premises rather than examples: which steps in his argument depend on there being an individual human author choosing the words, and can those steps be restated at the level of institutions, models, or the surrounding linguistic environment so that the argument retains its force? I want to know whether the doctrine still bites, needs repair, or largely stops applying when the worst prose is produced by no one in particular.
