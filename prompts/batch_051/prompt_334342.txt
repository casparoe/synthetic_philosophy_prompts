I teach an upper-level undergraduate epistemology seminar with 12 students and a 75-minute session. The students have not yet encountered the surprise exam paradox, and I want them to meet it as a live problem before it is named. Design a structured classroom game with no real stakes — a points currency is fine — in which the force of the paradox emerges from what students do, not from anything I say beforehand. By the end they should see three things: why the students in the original story feel rationally entitled to their backward-induction conclusion, where that reasoning slips, and why the instructor's announcement can still turn out to be true.

The opening announcement should have roughly this structure: there will be exactly one designated event across the next five rounds, and at the start of the designated round you will not be able to predict, on the basis of this announcement plus the public record, that it is that round. Each round the group must lodge predictions, or explicitly pass, under a payoff rule. To keep the reasoning from collapsing into an all-or-nothing deduction, make the predictions graded rather than binary: for example, each student must distribute a fixed number of points among the remaining rounds each round, with scoring that rewards points placed on the round that actually occurs. That way the eliminative reasoning shows up as a gradual reallocation of points, and the group can feel the pull toward concluding that no remaining round deserves them — and then the event occurs in a round they failed to predict anyway.

Please give me: the rules and any materials; a 75-minute timeline covering open play and a debrief; the point-scoring scheme; and a debrief that maps the group's round-by-round elimination onto the familiar backward induction from the last day backwards, maps the failed prediction onto the distinction between the announcement being inconsistent with the group's eventual state of knowledge and its being false of the actual round, and maps the public character of their predictions onto the role of common knowledge.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run the activity and how to steer it back without giving away the resolution too early, and be explicit about which feature of the paradox the activity cannot reproduce — for instance, the gap between real finite agents and the idealized common knowledge the argument assumes — and what I should supply myself in the last few minutes of the debrief.
