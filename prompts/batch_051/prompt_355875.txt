I am writing my final paper for an undergraduate course on the meaning of life, and I want to center it on the film Everything Everywhere All at Once. Please do not summarize the plot; what I am after is a critical reading that takes both the film and the philosophy seriously. Use two frameworks we covered in the course. One is Camus's absurdism: the human demand for meaning meets an indifferent universe, and the appropriate response is lucid revolt without appeal to transcendent meaning, suicide, or hope. The other is the contemporary analytic position, associated most with Susan Wolf, that a life is meaningful to the extent that subjective attraction meets objective worth, so meaning is neither a cosmic given nor a sheer feeling of significance but a matter of engaging wholeheartedly with things that deserve it.

I am especially interested in where the two lenses pull apart. Is the everything bagel a faithful image of the absurd, or does the film present something closer to depression, burnout, or affective nihilism, quietly dropping the philosophical problem while seeming to face it? Does Evelyn's final choice to stay in her messy, particular life and repair her relationships count as Camusian revolt, or does it smuggle in exactly the kind of hope and homely meaning Camus would refuse? Would Wolf say that Evelyn's ending is meaningful because her engagement finally attaches to genuinely valuable objects, or is the film making a more radical claim about affirming a life that still lacks any cosmic justification?

I want the reading to be faithful in both directions: use the philosophy to articulate what the film only gestures at, but also let the film test the frameworks, especially where a scene resists them. In particular, tell me honestly whether the film's resolution addresses the philosophical problem of absurdity or dissolves it into an emotional one. End by saying what the film contributes to this debate that reading Camus or the analytic literature alone cannot. Throughout, separate what is settled about Camus's view and the fitting-fulfillment view from what is genuinely contested among philosophers, and do not present a contested interpretation of either as the standard one.
