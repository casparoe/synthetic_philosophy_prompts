Before I retired I worked as a physicist, where a governing habit was to treat a system's present state as sufficient for its future evolution: history matters only insofar as it is encoded in the present. In my recent reading on practical rationality I have come to suspect that this is not quite right for decision-making, and I have been developing a distinction I would like checked.

I distinguish bookkeeping sunk costs from diagnostic sunk costs. A bookkeeping sunk cost is a past outlay that is now causally inert except that it cannot be recovered. A diagnostic sunk cost is a past outlay whose existence, and whose having been accepted, carries information about the agent's own future dispositions—for example, a long record of staying with difficult projects is evidence that one will persist in future projects, and abandoning the present project would weaken that evidence. My tentative principle is that honoring a sunk cost is irrational insofar as the cost is merely bookkeeping, but can be forward-looking rational insofar as the cost is diagnostic, because the act of abandonment itself reshapes a rational agent's model of her own reliability.

I would like an honest assessment. Is the distinction doing genuine work, or does it collapse into something already discussed under another name, such as self-signaling or project-level commitment? What are the strongest objections—cases in which a diagnostic sunk cost should still be ignored, or a bookkeeping sunk cost should still be honored—and is any of them fatal rather than merely uncomfortable?
