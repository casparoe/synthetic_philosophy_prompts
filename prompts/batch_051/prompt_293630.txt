A friend pressed this argument on me and I cannot find the flaw, though I find its conclusion unacceptable.

(1) Suppose Mara's failure to water your plants while you were away caused them to die; then an omission caused an outcome. (2) An omission is an absence rather than an event, so if an omission can be a cause at all, it can only be because the omitted action would have prevented the outcome. (3) But that counterfactual holds for everyone else who did not water the plants, not just Mara — the neighbor who noticed the drooping leaves, a plumber working next door, a stranger who had never heard of the plants — so if Mara's omission caused the death, each of theirs did too. (4) That cannot be right; those people's failures did not all cause the death. (5) Therefore Mara's omission did not cause the death. (6) Yet the same reasoning applies to every omission, so a physician who fails to administer clearly indicated insulin no more causes a patient's death than a total stranger does.

I would rather keep (1) than accept (6). Which step should I reject, and what would rejecting it commit me to about how causal judgments concerning omissions relate to contrastive focus, salience, or normative expectation?
