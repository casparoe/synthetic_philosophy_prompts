I work in the state archives, mostly shelving birth and death records, and last month a lottery made me the designated survivor for the Governor's address. I've been turning it over ever since, because the question of whether I was governor for a few hours is still not settled.

Section 3 of our Continuity of Government Act says: "Whenever the Governor, the Lieutenant Governor, the Secretary of State, the Treasurer and the Attorney General are assembled at a single location, an employee of the State chosen by lot that day shall be sequestered at a secure location and shall remain there for the duration of the assembly. Should all the officials so assembled be killed or rendered incapable, the employee then sequestered shall forthwith assume the office of Governor and exercise all the powers and duties thereof until a special election is held."

That evening all five officials were at the capitol for the Governor's speech. I was in a windowless room under the emergency operations building with two security officers. At 6:50 the smoke alarm went of, and the guards made me evacuate — fire code says everybody out, no exceptions. So from about 6:52 to 7:10 I was standing in the parking lot next to the building, outside the secure room. At 7:04 a bomb at the capitol killed all five officials. I was the only person who had been chosen as the designated survivor, so for about two hours the emergency staff treated me as acting governor, until the deputy attorney general, who was home with a fever and was the most senior legal officer left, got on the phone and said the words "employee then sequestered" mean I never assumed the office, because at the exact moment the officials were killed I wasn't sequestered. Then they stopped treating me as the governor.

I can see a few ways to read the law. (1) Plain words: at the moment of the blast I was not sequestered, so the condition wasn't met. (2) Purpose: the whole point is to have a live person kept out of harm's way, and that's exactly what I was; the fire alarm is the kind of detail the drafters never pictured. (3) What the drafters would have said if asked: I suspect they'd have said "don't be silly, he stayed away from the capitol, of course he takes over," but I can't know that. And then there's what an ordinary person reading the statute would have expected: probably that the survivor is whoever was picked and kept away, not somebody who forfeits the office because a smoke detector went off.

I'd like you to argue it from the text, from the purpose of the clause, from what the drafters would likely have said, and from what a reasonable reader would make of it, and tell me which approach should win in a case like this and why.

And there's the part that sits less easy with me. Even if the best reading is that I was governor, I'm a records clerk chosen by a random draw; nobody elected me, and I have no qualifications, and the only reason I was alive is a faulty smoke detector. Does the legal answer settle whether I ought to have acted as governor, or is there a separate ethical question about whether a lottery survivor should govern? I'm less interested in the legal fallout than in whether the legal answer and the moral answer can come apart here.
