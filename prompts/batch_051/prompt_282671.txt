A small jurisdiction is replacing its case-by-case anti-discrimination doctrine with a standing administrative mechanism. An independent Suspect Grounds Board maintains a public register of protected characteristics. Any trait may be nominated by a recognized interest group or by the Board itself. The Board assigns each registered trait a continuous suspectness score from 0 to 100, computed as a weighted sum: 30% immutability (or cost of self-directed change), 20% current social salience, 30% documented history of state and private discrimination, and 20% the group's political weakness relative to the median voter. The score sets the default burden a government must meet to justify any classification by that trait, on a sliding scale from minimal scrutiny near 0 to the strictest review at 80 and above, with intermediate scrutiny around 50. The Board must reconsider a trait's score whenever the legislature enacts a classification by that trait, so legislation feeds back into the scores. Interest groups may petition to add a trait, raise or lower a score, or remove a trait from the register.

Analyze this as a mechanism rather than as a constitutional position to endorse or reject. In exactly five numbered items, each no more than two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, walk through how it would actually behave: what it incentivizes among petitioning groups, legislators, and Board members; where it would be gamed first; what second-order social effects the public register itself would have; and which failure mode is most structural.
