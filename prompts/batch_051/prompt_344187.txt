I am a retired chemist who has been reading Hume's economic essays alongside more recent debates about monetary policy, and I keep returning to one of his central arguments wondering whether it survives the world we actually live in now.

Hume argued against the mercantilists that a nation's stock of money is not its wealth. Money, on his account, is only a device for circulating real goods and labor; in the long run the quantity of money adjusts to the level of prices, and an unfavorable balance of trade tends to correct itself automatically. In a metallic-currency world the mechanism ran like this: a trade deficit drains specie abroad, the domestic money supply contracts, domestic prices fall, exports cheapen while imports dear, and the balance swings back toward equilibrium. Doubling the money in circulation would, in the end, only double prices. He allowed that an inflow of money might stimulate industry during the interval before prices caught up, but the terminal state was simply a higher price level. From all this Hume drew a larger conclusion: trade between nations is not a zero-sum struggle over a fixed stock of precious metal, the prosperity of one's trading partners is not a threat, and the jealousy of trade between nations is a mistake.

That argument was built for commodity money — coin, bullion, convertible paper. We now use irredeemable fiat currency, central banks that set interest rates and supply reserves, and floating exchange rates, and the adjustment mechanism Hume relied on no longer operates as he described. A country can run a large current-account deficit for decades without undergoing a Humean internal deflation; the exchange rate, capital flows, and the position of reserve-currency issuers absorb much of the pressure. The link between the stock of money and the price level is also far looser than the specie-flow story assumes, and monetary expansion works through credit and asset prices as much as through a simple proportional rise in the price of goods.

My question is not historical but philosophical. Does Hume's argument survive this development? I want to know precisely which premise carries the weight: the quantity theory of money, the specie-flow adjustment mechanism, the claim that money is not itself wealth, the assumption that prices are flexible, or the identification of money with metal. Can some of these premises be discarded while keeping the core conclusions — that trade is mutually beneficial and that accumulating money is not the proper end of economic policy — and if so, what is the repaired argument? Or does the conclusion now rest on entirely different grounds, so that we should say plainly that Hume's original argument is dead even if his anti-mercantilist conclusion survives?
