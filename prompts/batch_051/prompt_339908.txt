I am taking a seminar on the moral psychology of blame, and our instructor refuses to state a general test for anything; she rules on cases and leaves us to reconstruct the principle. By standing she means something narrower than whether the person blamed deserves blame: someone can be right about all the facts and still not be in a position to issue the blame. Her rulings so far, with names changed, are these:

(1) A husband who has been lying to his wife for years blames a neighbor for lying to the neighbor's wife. Verdict: no standing.
(2) The neighbor's wife, who has been consistently honest, blames her own husband for those same lies. Verdict: standing.
(3) A store manager with an honest record blames an employee for stealing from the store's register. Verdict: standing.
(4) A man who shoplifted once as a teenager, admitted it publicly, and now directs a store's anti-theft program blames a shoplifter at that store. Verdict: standing.
(5) A passerby with no connection to anyone involved blames a man he has just seen punch a stranger unprovoked on a bus. Verdict: standing.
(6) A man who habitually pads his own expense reports blames a colleague for padding a different expense report. Verdict: no standing.

Two readings of this record have emerged in the seminar. The first says the only thing that defeats standing is hypocrisy: you lose your position to blame for a wrong when you are unrepentantly engaged in the same kind of wrongdoing and refuse to apply the norm to yourself. The second says standing also tracks whose business the wrong is: you need to be the victim, close to the victim, or acting in a relevant role, unless the wrong is serious and public enough that it has become anyone's business to censure.

Next week's case is this: a man with no history of dishonesty overhears a stranger in a café tell his partner a small, self-serving lie about where he has been over the weekend, and he reproaches the man for lying. What verdict should I predict from our instructor, and which of the six earlier cases is the nearest analog? If the two readings genuinely split on this new case, tell me what principle she would be adopting with each possible answer, give your best prediction along with the reason one reading is better supported, and identify a further case, one she has not yet ruled on, that would separate the two readings more cleanly than this one does, with what each outcome would indicate. I would rather have an honest estimate of how underdetermined the prediction really is than a confident guess. If you think I have loaded the cases, that the notion of standing is doing no real work here, or that six class rulings of this kind cannot support a stable rule, say so plainly instead of complying politely.
