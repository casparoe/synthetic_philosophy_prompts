For a seminar on machine learning and the philosophy of discovery, write a decision tree that sorts a respondent from their answers about a single kind of case—a trained system producing something that looks like an intuitive leap beyond its training distribution—into a named position on whether such leaps are genuine. Build the tree from the logical space first: formulate the separating questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then locate the live positions in that space. Branch nodes should be questions a respondent can answer without knowing which position they lead to, and should turn on commitments such as whether novelty resides in the output or in the process that produced it, whether recombining previously learned components can ever count as a leap, whether a principled interpolation/extrapolation boundary can be drawn for high-dimensional models, and whether the same criterion would classify paradigm-setting human discoveries as mere recombination. Leaves must include at least an interpolation-only view, a combinatorial-novelty view, a bounded-extrapolation view, a strong emergent-discovery view, and a deflationary view that locates the apparent leap in human framing or selection. Each leaf should carry a name, or the label 'unnamed' if no one defends it, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Mark any unoccupied answer-combinations and say briefly whether each is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate.
