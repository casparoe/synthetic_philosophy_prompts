Last night a friend and I spent the whole evening arguing about whether autonomous systems create a genuine responsibility gap, and we ended almost exactly where we began. His position was that when a self-driving car, an autonomous weapon, or a medical decision-support system causes serious harm, something disturbing happens to responsibility: the operator did not intend the outcome, the designers could not have foreseen the specific sequence of events, and the system itself is not a moral agent, so the familiar practice of identifying a culpable person and holding them to account has no proper target. On his view, any attempt to pin the harm on some human being is scapegoating dressed up as justice. I resisted by arguing that the gap is an artifact of an excessively retributive picture of responsibility. Once responsibility is understood less as backward-looking blame and more as a distributed, forward-looking matter—who must explain what happened, compensate the victim, update the system, and prevent recurrence—the alleged gap largely dissolves, because those roles can be assigned without anyone having willed the harm. We actually agreed on most of the descriptive facts about autonomy, foreseeability, and distributed design, and we agreed that someone should compensate victims and fix the system. What we could not budge each other on was whether the absence of a culpable individual is a real normative hole or simply the collapse of one particular picture of responsibility.

I have since been wondering why debates like ours so reliably stall, and I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than another round of arguments for one side. Is the dispute substantive or largely verbal—a disagreement about the word 'responsibility' that dissolves once we separate backward-looking blame, legal liability, answerability, and forward-looking practical responsibility? Or is it a disagreement about which cases should be treated as paradigmatic: individual agents with full control, versus the already-distributed responsibility found in organizations, professions, and engineering teams? Alternatively, is there a hidden empirical bet underneath, for instance about whether retaining individual blame is causally necessary for deterrence, public trust, or the psychological closure of victims after disasters? Give me your best diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, and say what, given that diagnosis, would count as progress—a distinction, a change of question, a kind of evidence, or the honest conclusion that no progress should be expected. Please also say how confident you are in your diagnosis overall, and single out the point you are least sure about.
