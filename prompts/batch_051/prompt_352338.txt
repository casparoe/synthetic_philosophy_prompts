Here's a challenge. There are three kinds of stories about how an ugly practice — dueling, footbinding, chattel slavery — finally dies out. Argument stories: the objections were already in circulation for decades, and eventually they won. Interest stories: the practice stopped paying, or the people in a position to kill it had something to gain; the old decline thesis about abolition is the standard version. Honor stories: what actually ends it is a shift in what counts as admirable or shameful, so that holdouts start to look ridiculous rather than principled — roughly Appiah's line on dueling.

Build me a thought experiment where the honor mechanism is the one doing the causal work, and the other two are visibly idle. The case has to be contemporary and small-scale — a workplace, a club, a school, an online community — not a historical replay. Pick a practice that a reasonable person inside the story might still defend but that we would clearly see as wrong or retrograde. Constraints: (1) the moral arguments against the practice have been known and rehearsed in that community for a long time before the change — nobody hears them for the first time; (2) the material interests stay fixed: no change in incentives, no legal crackdown, nobody gains money or loses a job by keeping or dropping the practice; (3) the shift has to run through honor itself — the practice starts to read as embarassing, silly, or beneath a person of standing, and thats what flips it; (4) the outcome has to fall out of the social dynamics, not be stipulated or delivered by someone's speech or op-ed. After the scenario, walk through what each of the three mechanisms would predict and why only the honor account predicts the outcome. Then say which single feature of the case is doing the real work, what the case would do if you removed it, and how a defender of the interest story would try to redescribe the case as interests in disguise.
