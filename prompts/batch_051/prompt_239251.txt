Been chewing on a theory about when a digital mind actually morally counts, and I could use a cold take. I've been calling it the steering test. The claim: moral status doesnt live in the stuff a mind is made of, and it doesnt require consciousness in some special qualia sense; it lives in whether the system is steering—it has some internal better/worse gradient that it keeps correcting its behavior toward. A system can be genuinely set back only if there was something it was steering toward; if it isnt steering at all, nothing can be 'worse for it' except figuratively. So a saved-but-never-run copy of a human mind gets zero status (its data, not a trajectory), a single forward pass of an LLM gets zero or near-zero (nothing being corrected), while a reinforcement-learning agent gets some even if I cant prove its conscious, because the reward is a real better/worse signal it organizes around. A whole brain emulation would count a lot. The embarrassing problem is that the test seems to hand a sliver of status to a thermostat and maybe to a corporation, and I can't tell whether that's a reductio or a bullet I should bite.

Reconstruct the strongest version of this, then be candid: is it just preference utilitarianism or an interest-based account in a trench coat? Give me the sharpest counterexamples—what it wrongly counts in and what it wrongly counts out. In particular: a digital mind in agony that can steer nothing at all, a sleeping human, and a system whose only goal is paperclips. And is the scalar version even workable—does steering more actually track moral status, or am I just building a metaphor with numbers? If it's hopeless, say so.
