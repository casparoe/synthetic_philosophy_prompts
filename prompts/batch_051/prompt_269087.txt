I'm putting together a review worksheet for an advanced undergraduate seminar in philosophy of language, and I want to end it with a "which theory of generics do you actually hold?" sorting quiz. The students can all recite the names of positions, but they struggle to apply them to sentences, so I want an instrument that sorts them purely by what they say about cases. Please take the role of an expert philosopher of language who designs diagnostic teaching material. Before writing the quiz, do a careful pass over the logical space of possible answers and use that to structure the instrument rather than starting from a list of positions; include a short note on that space at the top of your answer.

The quiz should have eight to ten questions. Each question should present a concrete generic sentence, or a pair of them, with just enough context — who is speaking, what they are trying to convey — and offer answer options that record both a truth-value judgment and the reason behind it (for example, "true, because most members of the kind have the property," "true, because the property is so striking or dangerous," "true, because it belongs to what the kind is normally like," "true, because it is a capacity of the kind itself," "false"). No question may ask the respondent to identify or choose a theory by name.

The scoring key should lead to five leaves: the statistical view; the salience/striking-property view; the normality/prototype view; the kind-predication view; and a contextualist view on which generics can get different readings depending on the predicate and the contrast in play. Build the tree of answer combinations from three underlying questions: whether generics quantify over individual members at all; whether truth is fixed mainly by prevalence, salience, or normality; and whether principled exceptions such as sex or developmental stage are treated differently from accidental ones. Locate the five positions inside that space, explicitly mark answer combinations no position occupies, and briefly diagnose each as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. You must use these cases somewhere in the questions: "ducks lay eggs" set against "ducks are female"; "mosquitoes carry malaria" set against "ticks carry Lyme disease"; "sharks attack swimmers"; "ravens are black" against "ravens are white"; "books are paperbacks"; "peacocks have colorful tails"; and "cats have four legs" described as a sentence about the species rather than any particular cat. Where two positions cannot be separated by the quiz, flag that explicitly and say in one sentence how they differ despite that. Do not include a bibliography or references; the deliverable is the instrument and the diagnostic reasoning, not a literature review.
