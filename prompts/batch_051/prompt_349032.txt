You are a professional philosopher of language and metaphysics, with a special interest in the semantics of fictional discourse. Begin with some preliminary brainstorming: sketch the strongest versions of the two positions described below before committing yourself. Think carefully, and write your answer in British English. Your response must be no more than 800 words.

For years I have found myself torn between a theory I admire and an intuition I cannot shake. The theory is the direct-reference, or Millian, account of proper names: a name's semantic contribution is exhausted by its bearer — no descriptive content, no Fregean sense, no way of thinking of the object. Its virtues are systematic. It is economical and uniform, it gets the behaviour of co-referring names in ordinary extensional contexts right, and it accounts for the informativeness of true identity statements involving names without multiplying semantic machinery.

But I also have a firm intuition that sentences containing the empty name 'Sherlock Holmes' are perfectly meaningful, and that some of them are true or false. 'Sherlock Holmes is a detective' is true in the way claims made within fiction are true; 'Sherlock Holmes was a dentist' is false; and, most awkwardly for the Millian, 'Sherlock Holmes does not exist' does not present itself as a gappy failure to express a proposition at all. It presents itself as a plain truth. If a name's meaning is simply its bearer, then an empty name should contribute nothing, and any sentence in which it occurs should be semantically incomplete rather than true or false. Yet that is very far from how these sentences strike me.

I am not after a simple adjudication. I want a negotiated verdict. Present both sides at their strongest, then tell me under what conditions the intuition should be debunked and dropped, under what conditions the theory should be amended or abandoned, and whether there is a distinction on which both may be kept.

Work through at least the following candidate resolutions, and be explicit about what would justify each one and what it costs. First, the intuition might give way: the apparent truth of these sentences might be explained away pragmatically, as a projection from pretence, from story-relative assessment, or from a metalinguistic claim about the name itself, so that literal semantic content remains Millian throughout. Second, the theory might give way: one might take fictional names to refer to abstract artefacts, or allow empty names to have contentful but referentless senses, thereby preserving the intuitions while retaining a direct-reference story for ordinary names. Third, a division of labour might reconcile them: truth in fiction and negative existentials would be handled by a pretence-layer or by a shift in what the sentence is about, while base-level semantics stays direct-referential.

For each candidate, say whether the resolution is stable, and what further data or argument would unsettle it. If you think part of the dispute is merely verbal, identify that part precisely. Close by stating which outcome you yourself would defend — the intuition dropped, the theory amended, or both preserved on a distinction — and describe what would have to be shown to change your mind.

This is an exercise in reasoned negotiation rather than exegesis: cite nothing, and do not supply a reading list.
