Last month a man outside the station asked me for five pounds. He was calm and specific. He said that unless I paid, he would later acquire control of a machine capable of creating a vast number of conscious simulations, and he would arrange for everyone who had refused him money to be tortured inside it. I assumed he was unwell, laughed, and walked on. It would not have occurred to me to pay, and if a friend had paid I would have thought them credulous. Yet the same week I renewed my household insurance, hesitated over whether to fly because of a risk of roughly one in several million, and bought a lottery ticket. I evidently find it natural to act on very small probabilities of large losses and sometimes large gains. That left me uneasy. Someone later told me this scenario has a name in decision theory: Pascal's mugging. The thought is that once expected value is taken literally, a small probability multiplied by a sufficiently large alleged penalty or reward can dominate every other consideration, so a person can extort arbitrary sums simply by asserting an astronomically bad outcome. But my reaction to the stranger and my reaction to insurance or flight risk diverge. Is that divergence defensible? Can one find a principle that licenses ignoring the stranger without undermining ordinary caution about very small probabilities of serious harm? Or would the best principle force me to give up everyday precautions that are statistically similar? I want the strongest case for treating the two differently, and an honest verdict on whether I should keep the asymmetry.
