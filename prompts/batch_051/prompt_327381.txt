A friend and I have just spent a long argument over one comparison, and I want the comparison itself audited rather than another round of advocacy. Her position was that what adult children owe their parents is best understood on the model of friendship. On this view—I have since learned it is called the friendship model—the years of care and sacrifice a parent puts in do not create a debt to be repaid later, any more than the good things a friend does for you create a running balance to be settled. Obligations, if they exist, arise instead from the ongoing relationship itself: affection, trust, a shared history of goodwill, current contact and mutual concern—not from a ledger of past benefits. She used this to defend a strong conclusion: a grown child who is estranged from an emotionally distant or difficult parent owes that parent nothing beyond what they would owe any stranger in comparable need, and staying close to a hard parent is not the discharge of a duty but more like keeping up a friendship one has chosen to maintain.

I resisted this because something about the word friendship seemed to be doing the loading, but I struggled to say exactly where the comparison gives way. When I pushed back that a childhood of care looks more like a debt, she replied that debts are precisely repayable and then finished, whereas the obligations in close personal relationships are open-ended and not settled by any particular transfer—and I did not have a good answer in the moment.

I would like you to audit the analogy in detail, rather than just tell me who won. Lay out what corresponds to what: parent and child, the years of care, the obligations, the estrangement, the affection—what plays the role of what in the friendship picture. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for her conclusion from the ones that do not. For instance, the relationship is unchosen on the child's side, radically asymmetric in its early years, and structured by dependency rather than by the rough equality friends enjoy; say which of these actually undercut the conclusion and which are incidental. I especially want to know what the image of friendship quietly imports—voluntariness, symmetry, reciprocity, parity, the priority of feeling over obligation—and whether her conclusion survives once those imports are made explicit. If the friendship model fails in important respects, say what the better framework would be: debt, gratitude, role obligation, a need-based view, something hybrid, or something else.

Finally, keep the answer disciplined about the state of the debate: distinguish what is fairly well settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested reading as if it were the consensus.
