I cover AI for a general publication, and for years I've had a hard rule: I do not write that a model "believes," "wants," "expects," or "understands" anything. I rewrite all of that as what the system was trained to predict or what its designers intended—so I say "the system was optimized to output answers users rate as helpful" rather than "the system is trying to be helpful." I think of this as resisting anthropomorphism, and the intentional-stance line of thinking seems to support it: you're allowed to talk in belief terms when treating the thing as an agent is the best way to predict it, and whether that's true of these systems is exactly what I don't want to concede. But I've started to suspect the rule only looks clean on paper. Even "trained to predict the next token" smuggles in a task and a goal, and my readers leave with a folk-psychological picture either way. So: is my policy defensible as it stands, or should I treat belief-talk as a pragmatic shorthand that's fine as long as it's flagged as shorthand? Is there a version of the rule that keeps the anti-anthropomorphism without pretending I can purge all intentional vocabulary—and how would I know if I've drawn that line in the right place?
