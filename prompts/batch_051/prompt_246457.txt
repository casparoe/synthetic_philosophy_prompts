Just got back from a two-hour argument with a friend about Friedman's old positive economics essay and I think I've come around to the F-twist: assumptions don't need to be realistic, they need to deliver predictions, full stop. Firms don't literally solve maximization problems and billiard players don't literally compute trajectories, but the models act as if they do and their predictions land, so who cares. My friend kept insisting that a model with false guts can't explain anything even when it predicts, and that nobody would accept that tradeoff in physics.

I want the actual case against the view I now hold, not just my friend's line. What would have to be true, or what cases or arguments would have to work, for the F-twist to be wrong or at least badly qualified? Rank them from fatal to merely awkard. Be honest about which ones a committed F-twist defender can shrug off as not really counting, and which ones they'd have to take seriously. My hunch is the strongest pressure comes from regime change: a model that predicts beautifully inside one setting and then falls apart in a nearby one because its assumptions were quietly load-bearing, in a way the just-judge-by-predictions rule couldn't warn you about. Is that actually a defeater, or is it just a model being used past its domain? And is the F-twist self-insulating in a bad way, where any failed prediction gets blamed on the application instead of on the false assumption?
