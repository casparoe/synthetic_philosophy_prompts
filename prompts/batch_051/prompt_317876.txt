I help run a small private research funder (mostly theoretical biology and a bit of economics), and we've been arguing internally about whether to restructure our grant process around Lakatos's picture of research programmes instead of paper-counting. The scheme on the table is this. Every applicant has to declare a 'programme': a hard core of commitments that won't be directly tested, a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses and modelling choices that can be revised, and a positive heuristic saying roughly what kind of novel predictions the programme commits to producing. Our reviewers then score each proposal on a progressiveness index: how specific the promised novel predictions are, what fraction of the programme's recent predictions were confirmed rather than falsified, whether its empirical content grew over the last cycle, and whether anomalies got handled by independently confirmed changes rather than ad hoc patching. A programme that stays 'progressive' on this index get's its grants renewed almost automatically; a programme rated 'degenerating' for two reviews in a row gets cut, and the same group can't submit under a knew hard core for a year. We'd make exceptions for a few big-name labs so the policy doesn't die at the first departmental meeting.

I want you to actually walk through this thing, not just tell me whether it's a good idea in the abstract. Who games it first, and how? What does it do to fields where predictions are messy or slow, and to high-variance work that will mostly fail? What happens to the relationship between the main text of a paper and the 'programme' section of the grant, once everyone knows the index is doing the real filtering? Does the progressiveness index itself get Calvinballed (rules quietly rewritten to fit the score), and can any of that be fixed by making the index harder to game, or would that just push the gaming somewhere else? You're an expert in institutional design and philosophy of science; start by laying out the first-order incentives, then the second-order ones, and be concrete about the points where the scheme falls apart.
