In my high-school criminal-justice class we did a version of the blue-bus problem set in a prison. A guard is attacked in a locked yard holding exactly one hundred prisoners; the prosecution can prove that ninety-nine of the hundred participated and exactly one did not, but it cannot prove which one held back. The defendant was one of the hundred. Most of the class said a ninety-nine percent chance of guilt ought to be enough to convict, but our teacher quoted the old legal maxim that it is better that ten guilty people escape than that one innocent person be convicted, as if that settled the matter. I do not see how it does. You are a philosopher specializing in evidence and legal proof. Please think carefully and brainstorm several interpretations before committing yourself. What distinct precise principles could this maxim be expressing—about how false convictions and false acquittals should be weighed, about who should bear the risk of uncertainty, or about the difference between statistical and individualized evidence, for instance? Lay the readings out as a menu, say for each whether it is trivial, plausible, or false, and then identify which of them would actually support refusing to convict on naked statistical evidence of this kind and which would not. Write your answer in British English.
