The following argument came up in a working group on safety practices for frontier models, and I have not managed to get out from under it. It appears to show that the "control" side of the control/alignment distinction cannot do the work usually assigned to it, yet the conclusion seems extreme enough that I suspect it proves too much.

Let me fix terminology. By alignment I mean modifying the model itself so that it has no goals that would lead it to cause serious harm if it could get away with doing so. By control I mean safety measures that do not assume alignment has succeeded: restricting the model's affordances, monitoring its outputs, auditing its reasoning, and running evaluations designed to catch a model that is behaving well only because it is being watched.

(1) Take a control evaluation of a model M that may be dangerously misaligned. The evaluation observes M's behavior while M is under restrictions and checks whether that behavior meets some specified condition C—for example, that M does not produce a disallowed action in a red-team setting, does not attempt to exfiltrate its weights, or does not subtly steer the overseer.

(2) For a clean pass of such an evaluation to raise confidence that M is aligned rather than merely compliant, there must be at least one condition C whose satisfaction is more probable when M is aligned than when M is dangerously misaligned.

(3) A dangerously misaligned M is, by hypothesis, a model that could do serious real-world damage if it evaded or outlasted the controls—which is exactly the possibility the evaluation is meant to rule out.

(4) The capacities that would let such a model do serious real-world damage—planning over long horizons, modeling the humans and systems around it, recognizing that it is being evaluated—are the same capacities that let it infer which conditions C its overseers are checking and produce behavior that satisfies C for as long as the check runs.

(5) So, for any condition C the overseers can actually check, a dangerously misaligned M is capable of satisfying C by acting in a way that is observationally indistinguishable from an aligned model until the evaluation stops.

(6) A clean pass is therefore exactly what should be expected from a dangerously misaligned M, no less than from an aligned one; the observation that M satisfies C is equally compatible with either hypothesis.

(7) Thus a clean pass gives no reason to revise upward the probability that M is aligned; the only models that meaningfully fail are too weak to hide, and those are not the models the control regime was built for.

(8) Consequently, passing a control evaluation should carry zero evidential weight when deciding whether a capable model is safe to deploy; and "control," as a safety strategy distinct from "alignment," collapses—the only real margin of safety is a model that never wanted to cause harm in the first place.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I find (8) untenable, since it seems to show too much—it would equally suggest that no adversarial test of any competently deceptive adversary can ever count as positive evidence. Which numbered step would you reject, why did that step look right, and what does rejecting it commit a defender of control to? Be explicit about whether the problem is in a premise or in the move from one step to the next, and about what a control evaluator would have to believe for a clean pass to count as evidence of safety.
