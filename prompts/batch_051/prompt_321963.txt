Ok so I'm in a seminar on Kant's ethics and we just spent a session on his views about animals, and I can't figure out wether he's actually being consistent or just trying to have it both ways. On the one hand, in the Groundwork he seems pretty explicit that only rational beings can count as ends in themselves, and nonrational animals have merely relative worth, as things, so there can't be direct duties toward them. On the other hand, in the Doctrine of Virtue part of the Metaphysics of Morals he just as clearly says we have a duty not to treat animals cruelly or kill them brutally, but he traces that duty back to ourselves: being cruel to animals reduces our capacity for compassion, and that in turn weakens a natural disposition that Kant thinks is important for how we treat other human beings. So it sounds like the duty is ultimately about preserving our own moral equipment, not about the animal at all.

Those two moves look like they're in genuine tension to me. If an animal is just a thing, why would the prohibition on cruelty be any stronger than a contingent piece of psychological advice, and why would it bind someone whose sympathy wouldn't be dulled by kicking a dog? But maybe I'm reading the Groundwork too flatly, or maybe indirect duty is doing more work for Kant than I'm giving it credit for, or maybe he actually changed his mind between the two books. Could you walk me through the most plausible reconciliations — different senses of duty, the role of sympathy in moral character, what indirect duty actually amounts to for him — and then give me a verdict on which readings are honest and which ones are special pleading? I'd rather you not end on a wishy-washy both-sides note; commit to a view even if it's a qualified one. Please write in American English.
