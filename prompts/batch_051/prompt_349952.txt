In the Pensées Pascal appears, at first glance, to make two incompatible kinds of claim about justice. In one set of fragments he says that justice unsupported by force is impotent, while force unsupported by justice is tyrannical, and that since human beings could not make what is just prevail by its own strength, they have instead conferred the name of justice on whatever is strong. In another set of fragments he says that custom is the whole of equity for the single reason that it is received, and that laws owe their authority not to their justice but to their being laws and long accepted. The first seems to presuppose a standard of justice independent of force, which power ought to serve; the second seems to dissolve justice into habit, interest, and the self-love of the strong. Are these two strands of the Pensées genuinely in tension, or can they be reconciled? Lay out the strongest candidate reconciliations — for instance, a contrast between an ideal or prelapsarian justice and merely human positive justice, an Augustinian account of the fall and the corruption of reason, an epistemic thesis that unaided human beings cannot know true justice, or another distinction of your own — and then take a definite position on which reconciliation is most faithful to Pascal's larger project, explaining where the others amount to special pleading.
