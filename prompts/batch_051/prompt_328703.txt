Something I keep chewing on: when a lab releases a new model, the headline result is a big table of benchmark scores — math, code, reading comp, maybe some "agentic" tasks — and the numbers are averages. Accuracy, pass@k, mean reward, sometimes a standard deviation if you're lucky. The implicit message is that average-case behavior on held-out evals is enough to license deployment. But then for safety the same lab will publish a red-team appendix that is basically "we adverserially probed it and here are the bad outputs we found." No worst-case bound, no coverage guarantee, no estimate of how much of the input space they actually searched — just a list of failures that happened to turn up. So capabilities get mean-and-variance treatment, and safety gets "we found some bad stuff or we didn't."

What theory of risk, or of what it means for a system to be safe enough, does that practice presuppose? Does it commit the lab to treating expected performance over the deployment distribution as the relevant quantity, with tail failures assumed small enough to round down? Or does it commit them to the view that worst-case guarantees for LLMs are impossible in practice, so the best you can do is produce existence evidence of harm and hope the red team was thorough? Is the practice even coherent, using average-case logic for capabilities and worst-case logic for safety at the same time, or are those two different models of what the artifact is?

And I dont just want a survey of options. Give me a committed answer: reconstruct the strongest version of the implicit theory, say flat out whether you think it's defensible or confused, and tell me the one concrete change you'd make to the practice if you ran an eval team.
