I am drafting a one-year postdoctoral fellowship proposal in macrohistory, and I want it organized around a question that is precise enough to be settled by evidence but not so narrow that a null result is uninteresting. The background I am working from is the secular-cycles tradition in cliodynamics, associated with Turchin's structural-demographic theory. On that theory, large agrarian polities cycle through phases of expansion, strain, crisis, and recovery. The engine is demographic: population growth expands the labor supply and depresses real wages; elite numbers and elite expectations grow faster than the positions and resources available to them; the resulting competition among elites fragments the ruling coalition, undermines state finances, and, together with popular immiseration, drives political instability upward until a crisis restores the balance. The theory's appeal is that it unifies a wide range of phenomena and derives quantitative predictions about instability from a small set of slow-moving variables.

My half-formed worry is that the theory's central explanatory variable, elite overproduction, is dangerously elastic. It can be operationalized as too many elite persons, as too many aspiring elite persons relative to the number of offices, or as a rise in elite consumption norms that makes positions that were once adequate feel inadequate. Each reading is measured differently, and it is often possible to tell a cycle's story in terms of whichever reading fits the evidence best. If the variable is not fixed independently of the outcome it is meant to predict, the theory risks becoming a flexible redescription of the instability it claims to explain.

Turn this into a short proposal. State the question precisely, explain why the existing uses of elite overproduction leave that question open, and lay out an approach that tests whether some non-elastic operationalization of elite overproduction predicts instability better than the obvious alternatives (for instance, fiscal strain alone or population pressure alone). I am open to any historical case or comparison of cases you recommend, provided the data could realistically be assembled within a year from published sources. Say what a positive result would establish, what a null result would force me to concede, and what would make the project too large for one year.
