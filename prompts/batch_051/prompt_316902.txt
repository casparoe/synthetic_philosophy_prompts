I've got a friend in statistics who keeps insisting that probability isn't just a handy convention for tracking uncertainty — it's the logic of partial belief, the only coherent way to assign degrees of confidence to propositions. He says any rival system of confidence scores, if it violates the usual probability rules, is making the same sort of error as believing a contradiction, just with different-looking numbers. He waves at some theorem, but I don't want to argue about a proof; I want to know whether the claim itself can be tested.

So here's the challenge. Take a concrete rival: a system that scores its confidence in A and its confidence in not-A independently, and reports 0.7 for both. What observable behaviour — in its predictions, its bets, how it updates, whatever you think is fair — would show that this system is genuinely incoherent, as opposed to just using '0.7' to mean something different by 'confidence'? Design the strongest test you can. Tell me what result would favour the view that probability is the unique logic of plausible reasoning, what result would favour the view that it's just one convention among others, and be blunt about what the test could never settle even in principle, however good the observations get.
