I'm putting together a revision handout for an introductory philosophy of biology module, and I want a poem — a mnemonic really, but a proper poem — so that students stop just parrot the definitions back to me. The distinction I keep failing to get across is between two ways of understanding what it is to say that a biological trait has a function. On the one hand there's the etiological, or selected-effects, account: the function of a trait is the effect it was selected for in the past, the job that explains why the trait spread through the population. It's backward-looking, a matter of history and ancestry. On the other hand there's the causal-role account: the function of a trait is what it currently does in the system it sits in, the contribution it makes to some capacity of the organism right now. Both accounts happily agree that the function of the heart is to pump blood, but they mean rather different things when they say it.

Here's what I'd like. A Petrarchan sonnet with the volta at line nine, and the volta should do actual philosophical work rather than just being a rhetorical turn. Let the octave render the etiological account in its own idiom — selection, ancestors, why the trait is there. Then let the sestet turn, at the volta, into the causal-role account in its own idiom — the organism as a going concern now, capacities, present contributions to a present system. Keep the heart pumping blood running through the whole poem, so the form itself demonstrates that the two accounts converge on the same verdict for the heart. And, if the rhyme scheme will allow, end the sestet with a closing couplet that leaves the reader with the thought that the two accounts can come apart: a structure can have a current role without any selection history for it, or a trait can keep an old etiological function while no longer performing it. Don't explain the theory outside the poem — the poem should do the teaching. And it should actually scan if read aloud.
