Here's the deal. I'm a philosophy professor and in my early modern unit there's a standing fight between two camps of students, and I'd like to formalize it as an adversarial collaboration and have them produce a joint statement — so draft the document they'd end up with. The two camps: the rupture camp holds that the century-and-a-half from Copernicus to Newton was a real, unusually fast and unified transformation in what counted as knowledge of nature; the continuity/constructivist camp holds that "the Scientific Revolution" is a retrospective label lumping together changes that were gradual, heterogeneous, and mostly continuous with medieval natural philosophy, and that the revolution was something later historians did to the period rather than something the period did to itself. I want it formatted as a numbered agreed-claims section first (claims both can sign, no weasel wording — about the textual record, about what actually changed in astronomy and mechanics, about what the label obscures), then a disagreements table whose entries each camp would accept as a fair statement of it's own view rather than a strawman of the other's. Then the cruxes. I suspect one crux is whether a period counts as real only if its participants shared a project or self-conception, or whether a retrospectively detectable pattern is enough; another is how much weight to put on words meaning different things across the divide — "experimentum" already had a live medieval usage, "law of nature" did some new work, "hypothesis" shifts too. For each crux, give a concrete episode or piece of evidence both sides would agree bears on it — and this is where I want it in the weeds: Kepler is a great borderline case, since he's doing genuine quantitative astronomy while doing what looks to us like astrology and theology, and the medieval calculators already had some of the kinematics in place. Write down in advance what each camp predicts that evidence shows. And if part of the fight turns out to be verbal, say which part and why. Start the response directly with the document itself, no preface or commentary. Oh, and leave out citations; the students will add those.
