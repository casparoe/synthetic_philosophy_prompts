I have been trying to state Sellars's attack on the Given carefully, and in doing so I built an argument from premises I find individually compelling to a conclusion I find unacceptable. I need a diagnosis: which numbered step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it commit one to?

(1) A belief is justified for a subject only if it is justified by something the subject has or does—a state, event, or performance within that subject's cognitive life.
(2) No belief could be justified by an infinite chain of further beliefs, and no belief could be justified by a circle. So any justified empirical belief must rest on a foundational item that justifies other items without itself standing in need of justification.
(3) The only plausible foundational items are either sensory episodes themselves—a particular look, sound, or texture—or the judgments by which one reports such episodes, for example, the judgment that something now looks red.
(4) If the foundation consists of sensory episodes, then it consists of non-propositional states: a look or a feeling is neither true nor false and carries no conceptual content.
(5) A non-propositional epistemic item cannot stand in an evidential or logical relation to a belief; it can at most cause a belief.
(6) Causing a belief is not justifying it. A conviction produced by a blind cause, however reliably, is not a conviction the subject holds for a reason.
(7) Therefore, if sensory episodes are the foundation, foundational beliefs are not justified, and nothing inferred from them is justified.
(8) If the foundation consists instead of appearance-judgments, then the foundation is propositional and can serve as evidence. But a judgment can be false or conceptually miscast, and a judgment that can be false or miscast seems to stand in need of justification as much as the beliefs built on it.
(9) So the foundation must be both propositional, in the manner of a judgment, and immune from any need for justification in a way judgments never are. This is exactly the hybrid Sellars calls the Myth of the Given.
(10) Sellars's position is that no such hybrid can exist: what is propositional is already answerable to further justification, and what is never answerable is not propositional.
(11) Hence, by (2), if every chain of justification must bottom out in something, and no item can serve as that bottom, then no empirical belief is justified.
(12) But that is not a conclusion I can accept: it would turn Sellars's critique of empiricism into a proof of global empirical skepticism.

I am most inclined to retain (1) and (6), and more willing to give up (2) than (6). But I suspect that preference may itself be the weak point. Which step or premise is the right one to reject, and what is the philosophical cost of rejecting it? If the error is in an inference rather than an assumption, identify the exact move.
