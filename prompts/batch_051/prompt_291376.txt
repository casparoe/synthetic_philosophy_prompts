I have been developing a position I call *graded transparency*, and I want an honest assessment of it. The background is the ongoing debate over whether photographs are transparent—whether, when I look at a photograph of a person, I literally see that person in something like the way I see her through a telescope, or whether I only see a representation and infer from it how she looked. Most participants treat transparency as all-or-nothing; I suspect that is the mistake.

My proposal is that a visual device is transparent to the degree that two conditions are jointly satisfied. *Source-dependence*: the imagery reaching my eye must be counterfactually dependent on the target object through a reliable, non-intentional channel, so that it carries visual information about the object rather than merely a depictor's beliefs about it. *Egocentric uptake*: the experience must also preserve the first-person spatial relations that ordinary seeing uses for action—roughly, where the object is relative to me and how the scene would shift as I move.

On this two-dimensional view, windows and telescopes are highly transparent; photographs score high on source-dependence but low on egocentric uptake, and so are only partially transparent; paintings score low on both and are not transparent at all.

I have three questions. First, is this a genuine middle position, or does it collapse into one of the existing sides of the debate? Second, is it already worked out under another name—perhaps simply the standard objection that photographs lack the spatial information needed for genuine perception? Third, where does the scale break? I am particularly unsure about photogrammetric scans, live video feeds, and virtual-reality displays, which seem to preserve egocentric spatial relations without a direct optical path from object to eye, and about whether my second condition is really a condition on seeing rather than merely on acting.
