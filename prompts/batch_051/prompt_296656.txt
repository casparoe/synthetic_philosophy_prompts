Most of us treat this arrangement as unremarkable: commercial aircraft fly over residential neighborhoods every day, imposing on each person below a small risk of death from a crash, a risk to which no one below has consented, for the benefit of distant passengers and shippers. Now imagine a private aerobatics club that proposes to fly over the same neighborhood just as often, in aircraft built and maintained to the same standard, imposing exactly the same risk per flyover on each resident, purely for the pilots' own enjoyment. Assume the pilots value that enjoyment at least as highly as the airline's passengers and shippers value their flights. I take it that most of us would judge the two practices very differently: the first seems an ordinary and acceptable feature of modern life, while the second seems like the sort of thing residents should be able to veto, and which the law should treat far more harshly.

Yet the expected harm imposed on the people below is identical. The differences are that the first risk is familiar, publicly regulated, and embedded in a practice many people use, while the second is novel, private, and benefits only the pilots. My question is whether any of these differences can justify the asymmetry, or whether familiarity and mutuality are doing moral work they cannot bear. In particular, consider a resident who never flies and never uses goods carried by air: does the first practice still have a defense against her complaint that the second practice would lack? Please identify the strongest candidate justification for the asymmetry, test it against that resident and against a novel technology that later becomes familiar, and say whether the asymmetry should be kept, narrowed, or abandoned. Finally, state how confident you are in your conclusion and identify the single point on which you are least certain.
