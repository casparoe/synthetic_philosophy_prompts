So I've been burned by Simpson's paradox in real life recently — my mom was comparing two treatments for kidney stones and the overall success numbers pointed one way while the per-size breakdown pointed the other, and I realized how easy it is to see the aggregate and not notice what's hiding underneath. I teach a data-literacy unit in a community-college critical thinking class, about 20 students, one 50-minute session. I want an in-class game that makes them run into the reversal themselves before anyone names it, so they don't just file it away as another stats quirk.

Could you design the activity? It should be a game with rules and small stakes (candy or points, no real money), where the class generates or handles the data themselves and has to commit to a choice at least twice — once seeing only the aggregate and once seeing the breakdown. The numbers should be rigged so the paradox actually shows up, but the students shouldn't be told its rigged until the debrief. Include roles or teams, materials, and a timeline for the 50 minutes. Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the structure of Simpson's paradox: the lurking variable, why the aggregate and subgroup comparisons can both be true without contradiction, and which treatment you should actually pick for a new patient depending on what you know about them. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which part of the real phenomenon the activity can't reproduce.
