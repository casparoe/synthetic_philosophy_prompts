Suppose that, from the next academic year, a country comparable in size and wealth to Denmark makes Wisdom a compulsory secondary-school subject from ages eleven to sixteen, with national examinations, and that within a few years universities and large employers treat the result much as they now treat mathematics. Predict, in a reasoned way, what follows over the next thirty years. In your answer, lay out the mechanisms rather than a narrative: which philosophical accounts of wisdom — practical judgement, epistemic humility, self-knowledge, perspective-taking, skill in handling uncertainty — will be favoured by the pressures of a mass examination system, and which will quietly drop out because they resist standardised assessment; how the curriculum is likely to drift; whether the enterprise can succeed if wisdom is, as some traditions hold, acquired only through experience and reflection rather than instruction; and how the policy will come to be judged at the end, including the risk that the subject collapses into a measurable substitute such as factual knowledge about ethical traditions, formal decision techniques, or emotional regulation. I am less interested in an overall verdict than in a visible chain of reasoning, with each link identified as robust or conjectural and with a statement of what would have to be true for the prediction to fail.
