I spend much of my time reporting on the gap between what voters decide and what experts think they can safely decide, and over the years I have noticed an asymmetry that I cannot quite explain away. When a policy question is moderate in scope and technically manageable—a drug-safety ruling, a change to an emissions standard, a recalibration of a school-funding formula—there is broad consensus that it should be settled by agencies, advisory bodies, and elected representatives, with voters shaping the general direction at elections. Almost no one argues that next year's monetary policy rule should be put to a popular vote. Yet when a question is largest, most technically dense, and hardest to reverse—Britain's exit from the European Union, California ballot measures on rents, sentencing, and energy regulation, Swiss initiatives on immigration and corporate taxation—many of the same people insist that nothing short of a direct popular vote can confer legitimacy, and then treat the outcome as more binding than ordinary legislation. In short, we delegate the complexities we can manage and reserve the unmanageable ones for the mechanism least equipped to process detail, while granting that mechanism the greatest finality.

I want to know whether anything justifies this pattern, rather than merely explaining it psychologically. Candidate justifications come to mind: direct votes may be the only legitimate way to decide questions that touch national identity or the fundamental rules of political life, because representative bodies would face conflicts of interest or would lack authority to speak for the people as a whole; or referendums may perform a function of public authorization that remains valuable even when voters are poorly informed. But both justifications strain against nearby cases: many ballot measures are narrow, technical, and interest-laden rather than constitutional, and parliaments routinely handle identity- and rights-laden questions without any obvious crisis of legitimacy. I would like the strongest principle you can find that generates the asymmetry, a candid test of that principle against the awkward cases, and an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
