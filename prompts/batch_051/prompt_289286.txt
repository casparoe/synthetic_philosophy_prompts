As a novelist, I spend a good deal of time thinking about the difference between doing the right thing and doing it in a way that earns admiration, and I have noticed a pattern in ordinary moral judgment that I do not have a name for. Consider three people who each give up a weekend to help a sister through a crisis. The first is moved by the fact that her sister is in trouble. The second is moved by the thought that this is what a dutiful sister would do. The third, it emerges, is moved by the fact that she stands to inherit the house. The acts and their consequences are identical, but our judgments of the three people differ sharply. It is not simply that some motives are self-interested and some are not: the first two are both unselfish, and yet many people still feel that something distinguishes the first from the second. The pattern seems to be that we reserve a certain kind of credit for the person who is moved by the very thing that makes the act right. But that seems to require that an act have one privileged feature in virtue of which it is right, fixed independently of how the agent sees it, and I am not convinced that is always so. Is there an established name for this demand that a person's motive answer to the particular feature that makes their act right? Is that demand defended anywhere as a real requirement, or is it only a habit of folk judgment? And if no single name fits, how should I carve the phenomenon so that the first person and the second come apart without my having to posit a single privileged feature in virtue of which an act is right?
