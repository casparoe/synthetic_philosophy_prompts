I keep tripping over the same move in arguments about whether psychopaths have moral knowledge and are morally responsible. One side says psychopaths "know right from wrong, they just don't care," and uses that to treat them as fully culpable. The other side says no, they don't know it the way the rest of us do; something is missing that the word "know" is covering up. I spent my career in biology and neuroscience before retirement, and the habit I can't shake is that a claim isn't a claim until you've said what you'd observe to test it. But I've realized I don't actually know what this sentence is predicting.

So take the sentence "he knows right from wrong" as said about a psychopath. Lay out the different precise claims it could be making, as a menu. I can already see a few candidates, and there are probably others:

- the person can state the rule, or point to the right answer on a multiple-choice test;
- the person can correctly classify novel cases;
- the person has the right emotional response to the wrongness (some kind of "appreciation" that goes beyond classification);
- the person takes the rule to apply to themselves and not just to other people;
- the person is motivated not to do the thing; etc.

For each candidate, tell me: which ones would actually support the claim that psychopaths are fully culpable in the way the first person is using it, which are platitudes, which are empirically doubtful or false, and which are just confused. Also say whether there's a reading on which the two sides aren't really disagreeing, just using "know" differently. I don't want the "this is contested" preamble — I know it's contested. I want the best decomposition you can give, with a verdict on each reading, and a note on what evidence would shift the empirical parts. Keep the seperate readings genuinely seperate.
