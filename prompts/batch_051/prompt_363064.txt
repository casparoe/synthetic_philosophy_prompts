I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and the closest I get to philosophy is a naming review. A recurring dispute has made me realise I cannot articulate a boundary I am fairly sure exists. One colleague objected that we could not call a cleanup routine "decimate" unless it kept exactly one item in ten, because he had read that the word comes from a Roman punishment in which every tenth soldier was killed. Another argued that our API style guide was wrong to call an operation "idempotent" on engineering grounds, since the Latin roots mean "same power" and that ought to settle what the term can be used for. I can see that these moves are mistaken, but I cannot say exactly why without a definition rigged to match my own naming preferences. I would like a working definition of the etymological fallacy — or, if you prefer, a definition of when an appeal to a word's history is a legitimate constraint on its current meaning.

Please test your definition against these cases: (1) insisting that "decimate" should today mean only "reduce by one tenth" because of its Latin origin; (2) claiming that "nice" really means "ignorant", since it descends from Latin nescius, and that modern uses are therefore loose; (3) objecting that a present-day writer who calls a storm "awful" cannot mean "very bad", because "awful" once meant "inspiring awe"; (4) translating a seventeenth-century text and taking "let" in its then-current sense of "hinder" rather than its modern sense of "allow"; (5) an API style guide that defines "idempotent" by the engineering property and treats the Latin roots purely as a mnemonic; (6) a court interpreting a statute written in 1800 by asking what its words meant to competent speakers at enactment; (7) a dictionary etymology note explaining that "villain" descends from a word for a farmhand, with no claim about what it should mean now; and (8) someone who says "sinister" still means "left-handed" whenever it is used today, because of its Latin ancestor. Run the definition through all eight, say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, revise it if necessary, and defend any surprising verdicts. Then tell me which of the eight classifications you are least sure of. Begin with your definition in the first sentence, then reason from it; no preamble and no restating my question.
