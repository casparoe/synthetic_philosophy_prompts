I came across a feature in a general-interest magazine on why economic growth took off where it did, and its one-paragraph summary of Max Weber will not leave me alone. As I reconstruct it, the author's account of The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism ran roughly as follows. Weber showed that capitalism emerged only in the Protestant regions of Europe because the Calvinist doctrine of predestination left believers terrified about whether they were saved. Worldly success was the only available proof of election, so disciplined work, frugality, and the reinvestment of profits became religious duties; the habits generated by this anxiety gradually built the modern capitalist economy. From this the author concluded that Weber had demonstrated that religious values are the true engine of economic systems—change a society's theology and its institutions and economy will follow—and added that Weber himself admitted that, once mature, capitalism shed its religious scaffolding and now operates on a secularized version of that same work ethic.

I have just enough exposure to Weber to suspect this is mostly right but too neat. I seem to remember that Calvinists denied that good works could prove one was elect, and that Weber was careful to insist he was not giving a monocausal account of capitalism. But the essayist is respected, and I do not know which of my hesitations are real. Could you go through the summary sentence by sentence and tell me what a careful Weber scholar would accept, what is a standard popular simplification, what is an overstatement or distortion, and which of the author's larger conclusions—most of all, the claim that culture in the form of religious values is what causes economic institutions—survive once the accurate parts are separated from the questionable ones?
