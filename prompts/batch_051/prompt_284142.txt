In a recent graduate seminar on animal ethics, the discussion turned to whether a vegan who keeps rescued hens may eat their unfertilized eggs and whether a freegan may eat meat recovered from supermarket dumpsters. One participant repeatedly invoked the proverb "Waste not, want not" as though it settled both questions. I want that proverb taken apart carefully. What distinct precise claims could it be expressing in this context — for instance, that allowing usable goods to be destroyed or discarded is intrinsically objectionable, that using a byproduct does not cause further production of it, that using it does not express endorsement of or complicity in the practices that produced it, that the wrong lies in the waste rather than in the use, that an animal's remains are owed something like respect, or that public behavior shapes the norms on which future production depends? For each reading, say what "waste" and "not wasting" would have to mean, classify the reading as a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false, and explain the verdict. Then identify which reading, if any, would actually license eating the eggs or the recovered meat, whether any reading supports an obligation to use them rather than a mere permission, and which reading the participant was most plausibly relying on — and whether that reading would in fact get them their conclusion. Please answer in American English.
