I'm a retired marine biologist and I've somehow ended up on the board that administers our small country's animal sentience rules. I've been reading a lot of philosophy of mind since I retired, and it's made me lose all my old confidence about where the line goes, so I keep going back and forth on one clause and I'd like to talk it through. The Act says:

"For the purposes of this Act, an animal is to be regarded as sentient if it is a member of the subphylum Vertebrata or of any invertebrate taxon declared sentient by the Minister; the Minister may make such a declaration only if satisfied that the members of that taxon are capable of feeling pain."

Here's the case. A marine lab has applied for a permit to run procedures on shore crabs. The procedures involve cutting one walking leg (they naturally shed and regrow them, but a clean cut is different) and then measuring how the crabs respond to a mildly noxious probe. Whether the stricter permit conditions kick in depends entirely on whether shore crabs count as sentient under that clause. The lab submitted evidence, and for this decision the board is taking the findings as given: crabs with an injured leg spend more time in a dark shelter, but when the shelter floor is occassionally made noxious they leave it and stay away; if the injured leg is treated with local anaesthetic, this trade-off disappears even though the crabs still move and feed normally. There's also data that they groom an injured leg far more than an uninjured one.

The drafters of the clause, by which I mean the people who wrote it in the late 1970s, clearly had the vertebrate/invertebrate line in mind and assumed "capable of feeling pain" would track having a vertebrate-style central nervous system. Shore crabs were not on their radar. So I'd like a written opinion that argues it three ways: from the words of the clause, from the purpose of the Act, and from what those 1970s drafters would have said if you had asked them about crabs. Then say what a reasonable reader today would take the clause to require, and which of these approaches should win and why.

And please, since I'm the one who has to sign this: seperate what's genuinely settled from what's contested among people who work on this. Don't tell me the science has settled whether decapods can suffer, and don't imply there's a consensus among philosophers about what "pain" requires. If you're leaning on a contested reading of the evidence or the law, say so.
