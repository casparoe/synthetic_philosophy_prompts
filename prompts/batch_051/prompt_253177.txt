I'm a high school student, and I've been going back and forth with my physics teacher about a rule I use for deciding what to believe in science. The rule is this: I don't give a theory any real credence until it has made at least one novel, risky prediction that was later confirmed; before that, I treat elegance, mathematical beauty, explanatory unification, and internal consistency as reasons to work on a theory, but not reasons to believe it. I've been applying this rule to string theory and to multiverse cosmologies, and it makes me very reluctant to say they are probably true, since, as far as I understand, neither has produced a confirmed novel prediction of that kind. I can't check the equations myself, so the rule is also a shortcut that lets me suspend judgment without having to master the mathematics.

My teacher says the rule would have been a bad rule historically, because many theories spent a long time being developed for non-empirical reasons before their predictions arrived, and that I may be confusing not-yet-tested with disconfirmed. She also says some physicists now defend a position on which non-empirical virtues can legitimately raise a theory's probability even without new data. I can't tell whether my rule is a sound guard against hype or just a bias in favor of laboratory physics and against speculative theory.

I'd like an assessment of whether this policy is defensible, and if so in what form. When, if ever, can theoretical virtues justify increasing confidence in an otherwise untested theory, and does the answer change depending on whether we are talking about belief, acceptance for practical purposes, or allocating research effort? Please separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and don't present a contested position as the consensus. If my rule needs restating, give me the best version of it.
