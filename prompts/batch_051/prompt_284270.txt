I've been going in circles on why the US still runs on inches and pounds when basically the whole rest of the world uses metric, and I want to use it to get a handle on path dependence. The story I keep hearing is that once enough machines, standards, textbooks and habits get built around an old system, the switching costs get so high you're locked in even if the old thing is worse, history just kind of freezes. And the US did try to go metric back in the 1970s, there was a law and everything, and then it fizzled. But I can't tell whether that's the theory actually explaining the case or just flattering it. We already use metric in a lot of places, medicine, science, two-liter bottles, nutrition labels, so it's not a total lock-in. And maybe we never finished switching because the remaining costs really do outweigh the benefits, which isn't the same as being trapped. So run the failed US conversion to metric through path dependence in both directions. First: what does the theory predict about who the players were and what options they actually had back then, which parts of the episode does it explain cleanly, and where does it have to stretch? Second, flip it: what does the case do to path dependence, does it show the theory needs a sharper line between 'locked in' and 'expensive but still a rational choice', or between partial and total lock-in? And finally, would the US have wound up metric anyway if a couple of early decisions had gone different, and how would we even tell?
