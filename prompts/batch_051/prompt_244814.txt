I've been trying to nail down what makes a philosophical disagreement merely verbal rather than substantive, and I want you to stress-test my test. My working criteria are: (1) the two sides agree on all the same predictions about what you'd observe under any possible experiment; (2) the disagreement traces entirely to how they use some key term, so that if you paraphrase the disputed claim in a neutral vocabulary, both sides say "yes, obviously"; (3) nothing hangs on it — neither side would act, plan, or revise their views differently if they conceded the linguistic point.

Now here's the case I can't decide weather it's verbal or not. Two metaphysicians, A and B, are arguing about whether tables exist. A says: of course tables exist; a table just is a bunch of particles arranged tablewise, not a further thing over and above them. B says: no, strictly speaking there are only the particles; "there is a table" is true in ordinary English, but it's not literally true, because the world doesn't contain a table — it just contains particles arranged tablewise. They agree on every physical fact — positions of particles, forces, how the arrangement behaves if you lean on it, everything — and they even agree that ordinary people are right to say "there's a table here." But B insists there's still a real question left over: whether the world is such that the particles compose something, or merely such that they're arranged tablewise. A says that's a fake question, because "compose something" adds nothing beyond "arranged tablewise." B says A is just refusing to take the question seriously.

So: go through my three criteria one by one and say whether their dispute qualifies as merely verbal. Also say where the criteria themselves are doing the real work or where they'd need changing, because I have a feeling criterion (2) is smuggling in the answer. And I'd like you to say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you're least sure of.
