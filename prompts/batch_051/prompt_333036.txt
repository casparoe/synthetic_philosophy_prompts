For an upper-level undergraduate course in political philosophy, I have roughly eighteen students and a single seventy-five-minute session on the comparative politics of inequality. The aim of the session is for students to feel, rather than merely describe, the difference between two broad public orientations toward economic inequality. The first orientation, which I will call the American one for shorthand, tends to tolerate substantial inequalities of outcome provided that background opportunities are roughly fair and upward mobility is live; it treats compulsory redistribution with suspicion because it can seem to punish effort and choice. The second, which I will call the European one, treats large disparities as objectionable in themselves or as damaging to social relations and standing, and regards redistributive institutions as a demand of justice rather than a discretionary safety net. These are stylized ideal types, not claims about every citizen of either region, and the game should treat them as such.

Please design a runnable classroom game for this session. It must not begin with any lecture, and the labels American and European should not appear until the debrief: the idea should emerge from what participants do. The game needs explicit rules, roles, materials, and a point-based payoff structure that does not use real money. The design should force at least two distinct moments of choice: one made before participants know their place in the invented social order, an ex ante or constitutional choice, and one made after positions are revealed, an ex post choice. The two moments should be arranged so that participants can discover a genuine conflict between their own earlier and later assessments of the same distribution, for instance between the principles they chose for an institution and their willingness to leave that institution's outcomes untouched once they know where they landed. If feasible, include a later round in which some participants do move between positions, so that the promise of mobility is experienced rather than merely asserted, and so that the debrief can examine how much, and what kind of, actual mobility is doing the normative work.

Then provide a debrief, with a timeline, that maps specific moments in the game onto the underlying distinctions: ex ante versus ex post evaluation, equality of opportunity versus equality of outcome, brute luck versus chosen effort, mobility versus level, perceived desert, and the status or relational dimension of inequality. The debrief should also indicate where each orientation can be resisted and what it costs to resist it there.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, such as risk-loving students treating the game as mere amusement, participants colluding, or the group converging on a middling compromise that hides the conflict, and explain how to handle each difficulty. And state plainly which feature of the real American–European disagreement the game cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator in the debrief, such as the thick historical and institutional context in which these attitudes actually formed.
