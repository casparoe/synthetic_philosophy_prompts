I have read the standard expected-utility arguments many times over the years, and I still cannot state precisely what it takes for an agent to count as an expected utility maximizer. I would like a working definition rather than a survey or a set of references. The definition I keep reaching for is the standard one: there exist a real-valued utility function u and a probability measure p such that, for every pair of options, the agent weakly prefers one to the other exactly when its expected u is at least as high. But this definition seems to me to get several cases wrong, or at least to sort them too crudely, and I want it repaired or replaced.

Here are the cases as I see them.

1. A person who pays a small fee to trade an apple for a banana, then pays again to trade the banana for a cherry, then pays again to trade the cherry back for the apple. I take this person to be the clearest possible non-maximizer: their preferences cycle, and no u and p of the sort above can represent them.

2. A person who, in every choice, maximizes expected monetary value and nothing else. I take this person to be a maximizer, if a boring one.

3. A person with the usual Allais pattern. In one choice they take a certain one million dollars over a gamble offering a 10 percent chance of five million, an 89 percent chance of one million, and a 1 percent chance of nothing. In a second, paired choice they prefer a 10 percent chance of five million, otherwise nothing, to an 11 percent chance of one million, otherwise nothing. I believe these preferences are complete and transitive but inconsistent with any single u and p, so the standard definition counts them as a non-maximizer. Yet they feel far less broken than the cycle case, and I want the definition to be able to say so.

4. A person with Ellsberg-style ambiguity aversion. One urn contains 50 red and 50 black balls; another contains 100 balls in unknown proportions of red and black. The person prefers betting on red in the known urn to betting on red in the unknown urn, and also prefers betting on black in the known urn to betting on black in the unknown urn. As with the Allais case, I think no single probability measure makes them an expected utility maximizer, but I would not put them in the same bin as the cycle.

5. A satisficer who searches through options and takes the first one that clears a fixed threshold on each of two dimensions. They never compare the chosen option against the ones not yet examined, and there is no utility function they are maximizing. I think they are not a maximizer, but I do not want the definition to imply they are irrational in any thick sense.

6. A person with lexicographic preferences over jobs: between any two jobs, the one allowing more remote-work days per week is preferred, and only if the two tie on that dimension does salary decide. I believe no real-valued utility function represents this ordering, so the standard definition says they are not a maximizer. But the ordering is completely orderly, and I hesitate to call it incoherent.

7. A person who refuses to compare two options at all, say accepting a tenure-track job in a city they dislike versus leaving academia, while their preferences over every other pair are complete, transitive, and representable in the expected utility form. The standard definition requires completeness, so it counts them as a non-maximizer. I am inclined to say they are a maximizer over an incomplete domain, or at least that their incompleteness is not the same kind of failure as intransitivity.

8. A person whose choices are exactly those of an expected utility maximizer relative to some u and p, but whose stated probabilities are badly miscalibrated. I want the definition to count them as a maximizer, because I take the concept to be about internal coherence rather than about being right.

9. A committee whose collective choices are produced by majority vote over three alternatives and exhibit a Condorcet cycle. I think the committee is not a maximizer, even though each member is one.

10. A language model fine-tuned on human preference data and then used to select the action with the highest expected reward according to a learned reward model. I genuinely do not know whether to call this an expected utility maximizer. It looks like one locally, but the reward model is a proxy, and I am unsure whether the as-if representation is doing real work.

I would like a definition, or a small family of definitions, that does the following: clearly excludes the cycle and the majority-rule committee; clearly includes the expected-monetary-value person and the miscalibrated-but-coherent person; classifies the Allais, Ellsberg, satisficing, lexicographic, and incomplete-preference cases while saying whether each is the same kind of failure as intransitivity or a different and milder one; and gives a definite answer, with reasons, for the language model case. If no single definition does all of that, I would rather have the definition with the cleanest boundaries and an honest list of which of my verdicts it overturns.
