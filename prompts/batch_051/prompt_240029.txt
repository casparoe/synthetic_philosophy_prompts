I'm a science journalist at a general-interest magazine, and every few weeks I have to write a story where some agency, paper, or model produces a number that is presented as "the chance" of something — a 30% chance of a recession next year, a 4% chance a new drug beats placebo, a 70% chance of an above-normal hurricane season. My editors are fine with me being a sentence-level skeptic, but they want the skepticism to follow a method, because otherwise "the model says 30%" becomes "the reporter says 40%" with no audit trail and the fact-checkers stop trusting me. I also cover ideas, and I've been half-remembering a principle from formal epistemology — the Principal Principle, or deference to objective chance — which says, more or less, that if you know the objective chance of an event is x, your own degree of belief should also be x, unless you have "inadmissable" information — information that bears on the outcome itself rather than only on what the chance is — in which case the principle goes silent.

In my line of work the numbers that matter are rarely clean objective chances like a die roll; they're model outputs, and I almost always have background knowledge the model didnt have. So I want to turn the principle into a reusable decision procedure I can actually run before filing — say, 30 to 90 minutes — for deciding when to defer to a stated probability, when to adjust it in print, and how to tell the reader which I did. I need the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with "just report the stated probability and flag its source," where it stops with "adjust the number in copy," and where it hands the decision to me or an editor because it has become a values call rather than a fact call. Be honest about which steps are judgment dressed up as procedure.

Then run it on three cases: (1) a government hurricane outlook puts a 10% chance of major landfall on a stretch of coast where I happen to know the evacuation infrastructure is badly underfunded; (2) an election model gives 65% to the incumbent, and a reporter in one swing county sends me a photo of a line of young voters that doesn't match the model's turnout assumptions; (3) a seismic hazard model gives a 3% chance of a magnitude 8 quake in a region next year, and I've interviewed a geologist who says the model's recurrence interval is based on an outdated fault map. For each, say what the procedure outputs, make sure at least one case gets an answer I won't like, and say plainly what the procedure cannot do.
