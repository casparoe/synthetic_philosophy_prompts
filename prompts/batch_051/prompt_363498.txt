I'm putting together a small dialogue series for a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of consciousness, and I'd like a focused exchange between two philosophers who disagree about the zombie conceivability argument. Both characters accept the standard setup: a philosophical zombie is an exact physical duplicate of a conscious human being, with the same brain states, the same behavior, and the same verbal reports of pain, but with no phenomenal consciousness and nothing it is like to be it. Both also agree that the crucial step in the anti-physicalist argument is the move from the conceivability of such a creature to the conclusion that physicalism is false or at least in serious trouble; what they disagree about is whether that move is legitimate, and in particular whether phenomenal consciousness is disanalogous to ordinary cases of a posteriori necessity such as water's being H2O.

The first character defends a broadly Chalmers-style position: the conceivability of zombies is not the cheap, merely apparent conceivability we have when we imagine that water might have failed to be H2O; it is ideal, primary conceivability, and it reveals a gap in nature that no amount of future empirical discovery could close. The second character defends a type-B physicalist position: the mind–body connection holds with full metaphysical necessity but is known only a posteriori, and the apparent possibility of zombies is an artifact of the way our phenomenal concepts are insulated from our physical and functional concepts. On this view, the explanatory gap is generated by the structure of our concepts rather than by the structure of the world, much as the apparent contingency of water's being H2O is an artifact of the distinct concepts involved.

Please make the dialogue turn on whether the water/H2O analogy can bear the weight the physicalist wants it to bear. The physicalist should argue that in the water case, the apparent possibility is explained away once we discover a hidden essence that the reference-fixing description failed to capture; the dualist should reply that conscious experience has no such hidden essence, because with pain what you feel is all there is, so there is nothing a future discovery could reveal that would close the gap. The physicalist should then answer that this very intuition, that experience has no hidden essence, is exactly what the phenomenal concept strategy would predict, so the dualist cannot simply invoke it as independent evidence against the analogy. Ensure that neither character wins by stipulation: each should make at least one genuine concession to the other, and each should respond to the other's strongest point rather than restating an opening position. Conclude with each posing one diagnostic question the other would need to answer for their view to survive.
