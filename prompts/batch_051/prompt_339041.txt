A colleague has been arguing that the no-free-lunch theorems give a rigorous version of the problem of induction, and I find the argument unsettling because I cannot accept the conclusion, but I also cannot find the step to reject. Here is the argument as I understand it.

(1) The problem of induction asks what justifies expecting unobserved cases to resemble observed ones, given that any finite body of evidence is compatible with indefinitely many different extrapolations.
(2) The no-free-lunch theorems show that, averaged over all possible target functions, every learning algorithm has the same expected error; no algorithm does better than random guessing unless it is tailored to a restricted class of target functions.
(3) Therefore an inductive policy can be justified only if we are entitled in advance to assume that the world is drawn from some favored class of processes, such as the smooth, simple, or law-governed ones.
(4) But that assumption is itself a substantive empirical claim, and any defense of it would have to be inductive; so the justification is circular.
(5) Therefore no particular inductive expectation is more reasonable than any rival: the sun rising tomorrow, a drug working in the next patient, and a bridge design holding are all rationally unsupported on a par with their negations.
(6) That conclusion is absurd, since some practices plainly do better than the alternatives and a view that makes them all equally unsupported must have gone wrong somewhere.

I would prefer to keep (1) and (2) as stated, and I take (6) to be clearly true, but I cannot identify which of the remaining moves is the weak one. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
