I am writing a novel set in a city whose legal order has accumulated over a century, and I need to determine whether an old, unenforced prohibition is still law. I have been using three criteria for a rule's being law in force: it was validly enacted and has not been repealed; its content is determinate enough to guide the conduct of those subject to it; and it is at least occasionally taken up by officials as a live ground of decision rather than lying dormant in the code. Now consider this episode. An 1893 ordinance forbids selling goods in the market square after the evening bell. It was never expressly repealed, and its wording is clear. But for at least seventy years the city has licensed and collected fees from an evening market in that square, has never prosecuted or cited anyone under the ordinance, and most of its current officials would say they have never heard of it. A newly appointed prosecutor now charges a stallholder under the ordinance. Work through the three criteria one by one and say whether the ordinance qualifies as law in force. Where the criteria pull in different directions, say which criterion is doing the real work and whether the case exposes a gap in the criteria themselves — in particular, whether they should be supplemented by a doctrine of desuetude, under which a valid norm can lapse through long, open, and consistent non-enforcement. Close by saying which verdict better serves the rule-of-law values of notice and reliance.
