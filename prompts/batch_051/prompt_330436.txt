The position that has come to seem most compelling to me in the philosophy of biology is the gene's-eye view of evolution, roughly as articulated by George C. Williams and Richard Dawkins. On this view the gene — or, more carefully, the replicator — is the fundamental unit of selection; organisms are best treated as vehicles, survival machines, or temporary coalitions whose apparent design exists because it promoted the differential survival of the replicators that built them; and phenomena such as altruism, cooperation, and group-level adaptation are to be explained through inclusive fitness and kin selection rather than through selection for the good of the group or the species. I find the view hard to resist because one spare accounting scheme handles such a wide range of phenomena: sterile workers in social insects, meiotic drive and other selfish genetic elements, parent–offspring conflict, and the peculiar logic of genes that spread while harming the organisms that carry them. But I want to know what this position actually risks — what observations, formal results, or theoretical developments would count against it, and where its defenders themselves would feel the pressure.

To focus the discussion, here are some candidate areas of exposure I have been turning over. First, the group-selection revival and the multilevel-selection framework suggest that when between-group differences are strong, selection at the group level can be a real causal process and not merely an alternative way of bookkeeping gene-frequency changes. Does that make the gene's-eye view one equivalent representation among several, or does the replicator remain the explanatory bedrock even when groups serve as vehicles? Second, the unit of the view — a discrete gene with a definite fitness — is far less crisp in contemporary molecular biology than in the particulate picture of the 1960s and 1970s: overlapping reading frames, alternative splicing, complex regulatory regions, transposable elements, and epigenetic modifications all blur the boundaries. Third, evolutionary transitions in individuality show that new levels of organization, such as chromosomes, eukaryotic cells, and multicellular bodies, arise precisely through the suppression of lower-level competition, after which the higher-level unit increasingly looks like the thing whose persistence explains the adaptations. Fourth, transgenerational forms of inheritance that do not run through DNA sequence — epigenetic, cultural, and ecological inheritance — appear to carry heritable variation that affects fitness. For each of these, I would like to know whether a committed gene's-eye theorist would concede that it counts against the view, or would instead absorb it as an extension, a special case, or something the view never denied, and which candidates come closer to fatal than to merely awkward.

Please respond as a professional philosopher of biology who is sympathetic to the gene's-eye view but prepared to press on its weak points. Begin by stating, as precisely as you can, what the strongest form of the position is committed to and what it need not claim; that will make its points of exposure clear. Then work through the candidate defeaters above, add any others you regard as more serious, and distinguish carefully between evidence that would be decisive, evidence that would force a revision or narrowing, and evidence that would merely be awkward. Where you can, say what the gene's-eye view predicts that rival positions do not, and assess whether the position has been insulated by qualifications — and whether that insulation reflects explanatory maturity or unfalsifiability. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward as your concluding summary. Please write your answer in American English.
