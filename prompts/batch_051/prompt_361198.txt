I am writing a novel whose central character is an elder in a Presbyterian congregation, and I have been trying to render his frame of mind from the inside rather than as an outsider's curiosity. The feature of his thought that most resists me is the sharp difference between how he reasons about public worship and how he reasons about everything else. In ordinary affairs he holds the usual default: an action is permissible unless some principle forbids it. But in the ordering of public worship he holds what his tradition calls the regulative principle, according to which public worship may include only what Scripture commands or positively warrants, and uncommanded ceremonies, images, or rites are excluded not because they are shown to be harmful but simply because they lack warrant. The same mind that treats liberty as the default of ordinary life treats silence as prohibitive in the liturgy.

I want to know whether that asymmetry is defensible on its own terms, not historically or confessionally. The obvious justifications each seem to prove too little or too much. One may say that God has reserved to Himself the terms on which He is approached, so that worship is uniquely a matter of divine prerogative. One may say that unauthorized worship is uniquely dangerous, since it risks offering to God what He has not asked for and slipping into idolatry. Or one may say that in a covenant the superior party sets the forms of meeting, so that human invention in the service is a kind of breach. My difficulty is that each of these seems either to stop short of the conclusions its logic would demand or to overshoot them. If unauthorized worship is uniquely dangerous, why does the same reasoning not impose a default of prohibition on doctrine, on private prayer, or on the whole of the moral life, in which the same God is no less concerned? If all of a believer's life is lived before the face of God, why is the public liturgy alone placed under the rule that silence forbids? And if the principle rests on divine prerogative, what marks off worship as the sphere in which that prerogative takes this restrictive form rather than a permissive one?

Tell me whether the asymmetry can be justified, and if so, which candidate justification does the essential work and precisely where it stops. Should the default of prohibition be extended into other spheres, should the default of liberty be extended into worship, or is the asymmetry itself a principled one? In answering, test your preferred justification against the cases I have mentioned and state plainly where you would draw the line and why.
