I've been trying to get clear, in an amateur way, on two ideas that come up whenever people discuss who gets believed and who does not. As I understand them, a testimonial injustice occurs when a hearer gives a speaker less credibility than the speaker's word deserves, and the hearer's misjudgment is driven by a prejudice about a social group to which the speaker belongs, so that the speaker is wronged specifically in their capacity as a giver of knowledge. A hermeneutical injustice is different: it occurs when someone is put at an unfair disadvantage in making sense of, or communicating, an important part of their own experience, because the shared stock of concepts and categories available in their community has a gap in it — a gap that exists because people in that person's position were never in a position to shape those shared resources. I am not fully confident that I have either definition exactly right, and part of what I want from this exercise is a clearer sense of where the boundary between the two falls.

Please design a single realistic scenario, set in a hospital ward, a school, or a small business over a period of weeks or months, that meets all of the following conditions. It must contain a credibility deficit: a hearer gives a speaker, whom I will call S, clearly less credibility than the available evidence warrants, and as a result S suffers a serious, concrete setback. Yet the scenario must contain no testimonial injustice at all; the credibility deficit should flow from something other than identity prejudice, for example an honestly held but badly calibrated response to a generic feature of how S presents, and nobody in the story should act maliciously or lie. The same scenario must also contain a hermeneutical injustice suffered by a different person, T. T's disadvantage should not involve anyone disbelieving T; it should consist in T's lacking, within that setting, the concepts needed to make sense of a recurring and significant pattern in T's own experience, where the absence of those concepts is plausibly the product of the marginalization of people in T's position. The scenario itself should remain compact, and it should not depend on anyone being a caricature or acting with gross incompetence. The point of the exercise is to show that the two phenomena can come apart in both directions: a credibility deficit need not be a testimonial injustice, and a hermeneutical injustice need not involve anyone's word being discounted.

After presenting the scenario, explain why the credibility deficit suffered by S does not amount to a testimonial injustice, and identify the specific feature of the case that is doing that work. Then explain why T's situation does amount to a hermeneutical injustice, and say what would have to change, holding as much else fixed as possible, for the S side of the case to become a straightforward example of testimonial injustice instead. Finally, consider the sharpest objection a critic might make to classifying T's situation as a hermeneutical injustice — for example, that the label is too broad and covers an ordinary personal failure of vocabulary rather than a structural wrong — and answer that objection.
