I'm teaching a small seminar on rigid designation and the necessary a posteriori, and I've found that my students can rehearse Kripke's arguments but struggle to construct a fresh case in which the descriptivist and the Millian actually come apart in the right way. I'd like you to build a thought experiment to the following specifications. Begin by stating precise working versions of two views: (a) a descriptivist semantics on which a proper name contributes something like a cluster or definition of associated descriptions — the referent is whatever uniquely satisfies them, and the name's cognitive value is tied to that descriptive content; and (b) a Millian direct-reference semantics on which the semantic contribution of a name is just its bearer, so that a true identity sentence with co-referring names expresses a singular proposition that is necessarily true but knowable only a posteriori. Then construct one realistic scenario — an office, a courtroom, a hospital, a local historical society; no Twin Earth, no amnesia, no science-fictional machinery, no omniscient narrator — in which both views assign exactly the same referent to a proper name in a single key utterance, and agree on the utterance's truth-value, but disagree about what proposition it expresses and about whether the relevant claim is necessary a posteriori, contingent a priori, analytic, or some other combination. Avoid the familiar stock cases (Hesperus/Phosphorus, Aristotle and the teacher of Alexander, Gödel and the incompleteness proof); the scenario itself should be new. The divergence must be generated by the mechanics of the two theories as the case is described — if the case simply stipulates that one view finds the sentence informative and the other does not, it fails the exercise.

After the scenario, walk through what each theory says about it and why, deriving each verdict from that theory's stated commitments rather than from the design brief. Then tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the divergence if that feature is removed or altered. How would a defender of whichever theory comes out of your case looking worse attack the case? Finally, I want the epistemic self-assessment: say what would change your mind about whether the case meets the specifications. Which step in your reasoning is most vulnerable — for instance, a charge that the two theories do not in fact agree on the referent, or that one side's prediction about informativeness has been built into the description rather than derived from the semantics — and what argument against that step would lead you to reassess the construction?
