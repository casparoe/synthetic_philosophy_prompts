I am taking a course in environmental ethics this term, and the readings have left me unsure which of the standard accounts of the value of nature — intrinsic, instrumental, or relational — I actually hold. I have worked through my own responses to a series of cases, and they seem to pull in different directions. I would like to know whether there is a single principle that captures all or nearly all of them, and if not, which of my verdicts I would have to give up.

My cases and verdicts are as follows.

(1) A remote subalpine valley that no one has visited and no one has any plan to visit is dammed and flooded for a hydroelectric project whose power is not needed, and no one will ever learn about the place or its loss. I judge this wrong, and I do not think the wrongness is exhausted by lost future human enjoyment, lost scientific information, or any effects on anyone's interests.

(2) My neighbour's allotment garden, which she has tended for thirty years, is valuable, and what makes it valuable seems to be the care, skill, and history of that particular relationship rather than anything wild or ecologically significant about the plot. The fact that it is planted mostly with non-native cultivars does not seem to me to be a defect.

(3) Conservation managers eradicate every rat on an island so that nesting seabirds can recover. I judge this the right thing to do, even though it means deliberately killing many thousands of sentient animals. Something about restoring the island's prior ecological state seems to matter more than the rats' individual interests.

(4) A natural wetland is destroyed and, under a legal requirement, replaced with a constructed mitigation wetland that within a decade functions indistinguishably from the lost one — the same water filtration, the same birds, the same plant communities. I accept the policy as better than nothing, but I do not think the replacement fully makes up for the loss. Something about the original's having grown rather than been built is lost and never fully regained by the replacement.

(5) A subspecies of beetle becomes extinct, with every remaining individual dying painlessly in a way that no one notices or regrets. I still judge that something bad has happened, and not merely because a type of organism or a set of genetic possibilities has gone out of the world.

(6) My dog corners a wild fox in our garden. If I must intervene on one side, I would protect the dog, and I do not think this is mere bias or selfishness: the dog's value to me, and the relationship between us, is a real kind of value. But I also think the fox has a kind of value the dog lacks, tied to its being wild rather than bred for us.

(7) A nineteenth-century mill dam has created a pond that the town loves and uses. Removing the dam would restore the river's free flow and its native fish runs. I judge that the dam should come out, but that the loss of the pond as something shaped by and valued through generations of use is a real loss — not simply outweighed by the river as though both were entries on a single scale.

Is there one account of the value of nature that delivers all seven of these verdicts? If not, which verdicts must be overturned, and why should it be those rather than the contrary ones? Finally, what does the account you recommend say about a case I have not listed: an old industrial canal in a city that has, without any planning or care, become a spontaneously self-organising ecosystem of introduced species — herons, crayfish, aquatic plants — that local people walk past and enjoy? Does it have the same kind of value as the subalpine valley in (1), the same kind as the garden in (2), some combination of the two, or neither, and why?
