**Question.** Evaluate the thesis that elegance is evidence for the truth of a scientific theory. Your answer should (a) distinguish elegance's heuristic role in theory construction and theory choice from any justificatory or evidential role; (b) discuss what historical examples of elegant theories can and cannot show; and (c) state the conditions, if any, under which elegance could function as evidence.

**Student answer.**

> Elegance is best understood as a cluster of theoretical virtues: economy of basic principles, symmetry, and unification of previously disparate phenomena under a single framework. Its heuristic role is uncontroversial: elegance guides theory construction by telling researchers where to look, which of two otherwise adequate theories to develop first, and what to test next. The live question is whether elegance also has a justificatory role — whether the elegance of a theory is itself a reason to think the theory true.
>
> I argue that it is. The central argument is that elegance is a species of simplicity, and simplicity is already a cornerstone of scientific inference. Occam's razor, the preference for models with fewer free parameters, and the practical success of simple models in avoiding overfitting all show that simplicity tends to track truth. Since elegance is simply simplicity applied to laws and fundamental principles rather than to data fitting, it inherits this evidential force. A theory that covers the phenomena with a single symmetric principle is, in the relevant sense, simpler than one that covers them with a patchwork of special assumptions, and the usual arguments for preferring the simpler carry over directly.
>
> The historical record points the same way. General relativity and Dirac's equation are canonical cases in which aesthetically motivated theorizing preceded dramatic empirical success. But I will not claim these examples settle the matter on their own: elegant failures are remembered less often than elegant successes, so the historical record suffers from survivorship bias. Examples illustrate the possibility; they do not by themselves establish an evidential link.
>
> There is, however, a principled route to treating elegance as evidence, parallel to the case for novel prediction. When a theory is constructed for aesthetic reasons before the data that would confirm it have been obtained, and those data later arrive, the theory could not have been engineered to fit those observations. Elegance therefore plays the same role as the prior theoretical motivation that predictivists count in favour of a hypothesis: an elegant theory is staking a risk in advance, which is exactly what gives novel predictions their special weight.
>
> The standard objection is that for any body of data there are infinitely many empirically equivalent theories, many of them inelegant, and that symmetry principles may reflect our own preferences about how to describe nature rather than nature itself. But fallibility is not the absence of evidential value. All scientific evidence is theory-laden and defeasible; elegance is unremarkable in this respect.
>
> I conclude that elegance is genuine but defeasible evidence for the truth of a scientific theory. Its evidential force is strongest when elegance genuinely predates the confirming data, and it operates by making a theory simpler and therefore, other things held equal, more likely to be true.
