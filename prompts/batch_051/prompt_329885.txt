I run the appeals unit at a state catastrophic-loss fund. The point of the fund is to make people whole after disasters — crop failures, house fires, sudden medical costs — but the statute says we pay for losses that are "a matter of bad luck, and not a gamble the claimant chose to run." Everyone on my team says they understand that line, but we draw it in completely different places. A farmer who loses an orchard to hail gets paid; a man who loses his savings in a speculative business deal doesn't. Fine. But the cases in the middle are where we're burning our legitimacy. My reviewers each process about forty files a week, the average grant is around $80,000, and the decisions are becoming precendent, so the hearing officers have started writing opinions that contradict each other.

I want a decision procedure my reviewers can actually run in twenty minutes on a case file, with one phone call allowed, before the decision goes to the hearing officer. Ground it in whatever the relevant political philosophy actually establishes about this line, but write it for a reviewer who has never taken a philosophy class and is under time pressure. It has to handle at least the following: the claimant chose the activity but not the particular harm; the claimant knew a risk existed but not how big it was; the claimant chose not to insure but couldn't realistically afford coverage; the claimant's taste for the risky activity is itself something they didn't choose (upbringing, temperament); the claimant chose the risk years ago but the risk has since changed under their feet (new flood maps, a warming climate); a decision that was reasonable at the time and only looks reckless in hindsight; and the difference between a gamble that didn't pay off and a danger that was put on them.

The procedure should be a sequence of questions in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the chain stops with a no (deny), where it stops with a yes (pay), and where it hands the file to a human because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls wearing a checklist costume. And tell me where the thing will misfire: the claims it will overpay, the ones it will underpay, and the ones it will wrongly treat as straightforward. I'd rather it drew the line in the right place than used the right vocabulary.

Then run it on three claims I've pulled because each one stresses a different joint in the distinction:

1. A farmer bought river-bottom land 25 years ago, when the official maps put the flood risk at once a century. Upstream development and a changing climate have since changed the runoff; the current maps put his land in a 1-in-10-year flood. His crop, his equipment, and half his house drowned. He knew the land "sometimes" floods, but not with anything like this frequency, and he's been trying to sell for five years without a single offer.

2. A backcountry skier was caught in an avalanche and spent six months in hospital. He had read the morning avalanche bulletin that rated the danger "considerable," and he chose the terrain and the day anyway. His file includes a psychologist's report saying his risk-seeking temperament traces pretty directly to a childhood nobody would call lucky, and arguing that his appetite for risk is every bit as unchosen as a bad knee.

3. A 40-year-old with an inherited kidney condition needs treatment that costs more than she would have earned in ten years. The condition couldn't have been prevented, and no insurer would have covered her at any price she could have paid.

These are meant to stress different parts of the distinction, and I expect to disagree with your procedure on at least one of them — please say which and why.

For the deliverable: first the procedure as a table with columns Step, Question we ask, Sufficient answer, Where we go if insufficient, Factual or values judgment, and What edge case this step is catching. Then a JSON array with one object per test case, with fields: case_id, verdict (use exactly "pay", "deny", or "human_review"), path_taken (a list of "step_number:yes/no" through the procedure), decisive_step, and what_a_contrary_reviewer_would_need_to_show to flip the result.
