I'm a math PhD student, and I've somehow become the referee between two factions in our department that have been stuck for about two years on whether it matters which square root of -1 we label i. It started as a joke: someone wrote i on the board, crossed it out, wrote -i, and said "same field either way." Now it's a genuine standoff. The first side leans on the automorphism argument: conjugation z ↦ z-bar fixes the real numbers and swaps i and -i, so as a field the complex numbers have a symmetry that exchanges the two roots of x²+1. Nothing internal to the structure tells them apart, so, they say, there is no fact of the matter about which root is i simpliciter; saying "i is the positive one" only means "the one I've singled out by my labeling." The other side says that's too quick. Once you fix the standard presentation of C as R² with (0,1) doing the job of i — or fix a choice of algebraic closure of R, or an orientation of the plane — there's a perfectly determinate fact about which root plays that role relative to that presentation. And, they add, everyone converges on the same convention in practice (upper half-plane, principal branch, and so on), so the claim that the labels are arbitrary conflates absolute arbitrariness with presentation-relativity.

What kills me is that neither side has moved in two years even though they agree on every piece of algebra involved. They both accept the automorphism argument, they both accept the presentation-relative facts, they both accept the sociological fact about shared conventions, and the argument still just reruns. I honestly can't tell wether this is a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about "fact of the matter" or "the root," or two groups answering different questions — one about fields up to isomorphism, one about how mathematical language fixes reference. My working theory is that it's downstream of a deeper split about individuation: whether mathematical objects are exhausted by their structural role or whether they have label-sensitive identity in a presentation. But I could be missing a hidden formal question (something about embedings, or whether conventions can be justified without circularity) that's really doing the work.

Give me a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, engaging with what the two sides actually say, not a vote on who's right. Is there a hidden technical or formal question underneath? A distinction that would let both sides be right about different things? A difference about what would even count as evidence for "there is no fact of the matter"? And given the diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a theorem, a distinction, a reframing — or should we accept that the question is ill-posed and stop expecting it to resolve?
