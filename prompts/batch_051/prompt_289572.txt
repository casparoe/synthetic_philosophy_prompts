I've been working through the appraisal/bestowal debate in the philosophy of love, and I've written up a set of notes toward an essay. I'd like you to read them and give me substantive, critical comments. Treat yourself as an expert on the philosophy of love and the emotions; engage with the strongest version of the argument, not a straw one, and begin by listing the two or three most serious objections you see before responding in detail.

The notes: The appraisal view says love is a response to the value of the beloved — to her qualities — and that this is what makes love rational and answerable to reasons. The bestowal view says this gets love backwards: love does not discover an antecedently existing value, it confers value on the beloved, which is why the lover would be making a mistake if she traded her beloved in for someone with the same qualities ranked higher. I think the debate as usually framed conflates two questions: whether love answers to the beloved's properties, and whether the beloved's value is independent of the lover's attitude. My proposal is that love answers to relational and historical properties — above all, the property of being the person with whom the lover shares this particular history — and that these properties are partly constituted by the relationship itself. On this picture, love is reason-responsive without being a fungible evaluation: a third party cannot simply inspect the beloved's intrinsic qualities and recover the lover's reasons, because those reasons exist only from inside the history the love has partly created. To test the view, I use a twin case. Imagine a stranger intrinsically indistinguishable from the beloved but lacking the shared history: preferring the beloved seems rationally fine, which the bestowalist treats as its key datum. Now imagine the history itself is somehow duplicated too, so the twin has as good a relational claim as the original. The intuition becomes much less clear, which suggests that what carries the weight is the history rather than bare numerical identity — and that bestowal theorists may have mistaken a reason that is not publicly available for no reason at all.

What I want from you: (1) Is my diagnosis of the debate fair, or have I built the answer into the notion of a 'relational property'? (2) Does the duplicated-history version of the twin case actually force the bestowalist to concede that non-fungibility is a matter of degree, or can she resist it cleanly? (3) Where does the view collapse: can a love outlive every shared history and every surviving quality, and would it be irrational if it did? (4) Is my conclusion more favorable to one side than I am admitting? Please separate what is settled or widely accepted in the area from what is genuinely contested, and flag any place where I am leaning on a contested reading as if it were neutral ground.
