Something I can't get straight is whether the hindsight bias / narrative fallacy argument against historical explanation still works now that we have scored forecasting.

The argument, as I understand it, goes like this. When someone explains a big past event — the 2008 crash, say, or the collapse of the Soviet Union — the explanation was nearly always put together after the ending was already known. The person telling the story didn't predict it beforehand, and if the opposite thing had happened they would probably have an equally tidy story about that too. So the feeling that the event was inevitable is just hindsight, and the explanation never had to stick its neck out ahead of time, so it can't really be tested. Taleb's label for the storytelling side of this is the narrative fallacy.

But the development that bothers me is this. In the last couple of decades we've ended up with prediction tournaments and platforms where people really do put numbers on messy social and political events before they happen, and then get scored against what actually occurred. Some forecasters — the ones called superforecasters — beat the crowd and beat subject experts over hundreds of questions. So it looks like at least some anticipation of these same chaotic events is possible, in advance, better than chance and better than the obvious benchmarks. Its not perfect, but it's real.

My question is whether the hindsight-bias attack on historical explanation survives that fact. Does the forecasting evidence kill the argument, leave it untouched, or force a repair? I want you to work through which premise of the hindsight argument the evidence actually bears on, and say whether it can be restated in a weaker form that still does some work — for example, does the problem move from "retrospective explanation is hopeless" to something about base rates, calibration, or being forced to pre-register your claims? If forecasting and explaining turn out to be different activities, I want to know exactly where they come apart.

Columns: (1) Premise the hindsight argument needs; (2) What the forecasting evidence does to it; (3) Nearest surviving version of that premise, if any; (4) Verdict — untouched, weakened, or killed.
