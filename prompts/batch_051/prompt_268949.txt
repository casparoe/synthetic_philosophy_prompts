Write a dialogue between two senior grad students in a robotics lab, Maya and Dev, who are arguing about Asimov's Three Laws of Robotics treated as a serious alignment proposal rather than a sci-fi plot device. Maya thinks the laws get something importantly right: they impose a strict priority ordering of duties—avoiding harm first, then obeying orders, then preserving yourself—which looks like a real, inspectable safety architecture instead of vibes. Dev thinks the whole approach is unfixable because the central concepts (harm, human being, allowing harm through omission) are too vague and context-sensitive, and because under uncertainty a strict lexical ordering makes robots do absurd things. Have them work through one concrete case: a home-care robot whose elderly owner has stopped breathing and who has a standing order never to leave the apartment without asking; the robot's battery is low enough that going for help might strand it outside and leave the owner alone either way. Let them directly respond to each other's proposed patches, like replacing bright-line concepts with probabilities, whitelisting which orders count as binding, adding a higher law meant to protect humanity in general, or treating the laws as defeasible heuristics rather than constraints. Neither should be a strawman, and each should have to revise their view at least once under pressure from the other. End without a tidy verdict.
