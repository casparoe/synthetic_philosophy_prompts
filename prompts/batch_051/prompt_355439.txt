I am a philosopher of criminal law, and I have been trying for some time to extract the implicit theory of coercion embedded in a familiar practice: the criminalization of blackmail. The practice, shared in broad outline across many legal systems, is this. It is a serious offense for one person to demand money, or some other advantage, from another while threatening, should the demand be refused, to disclose true but damaging information about the target. The puzzling feature is that the ingredients, taken separately, are mostly lawful. A person may ordinarily announce an intention to reveal unflattering truthful information; if the revelation is not always free of civil liability, it is at least not criminal by itself. And a person may ordinarily ask another for money, even insistently, and may propose an exchange that the other happens to dislike. Yet when a request is backed by a threat to reveal, the joint act is criminal—even in cases where the threatened disclosure would itself have been perfectly lawful. The puzzle intensifies once one notices how much ordinary economic bargaining consists of conditional threats: a supplier may refuse to continue dealing unless the buyer pays more; an agent may offer a benefit only on terms the other party would prefer not to accept. Why does the law mark out, for special condemnation, the conditional threat to disclose what one has a right to disclose?

I am not looking for a doctrinal or jurisdictional answer. I want the philosophy. What theory of coercion, rights, wrongfulness, or the proper scope of bargaining does the practice of criminalizing blackmail presuppose? Does it presuppose, for example, that some rights may not be monetized or wielded as leverage, that conditional threats are wrongful even when the threatened act, performed unconditionally, would not be, or that information has a peculiar status in exchange? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as the practice allows, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and then consider what would have to change if that theory were replaced by the straightforwardly liberal view that any voluntary bargain both parties prefer to their status quo is legitimate. End with a one-sentence summary as your last line, beginning "In short:".
