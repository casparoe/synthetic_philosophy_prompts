I've got into a habit over the last couple of years of refusing to give single-number probabilities for anything that matters. If my boss asks whether the server migration will finish by Friday, I say something like "probably, but I'm not going to give you a number—call it forty to sixty percent, and I don't think the bottom end is the same kind of uncertainty as the top." I work in risk modelling, and I've noticed that once a 0.72 goes into a report, people downstream start multiplying it with other numbers as if it had been measured with a ruler. A colleague pointed me to Keynes's A Treatise on Probability, where he argues that many probabilities are not numerically measurable at all, and that the best you can often say is that one hypothesis is more probable than another without assigning any figure to either. I find that persuasive, but I also catch myself using it as a shield. For example, last week someone asked me the probability of a particular vendor going bust in the next quarter, and I said "a wide band, maybe ten to forty per cent" when honestly I did have a vague feeling that the lower end was much more likely than the upper. I worry my habit is just a way of dodging the embarrassment of being wrong, and that I'd have better calibration if I forced myself to write down a point estimate and then update it. Is my policy defensible? Where does the line run between genuinely imprecise probabilities and a failure to introspect properly? And is there a version of this habit that keeps the honesty of imprecision without becoming an excuse for vagueness?
