I'm taking a decision theory seminar, and we've been treating the Ellsberg paradox as a refutation of subjective expected utility theory: from an urn with 30 red balls and 60 balls that are black or yellow in unknown proportions, people prefer a bet on red (a known 1/3 chance of winning) to a bet on black, while also preferring a bet on black-or-yellow (a known 2/3 chance) to a bet on red-or-yellow, even though no single assignment of probabilities to the states makes both preferences consistent with maximizing expected utility. A fellow student pushed back by saying the paradox only refutes the requirement that uncertainty about the composition collapse into a single prior: on newer models, an agent can hold a distribution over the possible urn compositions, be averse to uncertainty in that higher-order distribution, and still be described as maximizing expected utility over a richer state space. I'm not sure whether this rescues expected utility theory or changes the subject. Does the Ellsberg paradox survive as an argument against expected utility theory, or was it only ever an argument against ambiguity neutrality under a restrictive formalism? Give me the strongest version of each side: if it survives, say which precise premise of the newer models it still catches out; if it doesn't, say what weaker but still important claim we ought to keep from Ellsberg. End by saying where your answer is most fragile—what formal result or pattern of choice data would make you change your verdict.
