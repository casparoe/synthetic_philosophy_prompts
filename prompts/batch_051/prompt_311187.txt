I am a novelist, and I serve on my city's Public Commemoration Commission, the body that must approve every proposed street name, plaque, and monument on public land. A case has come before us that has divided the commission, and I want to think it through with some care before we are asked to vote. Our enabling bylaw provides: "No street, square, bridge, or other public place shall be named, and no monument erected, in honour of any person unless that person's life, taken as a whole, was one of enduring service to the city and of exemplary moral character." The clause was adopted in the 1960s, when the commission was created to decide which of the city's own dead — its mayors, benefactors, and reformers — deserved to be remembered in its streets and squares.

The proposal now before us is to name a small square after the central character of one of my novels, a working-class woman, Miriam, who in the book organizes a rent strike in a 1930s tenement and delivers the speech that turns the strike from the steps of that tenement, at the edge of the square in question. The novel is widely taught in the city's schools; the character has become a local emblem, and the residents' association argues that she embodies precisely the qualities the bylaw names. She did not exist, and everyone on the commission knows it, but the association insists that her example is real in every way that matters. I have recused myself from the vote, though I cannot quite set aside the interpretive question underneath it.

Does the bylaw permit the naming, forbid it, or fail to reach the case? I would like you to work through what the plain words require, what the purpose of the clause suggests, what its drafters would probably have said if asked about a fictional character, and what a reasonable reader of the bylaw would understand it to mean. Then say which of these approaches should govern here, and why. It would also help to know whether the fact that Miriam represents real historical struggles should make any difference to the analysis.
