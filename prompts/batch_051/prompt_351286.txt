I'm trying to pin down what, if anything, is philosophically different about computer-assisted proofs, and I keep coming back to the four-colour theorem as the test case. As I understand it, Appel and Haken showed in 1976 that every planar map is four-colourable by producing an “unavoidable set” of something like two thousand configurations — every planar map has to contain at least one of them — and then using a computer to verify that each one is reducible (it can’t occur in a minimal counterexample). No single human could ever look at the whole proof, and a lot of mathematicians at the time simple refused to call it a proof. I want to analyze that episode through one specific lens: the view that a proof only counts if it can be surveyed — held in the mind as a whole by a human mathematician — and that once a proof stops being surveyable it becomes more like an empirical report (“the program ran and printed QED”) than a proof.

The mapping part: what exactly did the Appel–Haken proof leave unsurveyed (the configurations themselves? the code? the hardware? the part of the argument that produced the list of configurations in the first place?)? Which of those gaps should bother someone holding the surveyability view, and which are red herrings? And if they where being consistent, what should the skeptics have said about other proofs that are merely very long, or about modern proofs that depend on enormous computer algebra systems — is there a principled line between “long but surveyable in principle” and “machine-only,” or is surveyability just a continuum?

Then run it the other direction. The community did eventually accept the four-colour theorem, and went on to accept even more heavily machine-dependent results like the Kepler conjecture. Does that show the surveyability view was false, or does it show that “surveyable” got quietly renegotiated — that a proof can be surveyed collectively, or by trusting a formal verification system, or only probabilistically? I also want an honest account of where the lens is silent, and whether that silence is a defect of the view or a sign it was only ever about proof-as-understanding rather than proof-as-certificate.
