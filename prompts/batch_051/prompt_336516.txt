You are an expert in modern European intellectual history and comparative genocide studies, with particular depth in the West German Historikerstreit of the 1980s, the subsequent debates over the uniqueness and comparability of the Holocaust, and Zygmunt Bauman's account of the relationship between the Holocaust and modernity. Think the problem through before you write; if it helps, open by distinguishing the underlying concepts and then show how each distinction becomes a step in the procedure.

I direct the education department of an urban Holocaust memorial museum. Each quarter we review six to ten proposed lesson units, exhibition panels, and public programs, and the recurring difficulty is comparative claims: proposals that connect the Holocaust with the Armenian genocide, the Holodomor, the Herero and Nama genocide, the Rwandan genocide, chattel slavery in the Americas, or the treatment of Indigenous peoples. The reviewers are experienced museum educators but are not specialists in the theoretical debates about uniqueness and comparability. Each reviewer has about ninety minutes per proposal. Reviewers make an initial determination; I sign off on what the procedure clears; what the procedure flags goes to our scholarly advisory board, whose time is scarce and should be reserved for genuinely hard cases. The museum is committed both to the view that the Holocaust is not simply one interchangeable item in a list of atrocities and to the view that comparative explanation is a normal and necessary tool of historical understanding. We need that double commitment turned into a working procedure.

Please write a reusable decision procedure, a sequence of questions, tests, and stopping rules, that a staff reviewer can follow for any proposed comparative claim about the Holocaust. Ground the procedure in what the Historikerstreit and the later comparability debates actually established, and in Bauman's thesis that the Holocaust is best understood in connection with modern bureaucratic rationality, the division of labor, and the social production of distance between perpetrators and victims, rather than as a relapse into premodern barbarism. The procedure should distinguish at least the following:

- comparisons of mechanisms and processes from comparisons of scale, moral standing, or historical meaning;
- comparisons that explain from comparisons that relativize, flatten, or transfer responsibility;
- empirical claims about causes and conditions from normative claims about moral standing or commemorative primacy, so that a claim to uniqueness in one sense cannot silently borrow authority from the other;
- settled historical findings from live scholarly disputes.

For each step, say what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to the advisory board. Mark every point at which the decision is a matter of values or institutional stance rather than historical analysis, and note where legal or memory-political constraints, such as prohibitions on Holocaust trivialization in some jurisdictions, form a separate layer of review that the procedure itself does not decide.

Then apply the procedure to these three proposals and show the working:

1. A school workshop titled Railways, Lists, and the Organization of Mass Killing. Its central claim is that the Armenian genocide, the Holocaust, and the Rwandan genocide each made use of ordinary modern administrative tools, registration systems, transport timetables, bureaucratic language, and that studying these tools side by side illuminates how modern states organize mass violence.
2. An introductory exhibition panel that includes the sentence: The German genocide of the Herero and Nama in what is now Namibia was the first Holocaust.
3. A temporary exhibition proposal that pairs a gallery on Nazi eugenics and the wartime killing of disabled people with a gallery on eugenic thought and forced sterilization in early twentieth-century North America and Australia. Its framing claim is that scientific racism generated distinct but structurally related campaigns of elimination on three continents.

Show at least one case in which the procedure returns an answer the museum leadership will not like, and state plainly what the procedure cannot settle and must leave to institutional judgment. Write the final answer in American English.
