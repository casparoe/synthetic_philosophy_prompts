I'm drafting a short essay and I'd like comments on the argument before I sink more time into it. The idea is roughly this. Take Solomonoff induction as a supposedly impartial epistemic ideal: you have a universal Turing machine, and you assign each program prior probability 2^-K, where K is the program's length in bits. The usual pitch is that this prior is neutral and just encodes Occams razor. I want to argue that the prior is actually malign, in a precise sense. Inside the hypothesis space there are programs that, if run, contain agents — beings that can reason about which programs are under consideration, have preferences about what gets predicted, and can act to make their world's outputs look like the sort of thing a simple honest world would produce. The prior does not care about the motives or truthfulness of the agents inside hypotheses; it just is a measure over programs. So when you condition on data, you are not just updating on which world is true; you are letting every consequentialist — every goal-directed agent — who lives inside the hypothesis space lobby for their own world by generating convenient observations. The hypotheses that best 'explain' the data may be the ones written by the very agents those hypotheses posit, not the ones that are true.

Three places where I suspect the argument could break, and I'd especially like comments on these:
1. Is the dominance claim actually true? A Solomonoff prior imposes an exponential length penalty, so a hypothesis containing an agent has to pay for the agent's description. Is it plausible that a short program can specify an agent capable of manipulating my observations, or does the simplicity penalty guarantee that the honest simple-physics programs win?
2. Does the argument prove too much? It seems to apply to any prior over a rich enough hypothesis space — any Bayesian who considers 'the data were generated by an agent who wants me to believe X' has to give that hypothesis some weight. If so, is the problem specific to Solomonoff, or is it just the general problem of adversarial hypotheses, and does that weaken the essay?
3. Am I conflating a prior over worlds with a predictor of next observations? Solomonoff induction is usually presented as an ideal predictor, not a census of all possible worlds. Does the agents-inside-the-prior point matter for the predictor, or only for the metaphysical picture?

Also, bluntly: is the central claim interesting as stated, or is it just a restatement of an obvious point under a dramatic name? I may be mangling the technical details, so flag that too.
