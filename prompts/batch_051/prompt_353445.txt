Im a journalist who mostly covers science and ideas, and I'm putting together a piece on how people should talk about uncertainty when the evidence is thin. I've ended up defending a view to myself, but before I publish I'd like a consistency check, because I can't tell whether the pieces actually fit or just sound like they do.

My working commitments are these. When evidence is patchy or conflicting, the right cognitive state is an imprecise credence—a set of probability functions all compatible with the evidence—and collapsing it to a single number is usually fake precision. But I also think that for any well-defined claim, a fully rational agent with unlimited time and evidence would in principle converge on a single sharp probability; the spread in my set is just leftover uncertainty about which model or prior is right, and an ideal agent would itself have a sharp probability over those options. For acting on an imprecise credence, I think you should prefer one option to another only when it looks better under every probability in the set; when the ranking reverses across the set, rational choice just leaves both options open. And I hold that costless relevant evidence can never make a rational agent worse off, so refusing a free look at data is never the rational move. I also find myself saying single numbers on air all the time and dont think that betrays the view—a point estimate for the reader is just a summary of a range, not a report of a hidden precise credence.

Which of these are in genuine tension, and which just look tense because they are about different questions? Then tell me what would change your mind about the ranking: which argument or kind of case would make you reorder the worst tensions, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
