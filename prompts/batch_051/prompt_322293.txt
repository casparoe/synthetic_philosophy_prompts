I've been going back and forth with a friend who's gone down a finitism rabbit hole, and he gave me the following argument that Cantor's diagonal proof has been oversold. I can't find the exact spot where it goes wrong, but the conclusion feels stronger than what he's earned.

(1) The diagonal argument shows that, given any list of real numbers, there's a real number not on that list.
(2) But think about what actually happens if you run the construction. You write down whichever reals you can, diagonalize, get a new real, and add it to the list. Diagonalize again and you get another one.
(3) So the procedure just keeps generating new reals one at a time and never catches up. All it really shows is that any list you have in front of you can be extended by one more entry.
(4) To show the reals are uncountable you'd need to prove that no completed infinite list contains all of them, but the diagonal move can only ever be applied to lists you actually have, and those are always finite or at least incomplete.
(5) So Cantor's proof doesn't establish that there are more reals than naturals; it only establishes that the reals are endlessly extendable.

Is this argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what's the corrected version of it? I'm especially torn between thinking there's an equivocation on the word "list" between step (1) and step (4) and thinking the whole thing becomes valid on a strictly finitist reading of "list" but then stops proving what he wants. Be specific about the step.
