You are an expert in moral epistemology and the ethics of belief. Think the question through carefully, and begin by brainstorming the competing considerations before you settle on a proposal.

I am a philosophy professor working on the ethics of belief, and I have been collecting my own verdicts about when doubt is a moral duty rather than merely an intellectual virtue or a pragmatic precaution. By a moral duty to doubt I mean that a person who fails to doubt in that situation has wronged someone, or failed themselves in a way that deserves moral criticism, not just made an epistemic mistake. I would like you to find the principle, if there is one, that best captures my verdicts.

Here are the cases as I see them.

1. A patient with no medical training is told by an emergency-room physician that they need an immediate appendectomy. I do not think the patient has a moral duty to doubt the diagnosis or seek a second opinion before consenting. Trusting the physician is permissible.

2. A juror in a criminal trial feels visceral certainty that the defendant is guilty, though the evidence is entirely circumstantial and the defense has offered a plausible alternative account. I think the juror has a moral duty to doubt that felt certainty and to demand proof beyond a reasonable doubt.

3. A person raised in an isolated community has always been taught that outsiders are dangerous and deceitful. On meeting an outsider who is plainly kind and honest, I think the person has a moral duty to doubt the teaching they were raised with, at least enough to investigate further rather than dismiss the evidence of their own eyes.

4. A parent reads one vivid online anecdote about a vaccine injury and begins to question a vaccine that has been tested in large trials and is endorsed by an overwhelming medical consensus. I do not think the parent has a moral duty to doubt the consensus; the anecdote is too weak to ground that duty. But I do not think the parent should simply suppress the worry either, and I cannot yet say what residual obligation that leaves.

5. A voter hears a charismatic candidate make sweeping, self-serving claims about a disliked minority group. I think the voter has a moral duty to doubt those claims.

6. A person in a crowded theater is told by an official that there is a fire and that they must leave through a particular exit. I do not think they have a moral duty to doubt the instruction or to verify it before acting.

I can already see a tension: cases 2 and 5 treat unearned conviction and convenient claims as generating a duty to doubt, while cases 1, 4, and 6 permit or even require deference to authority and consensus. But case 3 treats an apparently authoritative consensus as something one can be morally required to doubt. Is there one principle — about stakes, social roles, the reliability of sources, the weight of first-hand evidence, or something subtler — that captures all these verdicts? If not, which verdicts should I give up, and what does the surviving principle say about a case I have not listed: a patient whose doctor recommends a procedure from which the doctor will profit, and who has no independent way to check the recommendation?
