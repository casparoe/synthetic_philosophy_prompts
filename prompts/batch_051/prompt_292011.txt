I have been rereading Keynes on the good life—the line of thought in which he predicts that the prosperous societies will eventually solve the economic problem of producing enough, and will then face what he took to be a much harder problem: knowing how to use the resulting freedom and leisure. On this view, the money motive is a temporary discipline rather than an end, and the real test of a society is how it handles abundance, not scarcity.

A friend pushed me on how this cashes out in the judgments we already make, and I keep noticing an asymmetry. Consider two people whose days look nearly identical: each works only a few hours a week, keeps a garden, plays music, reads seriously, sees friends often, and spends little on status. One of them has an unearned income that makes this comfortable. The other has a perfectly ordinary job at reduced hours and deliberately keeps expenses low so that the same life is affordable, though with far less margin for bad luck. The first is readily admired as someone who has figured out how to live, perhaps even as wise for not letting money rule her. The second is more likely to be described as lazy, irresponsible, or coasting, even when no one else is made worse off by the choice.

I want to know whether anything in a Keynesian account of the good life can justify that difference, or whether the view is in tension with the very judgments people make. Please give me the strongest principle you can find—something about security, risk, desert, productivity, or the example one sets—that would generate both judgments, test it against nearby cases, and then give an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up. In particular, if the first life really is the better one, the good life starts to look like something one can own rather than do; if the second life is the better one, much of our everyday blame is probably status anxiety. I want to know which conclusion you would accept and why.
