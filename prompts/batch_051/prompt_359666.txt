I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about why knowing better doesn't stop us from doing the dumber thing. I want you to play the role of an expert on ancient philosophy and behavioral economics. Please start by brainstorming the exact meanings of akrasia and hyperbolic discounting, and the points where they might overlap or conflict. Then think carefully before answering; I care about the reasoning, not just the verdict.

In Book VII of the Nicomachean Ethics, Aristotle makes a distinction that I've always found more interesting than the usual "knowing the good but doing the bad" summary. He says, roughly, that a person can have the words of knowledge without the knowledge being fully active, the way someone who is drunk can rattle off the verses of Empedocles, or the way a novice can repeat a proof without having got it into his bones. (I'm paraphrasing; don't get hung up on the wording.)

Let's define akrasia with these working criteria:
1. The agent holds an all-things-considered judgment that action A would be better than action B.
2. The agent voluntarily does B anyway.
3. The judgment is not merely rote or verbal; it is available to the agent in the moment.
4. The cause of acting against judgment is some appetite, emotion, or temptation, rather than a settled intellectual mistake.

Now consider Greg. On Tuesday evening, he can articulate exactly why he should get up at 6 a.m. and exercise. He says the future health benefits outweigh the immediate comfort, and he genuinely means it. He sets the alarm. At 6 a.m., the immediate comfort of staying in bed is overwhelmingly vivid, while the future benefit feels remote and small. He tells himself, "I knew this would happen. I'm going to do it anyway," and goes back to sleep.

Does Greg's behavior qualify as akrasia under the criteria? Go criterion by criterion, and be honest where the criteria underdetermine the verdict. Then use Aristotle's drunk-reciting-Empedocles point to argue whether Greg's 6 a.m. judgment was fully active knowledge or something less. Finally, compare this with a hyperbolic-discounting story: is the scaling down of the future benefit just a modern way of describing the same gap Aristotle was pointing at, or does it actually change the philosophical diagnosis?
