I've ended up a luck egalitarian about health, mostly because the last two years of arguing with my insurer over something that was plainly bad luck pushed me there. The view I'd now defend is something like this: a health inequality is unjust to the extent it traces to brute luck rather than option luck, so a smoker's lung cancer and a never-smoker's lung cancer make different claims, but only if everyone genuinely faced fair odds going in; once the risk has been run, society owes the unlucky nothing beyond what a fair ex ante distribution of chances already promised. I dont want the view defended. I want to know what would count against it, and I dont mean the usual line about abandoning the imprudent. Work through the defeaters the way a committed defender would: which cases have to be misdescribed, which consequences I have to swallow, where does the view slide into plain egalitarianism or into crass libertarianism? For each, say whether it's decisive or merely awkward, and rank them. And one thing I definitly want you to be honest about: is the ex ante/ex post distinction doing real work here, or is it a movable buffer that keeps the view from ever touching the ground?
