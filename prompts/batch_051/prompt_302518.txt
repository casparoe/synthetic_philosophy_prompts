Design a ninety-minute classroom simulation for an undergraduate seminar in political philosophy or social choice (roughly 16 participants) that makes participants feel, rather than merely hear, the core idea behind futarchy: that a group should first vote on what outcomes matter, and then let markets bet on which policies are expected to produce those outcomes, so that information aggregation and preference aggregation come apart. The activity must use play currency (no real money), must be resolvable by the end of the session than the session itself and must be arranged so that students run into the separation of values from predictions through the incentives themselves; withhold the name "futarchy" until the debrief. The decision rule in the game should mirror the logic of the actual proposal — for instance, a policy is adopted when the market's conditional forecast of the chosen outcome metric under that policy is higher than under the status quo — but participants should discover this logic by acting under it, not by being told. Include roles, rules, materials, a timed sequence, a payoff scheme, and how the outcome metric is chosen and scored. Then provide a debrief that maps specific moments in the simulation onto these components: the metric-setting step, conditional bets, the adoption rule, and the division between what participants want and what they predict. State what is likely to go wrong on a first run and how to handle it, and be candid about which features of real futarchy the simulation cannot reproduce and that the facilitator must therefore supply. Prescribe the sections of the response in this order with these approximate lengths: Setup and materials (150 words), Rules and timeline (400 words), Debrief and mapping (300 words), Likely first-run failures and fixes (150 words), What the simulation cannot reproduce (100 words).
