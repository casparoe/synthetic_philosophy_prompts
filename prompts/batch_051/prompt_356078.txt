I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I will be presenting next month at a reading group I attend at work on probability, decision theory, and reasoning under uncertainty. The topic I chose is the two-envelope paradox: you are shown two envelopes, one containing twice as much money as the other, you choose one, and before you open it a slick expected-value argument seems to show you should switch, since the other envelope offers a 50% chance of doubling your amount and a 50% chance of halving it (0.5 × 2x + 0.5 × x/2 = 1.25x). The same argument would then seem to tell you to switch back, which is absurd. The position I want to defend is that there is no single localized flaw in the switching argument that fixes the paradox once and for all. Instead, whether switching is justified depends on modeling commitments the puzzle leaves unspecified — most importantly, a probability distribution over the smaller amount and a precise statement of how the envelopes were prepared and what you observe. On my account the puzzle does not contain a contradiction to be dissolved; it shows that expected-value reasoning is not well-posed until the probability model is fully specified.

The audience is about a dozen colleagues: mostly software engineers comfortable with probability and some Bayesian inference, two data scientists who think in terms of simulations and Monte Carlo error, one older statistician with strong frequentist instincts who gets visibly irritated by improper priors, and one machine-learning researcher who tends to reduce every probabilistic puzzle to a sampling error or a conditioning error. None of them has read the philosophical literature on this paradox, and they will not accept a purely verbal resolution; they will want formal statements or at least a clean simulation.

Write me a Q&A prep sheet for this talk. For the eight or so objections I will actually get from this group — not the objections a philosophy journal would raise — order them by roughly expected damage, phrase each one the way the relevant person would actually say it, and for each give me: what I should concede up front, what I should refuse to concede, a two-minute reply, and a note on where that reply is soft. End with the one question I should be hoping nobody asks.
