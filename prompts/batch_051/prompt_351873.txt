After a relative of mine spent a month in hospital this year, I started noticing an assymetry in how medicine treats negative and positive experience, and I can't figure out what theory of value the practice is actually committed to. Hospitals take pain very seriously: it gets charted as a kind of extra vital sign, there are numeric 0-to-10 scales, whole specialties exist for pain management and palliative care, and a doctor who under-treats someone's pain can face real consequences. But there's no corresponding machinery for pleasure or other positive experience. Nobody records whether a patient is enjoying themselves, there's no such thing as a consultation for pleasure, and making a patient happier is not treated as a medical outcome in the way that making them hurt less is. If you just looked at the practice, you might conclude it presupposes something like a threshold negative utilitarianism: the idea that below some intensity threshold experiences don't count (or count lexically below more intense pain), and that no amount of mild pleasure can offset severe pain, so the system rightly ignores small goods and focuses on big bads.

But I'm not convinced the practice actually needs that strong a theory. Ordinary symmetric utilitarianism plus a division of labor could explain the same pattern: medicine handles the suffering, while other institutions are supposed to handle the flourishing. So what I'd like is a reconstruction of the implicit theory: what does the practice strictly presuppose, what does it merely fit with, and where is it philosophically underdetermined such that several theories would license the same behavior? And what would have to change in, say, hospital intake forms, triage rules, or end-of-life care if you replaced the presupposed theory with a fully symmetric view where pleasure and pain sit on one scale? Is there anywhere in current practice where the presupposition is already inconsistent — for instance, where hospitals do act as if positive experience could offset or blunt pain, like distraction techniques during painful procedures, or quality-of-life scores that mix goods and bads into a single number?
