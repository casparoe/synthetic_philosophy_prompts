Give me a working definition of incorruptibility. If the word does two distinct jobs and no single definition handles all uses, give me the definitions you think are needed and mark their boundaries. Then test your account against each of the following cases:

1. A public-works auditor who refuses a six-figure bribe and reports it, knowing that reporting it may end his career.
2. A cabinet minister who steers state contracts to relatives in exchange for a cut, and regards the practice as normal.
3. A judge who is famously proof against money, but whose rulings reliably bend toward the approval of a circle of wealthy former classmates whose good opinion she craves.
4. A licensing clerk who would happily sell permits, but who works under supervision so tight that no one ever makes him an offer.
5. A mafia accountant who cannot be bought, threatened, or flattered into turning state's evidence or stealing from the family, because his loyalty to it is absolute — and whose bookkeeping keeps an extortion operation running.
6. A regulator who obeys every rule only because she is certain that violations would be detected, and who would take bribes if she believed detection were impossible.
7. A legislator who accepts large lawful campaign donations from an industry, votes for that industry's bills, and sincerely believes those policies are best for the country.
8. An automated procurement system with hard-coded rules and no channel through which a bribe could be offered, but which pays false invoices against its own criteria when the invoices are plausibly forged.

For each case, say how you are inclined to classify it — incorruptible, corruptible, or genuinely unclear — then apply your definition or definitions and reconcile the two. Flag any case on which your definition gives a counterintuitive answer or no answer, and revise it or defend that surprise.

Use the cases to test whether the word tracks a formal property of the will — being unmoved from whatever standard one has settled on, however chosen — or a substantive moral achievement — being unmoved from the standard one should hold because it is the right one — or whether it tracks both at once. If it tracks both, say which cases force the distinction and which use of the word you recommend preserving. Conclude by answering whether incorruptibility is always good; if it is not, specify the conditions under which it stops being good.
