I am a philosophy professor, and I chair the grants committee of a small private foundation that funds research on complex systems, cognition, and the origins of biological organization. We meet monthly and make roughly eight funding decisions per quarter, several in the six figures, so the cost of a bad theory call is real. Two failures recur. Some proposals explain several levels at once by invoking a single principle — self-organization, a tendency toward complexity, a drive toward consciousness, an attractor — without specifying a mechanism that could build the higher levels out of the lower, step by step. Others, including some of our own reviewers, dismiss mid-level explanations as merely chemistry, merely computation, or merely neurons, and demand that everything be reduced to the bottom level in one move. I want you to turn Dennett's distinction between cranes and skyhooks, together with his contrast between good reductionism and greedy reductionism, into a written screening procedure my committee can run in twenty minutes per proposal. The committee is interdisciplinary and has no training in philosophy, so the procedure must be usable by a working biologist, a computer scientist, and an economist, with a philosopher on call for the hard cases. Give the questions in the order they should be asked, state what counts as a passing answer to each, say where the procedure stops and rejects or flags, and mark the steps that are judgment calls rather than mechanical tests. Then run the procedure on two or three short illustrative proposals of your own invention, including at least one where the procedure returns an answer the committee will not like. Finish by saying what the procedure cannot do, and make the final line of your response a one-sentence summary.
