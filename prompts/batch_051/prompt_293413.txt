I retired a few years ago from a career in the natural sciences, where I spent most of my time fitting models to noisy data and learning to distrust conclusions that outrun their assumptions. I have recently begun reading philosophy more systematically, and I find myself circling a problem in the longtermist literature that I cannot resolve to my satisfaction.

The argument that initially impressed me runs like this: if humanity survives, the future could contain an astronomically large number of lives; therefore, even a very small reduction in the probability of extinction has an expected value that dwarfs almost any other intervention. I understand this to be the expected-value argument for existential-risk reduction, associated especially with Nick Bostrom. But I then encountered a newer line of thought about suffering risks: the idea that the far future might contain not only, or not chiefly, flourishing lives but astronomical quantities of suffering, especially if we begin creating large populations of artificial minds. A future in which digital minds can be copied as cheaply as files also seems to be a future in which vast numbers of minds might exist in states we would not wish on anyone—used as instruments, run in damaged or truncated versions, or discarded after serving some narrow function. That possibility appears to strike directly at the inference from huge future to hugely good in expectation.

I would like you to act as a professional philosopher working in population ethics and longtermism. Please think carefully, brainstorm the candidate repairs before committing, and then give a reasoned verdict. Does the classical expected-value argument for existential-risk reduction survive this development, need repair, or quietly collapse? I want the analysis to remain focused on the argument rather than drifting into policy recommendations, and I am not looking for further reading. In particular: which premise does the suffering-risk possibility bear on? Can the argument be salvaged by adding a premise that we can steer the future toward positive valence, or does that replace the original argument with a different one? Is there a symmetry problem—if the far future could be vastly positive or vastly negative, does a small reduction in extinction risk become a gamble rather than a robust good? And if the argument does collapse, what, if anything, is left of the claim that reducing extinction risk is the dominant moral priority?
