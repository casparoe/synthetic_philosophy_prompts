I have a friend whose judgments about whether future-tensed sentences are true, false, or neither are far more disciplined than mine. She is not a professional philosopher, but when I introduced her to the sea-battle puzzle she immediately began producing verdicts case by case, and I have been trying to reverse-engineer the rule that best compresses them. She treats every sentence as true, false, or neither at the time it is uttered, and she resists labels, so I am left with the data.

Here are five of her verdicts.

1. The tide. Spoken this evening: 'The tide will be high at this pier at 6:41 tomorrow morning.' The harbor charts, which she accepts, determine this; she says the sentence is true now.

2. The sea battle. Two fleets are in position. Neither commander has decided whether to attack tomorrow, and the weather is a genuinely chancy factor that would permit or prevent an engagement. Spoken this evening: 'There will be a sea battle tomorrow.' She says: neither true nor false.

3. The negation. Same setup as (2). 'There will not be a sea battle tomorrow.' She says: neither.

4. The coin. Immediately before a genuinely random toss of a fair coin: 'This coin will land heads.' She says: neither.

5. The exhaustive alternative. Immediately before the same toss: 'This coin will land either heads or tails.' She says: true, because one of the two outcomes is guaranteed even though neither specific outcome is.

Now consider the new case, in the same sea-battle setup as (2): the battle tomorrow is still open. The sentence is:

'Either there will be a sea battle tomorrow, or there will be no sea battle tomorrow.'

Assume she reads it as a disjunction of two future-tensed claims, not as a single future-tensed disjunction. What would she say at the time of utterance: true, false, or neither?

I am less interested in the bare prediction than in the structure. Tell me which rule or rules best fit her five verdicts, which other rules fit them just as well, and where those rules come apart. Then give me your best guess at her verdict on the new sentence, with a defense, and construct one further sentence from the same open sea-battle situation that would most cleanly tell the competing rules apart.
