Hey—I'm a software engineer with basically no formal philosophy background, so cut me some slack if I'm mangling terms. Lately I've been thinking about curiosity, and whether knowledge is really good for its own sake. On one side there's a view I've basically absorbed from decision theory that feels like the rigorous version of that idea: the expected value of information is never negative, because in the worst case you can just ignore the new info and act exactly as you would have acted before. From inside that frame, curiosity looks like a pure virtue, and 'more true beliefs' can only help. At work this is obviously right—better logs, better observability, better data, better decisions, full stop.

But I also have an intuition I can't shake: some knowledge makes a life worse, and some curiosity is a vice, not a virtue. If I could press a button to learn the exact date of my death, or hear unfiltered everything my friends and partner actually think about me, or satisfy a morbid curiosity about the details of something awful, I don't think I'd press it—and I don't think I'd be making a mistake. It feels like knowing things changes me, or changes the relationship, in a way the expected-value-of-information story is just blind to. It assumes 'me' and what I want stay fixed while the facts arrive, and that dosen't seem true.

I'm not looking for a reading list or citations. I want you to act as a professional philosopher who also knows some decision theory, think carefully, and start by brainstorming the strongest version of each side before you settle anything. Then walk me through: what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes (is it scope insensitivity, an imagination failure, something about how the button case misleads me?); what it would take for the theory to be the thing that goes (does the expected-value argument have real holes when information changes your preferences, when you can't commit to ignoring it, or when the knowing itself is what does the harm?); and whether there's a reading where both survive—like curiosity being a genuine virtue but a bounded one, or knowledge being worth wanting for its own sake only when it's integrated into understanding instead of raw facts. I want a negotiated verdict on which should yield and why, not just a vote for one side.
