I recently spent a long evening arguing with a close friend about whether petitionary prayer makes sense. My friend, though not religious, was arguing on the believer's own terms: if God is omniscient, God already knows what we need before we ask; if God is perfectly good, God is already doing what is best for the people we pray for; and if God is immutable, nothing I say today could move God's will from one course to another. He concluded that asking God for specific outcomes—a relative's recovery, a job, safe travel—is either redundant or implicitly treats God as changeable and in need of persuasion.

I pushed back that many of the people who pray also affirm that God's will should be done and that God already knows our needs, so they are not assuming a changeable God. My friend replied that this only makes the practice harder to understand: if the request is not expected to make any difference to what God does, why ask for outcomes at all rather than simply meditating or giving thanks? And why do the same traditions speak of God as someone who hears and answers petitions, and urge people to ask for good things, if the asking itself does no work? By the end I had the uncomfortable feeling that the practice contains the pieces of an answer without my being able to state it.

I want you to reconstruct what a person is implicitly committed to when they engage in the standard practice of petitionary prayer while also affirming that God is omniscient, perfectly good, and immutable. In particular: what does the practice strictly presuppose about whether and how a creature's request can figure in what God does? Is it committed to the claim that God would have done otherwise had the prayer not been offered, or can it be made coherent without that claim? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several different theologies could license the same outward practice. Finally, say what would have to change in the practice—or in the surrounding way of speaking—under each of the reconstructions you think are most plausible.

At the very end of your answer, write one line in the fixed form \answer{...} containing a short phrase (no more than six words) that best names what you take the practice to strictly presuppose about God's relation to petitionary requests.
