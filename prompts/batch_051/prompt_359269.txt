I spent my career in theoretical physics and have only recently begun reading philosophy of science in earnest. I have been asked to contribute a short research proposal to a small philosophy-of-science workshop, and I would like you to draft it for me, roughly 1,500 words, on a question I am trying to make precise about Wigner's problem of the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics.

My sense, from what I have read so far, is that the problem as usually received runs together two observations. The first is that mathematics developed with one empirical aim later proves apt for a remote and unforeseen physical domain. The second, stronger and harder to establish, is that mathematics developed with no empirical aim at all, often as a free exploration of pure structure, later turns out to be exactly the formalism some physical theory needs. The familiar examples — complex numbers in quantum mechanics, non-Euclidean geometry in general relativity, group-theoretic methods in particle physics — do not, I suspect, cleanly establish the second observation. Selection effects seem to be doing a great deal of hidden work: the pure mathematics that later proved useful is remembered, the mathematics that led nowhere is forgotten, and much allegedly pure mathematics was produced by people already steeped in the physical culture in which it later found use.

The proposal should therefore settle a determinate question rather than merely survey the puzzle. The question I want addressed is whether, once these selection effects and historical entanglements are controlled for as far as possible, the residual phenomenon still supports a substantive claim about the structure of the natural world, or whether it reduces to a fact about how mathematicians and physicists choose, remember, and evaluate problems. State this question in a form with a determinate answer, and specify at the outset what would count as evidence for each conclusion.

For the approach, I have in mind either a controlled set of historical case studies or a corpus-based comparison of base rates, with criteria fixed in advance for what counts as mathematical work undertaken without empirical intent, what counts as a genuinely unanticipated application, and what counts as failure to find one. You may prefer another method, but if so you must justify it. Explain why the chosen approach is better than the obvious alternatives, such as relying on the canonical examples or arguing only from general considerations about scientific realism. Close with a statement of what a successful outcome of the project would look like, what would count as failure — including the honest possibility that the method cannot settle the question — and what could make the project too large for its length, with a way to keep it tractable.

Since I will supply the scholarly apparatus myself, please write the proposal without citations or a reference list; where a named position matters, describe the position and its argument in your own terms.
