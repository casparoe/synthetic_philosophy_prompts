I am a novelist, and I have been asked to lead a ninety-minute session at a residential workshop for writers and literary translators, with two philosophers also in residence, one a philosopher of language and the other a moral philosopher; I want the session to leave the room able to think with, and not merely repeat, a single idea. The idea is one I keep returning to in my own craft: that a concept is not only something whose real content one discovers by studying how it is actually used, but also a tool for which we can be partly responsible. When I revise, I am not merely determining what my characters mean by words like pardon, inheritance, or work; I am deciding what those concepts should do in the fictional world, and the choice is answerable as much to what the world needs them to do as to what ordinary usage licenses. I have since learned that philosophers discuss something close to this under the headings of conceptual engineering, conceptual ethics, and ameliorative analysis, and I want the session to make that apparatus vivid and usable, rather than to survey the field or name its figures.

Act as a professional philosopher who works on conceptual ethics and conceptual engineering and has taught the material to writers, artists, and other non-specialists. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming possible pedagogical routes and the resistances this particular room will produce; if you consider several candidate examples before settling on one, say briefly why the others failed. Then give me a teaching plan, not the explanation itself. I need: the exact order in which the components should be introduced and what depends on what; the one carrying example that should do most of the work, with the properties it must have and must lack; the misconceptions this audience will predictably bring or form, and the precise point in the sequence where each should be headed off. In particular, I suspect the writers will hear the claim that concepts can be changed as a license for euphemism or branding, while the philosophers will suspect the whole enterprise of collapsing into changing the subject; address those, but do not let them crowd out the subtler misunderstandings. Also tell me what should be left out entirely at this level and how to say so, and give me a final check, a small task or question, that would show the idea has landed rather than merely its vocabulary.
