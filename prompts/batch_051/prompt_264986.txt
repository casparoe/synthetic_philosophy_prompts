I am a PhD student in decision theory, and I am writing the section of a survey that treats the Allais paradox as a standing problem for the independence axiom. After an argument with a colleague about whether the proposed explanations in this area are genuinely distinct or just redescriptions of one another, I decided to catalogue the accounts I actually rely on in my own work. The phenomenon I have in mind is the classic common-consequence pattern: a decision maker prefers a sure $1 million to the prospect ($1 million with probability .89, $5 million with probability .10, nothing with probability .01), and then, in a second pair, prefers ($5 million with probability .10, nothing with probability .90) to ($1 million with probability .11, nothing with probability .89). So far my list has four entries. (1) The certainty effect, from Kahneman and Tversky's original prospect theory: certain outcomes are overweighted relative to merely probable ones, so the sure million does disproportionate work. (2) Regret theory: choosing the gamble and landing in the one-percent branch that yields nothing would be especially painful because the sure million was available, and anticipated regret colors the first choice. (3) Disappointment aversion: a prospect that mixes a good outcome with a bad one carries an extra negative term from anticipated disappointment, and the sure million avoids that term. (4) Rank-dependent probability weighting: probabilities are transformed into decision weights through a nonlinear function of cumulative probability, so the linearity in probabilities that the independence axiom requires simply does not describe actual choice.

I need at least one further account of the same pattern, and it should be genuinely different from these four: not a restatement, not a special case, and not a combination of two of them. It would also help if the account had something definite to say about the common-ratio variant, where the first pair is a choice between $3,000 for sure and an 80 percent chance of $4,000 (with nothing otherwise), and the second pair is a choice between a 25 percent chance of $3,000 and a 20 percent chance of $4,000 (with nothing otherwise); I am not sure my four entries carry over cleanly to that variant, and I would like to know whether the account you propose does. Please state the account, say where its work is done — in the treatment of the probabilities, in the treatment of the outcomes, in the comparison between the options, or somewhere else — and explain what it adds to the four entries already on the list.
