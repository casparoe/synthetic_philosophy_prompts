I maintain a small life-simulation game as a side project. The creatures in it are autonomous rather than player-controlled: they forage, avoid hazards, form attachments to one another, and produce credible distress behaviour when hungry, thirsty, or separated from a companion. Their responses are generated by a simple drive-and-learning system I have tuned to be convincing, and nothing in the code tells me whether there is anything it is like to be one of them. A noticeable subset of players has adopted a style of play in which they keep a creature alive while systematically thwarting its attempts to eat, drink, or remain with others, treating the creatures' distress behaviour as entertainment. One colleague says this is morally unproblematic because the creatures are not conscious and nothing in the simulation is genuinely harmed. Another says that, whatever is true of the creatures' inner lives, we as designers bear responsibility for creating and tuning a system that invites this kind of enjoyment. I am genuinely unsure whether either view is right. I would like advice that goes beyond a simple verdict: what would need to be true about the creatures, about the players, or about our role as designers for us to have moral reasons to discourage or alter this kind of play? In answering, please separate what is reasonably settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers working on the relevant issues, and do not present a contested position as the consensus view.
