I recently had a long argument with a friend about whether hate speech should be regulated in the way the United States does or in the way many European countries do. My friend kept returning to a passage from John Stuart Mill's *On Liberty*, and I want to make sure I understand the passage before I either concede his point or push back on it.

The passage is from Chapter III, "Of Individuality, as One of the Elements of Well-Being." Mill writes: "even opinions lose their immunity, when the circumstances in which they are expressed are such as to constitute their expression a positive instigation to some mischievous act. An opinion that corn-dealers are starvers of the poor, or that private property is robbery, ought to be unmolested when simply circulated through the press, but may justly incur punishment when delivered orally to an excited mob assembled before the house of a corn-dealer, or when handed about among the same mob in the form of a placard."

My friend took this passage to show that Mill draws exactly the line drawn by the American First Amendment tradition: opinion is protected in the abstract, but not when it functions as incitement. I am not sure the passage supports that reading. It seems to me that Mill is distinguishing not between abstract speech and incitement as such, but between two ways the very same opinion can be expressed, and that the presence of an excited mob is doing much of the work. I also cannot tell whether Mill's test is closer to the European approach than my friend admitted, since European hate speech laws sometimes punish expression because of the climate or foreseeable environment it creates, even when no single harmful act can be traced back to a particular utterance.

Could you help me work through the passage? In particular: what exactly is the principle that makes the press case unmolested and the mob case punishable? Is the relevant difference the medium, the audience, the foreseeable consequences, or something else? And on the best reading, does Mill's position align more with the American approach, the European approach, or neither—perhaps cutting across both in a way that should make my friend and me revise our claims? If you are willing, apply Mill's distinction to one modern hard case of your own choosing, such as hateful content posted in an online forum whose readers are enraged but not physically gathered in one place.

Please answer in American English. Finally, I would like you to say how confident you are in each of your main claims and to single out the point in your interpretation that you are least sure of.
