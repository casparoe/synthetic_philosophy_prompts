In my moral philosophy seminar we've been discussing the distinction between facts and values, and I keep noticing a pattern in arguments that I don't have a word for. A speaker begins with a factual claim about what something is for or how it naturally develops—its evolved function, its role in normal maturation, the way it properly operates—and then concludes that certain actions, feelings, or arrangements are appropriate, required, or good. One student argued that because the human circadian rhythm evolved to align with daylight, people ought to avoid bright artificial light late at night. In a paper for another class, the author argued that because the function of jealousy is to protect a pair bond, experiencing jealousy is in some measure appropriate in a committed relationship, and its total absence is a defect. When challenged that these arguments move from an is to an ought, the speakers replied that they were not doing that: they were reasoning from proper function, and 'proper' is already an evaluative notion, so the inference stays within the evaluative domain.

Is there an established name for this specific move—starting with a claim about normal, natural, or evolved function and sliding into a normative conclusion while insisting that the move escapes the is-ought gap because the premise is already evaluative? How exactly does it relate to the naturalistic fallacy and to the is-ought gap more generally: is it a disguised instance of one of them, a distinct mistake, or a defensible form of teleological reasoning? If no name exists, I would like you to propose one and define it precisely enough to distinguish it from the naturalistic fallacy, from plain teleological ethics, and from an ordinary inference from fact to value. A bare label won't help me; I'm after the exact carving of this move and where its boundaries lie.
