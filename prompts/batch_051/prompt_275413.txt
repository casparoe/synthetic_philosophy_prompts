I spent my career in a lab, so my approach to this stuff is to treat a page of political theory like a reaction setup and ask what actually forms. I've been reading The Federalist and landed on this in Madison's Federalist 49, where he's answering Jefferson's proposal that constitutional breaches get settled by a convention of the people:

"In the next place, it may be considered as an objection inherent in the principle, that as every appeal to the people would carry an implication of some defect in the government, frequent appeals would, in a great measure, deprive the government of that veneration which time bestows on every thing, and without which perhaps the wisest and freest governments would not possess the requisite stability."

Suppose somebody took that worry as a design specification: a new constitution says that any amendment passed by the legislature enters a ten-year cooling-off period before a second vote can make it law, and during those ten years no public money may be spent advocating for or against it; an independent office just publishes a neutral digest of the original arguments once a year. Ordinary referendums on constitutional questions are also barred during the cooling-off window. What I want is a read on how this would actually run, not whether its noble. What does the delay do to who can afford to pursue a constitutional change and who can't, how do parties and interest groups route around the spending ban, what does the "neutral digest" turn into in practice, and where does the scheme get gamed first? Give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion.
