Take the classic lifeboat case: a boat with 40 people aboard, a fixed safe capacity of 50, and 100 swimmers in the water. Change one assumption. Rather than a fixed capacity, let the effective carrying capacity be a function of the number aboard, because each added person contributes labor to fishing, desalination, and repair up to a crowding point, while still consuming stores. Assume everyone knows the function

C(n) = 50 + 1.5(n − 40) − 0.025(n − 40)^2,    40 ≤ n ≤ 100,

where n is the number aboard and C(n) is the number the boat can now safely sustain. Keep the rest of the case fixed: swimmers not admitted drown, and if the number aboard exceeds the boat's effective capacity, the excess cannot be sustained.

Work out exactly what each side would press in this revised case: (a) Hardin's lifeboat ethic, according to which those already aboard must not jeopardize their own survival for the sake of those in the water, and (b) the spaceship or equal-consideration ethic, according to which the interests of all survivors count equally. Then answer: does the anti-admission conclusion of the original lifeboat argument survive this change, and if not, which specific premise of the original argument is doing the work? Finally, say whether the lifeboat/spaceship distinction still tracks an important moral line when carrying capacity is endogenous, or whether it reduces to a technical question about optimal crowding.
