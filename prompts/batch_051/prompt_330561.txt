I am a novelist, and for the past year I have been revising a manuscript set in a small town that accommodated itself to an occupying power and only afterwards had to reckon with what it had done. While redrafting, I noticed something about my own habits of attributing blame. My characters seem to incur guilt in four different registers. First, a few of them broke the law and can be tried in a court. Second, the town as a whole, including people who merely kept their jobs and looked away, is answerable for what was done in its name. Third, certain characters judge themselves in private for small acts of cowardice and compliance, for the letters they did not write. Fourth, and strangest, a man who survived when others did not carries a guilt that seems to attach to no particular act: a guilt of not having risked everything, of failed solidarity with the dead. It was only later that I recognised how closely these four registers track the distinction Karl Jaspers drew after the war among criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt. I was not consciously working from him, and I do not know whether the convergence should please or embarrass me.

What I want is help deciding whether this resemblance is deep or superficial. The shallow reading is that I have simply absorbed Jaspers's categories as cultural furniture and reached for them because they were there. The deeper reading is that narrative fiction is, among other things, a device for distributing responsibility across precisely these levels, and that it does so because blame really has these four distinct shapes, with stories serving as our means of keeping them apart. If the deeper reading is right, then Jaspers's typology would not be parochial to the Germany he was trying to understand, and the categories of fiction would not be merely aesthetic conventions: each side would illuminate the other. If it is wrong, the four labels line up by accident and the analogy is merely a pleasing symmetry. Could you adjudicate? I would like a precise statement of the mapping between the two sides, an account of what would have to be true of Jaspers's categories and of narrative practice for the parallel to be more than verbal, and at least one point at which the analogy noticeably breaks down.

Please separate what is settled in the interpretation of Jaspers from what is genuinely contested among scholars, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. Please also answer in British English.
