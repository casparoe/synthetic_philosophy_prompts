Design a realistic, one-paragraph matched pair of scenarios — a lying case and a misleading case — that isolates assertion as the putatively moral variable in the lying/misleading debate. I want to use this pair in a discussion of whether there is a principled moral difference between lying and merely misleading. Constraints: the scenarios must be a matched pair (same speaker, same listener, same subject matter, same practical stakes), and the only intended difference between them should be whether a given false proposition is asserted or merely implicated; in the misleading case, every literal assertion must be true, with the deception carried entirely by implicature; the speaker's intention to deceive, the listener's resulting belief, and all downstream consequences must be identical across the two cases; the false content must be of real consequence rather than a compliment, pleasantry, or social lubricant; and the implicature must be one a competent speaker could reasonably expect a competent listener to draw, not a mishearing, ambiguity, or idiosyncratic inference. Avoid legalistic contexts such as oaths, perjury, or cross-examination. Do not build the moral verdict into the narration of the cases; the verdicts should be generated by the two views when they are applied.

After presenting the pair, do the checks. State exactly which proposition is asserted in the lying case and which proposition is implicated but not asserted in the misleading case. Then explain why an assertion-based view — on which lying is morally worse because the liar stakes her word on the false content and licenses the listener's reliance in a stronger way — must give the lying case the more severe verdict, while an intention-and-harm view — on which the wrong of deception lies in aiming to induce a false belief and in the harm thereby done — must treat the two cases as morally on a par. Identify the single structural feature doing the work, say what the pair collapses into if that feature is removed, and finish with the strongest objection a defender of the no-moral-difference view would press against using this case to show that lying is worse.
