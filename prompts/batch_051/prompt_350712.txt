A mechanism I keep coming back to for putting ethical reasoning into a hospital triage bot, and I'd rather have someone walk me through the failure modes than tell me whether it's a good idea in general. The bot runs three submodels for each decision. One is a deontic checker: a fixed list of hard constraints (no age-weighting, no discounting someone for 'social worth', can't unilaterally take a ventilator off a patient to give it to someone with better prospects, that kind of thing). The second is a utilitarian ranker that orders patients by expected QALYs. The third is a prioritarian ranker that uses the same QALY estimate but applies extra weight the worse off the patient is before treatment. For each allocation the bot first filters options through the deontic checker. If the utilitarian and prioritarian rankers then agree on the top patient, it allocates to that patient. If they disagree, a human triage officer is paged — but the human can only choose betwen the two patients the rankers each put first; they can't introduce a third name. The rankers are retrained monthly on the hospital's own outcomes, and every human override gets logged as a training example with the human's choice as the label. Walk me through how this actually behaves in practice. Who games it first, and how? What does the retraining loop do once overrides become labels? Where does it start making decisions that look ethically stupid from the outside, and can the failure modes be fixed without scrapping the whole architecture? Answer in British English, please.
