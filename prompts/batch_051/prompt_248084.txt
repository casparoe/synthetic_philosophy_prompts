Longtime lurker on rationalist blogs and forums here, so I've already tried the obvious moves — tabooing the word "cause," swapping in counterfactual talk, etc. — and the argument keeps respawning. Two friends and I are stuck on whether omissions can be genuine causes. Friend A says obviously yes: if a nurse doesn't administer a drug and the patient dies, the nurse's not administering it caused the death, in the same literal sense that administering the wrong dose would have. Friend B says that's a category mistake: only events can be causes, and an omission is a non-event, so "causation by omission" is blame-talk wearing a metaphysics costume.

I want the strongest test you can design for whether there's a real difference-making relation being tracked when someone calls an omission a cause, rather than a projection of norm-violation or blame. I'd especially like a design that holds the counterfactual structure fixed across conditions — in every case, if the person had acted, the outcome wouldn't have happened — and varies the normative background seperately: was the omitted act expected, required, unusual, or none of those? What pattern of results would count for the "real causal relation" view, and what would count for the "just blame-talk" view? And be explicit about what even a clean result would not settle. For example, if causal talk about omissions tracks norm-violation, does that show the concept is normative, or just that people's judgments are noisy? If it stays high even for blameless, completely normal omissions, does that show omissions are causes, or just that causal language is loose enough to soak up absences?
