I am a philosopher preparing a paper for an interdisciplinary historical social science workshop next month. My thesis is that Karl Popper's argument in The Poverty of Historicism succeeds against a strong, teleological historicism — the view that history unfolds through a fixed sequence of law-governed stages — but not against weaker nomological claims: there can, I will argue, be coarse-grained, probabilistic regularities in state formation, institutional persistence, and major demographic transitions that are stable enough to support conditional forecasts, even though the growth of knowledge is unpredictable and shapes the future course of history. The audience will be mostly quantitative historians and historical political economists who rely on exactly such regularities while refusing to call them laws of history, plus one complexity scientist who reads them as emergent macro-dynamics; most know Popper only through the slogan about the unpredictability of knowledge growth. Prepare me for the question period: which of these listeners will raise which objections, in roughly the order they will come, phrased the way they would phrase them; which objections have real force; what I should concede up front and what I should hold; where my replies are soft; and the one question I should most hope nobody asks. Please mark clearly what is settled among specialists and what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading of Popper, or of the debate over historical science, as the consensus.
