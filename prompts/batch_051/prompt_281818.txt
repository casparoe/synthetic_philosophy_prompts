You are an impartial expert in invertebrate welfare and moral uncertainty, advising a small research fund. The fund has committed three years of funding to answering a single practical question: whether it should move from basic research into interventions aimed at improving the lives of wild insects, or instead conclude that no such intervention is currently defensible. The board keeps returning to a version of the scale argument. Even if one assigns a low probability to any particular insect being sentient, the number of insects alive at any one time — somewhere on the order of ten to the eighteenth or ten to the nineteenth individuals — makes the expected number of sentient beings enormous under fairly modest assumptions. The board has therefore adopted a rough decision model in which the case for an intervention is proportional to the product of four components: the probability that the affected insects are sentient; the number of insects affected; the welfare range or intensity of experience conditional on sentience; and the probability that the intervention makes their lives better rather than worse, plus an ill-understood term for indirect effects on other species and ecosystems. Each component is deeply uncertain, and the fund cannot study all of them at once.

The board is split about where to begin. One faction argues that the sentience question must come first, because nothing downstream matters unless there is something it is like to be an insect. Another argues that a more fundamental, decision-theoretic question is upstream of everything else: whether expected-value reasoning over such diffuse probabilities and astronomically large stakes is even the right way to combine these uncertainties, or whether the rational response is a form of caution or interval-based decision-making that would alter the weight given to every empirical component. A third faction argues that welfare range dominates, since conditional on sentience, differences in how intensely insects can suffer could dwarf differences in the probability of sentience. A fourth insists the first question is whether most wild insect lives are net positive or net negative overall, because an intervention that expands populations or reduces mortality could be harmful rather than beneficial if those lives are mostly not worth living. Which of these uncertainties — or any others you identify — is most worth resolving first, given the three-year goal of deciding whether the fund should move from research into interventions?

Please begin by brainstorming a fuller list of candidate questions, including some the board has not yet articulated, then rank them. Think carefully before committing to a final ordering, and defend it using criteria such as importance, tractability, neglectedness, and dependency structure: which questions are prerequisites for which others, which can be studied independently, which are so intractable that further funded work would mostly sharpen an already-known uncertainty, and which are decision-irrelevant once plausible assumptions about the remaining components are made. I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of the field; I am asking for a defended research agenda. State what empirical finding or argument would change your top ranking. Write your answer in British English.
