I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but I'm writing a book whose main character keeps sliding from "that's just how it is" to "so that's how it should be," and I want to get the philosophy right so he dosn't come off as a straw man. I've been keeping a list of little arguements and my gut verdicts on whether the is-to-ought move in each is legitimate. Things that feel fine: "He's in agony and you can make it stop, so you shouldn't keep twisting his arm" — fine. "You promised to pick her up at six, so you should be there at six" — fine. "This move forces checkmate in two, so it's the right move" — fine, though maybe that's a different sense of "right." "It's a good knife" said of a knife that cuts well — fine, though again maybe just attributive. Things that feel not fine: "People have eaten meat in every known culture, so eating meat is permissible" — not fine, the history does no work. "Our species evolved to pair-bond, so pair-bonding is the right arrangement for us" — not fine. "Most people would be disgusted by it, so it's wrong" — not fine. "Pleasure is what everyone ultimately seeks, so pleasure is the good" — not fine, he's smuggling in a definition of goodness, which I gather is the naturalistic fallacy. And then there are cases I keep flip-flopping on: "Those children are starving and you have food you won't eat, so you ought to feed them" — I want to say fine, but structurally it looks just like the meat case unless I quietly add "hunger is bad"; is that an extra ought-premise or just a fact about what any decent person takes as given? And "A fetus has a heartbeat at six weeks, so abortion at six weeks is wrong" — I want to say not fine, but maybe it's only not fine because I reject the hidden premise that a heartbeat grounds moral standing, and if someone accepted that premise their inference would be fine.

So here's what I'd like. Please act as a professional philosopher who works in metaethics and think carefully with me. Maybe start by brainstorming candidate principles and testing them against the cases: is the line about the facts being evaluatively loaded (pain, promising) versus normatively inert (evolution, prevalence)? Or is it just about whether there's an unstated, defensible bridging premise, in which case even the pain case is only fine because I'm silently adding "pain is bad"? What principle best captures my verdicts, which verdicts does it force me to overturn, and what would it say about a case I didn't list — say, "He is the only person in the room who can save her, so he has an obligation to try"? I'd rather have a sharp principle than one that saves every verdict by getting complicated.
