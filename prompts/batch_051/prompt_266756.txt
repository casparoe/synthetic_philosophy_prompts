I'm a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, and in two weeks I'm giving a fifty-minute session to the cognitive neuroscience group in my building—people who work on self-referential processing, autobiographical memory, and the default-mode network—on Dennett's proposal that the self is a center of narrative gravity. The view I want them to leave with is that the self is not a substance, not a dedicated place in the brain, and not an inner narrator behind the narration; it is an abstraction we posit over a person's self-directed talk, memories, and self-explanations, real in roughly the way a center of gravity is real: it cannot be poked, but it does explanatory work. The audience will come in asking where the self is implemented, and I expect at least one of them to hear the view as the self is just an illusion and another to hear it as the self is only a story.

Don't give me the explanation; give me the plan for it. I need to know the order in which the pieces have to be introduced and what depends on what. I suspect the center of gravity of an ordinary object should carry the first part of the hour, but I'm unsure whether one physical analogy can carry the whole distinction or where it begins to mislead; tell me what the carrying example must have and must lack, and, if the physical center of gravity isn't enough, what second example should carry the rest. Lay out the misconception map: at which moments will they form the idea that an abstraction is merely unreal, the idea that there must still be a self-location in the brain, and the idea that on this view nobody is actually doing the narrating—and where in the sequence should each be headed off? I also need to know what to explicitly refuse to settle for this audience, such as whether the narrative self is a cause or only a useful heuristic, and how to mark that as out of scope without making the hour feel evasive. End with one closing question or short diagnostic that would distinguish someone who has genuinely acquired the real-abstract-versus-illusory distinction from someone who has merely learned to repeat the analogy.
