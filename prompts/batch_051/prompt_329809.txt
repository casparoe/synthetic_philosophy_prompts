I'm a PhD student in environmental political theory, and I'm writing a seminar paper on the ethics of solar geoengineering. I want to use a specific episode as my case study: the 2021 cancellation of Harvard's SCoPEx test flight from Esrange Space Center near Kiruna, Sweden. As I understand the facts, the Stratospheric Controlled Perturbation Experiment planned a high-altitude balloon flight that would release a few hundred grams of inert calcium carbonate powder in the stratosphere to test the equipment and dispersal physics, not to alter climate. The launch site, Esrange, is in Sápmi, the Sámi homeland. The Saami Council, Swedish environmental organizations, and other civil-society groups objected, arguing that the test had been planned without meaningful consultation with the Sámi and that even a small outdoor experiment would normalize a technology whose deployment could pose global, unequally distributed, and potentially irreversible risks. Harvard's own advisory committee had recommended postponing until there was broader societal engagement, and the flight was ultimately called off.

I want to analyze this episode through the three central concerns in the geoengineering ethics literature: moral hazard (the worry that pursuing solar geoengineering will weaken mitigation efforts), termination shock (the risk that abruptly stopping deployed solar geoengineering would cause rapid warming), and the question of who decides about a technology with planetary consequences. Treat this as a case study that runs in both directions: what do these three theoretical concerns illuminate about SCoPEx, and what does SCoPEx reveal about the adequacy of those concerns as an ethical framework? Be disciplined about the mapping—who are the relevant actors, what are the options, what counts as evidence—and say explicitly where the theory is silent or misdescribes the case. For example, does the moral hazard worry apply to a test that was not itself deployment? Does the termination shock concern have any real bearing on this decision, or is it a mismatch of timescales? And is the "who decides" question here best understood as a procedural problem of Indigenous consent, a democratic legitimacy problem, or something the existing framework cannot capture?
