I'm a PhD student in the anthropology of religion, and I'm putting together a discussion section for an undergrad course where we read Ursula K. Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" alongside some material on scapegoats and sacrifice. The frame I want to bring is the scapegoat mechanism, roughly in the Girardian line: a community that's full of internal tensions and incipient violence fixes on a single victim, loads that victim with its guilt, and then expels or kills them, which restores peace and gets remembered in myth and ritual as something the victim deserved or the gods required. Now, Omelas looks at first like that exact story with the guilt made explicit: everyone in the city knows that their happiness is purchased by one child kept in a locked room, and most of them come to accept that this is just how it has to be. But the more I sit with it, the less it looks like a scapegoat story in the strict sense. The child isn't killed or driven out, isn't hidden or forgotten, and there's no myth convincing anyone the child is guilty—everyone knows the child is innocent and suffering. So I want to do more than use the story to illustrate the scapegoat idea; I want to use it to stress-test the idea. Could you give me a reading of Omelas through this lens: where the transfer-of-guilt and substitution logic really does illuminate what the story is doing, where the story complicates or refuses that model (the fact that the victim stays alive and visible, the fact that the acceptance is rationalized rather than mythologized, the ones who leave), and what the story might add to the philosophical debate over whether ritual substitution does something real or just gives a community a way to manage its guilt? I'm especially stuck on whether the people who walk away escape the mechanism, or whether they're still defined by it—maybe even the sacrifice's real function, weather they like it or not.
