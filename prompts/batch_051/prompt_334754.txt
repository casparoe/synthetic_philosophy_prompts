A colleague keeps pressing an argument on me that he calls the "bias collapse proof," and I want to know whether it is sound. It runs like this:

(1) A moral disposition deserves to be called a bias only if it gives weight to considerations that ought not to matter, or gives them too much weight.
(2) But every moral disposition gives weight to some considerations and not to others; that weighting is precisely what distinguishes one moral outlook from another.
(3) So where moral outlooks conflict, each side will count some weighting by the other as misplaced: each applies its own "ought."
(4) There is no outlook-independent "ought" from which to adjudicate such conflicts; any adjudication is just the application of some further moral outlook.
(5) If no neutral adjudication is possible, then there is no fact of the matter about which side in a conflict is the biased one. There are only facts about which outlooks count as biased from which standpoints.
(6) Therefore the distinction between a moral preference and a bias collapses. "Bias" is not the name of a defect in someone's moral judgment; it is just what we call a preference we reject.

The conclusion seems to me to prove too much: it would make "bias" a purely polemical word, and it would put a bigoted hunch and a conscientious moral disagreement on exactly the same footing. I would give up a lot before accepting that. But I cannot tell whether the weak point is a premise or a hidden slide between steps. Which premise or step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If you think the argument is valid, say which premise is least costly to surrender and what remains of the distinction afterward. Please keep your answer under 450 words.
