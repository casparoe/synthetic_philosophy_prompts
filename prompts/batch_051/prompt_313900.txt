I keep coming back to the paradox of phenomenal judgment, and it bugs me how much people talk past each other about it. One side says that if epiphenomenalism is true then our judgments that we have feelings can't be caused by those feelings, so the judgments are either a coincidence or have to be explained by some non-phenomenal chain. The other side says the brain just causes the feeling and the judgment in parallel, so there's no mystery. I'd like the full spec instead of another round of that. What would a theory of phenomenal judgment actually have to get right, on the assumption that qualia are causally inert? Give me the desiderata list: the cases it has to handle (make each one earn its place, don't just rattle off weird syndromes), the distinctions it can't collapse (judgment caused by the feeling vs both sharing a cause; reliability vs causal sensitivity; reporting an experience vs merely believing you had one), and the constraints from logic, neuroscience, or evolution it can't brush aside. For each requirement say what forces it. Then tell me which requirement the standard epiphenomenalist replies usually fudge, and which single requirement knocks out the most popular moves. A table is fine, and write in American English.
