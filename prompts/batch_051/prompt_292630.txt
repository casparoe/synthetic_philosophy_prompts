I keep running into the same move in arguments about radical life extension, and I still can't tell what it's actually claiming. Someone will grant that a 500-year lifespan could in principle be healthy and happy, but then say something like "death gives life its shape" or "a life without an ending isn't really a life," and treat the conversation as settled. A friend of mine dismisses this whole cluster as "deathism"—just status quo bias dressed up as wisdom—but I don't want to wave it off that quickly. My problem is that I can't even pin down the proposition well enough to argue with it.

Can you lay out the different precise theses that slogan could be expressing? At minimum I can see a psychological reading (a deadline is what makes us actually do things), an aesthetic/narrative reading (a life needs an ending to have the unity or shape of a story), a value reading (meaning requires finitude or scarcity), and maybe a natural-kind reading (a being that lived 500 years would no longer be human in the relevant sense). For each reading, I want to know whether it's plausible, whether its an empirical or a conceptual claim, and, crucially, whether it would actually support refusing or opposing life extension rather than just making a preference for ordinary lifespans understandable. I'm also interested in readings I've missed, especially any that would support the stronger conclusion that a person who would extend their life is making a mistake, not just choosing differently. And I'd like a verdict on which readings are merely explaining the psychology of liking finite life and which, if any, do real normative work.
