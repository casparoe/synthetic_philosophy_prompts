After a career in the lab I've been making my way through the Stoics, and I hit a passage in Seneca's *Moral Letters to Lucilius*, Letter 9, that's been gnawing at me. He writes:

"In this sense the wise man is self-sufficient, that he can do without friends, not that he desires to do without them. When I say 'can,' I mean this: he endures the loss of a friend with equanimity."

I've read enough around it to see the appeal of the larger view: the idea is that freedom from disturbance, including grief, is something you can train yourself into, and that relying on other people for your emotional stability is a kind of vulnerability. As a scientist I'm sympathetic to that sort of self-regulation; it looks like homeostasis extended to the emotions. But I also have a gut-level resistance. I've watched people I care about grieve badly, and their grief didn't feel like a malfunction; it felt like part of what it means to love someone. I'm not sure wether a person who could lose a friend "with equanimity" has a deeper kind of strength or has just flattened something important.

So the clash is this: the Stoic theory seems to have systematic virtues—it gives a clear training program, it's not hostage to fortune, and it explains a lot about why we suffer. But it contradicts the very firm intuition that deep attachments should make you vulnerable, and that grief at a real loss is appropriate rather than something to be eliminated. I want to know which should yield, and why. Should I treat my intuition as untutored and something to be trained away? Should the theory be amended so that equanimity doesn't mean the absence of grief but something else? Or is there a reading of Seneca's claim that makes it compatible with ordinary human grief? I'm less interested in someone just picking a side; I'd like to hear what would justify each move.
