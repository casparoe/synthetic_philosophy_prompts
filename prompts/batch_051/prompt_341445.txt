I've been trying to think through a version of the "who owns data about you" question, and I keep getting tangled up in the Lockean labor-mixing story from the Second Treatise, chapter V. Roughly, Locke's idea is that property arises when a person mixes labor with something unowned: the acorn becomes yours because you picked it up, the ground becomes yours because you tilled it. But a data profile doesn't look like an unowned commons in that sense. A platform points sensors and logs at your movements, your browsing, your purchases, and builds the profile; if anything, the platform's labor did the mixing, not yours. I want to know whether the Lockean argument survives that shift from acorns and farmland to passively generated personal data, or whether it's just a dead metaphor there. If it survives, which premise is actually doing the work once the resource is not a preexisting unowned thing but a representation of you made by someone elses instruments? If it doesn't survive, is there a repaired version that still gets you to a property right in data about you, without falling back on privacy or dignity or selfhood as separate justifiers? I'm not looking for citations or a survey of who said what lately, just the strongest version of the argument.
