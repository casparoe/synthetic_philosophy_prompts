I write for the science and ideas section of a magazine, and at least twice a month I'm covering moral psychology studies or popular books that claim to have found that some moral judgement is "really" a bias — status quo bias, omission bias, one side's partisan bias in a live argument, that sort of thing. I keep going back and forth on wether I should report the bias claim as a finding, present it as a contested moral view, or push back on the label altogether. What I've picked up from philosophy is that the distinction between a bias and a legitimate preference or value isn't itself just empirical, but I don't know how to turn that into something I can use when I'm filing in a hurry.

Could you write me a decision procedure I can run in about half an hour? It should have the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the matter is one of values rather than facts. Ground it in what the philosophy actually has to say about telling biases and preferences apart in morality, without smuggling in any particular moral outlook. Then run the procedure on two or three cases: a study saying omission bias distorts people's judgements about the side effects of a vaccination programme, a study saying the other side's moral outrage in some political debate is just motivated reasoning, and ideally one where your procedure gives me an answer I won't like.

And please write it in British English, continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you'd write an email or a letter.
