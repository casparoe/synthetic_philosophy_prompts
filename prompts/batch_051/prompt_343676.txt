I have spent my working life inside the institutions that publish, teach, and honor literary fiction, and I have been unable to make their underlying logic cohere. A small press will keep a slow-selling, formally difficult novel in print for twenty years at a loss. A prize jury will give its highest award to a book that very few people will ever read, and that even its admirers do not call pleasurable or useful. A writing program will reward refinement of style in a student's manuscript without asking whether the work will find an audience. In all these settings the word "excellent" functions as a conversation-stopper: the novel is very good, and that by itself is taken to be a reason to publish it, honor it, keep it alive.

Yet the same people, when they defend these institutions to funders or the public, reach for a different kind of reason: books enlarge compassion, sustain communities, provide models for living, enrich the culture downstream. Those are claims about benefits that eventually reach someone. I cannot tell whether the first answer is the sincere presupposition and the second merely fundraising rhetoric, or the reverse.

I would like a reconstruction of the theory of value this practice actually presupposes. Does it strictly presuppose that excellence is valuable in itself, apart from any benefit to readers, to the author, or to the future of the art form? Or is the practice compatible with several different theories — for instance, that difficult work holds option value or long-run value we cannot yet specify, that an excellent unread novel still constitutes a benefit in the form of the author's achievement, or that a literary culture needs a reserve of uncompromising work for reasons that are real but indirect? Do the separate elements — the backlist kept alive, the prize awarded, the pedagogy of refinement for its own sake — presuppose the same thing, or do they quietly rely on different theories? And where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, in the sense that more than one theory would license exactly what these institutions do? Finally, if the whole practice were rebuilt on the view that excellence matters only insofar as it benefits someone, what precisely would have to change in how presses, juries, and writing programs behave?
