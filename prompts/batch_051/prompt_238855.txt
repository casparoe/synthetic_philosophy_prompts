I have an epistemic rule about culture: the larger the promotional apparatus behind a film, album, or novel, the lower a prior I assign to its aesthetic value, and beyond a certain point I treat the marketing campaign itself as evidence that the work is standardized and aesthetically shallow. I arrived at this after taking a version of Adorno and Horkheimer's culture industry thesis seriously: mass-distributed culture tends toward sameness, surface novelty is manufactured to disguise that sameness, and the publicity machinery is part of that manufacturing rather than a neutral signal of quality. So when something arrives with an expensive campaign, I usually do not spend the hours it would take to check whether this one is an exception. A friend says this is bias masquerading as policy: a large promotional budget may simply mean the distributors recognized genuine excellence, and my rule makes the thesis unfalsifiable in my own case, since I never test it against the heavily promoted works that might be good. Is my rule rationally defensible as a filtering policy? Under what assumptions about how culture is produced and promoted would marketing spend genuinely be diagnostic of mediocrity, and under what assumptions is my friend right? Is there a revised version of the rule that keeps its diagnostic value without condemning every heavily promoted work?
