In my course on the ethics of long-term futures, we have been discussing value drift: the worry that our descendants might come to hold values that are not merely different but morally worse than ours. That discussion has left me puzzled about some very ordinary practices. Constitutions entrench certain rights beyond the reach of ordinary majorities, and universities and charities accept endowments whose purposes are fixed by donors in perpetuity, or at least for very long periods. Both practices deliberately bind later generations to values and priorities chosen earlier, even when the later generation would otherwise revise them. What view about the trajectory and reliability of human values does this practice of binding the future presuppose? Does it strictly presuppose that future values are more likely to decay than to improve unless they are checked, or is it compatible with expecting continued moral progress? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say what would have to change in these institutions if we instead assumed that future people's values will generally be an improvement on our own.
