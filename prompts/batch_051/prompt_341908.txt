I was the third person on a long drive last month, and the other two spent the last hour re-litigating a point from the previous week's reading group. The point was Abelard's thesis that sin is located in consent to an act rather than in the act itself: once a person has consented to what he knows to be wrong, the outward deed adds nothing to his guilt, and a deed that is prevented through no merit of his own leaves him no less guilty than one that succeeds. They pushed the thesis into territory the reading never touched, and I have since been asked to referee. The exchange, stripped down:

Lucía: Take two people with identical histories up to the decisive moment. Each decides to burn down a neighbour's house for the insurance, buys petrol, waits until two in the morning, soaks the door, and strikes a match. One match is damp. One fire starts and the other never does. If you judge the successful arsonist to be a worse person than the failed one, you are letting a cloudburst decide moral worth. What made each guilty was already complete before the match was struck, so the failed attempt is not a lesser sin but the same sin with less smoke.

Tom: I'll grant you the pair is equally blameworthy. What I resist is the reason you give, that the deed adds nothing. The deed is not just an outward symptom of an inner state. A completed arson is a different wrong in the world than a singed door, and moral judgment is not only a verdict on someone's inner ledger; it tracks what was done and to whom. If the act contributes nothing to guilt, you are forced to say that a person who consents to a murder in his head and never lifts a finger is just as guilty as the killer. That indifference to the deed is what I cannot accept.

Lucía: And I do accept it, provided the consent is real. But don't overstate the point. Of course the completed act is worse in its consequences and rightly draws a different social response — restitution, containment, the victim's claims. The question is what the agent deserves, and there the attempt and the deed stand together. A man whose match failed through no merit of his own is not a better man; he is only a luckier one.

Tom: Then your view has a neglect problem. A driver who kills a pedestrian while texting never consented to that death; he never said so be it to any particular person. If sin requires consent to a known wrong, the negligent killer is nearly innocent, and that is false. If instead you say he consented to driving while texting, then you have stretched consent to cover ordinary recklessness, and the word is now tracking whatever behaviour you already disapprove of.

Lucía: No, the distinction is between the act he chooses and the result he does not. The texting driver chooses to impose a lethal risk on strangers, and if he sees that risk, he consents to that wrong: reckless endangerment, not homicide. The principle does not make him nearly innocent; it says his sin is the endangerment, and that is a different sin in kind from murder. This is exactly right on the parallel case: two drivers with identical recklessness, one of whom happens to kill, are not made morally different people by whether a pedestrian stepped into the road.

Tom: Then test the boundary of consent on the inner side. A man spends each evening nursing detailed fantasies of killing a colleague — savouring the planning, the imagined fear, the alibis — and always stops short of deciding to do it. Has he sinned? If you say no because he withholds consent, then your principle treats someone who has cultivated murderous delight for years as innocent, and it has made the moral life absurdly easy. If you say yes, then sin cannot consist only in consent to the outward deed; consenting to dwell in the fantasy must already count. But then the clean final-yes criterion you advertised is gone.

Lucía: The second horn does not trouble me. To return to the fantasy deliberately, evening after evening, is not the same as being visited by a thought. Each return is an act of indulgence to which he consents, and the sin is consenting to nourish malice, not the mere occurrence of the fantasy. The principle survives by distinguishing temptation, which is not consented to, from indulgence, which is. That is why the principle is strict where it should be and merciful where it should be too.

Tom: But now consent has become an accordion. It covers the final yes to arson, the implicit yes in texting while driving, and the recurring yes of fantasy. Each yes earns the same word, but they are morally nothing alike. The reason the theory keeps extending the word is that the deed and its structure matter more than it admits: some wrongs are constituted by what actually happens, some by patterns of conduct, and not all of them reduce to a moment or a practice of consent. If every counterexample gets absorbed by a refined notion of consent, I no longer know what the thesis rules out.

They asked me to referee, and I could see each move partly undoing the previous one, but I could not articulate the underlying issues. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each move, say what is right in it, what is mistaken or overstated, and how much force it retains after the reply that follows. Then identify the distinctions doing the real work — for example, culpability versus harm versus punishability; temptation versus desire versus consent; attempted versus completed wrongdoing; resultant luck versus the agent's settled character — and end by saying whether Abelard's principle, as Lucía defends it, survives the exchange, and if it does not, which modification would preserve its core.
