I am trying to work out what, if anything, I actually believe about how much rule-following a society or community may demand. Leave aside the descriptive question of which cultures are tight or loose; I want the normative principle, if I have one. Below are my verdicts on cases, reported as honestly as I can. I would like you to tell me which principle or small set of candidate principles captures them, which of my verdicts it forces me to revise, and what it predicts about cases I have not listed — including, in particular, the last case, which I am genuinely unsure about.

1. A pedestrian crosses against a red signal at 3 a.m. on an empty street after looking both ways. I think this is fine, and a city that routinely fines such conduct is demanding too much rule-following.

2. A driver comes to a complete stop at a red light at a deserted rural intersection late at night, checks that no vehicle or pedestrian is approaching, and proceeds. I also think this is fine, though slightly less clearly than case 1, because the cost of being wrong is higher.

3. Speeding: driving 10 km/h over the limit on a dry, empty motorway at midday seems fine to me. Driving even 3 km/h over the limit in a school zone during pick-up time seems not fine, and I would support strict enforcement there. So what matters to me appears not to be the size of the deviation but what the rule protects.

4. A neighbor uses a noisy power tool at 7:50 a.m. where quiet hours begin at 8:00. I find this mildly irritating but not wrong, and would not complain. The same tool at 2:00 a.m. strikes me as genuinely wrong. Both violate the same rule, so I evidently do not treat the rule's boundary as sacred.

5. In an airport security queue, traveler A quietly takes a place three spots ahead because A's flight is already boarding. I think this is not fine if done without a word, but becomes acceptable if A turns to those ahead and they agree. Traveler B does the same thing while falsely claiming to be late. I think B's act is worse than A's, even though the effect on the queue is identical.

6. A dinner host has a clear no-shoes rule inside. A guest keeps shoes on because of a minor foot condition they never mention. Nobody notices. I think the guest is in the wrong, even though the harm is invisible and the rule exists only to protect the host's preference.

7. A wedding invitation states a dress code, and a guest arrives conspicuously underdressed because they value comfort and dislike formality. I think the guest has done wrong and that a cool response from the hosts and other guests is warranted; fining the guest would be excessive.

8. A client pays a tradesperson partly in cash so the tradesperson can underreport income on a small job. The tax lost is trivial. I think this is not fine, and I would not do it.

9. In a small village, hanging laundry outdoors on a Sunday is treated as a serious breach, and those who do it are cold-shouldered. I think this community expects too much, not just something different. But I find it hard to say why this is different from case 7, where I was willing to let a specific community enforce its aesthetic and expressive norms.

10. A suburban street has an informal rule that lawns are mowed every two weeks during summer for the sake of the street's appearance. One resident deliberately keeps a wilder, less tidy garden. I go back and forth: sometimes I think the neighbors are being petty and should leave the resident alone; sometimes I think the resident is free-riding on a shared local order and should mow.

Is there a single principle that captures these verdicts, or close to it? In particular, I am drawn to a distinction between three kinds of rules: those whose point is to prevent harm to others, those that solve coordination problems, and those whose point is to express respect for a shared local order. Test that distinction against the cases, and state the resulting principle as precisely as you can. If the principle cannot accommodate some of my verdicts, say so explicitly: which verdict would it overturn, and what would a more complex principle that preserves more of them look like? Finally, apply the principle to case 10 and to one further case of your own choosing that helps reveal what the principle really treats as rigor versus laxity.
