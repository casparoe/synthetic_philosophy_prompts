I've just finished a long argument with a friend about a claim in epistemic game theory, and I want you to build me the cleanest possible case to settle it. My friend kept insisting that if it is common knowledge that two players are rational and that they are playing a particular game, then the outcome must be a Nash equilibrium. I think that is too strong. Common knowledge of rationality gets us rationalizability, not Nash equilibrium: Nash equilibrium needs the extra ingredient of correct beliefs about what the other player will actually do, and common knowledge of rationality does not by itself supply that. The difference is between constraining what it is rational to believe and requiring those beliefs to be true.

You are an expert in epistemic game theory and the philosophy of game theory. Before drafting anything, take some time to brainstorm the distinctions among individual rationality, common knowledge of rationality, rationalizable strategies, Nash equilibrium, and accurate (or correct) conjectures. Also think carefully about which action profiles in a coordination game with conflicting preferences are Nash equilibria and which are merely rationalizable. Then work through the specifications below.

Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications.

The scenario should involve two professionals in a realistic workplace or hospital who must each choose, without any further communication, between two incompatible standards or systems. The payoffs should be the familiar coordination-with-conflicting-preferences structure: each player receives 2 from matching on their own preferred standard, 1 from matching on the other player's preferred standard, and 0 from a mismatch. Both players are individually rational, and it is common knowledge that both are rational and that this payoff structure obtains. There must be no mistakes, no misperceived payoffs, no hidden changes to the game, and no irrationality; the case must not work by making anyone ignorant or confused about the game.

The case must deliver the following result: the two players choose different standards, so that the realized action profile is not a Nash equilibrium, even though it remains common knowledge that each is rational. The engine of the result should be a pair of asymmetric, mutually inconsistent first-order beliefs. Let one player prefer standard A and the other prefer standard B. The first player sincerely believes the second will choose A, and therefore chooses A; the second sincerely believes the first will choose B, and therefore chooses B. Each belief is mistaken, but each is compatible with the other player's rationality: the action that the first player believes the second will take is itself rationalizable for the second player, and likewise in reverse. Do not merely stipulate the mismatch; derive it from these beliefs together with best-response reasoning.

Write the scenario in one paragraph.

After presenting the scenario, give me a point-by-point demonstration:

Then verify that common knowledge of rationality can be satisfied: show that both actions are rationalizable for each player, that each player's first-order belief is concentrated on an action that is rational for the other player under some rational belief, and explain briefly how the higher-order beliefs can be filled in consistently without the two players' first-order beliefs having to agree.

2. Verify that the realized profile is not a Nash equilibrium, and spell out precisely why: for at least one player, there is a profitable unilateral deviation once the other player's actual choice is held fixed.

3. Identify the feature of the case that is doing the work — presumably the gap between rational belief and correct belief. Then show what happens when that feature is removed. In your case, if each player knew the other player's actual strategy, explain why the realized profile would have to be a Nash equilibrium (for this matrix, one of the two pure matching profiles), and why the mismatched profile could not arise under correct beliefs.

4. Present the strongest version of an objection from someone siding with my friend, who thinks common knowledge of rationality should by itself force a Nash equilibrium. Such an objection will likely claim that the belief profile is too convenient, or that genuine common knowledge of rationality would force the players to predict each other's choices correctly. Answer the objection precisely, and in doing so identify the step at which the objection already assumes the very correctness of beliefs that Nash equilibrium demands rather than deriving it from common knowledge of rationality.

5. Close by stating exactly what the case does and does not establish about the relationship between common knowledge of rationality, rationalizability, and Nash equilibrium. Be scrupulous not to overclaim.
