In my craft as a novelist, I keep relying on a test that I now want to state as a philosophical proposal and have assessed honestly.

The test concerns why some emotions in fiction feel earned and others feel manipulated. The familiar craft rule is that an emotion is earned only if the reason printed on the page for it is a feature of the thing the emotion is about: admiration earned by the admired person's conduct, grief earned by the loss itself, belief earned by evidence. I want to turn this into something stronger.

I call it the staging test. A consideration is a reason of the right kind for an attitude when it can be staged — written as a visible, in-scene cause of the attitude without the scene collapsing into satire, horror, or the depiction of the character as self-deceived or merely rationalizing. A consideration fails the test when its honest dramatization cannot stay in one of the ordinary serious modes; the moment the reason is put on the page, the scene slides into comedy or menace. Take a character who admires a patron because he has promised to pay her debts, and who says so openly, while everything visible about the patron is petty and cruel. I can write that scene, but I cannot write it as a scene of earned admiration; it comes out as satire, or as a portrait of self-deception, or as something sinister. Yet the money is a genuine reason for her to bring herself to admire him — it strongly recommends being in that state — and the test, I think, picks out exactly that difference. The same holds for belief: a reason of the form believing this would save or comfort me cannot be staged as the visible discovery of a truth; staged honestly, it shows a person managing her inner states, not a person encountering the world.

My proposal is that this is not just a convention of realist prose. A wrong-kind reason cannot be staged because a scene can only show an agent responding to an object — the person, the proposition, the state of affairs before her — whereas a wrong-kind reason is a reason concerning her response itself, and that response is not yet part of the world to be shown. Staging forces the reason to confront the object it claims to answer to, and there the mismatch becomes audible.

I suspect this is the same boundary the wrong kind of reasons problem has been hunting for, transposed into craft terms. But I am a novelist rather than a philosopher, and I would like a candid verdict: is the staging test a real criterion, or merely a tic of my medium dressed up as one? Does it already exist under a name I have not read? And where does it break — which attitudes or reasons does it classify wrongly, and does any failure amount to a fatal objection rather than a local embarrassment?
