I'm on the board of a small radio-astronomy nonprofit, and next month we have to decide wether to co-sign a proposal from a partner observatory to send a high-power beacon at a star system about 40 light-years away: a one-off three-hour transmission with a math/physics primer and a short greeting. The proposal is framed as outreach and science, it has an engineering review behind it, and it would be the first deliberate beacon this observatory has sent above some power threshold; they want our institutional endorsement because it helps them with their own funding.

A bloc on our board wants us to refuse and adopt a written policy against any future beacons. Their argument is basically the dark forest hypothesis: any civilization has reason to stay quiet because detection is risky, the apparent silence of the galaxy might be exactly because intelligent life has figured that out, and a transmission can't be called back, so the expected-value calculation is dominated by a tail risk you can't put an upper bound on. The other side says this is sci-fi paranoia dressed up as decision theory, that the actual signal is weak and more or less isotropic over interstellar distances, and that a policy of total silence would kill the kind of research that could tell us whether the premise is even true.

We're not being asked to settle the astrophysics; we're deciding whether our institution should endorse and attach its name to this transmission, refuse on precautionary grounds, or endorse the science while declining to endorse the beacon. I need a short memo from you recommending what the board should vote for, and it has to treat the strongest version of the dark-forest objection fairly rather than strawmanning it. Also, since our board is split and people keep citing the consensus, please mark clearly which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who study it, and dont present a contested view as the agreed position.

One more wrinkle: the target planet already has published biosignature detections but no technosignatures. Two board members think that makes the case for silence stronger, because a biosphere is a future threat that could industrialize and surpass us; two others think it makes silence weaker, because if life is common and technology is rare then the dangerous bottleneck is behind such a planet, not ahead of it. The memo should break that tie, or at least tell us how much weight to put on it.
