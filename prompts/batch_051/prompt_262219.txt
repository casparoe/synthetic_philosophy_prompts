I sit on the governance committee for a university-affiliated project that builds "continuing bonds" technologies: interactive simulations of deceased people, reconstructed from text messages, voice notes, social media, and similar digital remains, offered mainly to the bereaved. Our responsible-use policy contains two provisions that we had always assumed worked together, but a case now before us seems to drive them apart. The first provision states: "Simulations of a deceased person created from that person's digital remains may be provided only when the deceased, while alive and competent, gave explicit and specific consent for the simulation, including which materials could be used, who could receive the resulting interaction, and how long it could continue." The second provision, from a later section of the same policy, states: "No bereavement service offered under this policy shall be withheld from a person for whom it is clinically indicated, where withholding would foreseeably prolong severe grief, merely because the deceased left no instructions governing posthumous digital use."

The case before us is this. A graduate student in our program died suddenly in an accident. She left no advance directive, and by all available evidence had never considered the possibility of being simulated. Her parents and younger sister have asked for a three-month arrangement in which they would receive short weekly messages written in her style, reconstructed from the many voice messages and texts she exchanged with them over the past decade. Our clinicians judge that, for these family members, the service is likely to materially reduce acute distress during the hardest part of the first year of bereavement. The first clause appears to forbid the service outright, since the deceased never consented while alive. The second clause appears to require, or at least strongly favor, providing it, since withholding it would foreseeably prolong severe grief and would be grounded entirely in the absence of prior instructions.

I would like you to work out what our committee ought to make of the conflict. Please lay out the strongest readings under which both clauses can govern this case without contradiction, making explicit what each clause's function might be: for example, which clause states a standing prohibition and which states a default; whether one governs the design of the system while the other governs individual access; how the terms "consent," "service," and "withheld" should be understood on each reading; and whether the clauses could operate at different stages of the decision. Then assess whether any of those readings is one the drafters could honestly have intended, rather than a piece of special pleading invented after the fact for this family. If the clauses cannot be honestly reconciled, say which one should yield in this case, and on what principle. I am less interested in a survey of existing positions than in seeing you build the strongest version of each side and then commit to a structured judgment.
