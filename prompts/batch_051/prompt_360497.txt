I'm putting together a handout for an advanced-undergraduate epistemology seminar, and I'd like a constructed example that exposes a specific feature of Jeffrey conditionalization that the usual textbook cases glide over. By Jeffrey conditionalization I mean the rule for updating on evidence that is uncertain: if an agent's evidence shifts her credences over a partition of an evidence variable without making any cell certain, she updates a target hypothesis H by taking the weighted average of her old conditional credences P(H | each cell), with her new credences in the cells as weights, while leaving those conditional credences themselves unchanged through the update (rigidity). The point I want the example to make is that the order in which two pieces of uncertain evidence are processed can change the final posterior for H, even when the two pieces of evidence arrive all at once, so that there is no natural temporal order of receipt to appeal to.

Please build the case to the following specifications. First, keep it ordinary and realistic—a physician reading two imperfect lab results that arrive together, an auditor seeing two noisy indicators in the same file, or something similar; no demons, no perfect predictors, no exotic technology, and the agent should be a rational person with a well-defined prior. Second, give the agent a prior over a substantive target hypothesis H and two binary evidence partitions, A and B, using simple numbers (fractions or two-decimal probabilities) so a student can do the arithmetic by hand; H should not simply be identical with either partition. Third, specify two pieces of uncertain evidence, one fixing new credences over A and one fixing new credences over B, neither of which makes any cell certain. Fourth, run the Jeffrey update in both orders and show that the posterior for H differs, with the numbers written out clearly enough that the difference is visible at a glance. Fifth, identify the structural feature of the case that produces the order-dependence—say, in terms of how A and B are related in the prior or how they bear on H—and confirm that removing that feature makes the two orders collapse to the same answer.

In your answer, please separate the settled mathematical facts from the genuinely contested interpretive issues. It is settled, for example, that Jeffrey updates on different partitions can fail to commute; it is contested whether this order-dependence is a defect of the rule, whether a rational agent facing simultaneous uncertain evidence should instead perform a single update over a common refinement of the two partitions, and whether the two reports should count as one body of evidence or as two separate epistemic events. Do not present a contested verdict as the consensus: after the worked example, tell me how a defender of Jeffrey conditionalization would resist treating the case as a counterexample, and say where you think that resistance is weakest.
