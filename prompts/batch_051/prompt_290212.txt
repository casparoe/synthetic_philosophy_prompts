I'm a TA for a course on self-knowledge and I've been trying to get students to take William James's warning seriously before we look at novels about people thinking through there pasts. Early in The Principles of Psychology (1890), in the chapter on the methods and snares of psychology, there's a passage where James says introspective observation is necessarily retrospective: you can't catch a thought in the act without turning it into a different thing. He says, roughly, that trying to observe your mental states as they happen is like grabbing a spinning top to catch its motion, or turning up a gas lamp quickly enough to see what the darkness looked like. I keep wanting to read that against Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day, because Stevens's whole narration is a butlers retrospective introspection on a motor trip through the countryside. The novel seems to be exactly the kind of after-the-fact self-examination James says is the only introspection we get, and at the same time it's obviously shaped by the fact that Stevens is crafting his account for an audience, maybe for himself, with all the formal restraint of his profession. I'd like a reading that takes both seriously: where does the novel actually dramatize James's point about the retrospective nature of introspective knowledge, and where does it complicate that point in ways James doesn't anticipate? Is the problem in the novel just that introspection arrives too late, or is it also that the introspector has motives for misdescribing the original feeling? And does the novel end up undermining the very idea that introspection can be a method of self-knowledge, or does it show something more subtle about how a self gets constructed in the act of looking back? Please point to specific scenes and stylistic choices, and don't be afraid to say where the James lens distorts the novel or where the novel pushes back.
