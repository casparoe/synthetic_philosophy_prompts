My opening move: You hold that the sense of a proper name is a way in which its referent is given to a speaker, and that the same name may be attached to different senses by different speakers, or by the same speaker at different times. You also hold that a declarative sentence expresses a thought composed of the senses of its parts, and that the thought is what we grasp when we understand the sentence. But consider a man who has never heard of any of your ideas. He has picked up the name "Aristotle" in everyday conversation and uses it confidently, though if you asked him to state the sense he attached to the name, he could only say something like "some famous ancient Greek, I'm not sure exactly who." Does his sentence "Aristotle was wise" express a thought in your sense? If it does, and if his sense is so thin that it might pick out any one of several historical Greeks, what determines that he is speaking about the same man you refer to when you say "Aristotle"? If it does not, then how is he not simply making noise while thinking he is saying something meaningful?
