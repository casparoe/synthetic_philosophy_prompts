I've been trying to get my views on prediction and epistemic modesty straight before I start writing something for a small forecasting group, and I'd like a consistency check on where I've landed. Here's the package:

1. For one-off projects like startups, big infrastructure, software rewrites, the reference-class base rate should dominate your forecast; the inside view—all the reasoning about the specific team, plan, and circumstances—mostly just generates overconfidence.
2. But for my own decisions, like whether to take a job at an early startup or stay in my current role, I think my inside view genuinely should override the startup failure base rate. I know details about the founders, runway, and the market that a generic reference class doesn't contain.
3. I think hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy mean that after-the-fact "why it failed" stories are mostly epistemically worthless, even when smart people tell them.
4. But I also think some retrospective post-mortems are useful when the people involved wrote down their expectations and reasoning before the outcome, in contemporaneous notes, instead of reconstructing it afterward.
5. I hold a general policy of epistemic modesty: when a good aggregate outside forecast exists (a prediction market, a crowd forecast), it should usually beat my own judgement and I should defer to it.
6. And yet I also think there are genuinely low-information one-off decisions where there is no decent reference class and no meaningful outside forecast, and in those cases trying to stay modest just becomes an excuse not to act; you have to go with a detailed inside-view plan and commit.

Does this hang together? I'm not sure where the fault line is, and I don't want a verdict that treats every pair as equally bad. I'd especially like to know whether (2) is in tension with (1) and (5), whether (3) and (4) are actually in conflict or just look like one, and what single revision would clean up the most while costing me the least.
