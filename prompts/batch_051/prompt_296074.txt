Ernest Renan’s 1882 lecture What is a Nation? argues that a nation rests neither on race, language, religion, nor geography, and that collective forgetting — even collective historical error — is indispensable to its cohesion. He also maintains that the nation’s existence depends on a continuing present-day will to live together, rather than on any fact about the distant past.

Write a dialogue between a defender of this Renanian position and a defender of the more thoroughly constructivist view associated with Eric Hobsbawm and Benedict Anderson, on which nations are largely modern constructions sustained by recently invented traditions and acts of collective imagination. Their dispute should turn on a precise question: if Renan already grants that national memory is built on forgetting and error, does the Hobsbawm–Anderson view add anything beyond Renan’s own concessions, or does it simply draw out their consequences? Each character should be equally competent, engage with the other’s actual arguments, and avoid setting up weak versions of the opposing view.
