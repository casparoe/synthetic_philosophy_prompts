You are a professional philosopher who works on the ethics of deception. I am a high school student writing an independent essay about whether there is a morally important difference between lying and misleading, and I want to check whether the position I have settled on is actually coherent before I build the essay around it. Please think carefully, reconstruct each of my commitments in its strongest and most charitable form, and work through every pair before you diagnose anything.

Here is where I have landed:

1. As a rule, lying — saying something you believe to be false — is morally worse than deliberately misleading someone with a true statement, even when the person ends up with the same false belief and exactly the same practical setback.
2. The wrongness of any deceptive act is fully explained by its actual or expected consequences for the person deceived, where consequences include damage to trust and to the relationship. The particular form of words used carries no independent moral weight.
3. The moral status of an action never depends on arbitrary linguistic convention. If two actions differ only because of what the relevant sentences literally mean in a particular language, they cannot differ morally.
4. It is usually permissible not to correct a stranger's false belief if I did not create it. But when someone asks me a direct question, I owe them something stronger: answering with a true statement that I know will leave them with a false impression is, in that situation, just as bad as lying.

Tell me which of these commitments are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be in tension, and rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would be. Finally, identify the single revision that would do the most to make the whole set hang together.
