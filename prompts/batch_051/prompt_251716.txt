Since retiring from a career in physical chemistry, I have been reading philosophy of mathematics, and I have grown particularly interested in what separates genuine understanding of a proof from mere procedural fluency. A colleague who teaches an upper-level course in real analysis has, over several semesters, told me how she classifies her students on exactly this question. Her track record, as nearly as I can reconstruct it, is as follows.

A student reproduced the standard parity proof that the square root of 2 is irrational without error. When asked why the proof insists on writing the fraction in lowest terms, the student could say only that that is how the proof goes. Her verdict: the student did not understand the proof.

A student could not reproduce the proof of Rolle's theorem in full, but correctly explained that continuity on a closed interval guarantees that the function attains a maximum and a minimum, that an interior extremum has derivative zero, and that if both extrema occur at endpoints the function must be constant because the endpoint values are equal. Her verdict: the student understood the proof.

A student could check every line of a proof that a continuous function on a closed, bounded interval attains its maximum, and was persuaded that the proof is valid, but could not say why the argument would not go through for a continuous function on the open interval (0,1), and a week later could not reconstruct the argument. Her verdict: the student did not understand the proof.

A student, given the hint to apply Rolle's theorem to a tilted function, reconstructed the proof of the Mean Value Theorem and explained that the tilt is chosen so that the endpoints of the tilted function have equal values, though she made small algebraic slips. Her verdict: the student understood the proof.

A student produced a correct proof that the square root of 2 is irrational using uniqueness of prime factorization rather than the parity argument in the textbook, but had difficulty following the textbook's parity proof when shown it. Her verdict: the student understood the proof.

She is now evaluating a student who has just given a polished presentation of the proof that every convergent sequence of real numbers is bounded. The student correctly explained why one chooses epsilon equal to one, how the triangle inequality yields a bound on the tail of the sequence, and why the finitely many terms before the cutoff must be handled separately. But when asked to prove the closely analogous statement that every Cauchy sequence of real numbers is bounded, the student could not see how to begin, and in particular did not think to choose a convenient epsilon or to split the sequence into a finite initial segment and a bounded tail.

I would like your best prediction of how she will classify this student, but I am more interested in the reasoning than in the bare prediction. Please lay out the candidate rules about understanding that fit her first five verdicts; say which of them genuinely diverge on this new case and what each predicts; give your best guess about which rule she is implicitly running, with your reasons; and describe the further case that would most cleanly distinguish the live options.
