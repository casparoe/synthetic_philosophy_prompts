In a reading group on the nature of language, one participant has been defending the claim that English has a real rule for past-tense formation by leaning on an analogy: a grammar is like the rules of a game. On this view, go/went is no more a counterexample to a past-tense rule than castling is a counterexample to the rules of chess; it is simply a special rule within the system. Audit this analogy. First give the mapping explicitly: what is supposed to correspond to a rule, a legal move, a player, learning the game, a violation, and the codified rulebook. Then identify where the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from those that do not. In doing so, weigh the fact that the rules of a game are stipulated, public, and largely stable, whereas grammatical regularities are abstracted from usage, change over time through sound change and analogy, and include suppletive forms that are the residue of historical processes rather than explicit stipulations. Consider whether the analogy fares better for productive syntax and default morphology than for suppletion and the lexicon. What, if anything, can the analogy actually establish about whether exceptions undermine the claim that a language is rule-governed, and is there a better analogy?
