I have been doing tenant-organizing work against institutional landlords, and I keep returning to Albert Einstein’s essay Why Socialism? in Monthly Review. The passage I have in mind is the one in which he argues that under capitalism, concentrated private capital tends to control the main sources of public information and thereby dominates the democratic political process, and that only a socialist economy can secure the free development of the individual. I find the argument powerfully plausible, but I want to stress-test it rather than merely endorse it.

Please act as a political philosopher who knows Einstein’s social thought and the later debate over structural theories of capital and democracy. Think carefully and work through the position before answering.

Treat Einstein’s view as a single position with two linked claims: (1) that in a capitalist economy, concentrated private capital tends to dominate the political process and the formation of public opinion, and (2) that this domination is an inherent feature of capitalism rather than a correctable policy failure, so that only replacing capitalist ownership can remove it. What would have to be true for this position to be wrong? Which empirical developments, institutional reforms, or counterexamples would count decisively against each claim, and which would merely be awkward? What would Einstein’s own defenders have to concede as evidence against the view, and what escape routes—such as saying that the observed cases are exceptions or that the tendency is latent—are live rather than merely logically possible? Finally, give an honest assessment of how well insulated the position is, and whether that insulation is a strength or a problem.

End by saying how confident you are in the overall assessment and singling out the point you are least sure of.
