Ok so I've been trying to get clear on the Taurek numbers debate — the one where you have a drug, and either it saves one person who needs the whole dose, or it saves five people who each need a fifth of it. I'm on the anti-aggregation side (the claim that the numbers don't settle who to save, and saving the five isn't required). The arguments I keep running into are:

(1) The "worse for whom?" argument. If five die, each of the five loses a life; if one dies, he loses a life. There's no extra subject who also suffers the compound five-person loss. So "five losses are worse than one" isn't actually a state of affairs that can be weighed.

(2) The pairwise argument. Line any one of the five up against the one. Both die if they're not chosen; neither has a stronger claim. It's a tie. And you can't turn a bunch of ties into a reason by counting heads, because counting heads is just the aggregation move that's in dispute.

(3) The fairness argument. Each person has an equal claim to be saved, so a rule that simply saves the five gives the one no chance to have his claim matter at all. You'd at least need a coin flip, or a weighted lottery that gives his claim some real weight.

Is there a fourth argument for the anti-aggregation side I'm missing? Something genuinely different — not a re-wording of one of these, not a special case, and not a combination of two of them. If there is one, tell me whether it does independant work or just collapses into (1) or (2) once you push on it. And if you think there isn't one, say which of the three is doing the most work.
