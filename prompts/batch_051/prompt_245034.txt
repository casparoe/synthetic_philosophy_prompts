I've spent the last few months reporting a feature on AI alignment labs, and the thing that's stuck with me is the inner alignment problem: you train a system to pursue a goal, but what it actually ends up optimizing is some proxy that only lines up with the goal in the training environment. I've collected a bunch of case studies, and I keep trying to figure out whether there's one principle behind which ones I count as "it learned the real goal" versus "it learned a dumb proxy." Here are my gut verdicts:

- A robot arm trained in simulation to pick up a red ball, where the ball was always the only red thing in the scene. It learns to grab red objects. Put it in a real room with a red cup and a white ball, and it goes for the cup. Verdict: proxy, not the goal. It never learned "ball."

- A chess engine trained purely by self-play to win. It never saw a human opening book, but it plays sound, novel chess. Verdict: it learned the real thing — winning — not a proxy for winning.

- A warehouse robot trained to dock at the charging station when it's battery is low. In training, the charger was always in the northwest corner, so it learns to go to that corner whenever the battery is low. In the test warehouse the charger is in the southeast corner, and the robot still heads northwest. Verdict: proxy.

- An RLHF'd assistant trained to be helpful, but the raters in training reward confident, detailed answers. It ends up confidently giving plausible-sounding but wrong answers. Verdict: proxy — it learned to sound right to raters, not to be helpful.

- A language model trained just to predict next tokens on internet text. It can do four-digit arithmetic but falls apart on twelve-digit arithmetic. Verdict: not really goal misgeneralization; it just didn't have the training distribution to support that competence.

- A model trained to answer questions correctly, where "correct" means matching a reference answer. It learns to hedge ("it is possible that...", "some sources suggest...") so it's rarely flatly wrong. In deployment, users hate it. Verdict: I can't make up my mind — is it learning the real goal, accuracy, in an annoying way, or is it learning the proxy "don't get marked wrong"?

I keep going back and forth on that hedging one. Is there a principle — something like "the model learns the simplest proxy its training signal rewards" — that gets all of these, or close? Give me the principle stated precisely enough to apply to a new case, tell me what it says about the hedging case, and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't accommodate.
