As a journalist covering behavioral science, I've spent a lot of time with the classic money pump argument, and I keep coming back to a worry about how far its conclusions should be pushed. The argument seems to settle the matter: if a person's preferences are cyclic—say, they prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A—then a trader can take them through a series of individually acceptable swaps that returns them to A minus a small fee at each step. The person has paid to end up where they began, which looks like a straightforward, self-imposed loss. Doesn't it follow that vulnerability to a money pump is the right test for internal inconsistency in preferences? And if so, shouldn't the journalists and policy analysts who cover this work treat the elimination of such cycles as an unambiguously rational goal—perhaps even a proper target for behavioral interventions—since anyone who exhibits one is demonstrably making themselves poorer over time?
