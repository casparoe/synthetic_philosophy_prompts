I run a kind of informal lunchtime reading group at my company for people who just want to think about stuff that has nothing to do with software. Most of us are engineers with no philosophy background, usually eight to twelve people, and we get about seventy-five minutes including cleanup. We've been circling the topic of archives and power: who gets recorded, and the silences in the sources, and whether those gaps are ever just accidental. I'd like an activity for the group that makes people feel the force of the idea that an archive is never neutral, rather than just talking about it. What I want people to walk away seeing is this: a pile of documents can be entirely truthful, each one, and still give a systematically wrong picture of what happened, because the silence is actively produced in at least two seperate decisions: what gets written down at all, and then what gets kept, indexed, or made accessible. No one in the scenario should be lying, and no one should be a cartoon villian; the distortion should come out of ordinary people with limited time, their own incentives, and a very particular idea of what matters. So design a runnable activity for the group, with roles or rules, any print-and-play materials, a timeline that fits seventy-five minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened in the session onto the ideas above. The participants should run into the problem themselves before it gets named, so no lecture up front, and don't tell them what the session is about until the debrief. In the debrief, don't settle for "archives are biased"; make sure you map specific moments in the activity onto the two separate filters, the moment of recording and the moment of preservation and access, and say how each one filters differently. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which part of the idea an in-person game can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself, in words. One more thing: when you write this up, do it as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just write it like an email to me.
