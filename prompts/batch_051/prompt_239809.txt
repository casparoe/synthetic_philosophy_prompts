A town council keeps a stone fence around a meadow even though no one can say what it is for; the original purpose has been lost with the people who built it. When a councillor proposes removing the fence, the council refuses, invoking the principle often called Chesterton's fence: one should not pull down a fence until one knows why it was put up. Later in the same meeting, a councillor proposes a new by-law requiring every household to keep a rain barrel. Asked what problem the by-law solves, the councillor cannot give a coherent answer, and the council rejects the proposal, saying that a rule whose justification cannot be stated should not be adopted.

Most people would defend both decisions without sensing a conflict. But the first treats the absence of a known justification as a reason to keep an existing arrangement, while the second treats the absence of a known justification as a reason to refuse a new one. In both cases the absence of an available rationale is doing all the work; the only obvious difference is that the fence already exists and the by-law does not. Is that difference enough? Is there a principle — such as that survival over time is itself evidence, or that the burden of proof always falls on whoever would change things — that generates both verdicts without collapsing into the motto that whatever exists is good? Find the strongest candidate principle you can, then test it against nearby cases: an inherited custom you never chose, an old institution whose ignoble origins are known, a new proposal whose effects have been carefully modelled, and a fence built by accident yesterday. Give an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry between old and new arrangements should be kept, revised, or replaced by a single standard.
