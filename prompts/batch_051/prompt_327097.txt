I got into an argument with my roommate about whether you still have to keep a promise when keeping it would help nobody and the person you made it to has forgotten about it completely. She said obviously yes, and I agreed but couldn't actually say why, which bugged me enough that I spent a couple weeks trying to build a reason from scratch. What I've landed on I'm calling the ticket theory of promising, or sometimes "claimed-future" theory, and I want to know if it's actually a theory or just a metaphor with delusions of grandeur. The idea: a promise isn't a prediction about what I'll do, and it's not mainly a way of getting someone to rely on me. It's me handing the other person a small piece of my future self, like a ticket drawn on a later version of me. Once I hand it over, that chunk of my future stops being fully mine to spend. If I don't show up, the wrong isn't that I disappointed someone or caused bad consequences; it's that I took back something I'd already given away, and it was no longer mine to take back. This is why the other person forgetting doesn't dissolve the promise: the claim is still out there even when nobody is looking at the ticket. It also explains why a promise yanked out of someone under pressure doesn't bind, because no real transfer happened; it was more like a mugging than a gift.

The edge cases are where I start to lose confidence. A promise made to someone who is about to die and will never know one way or the other, a promise made while drunk but that I fully meant at the time, a promise to myself to quit smoking: my ticket story gives a different verdict for each, and I can't tell if that's a feature or a bug. And I keep worrying I've just re-invented some boring old "rights transfer" idea that actual philosophers already dissected a hundred and fifty years ago. Please be straight with me: give me your best reconstruction of what I'm actually claiming, the nearest established positions or whether there's a name for it, and the three hardest cases where the ticket picture either overgenerates or undergenerates. If one of those cases kills the theory outright, say so.
