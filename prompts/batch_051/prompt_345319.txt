A friend and I recently spent an afternoon arguing about what thought experiments actually do in philosophy. I came in defending the view that at least some of the canonical ones — Gettier cases against the justified-true-belief analysis of knowledge, Frankfurt-style cases against the principle of alternative possibilities, Mary the color scientist against physicalism — can function as genuine evidence for or against a philosophical claim, in something like the way a carefully constructed experiment or counterexample does in science. My friend held that this is exactly the wrong way to describe them: on her view, a thought experiment is at best a vivid illustration of an argument that must be supplied separately, or an intuition pump that produces a psychological response from which no philosophical conclusion can be read directly. We agreed that both camps have talked past each other partly by using the word evidence too loosely, but we did not get much further than that.

I would like you to draft the document we should have produced at the end of that argument: a joint adversarial-collaboration statement between the evidentialist about philosophical thought experiments and the deflationist who treats them as illustrations or intuition pumps.

The document should contain the following.

First, a numbered agreed-claims section. The claims should be substantive rather than vacuous, and should state, in wording both sides would sign: what thought experiments are; what they paradigmatically do; which features of them are not in dispute; what they do not by themselves establish; and what each side concedes the other has shown. In particular, the section should separate three functions that are often run together: a thought experiment functioning as a counterexample that refutes a universal or sufficiency claim; a thought experiment providing positive support for a theory; and a thought experiment revealing the content or structure of a concept. The two sides may sign on to one of these while still disputing the others.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row should isolate a single point of remaining disagreement, with each side's position phrased so that its own proponents would accept the wording. The entries should not be caricatures. If any part of the disagreement is merely verbal, the table should say so and explain which wording dissolves it.

Third, a list of cruxes. For each crux — a sub-question such that a settled answer would move at least one side — explain why it is a crux rather than a restatement of the original disagreement.

Fourth, for each crux, at least one concrete test, case, or finding that both sides would agree bears on it. This may be a particular thought experiment (for example, Gettier-style cases, Frankfurt-style cases, Mary's room, Twin Earth, philosophical zombies, or the experience machine), an empirical finding about variation or stability in responses to such cases, or a methodological comparison with scientific experiments or models. For each item, record in advance what each side predicts it will show, and specify what pattern of results would count as a concession by each side.

Finally, a short closing statement that both sides could sign, stating what they now take the disagreement to be about and what the collaboration has forced them to make precise.

Avoid bibliographies and citations; the positions should be judged solely by how well they are formulated.
