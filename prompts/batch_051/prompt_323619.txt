I am a PhD student in philosophy of mind working as a teaching assistant for an undergraduate course on the problem of other minds. Below is a short-answer exam question and one student's response. Please grade the response in the way an instructor would: identify the substantive claims the student gets right, identify each genuine error or unsupported step, explain why it matters, and give a mark out of 10. I am looking for point-by-point comments rather than a general impression.

Question:

Descartes writes the following in Meditation II of the Meditations on First Philosophy:

"What then did I formerly think I was ? Undoubtedly I judged that I was a man. But what is a man ? Shall I say a rational animal ? Assuredly not; for it would be necessary forthwith to inquire into what is meant by animal, and what by rational, and thus, from a single question, I should insensibly glide into others, and these more difficult than the first; nor do I now possess enough of leisure to warrant me in wasting my time amid subtleties of this sort. I prefer here to attend to the thoughts that sprung up of themselves in my mind, and were inspired by my own nature alone, when I applied myself to the consideration of what I was."

Explain what Descartes is doing in this passage and how it fits into the argument of Meditation II. Then discuss what the passage implies, if anything, for the problem of other minds. In particular, evaluate the claim that Descartes's procedure makes the existence of other minds radically uncertain in a way that ordinary experience cannot correct.

Student response:

Descartes has just established that he exists and is now trying to determine what he is. He rejects the definition of man as a rational animal because that definition would require him to say what "animal" and "rational" mean, and doing so would drag him into scholastic disputes that are less certain than his own awareness of himself. Instead, he turns inward and decides to attend to the thoughts that arise spontaneously when he thinks about what he is. The key move is that Descartes makes the criterion for being a self a first-person one: what I am is whatever I find in my own thinking. This directly creates the problem of other minds, because a first-person criterion cannot be applied to another person from the outside. If the only way to be sure that something is a mind is to introspect its thoughts, then I can never be sure that other bodies have minds at all; they might be automata that produce speech and behavior without any inner thought. The example of hats and coats walking in the street later in the Meditations supports this reading: Descartes says we judge that they are men not by the senses but by the intellect. That shows that the existence of other minds is inferred rather than observed, and for Descartes every such inference is dubious until God guarantees the reliability of the intellect. The passage therefore does make other minds radically uncertain, but only inside the method of doubt; after the proofs of God's existence and veracity, the ordinary belief in other minds can be accepted as a natural propensity. So the claim is correct about the skeptical stage of the Meditations but not about the work as a whole.
