I am a PhD student in law, working on the boundary between defamation doctrine and the economics of information intermediaries, and I want to think through a scenario that my advisors keep telling me is too speculative for the dissertation itself. Under the current U.S. framework, a statement is actionable as defamation only if it asserts or implies a false fact about an identifiable person, firm, or other entity; a "pure opinion," or a statement that a reasonable reader would not take as making a provably false factual claim, is shielded, while ratings and forecasts sit uneasily in between. My thought experiment runs instead from the following premise. Starting tomorrow, every jurisdiction in the United States adopts a clean-looking rule: any published rating and any explicitly quantified forecast about an identifiable entity is treated as an assertion of fact for defamation purposes, with no opinion-based defense available merely because the statement is evaluative or future-directed. The rest of defamation law stays as it is—publication, identification, reputational harm, falsity, and the usual fault standards (negligence for private figures, actual malice for public figures) all still apply. A plaintiff prevails, in this imagined regime, if a court finds that the rating or forecast was materially wrong by the standard its presentation implied; there is no need to prove bad faith or intent to deceive.

Over the following fifteen years, tell me what you predict happens to four things. First, the business of assigning ratings—credit ratings, product scores, rankings of universities and hospitals, employer-review scores, restaurant stars. Second, published expert forecasts—equity price targets, earnings estimates, political handicapping, projections about athletes' performance or injury risk. Third, the insurance, indemnification, and contracting practices that grow up around ratings and forecasts. Fourth, how institutions, employers, lenders, and readers actually obtain evaluative information once public quantitative judgments become legally perilous. I do not want a narrative; I want a visible chain of reasoning about the incentives facing raters, platforms, plaintiffs' lawyers, insurers, and the subjects of ratings, with your confidence attached to each link. Say which downstream effects you regard as robust, which are fragile guesses, and identify the two or three load-bearing assumptions—for instance, about how courts would operationalize falsity for a five-star score or a price target, about how many claims would actually be filed and survive, and about how elastic the demand for public ratings turns out to be—such that, if those assumptions went the other way, large parts of the prediction would reverse. Finally, tell me whether this counterfactual makes you think the existing fact–opinion distinction in this corner of the law is doing real work, or whether a world of litigation-proof ratings would simply push the same evaluative speech into forms the doctrine has to pretend are not factual.
