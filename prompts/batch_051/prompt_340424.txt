I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who has recently moved into teaching an undergraduate philosophy of science course, and I am grading a take-home essay on reduction and emergence. Below is the question as set, followed by one student's answer as submitted. Please assign a mark out of 20 and give me a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression: work through the answer in the order it is written, and for each substantive claim say whether it is correct, partly correct, or mistaken, with a brief explanation, and where a mark adjustment is warranted, provide the wording I can write as a marginal comment.

Exam question:

Some physicists hold that although everything is composed of the entities studied by microphysics, and that the behavior of any composite system is in some sense fixed by its parts and their local interactions, the higher-level sciences retain a life of their own. Distinguish three claims one might make here:
(1) higher-level generalizations cannot be derived from microphysics in practice, given our computational and mathematical limitations;
(2) they cannot be derived from microphysics even in principle, however ideal the reasoner;
(3) even where derivations exist or could exist, higher-level descriptions are pragmatically indispensable for explanation, prediction, and control.
Using the spontaneous magnetization of a ferromagnet below its Curie temperature as your example, state which of (1)–(3) a non-reductive physicalist is committed to, and explain what would have to be shown for (2) to be established rather than merely asserted. Keep in view throughout the physicalist thesis that lower-level facts fix all higher-level facts.

Student answer:

The three claims differ over whether the obstacle is merely cognitive or a fact about the world. Claim (1) is uncontroversial: with on the order of 10^23 interacting spins, one cannot actually compute the magnetization of a real sample. Claim (3) goes further and says that even an ideal derivation from the microscopic Hamiltonian would not make the thermodynamic description useless, because we still need the order parameter, the phase diagram, and the notion of broken symmetry to understand what is happening. Claim (2) is the strongest.

For the ferromagnet, the microscopic interactions are known, and the canonical partition function can be written down. The striking point is that spontaneous magnetization appears only after one takes the thermodynamic limit and then lets the external field go to zero; for any finite number of spins, the magnetization at zero field vanishes. Since the order parameter is defined only in the infinite-system idealization, the higher-level property is not derivable from any finite microphysical description. The step from the spin Hamiltonian to the magnetized state therefore fails in principle, not merely in practice: the transition is a mathematical singularity, and no amount of computation over finitely many parts could cross it. It follows that the macrostate is not fixed by any finite arrangement of the parts.

A non-reductive physicalist is therefore committed to (2) and (3), not merely to (1). The result also shows why some physicists insist that each level of complexity brings in behavior that is different in kind. A residual physicalism is still available in the sense that each individual spin flips as the local field dictates, and a finite system's magnetization can be approximated by the infinite-system result, so the higher-level description does not float free; but the singular limit is what carries the emergence, and to deny this would be to mistake the finite-size approximation for the phenomenon itself. The physicalist thesis that lower-level facts fix higher-level facts therefore survives only in a weakened, approximate form: the ferromagnet shows that the lower-level facts genuinely underdetermine the macroscopically important property.
