I am a novelist, and the intellectual center of the book I am writing is a philosopher of mind who defends a position I have, with some disquiet, come to hold. The position is that thought experiments in philosophy are not evidence for the claims they are deployed to support. At best they illustrate commitments already in place; in their characteristic operation they are intuition pumps, small manufactured narratives that secure a verdict by controlling which details are visible, whose perspective the reader occupies, and which alternatives feel salient. On this view, what presents itself as the discovery of a modal fact is in fact an achievement of narrative technique, and it should be audited as craft rather than as testing. The question matters to me professionally as much as philosophically, because the device in question is a close relative of the devices I use in fiction every day; if the view is right, my own practice is epistemically suspect in precisely the same way.

I would like you to treat that position as one I hold and give me a map of where it is exposed. Please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I want the arguments and observations themselves, rendered precisely enough that I could put them into the mouths of intelligent characters.

In the course of the answer, do the following kinds of work. State the view sharply enough that I can tell whether I have drawn the target correctly, and distinguish it from its two main rivals: the view that thought experiments are a kind of evidence, and the view that they are merely neutral illustrations; tell me if my formulation needs strengthening or weakening to remain genuinely contested rather than trivial or obviously false. Then identify the considerations that would count decisively against the view: the observations, arguments, case-level pressures, formal results, and empirical findings that should genuinely move me, ranked from fatal to merely serious, including cases in which a thought experiment resisted prior intuition and was later vindicated by experiment or proof, if you take such cases to matter. Where a consideration is empirical, say what pattern of results would discriminate my view from its rivals. Next identify the weaker pressures, the things that are awkward for the view but survivable by refinement or by narrowing its scope. Then say what a committed defender of the intuition-pump view would themselves concede as counting against it, and what they would dismiss as not engaging the position. Assess whether the view as stated has been insulated from failure, for instance by treating every apparent case of a thought experiment correcting a theory as merely making explicit a commitment the community already held, and whether that insulation would be a defect in a view of this kind. End with a bottom line for me: what I, as a novelist who also holds the view, should find most unsettling about it, and what would be the first development that should lead me to abandon it.

Please organize the response in the following named sections, in this order, at roughly these lengths:

The view and its rivals, 200 words;
Decisive defeaters, 500 words;
Awkward pressures, 350 words;
What a defender must concede, 250 words;
Is the view insulated, 300 words;
What should move me most, 200 words.
