I've ended up in the Lucas–Penrose camp, or close to it: I think the argument has an underrated core, namely that for any formal system F I can recognize as sound, I can see that F's Gödel sentence is true, so no such F captures my arithmetic; the mechanist is left saying my mind instantiates some F I can't recognize, that I'm inconsistent, or that "soundness" is doing different work for me than it does for formal systems. I don't want the standard list of objections. I want a map of what would actually count against this position: which observations, formal results, or philosophical considerations would a committed Gödelian have to treat as real defeaters, and which of those are fatal as opposed to merely forcing a patch? And here's the thing I keep coming back to: the usual mechanist replies all seem to retreat to an existential claim about my own program that no empirical finding could touch, which makes me worry the anti-mechanist has insulated the view into unfalsifiability rather than winning. Is that a worse problem for the Gödelian than it is for other first-person-epistemic claims, or is it just what a disagreement about arithmetic self-knowledge looks like? I want an honest verdict, not a steelman of whichever side you personally prefer.
