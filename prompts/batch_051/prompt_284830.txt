Here is a parallel I have been trying to think through, and I cannot decide whether it is deep or superficial. The choice of a calendar's epoch — what should count as year zero, whether the era boundary should admit a mathematical year zero, whether the numbering should be pushed back before written history, and so on — seems to me structurally similar to the choice of a zero point for a coordinate system, or of a gauge in physics. In both cases there are invariant relations underneath that are unchanged when the origin or gauge is shifted: elapsed intervals and the order of events on the calendar side; fields and equations of motion on the physics side. And in both cases the choice is not therefore arbitrary in practice, because some choices produce fewer negative numbers, avoid off-by-one mistakes around a boundary, or make calculations better conditioned. But the suspected analogy may break exactly where gauge freedom is supposed to be physically inert. A calendar epoch is a public legal and historical convention, not merely a private calculational device. Changing it would leave durations and order intact, but it would reattach dates to different historical events, redistribute the salience of anniversaries, and disturb institutional continuity in a way that a change of gauge is not supposed to disturb anything observable. My question is whether this is a genuine structural parallel between convention in calendrics and invariance under gauge or coordinate transformations, or only a loose analogy built on the shared image of a movable zero. If it is genuine, what would a theory of calendrical convention have to adopt explicitly from gauge theory for results to transfer? If it is not, what precise feature of gauge freedom is missing on the calendar side?
