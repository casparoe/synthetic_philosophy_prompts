I've been reading about the Judy Benjamin problem, and I want to know how to apply its lesson in my own reasoning. The setup, as I understand it, is this. A soldier is dropped somewhere in a territory divided into two sectors, one of which is split into two smaller zones, and she receives a radio message that is entirely conditional: if she is in the first sector, the odds overwhelmingly favor one of its two subzones. The message does not say which sector she is in, and the information it carries is a constraint on a conditional probability rather than an ordinary proposition one could straightforwardly conditionalize on. I gather that different update rules in formal epistemology are said to handle this case differently, and that some of them imply she should revise her unconditional probability of being in the first sector even though no one told her where she is. That makes it sound as though the real lesson is about us rather than about the formalism: human beings do not naturally revise the antecedent of a conditional unless someone spells it out, and this is a bias worth correcting. So I would like a practical debiasing protocol. When I receive information of the form: if A, then B is much more likely than C, exactly how should I update my probability of A itself, and how do I know I am not overcorrecting? Please answer in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as if writing a careful letter.
