I have been rereading Thucydides’ *History of the Peloponnesian War* because I recently joined a university disciplinary board, and the Mitylenean debate now seems less like ancient history than a live argument about how an institution should respond after it has already made a harsh decision in anger. In the debate, Cleon urges the Athenians to keep their earlier vote to punish the whole city, insisting that an empire rules through fear and that a bad law firmly kept is better than a good one inconsistently enforced. Diodotus answers with a different principle. At one point he says:

“we are not in a court of justice, but in a political assembly; and the question is not justice, but how to make the Mitylenians useful to Athens.”

Write a dialogue between two equally sophisticated members of a modern university disciplinary board who are arguing about whether to uphold or reduce a severe sanction that was approved under public pressure. One takes a Cleon-like line about consistency, deterrence, and the dangers of compassion; the other takes a Diodotus-like line about future interests, the distinction between a court and a deliberative assembly, and the uses of leniency. Have them argue over what Diodotus means by “not justice, but how to make the Mitylenians useful to Athens,” and whether that distinction is admirable or corrupting. Let them respond to each other’s actual points, and do not let either side be a strawman.
