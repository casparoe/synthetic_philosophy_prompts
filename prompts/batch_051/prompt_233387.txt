Courts in many legal systems require a witness to take an oath before testifying; a false statement made afterward is perjury, while the same falsehood told to the same officials before any oath was administered is at most a lesser wrong and often no crime at all. Yet the same court will, on request, allow a witness to make a secular affirmation instead — no sacred text, no invocation — and attach exactly the same perjury penalties to it. The court does not normally ask whether the witness believes in what the oath invokes, fears anything, or means the words in any special way; it checks only that the prescribed words were said, in the prescribed setting, by someone in the prescribed role.

Meanwhile, in Greek myth the gods bind themselves by swearing by the river Styx. An oath sworn by the Styx binds even the immortals, and when a god breaks it the punishment simply falls on them: no investigation, no discretion, no lesser charge. A human courtroom oath, by contrast, appears to bind only because the state stands behind the perjury charge, and it binds identically whether anything sacred was invoked or not.

What does this cluster of practices presuppose about the binding force of an oath? I am not asking for a definition of the word "oath"; I am asking what theory of what makes an oath bind a person — or a god — is needed to make sense of what these institutions do. Is the binding force a product of the public speech act itself, a way of placing the speaker under a supernatural enforcer, a seriousness-signal that merely triggers the law's interest, or something else? Does the legal practice strictly presuppose one of these views, or is it compatible with several? Does the fact that swearing and affirming are treated as identical show that the law is not committed to any supernatural account — or show instead that it regards them as two routes to the same underlying thing? Does the Styx oath presuppose a different theory of binding force from the courtroom oath, or the same theory pushed to its limit? And what would have to change in perjury law if we concluded that oaths are just promises, with no special binding force beyond the promise itself?
