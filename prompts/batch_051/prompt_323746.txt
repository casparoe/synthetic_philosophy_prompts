I keep saying gauge freedom is surplus structure, but honestly if someone pushed me on what makes a piece of structure surplus rather than just hard to observe, I'd flounder. Give me a working definition of surplus structure in a gauge theory that gets these cases right. (1) The vector and scalar potentials in electromagnetism: changing A and φ by the standard gauge transformation leaves E and B alone, and I want that called surplus. (2) E and B themselves: not surplus, whatever 'real' amounts to. (3) The global phase of the wavefunction: multiplying ψ by a constant phase changes no observable, so it smells surplus, though I'm not sure it's gauge freedom in the usual local sense. (4) The Aharonov-Bohm phase: a charged particle going around a region where B is zero but the potential isn't picks up an interference phase that is gauge-invariant and measurable, which makes me suspicious of what I said in (1). (5) Coordinate freedom in general relativity: two metrics related by a diffeomorphism are supposed to stand for the same spacetime, but people genuinely argue over whether that is pure gauge. (6) Adding a total time derivative to a Lagrangian: it changes no equation of motion, but boundary effects make me hesitate to call it surplus. There's a couple of borderline ones in there on purpose. Propose a definition, run it against each case, say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. Then tell me which of your six verdicts is least secure.
