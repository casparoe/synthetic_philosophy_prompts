I've been going back and forth with a couple of friends about the moral equality of combatants — the idea that a soldier on the unjust side and a soldier on the just side are morally on a par when it comes to killing each other: neither is a murderer, and both are the sort of person the other side may intentionally kill. Every time we pushed on it the argument seemed to bottom out in a disagreement about who even counts as a combatant, at which point I realized a lot of our fight was a defintion problem rather than a moral one.

So: give me a working definition of combatant for the purposes of that principle — the class of people who get to kill and are liable to be killed in war. I don't mean the legal category in the laws of war; I mean the moral category the principle actually needs. It has to classify the following cases, and I want you to actually run the definition over each one, flag where it gives the wrong verdict or no verdict, and then either revise it or defend the surprising verdict. The cases: (1) a regular soldier in uniform at the front, which I take to be clearly in; (2) a child in a school, clearly out; (3) a munitions factory worker who never carries a weapon and lives far from the fighting; (4) a private military contractor guarding a supply convoy in a war zone; (5) a drone operator who flies a strike from a trailer in Nevada and then goes home and picks the kids up from daycare; (6) a military medic who carries a sidearm only to defend wounded patients; (7) a farmer who works his fields by day and joins an armed ambush by night after his village is occupied; (8) a university scientist designing a new explosive under a military grant; (9) a soldier who has thrown down their weapon and is trying to surrender; (10) a reservist on leave at a wedding; and (11) a civilian cyber operator who shuts down the enemy power grid from an apartment, which I honestly can't place at all. Then tell me which of your verdicts you're least confident about, and why.
