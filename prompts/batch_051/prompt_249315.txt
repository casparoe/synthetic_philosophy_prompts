One influential practice in AI alignment research is to model the supervision of advanced AI as a debate judged by a human, and to treat iterated amplification—recursively decomposing a hard question into human-checkable subquestions—as an idealization of what careful human deliberation could be. The practice leans on a complexity-theoretic analogy. In their 2018 paper AI safety via debate, Geoffrey Irving, Paul Christiano, and Dario Amodei write: “In an analogy to complexity theory, debate with optimal play can answer any question in PSPACE given polynomial time judges (direct judging answers only NP questions).” The resulting picture, often called HCH (“humans consulting HCH”), is closely tied to factored cognition: the idea that sophisticated reasoning can be decomposed into many small steps, each individually legible and checkable. What philosophical commitments does this modeling practice presuppose about the nature of judgment, reasons, and normative inquiry? In particular, does it presuppose that the quality of a conclusion can be checked locally, step by step, without any irreducible cognitive act of grasping the whole? Does it presuppose that truthfulness or normative correctness is decomposable in the way the complexity analogy suggests, or merely that trained debaters will be incentivized to surface checkable errors? Reconstruct the implicit theory of deliberation, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely hopes is empirically true, and identify where it is underdetermined or in tension with plausible rival theories of deliberation. I want a committed reconstruction: take a definite position on which presuppositions the idealization really carries, and defend it rather than surveying the options.
