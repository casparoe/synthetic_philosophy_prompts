Here's an argument I've been trying to get straight. I keep seeing it said that the axioms of expected utility theory aren't just arbitrary assumptions—they're coherence requirements, and violating any of them makes you irrational. I tried to lay the argument out properly and I'm not sure it's as tight as people think:

(1) If your preferences are intransitive (A > B, B > C, C > A), someone can offer you a chain of trades, each of which you prefer, that leaves you back where you started but a little poorer, no matter what happens.

(2) Any preference pattern that makes you a guaranteed loser in that way is a coherence failure, not just an unusual taste.

(3) So rationality requires transitive preferences.

(4) The same style of argument is standardly extended to the other axioms—completeness and independence especially—the idea being that violating them opens you to sure losses or to a kind of inconsistency that's just as bad.

(5) By the representation theorem, if your preferences satisfy all the axioms, then your choices can be represented as maximizing expected utility relative to some probability and utility function.

(6) So if you're rational, your preferences satisfy the axioms and you maximize expected utility.

(7) So being rational just is being an expected utility maximizer, at least in risky and uncertain choice.

I half-suspect the slide from (5) to (6) is doing something sneaky, but maybe the real trouble is back in (4), or maybe the whole thing actually goes through. Which step, if any, fails, and does the conclusion follow from a corrected version? Be specific about the step and the reading of it. Keep it under 300 words.
