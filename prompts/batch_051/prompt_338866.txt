I convene a philosophy of science MA seminar, and next week's session is devoted to Woodward's interventionist treatment of causation, organized around the circularity objection — in its standard form, that cause and intervention are defined through one another, so the account fails to ground causation in manipulation rather than presupposing it. The students will have read the core chapters, and most will arrive suspecting that the view is either quietly circular or smuggling in the everyday notion of intervening. Do not rehearse the theory for me; plan the delivery for a fifty-minute session. Specify the order in which the components must be introduced and what each step presupposes; choose the one worked example that should carry the whole session, and say what it must contain and what it must not; identify the confusions an MA-level but not formally trained audience will predictably form — for instance, identifying an intervention with an actual experiment performed by an agent, or reading invariance as ordinary robustness across conditions — and the exact stage in the sequence at which each should be headed off; and state what you would deliberately leave out at this level, and how you would say so. Finish with the single question you would put to the class to test whether they have grasped the difference between genuine circularity and an intervention characterized without presupposing the very causal relation it is meant to explicate.
