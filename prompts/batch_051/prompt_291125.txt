I am a retired physiologist who now sits on the council of a private research institute; I have recently taken up philosophy in earnest, and I find the following case more philosophically interesting than my colleagues do. The council must decide at its next meeting whether to adopt a binding working-hours and availability policy proposed by our early-career researchers' association. The proposal caps weekly working time at 48 hours, bars work-related email between 19:00 and 07:00 and at weekends except under a prior agreement tied to a defined piece of work, and removes visible after-hours presence from annual performance reviews. The proposal follows two stress-related medical leaves in the past year and is explicitly framed as preventing a culture of 'devotion through hours' from doing further harm.

The senior investigators' committee opposes the policy. Its central claim is that this is not a factory and that autonomous, self-directed research does not run by a clock; it argues that people who choose to push themselves should be free to do so, that the institute's best work has sometimes come from long voluntary stretches, and that a hard cap treats researchers as hazards to be managed rather than as adults capable of judging what their work requires. The committee adds that the policy would push work into the shadows and leave the real problems—poorly designed projects and weak supervision—untouched.

I want you to draft the short written opinion I shall circulate to the council before the meeting. It should lead with a definite recommendation: adopt the policy, reject it, or adopt it in a modified form with the modifications specified. The reasoning should make the underlying philosophical question explicit rather than treating this as routine HR policy. As I see it, the disagreement is ultimately about whether sustained effort has value in itself and therefore deserves institutional space and recognition, or whether effort is only instrumentally valuable, with rest, health, and control of one's own time treated as primary goods that working-time limits protect. I would like the opinion to take a stand on that question and use it to justify the recommended course, drawing where useful on the older Protestant-work-ethic defence of effort, the logic behind the 35-hour working week, and the Japanese recognition of karoshi as a systemic harm—but without turning into a history lesson.

Please give the strongest consideration on the losing side its due, not a straw man, and explain why it is outweighed or accommodated. Do not retreat into a survey of options: I need a committed opinion with an actionable bottom line, stated plainly enough that the council can vote on it. Keep the opinion to no more than 800 words, and write it in British English.
