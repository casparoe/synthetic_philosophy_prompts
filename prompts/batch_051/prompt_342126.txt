Two kids in my philosophy club got into it over the paradox of blackmail, and I honestly couldn't figure out whos right. Marco said the paradox is real: it's legal to tell someone's secret, and it's legal to ask someone for money, but combining them into "pay me or I'll tell everyone" is somehow a crime, and nobody in class could explain the difference. Jules said it isn't a paradox, because the wrong is in the threat itself: a threat doesn't just combine two legal acts, it coerces the victim, and coercion is wrong even when the thing you threaten is legal. Marco came back that we threaten people with legal acts all the time—it's legal to stop being someone's friend or to quit a job, and threatening to do those isn't blackmail, so coercion alone can't be the line. Jules said the difference is that a blackmailer turns the victim's private information into a weapon for his own profit, and that's exploitation, not just coercion, so the "two legal acts" framing misses the point. Marco replied that this makes wrongness depend on the blackmailer's motive, which is weird because motive usually doesn't change whether an act should be illegal, and anyway if blackmail were legal, people could just pay to keep secrets quiet and information might even be priced accurately. Jules answered that's exactly why the law shouldn't enforce deals made under that kind of pressure—"pay me or I'll expose you" is the sort of agreement you can't genuinely consent to. Marco said the pressure comes from the secret being embarrassing, not from the blackmailer, and you can't make it illegal for people to know things about you. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a brief justification, and say who's more right overall about what, including anything both of them are missing.
