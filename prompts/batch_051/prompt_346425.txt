I read a blog post that summarized Bernard Williams's integrity objection to utilitarianism, and something about the last step feels off to me. The post says: Williams thinks utilitarianism is wrong because it tells you to pick whatever action has the best total consequences, even when that would mean abandoning your deepest commitments. For him that's an attack on integrity — a life is genuinely yours only if it's organized around the ground projects you identify with — and a theory that asks you to throw those away whenever the aggregate calculation tips a little is asking you to quit being the person you are. His examples are supposed to show this: the chemist who can slow down a chemical-weapons program by taking a job he hates, the visitor who can save nineteen people by shooting one, and the man who saves his drowning wife without first checking whether saving your own spouse is permitted — the last one being the one-thought-too-many point. The post then concludes that integrity is a first-person value, so utilitarianism, which takes an outside view of everyone's welfare, can't count it at all — and therefore utilitarianism is refuted.

I'm not convinced by the jump from "utilitarianism treats integrity as just another cost" to "it can't count it at all," but I'm not sure where the fair paraphrase ends and the overstatement begins. Go through this piece by piece: which claims would Williams accept, which are simplifications he'd resist, and does the refutation conclusion survive one the inaccuracies are fixed? Also, is the drowning-wife example doing the same work as the other two, or is it a different objection wearing the same clothes?
