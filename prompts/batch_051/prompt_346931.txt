So my grandfather homesteaded a stretch of land out west back when that was still a thing you could do. Over thirty years he cleared it, built the barns, dug the wells, planted the orchard — everyone around agrees most of the value of the place is his labor, not the raw ground. He left it to me, and last year I put up a fence and started charging people from the nearby town to cross my land to reach the river, which they and their grandparents had been using for as long as anyone can remember. My neighbor says the original claim was only ever legitimate so long as there was good land left for everyone else to go work for themselves, and that locking up the river crossing was never part of what my grandfather was entitled to. I don't see why. Nobody made those families farm. My grandfather mixed his sweat with the soil, so it's his, and now it's mine, and a right of way across it is just a nice-sounding claim on somebody else's property. But the river point does nag at me, since the crossing mattered to the town before my grandfather ever showed up.

I'm trying to figure out whether the "he worked it, so it's his" story actually pulls its weight here. What exactly does that kind of story require for a claim on land to be legitimate — is the neighbor right that it only works while there's enough left for others, and does that condition still bite now, or only at the moment the land was taken? And is the river access a problem for the property claim itself, or a separate fairness issue that I'm wrongly lumping in with it? Don't just tell me who's being unreasonable — give me the principle.
