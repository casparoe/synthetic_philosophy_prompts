The constitution under which I live was adopted in the 1920s, and I have been re-reading its emergency article because of what has happened here over the past two years. The article reads:

"Article 29. In time of war, invasion, insurrection, or other grave public danger, the President may declare a state of emergency. While a declaration is in force, the Government may, by regulation, take such measures as are strictly required by the situation, including restrictions on freedom of movement, residence, and assembly, and may close or regulate the use of ports and borders. A declaration lapses after thirty days unless renewed by a majority of the Legislature; each renewal may not exceed thirty days."

The Government has relied on this article to deal with a respiratory illness that arrives in waves. During each wave it has closed the borders, ordered people to remain at home except for specified reasons, and prohibited indoor gatherings; between waves it has let most restrictions lapse and then re-imposed them as case counts climb again. The people who drafted Article 29 had lived through a decade of civil war and foreign occupation, and they clearly thought of an emergency as an event with a visible source, a place, and an end — troops massing at a frontier, an uprising in a city, a port seized. The illness is not like that: it has no visible enemy, it is everywhere and nowhere in particular, and the "emergency" has come to look less like a discrete event than a recurring condition that might not end.

I am trying to think clearly about this. Does Article 29, read according to its plain words, apply to a pandemic and to restrictions that are lifted and then re-imposed in this way? Does it apply when read according to its purpose, and what, precisely, is that purpose? What would the drafters have said if they had been asked about an invisible illness that could keep a country half-closed for years, and what would a reasonable reader of the article take it to mean? If these approaches diverge, which should govern — for a court, and for an ordinary citizen trying to decide whether to comply? And beyond the text: if the measures are lawful under Article 29, does their legitimacy depend on the legislature's repeated renewal, on whether less restrictive alternatives were available between waves, on how the burdens fall, or on something else? I am trying to separate the question of what the article allows from the question of whether this use of emergency power is something a citizen should accept.
