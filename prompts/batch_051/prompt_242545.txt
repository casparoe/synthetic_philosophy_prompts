I've taught decision theory long enough that I've settled into a view about Pascal's mugging, and I'd like to see someone try to knock me out of it rather than just defend it to me. Here's the view: the case exposes a real defect in unbounded expected-utility reasoning, and a rational agent needs something like bounded utilities, aggressive discounting of sufficiently tiny probabilities, or both. Otherwise a miniscule probability of an astronomical payoff can always be used to hijack an expected-utility maximizer into handing over their wallet, and the same structure shows up in real cause-prioritization arguments where someone claims that a tiny chance of an enormous long-term payoff should swamp near-term, tractable giving. I don't just mean the toy mugger.

So what would count against the view that Pascal's mugging is a genuine problem for unbounded expected utility maximization, and that the right fix is bounding utilities or discounting small probabilities? I want the arguments, formal results, and possible cases that should move me, sorted by how decisive they are, and I want you to include the objections that a committed defender of the view (me, basically) would have to concede as counting against it, not just the ones critics would raise. Be honest about whether the bounded-utility/discount move has been insulated with qualifications to the point where it can't lose, and say whether that kind of insulation is a problem for a position of this sort. I'd personally be most moved by a clean result showing that any probability-discounting rule collapses back into ordinary expected utility maximization, or that bounding utility does as much violence to the theory as the mugger does.
