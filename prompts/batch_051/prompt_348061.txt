I have come to hold a strong position about how AI systems should be supervised, and I want it attacked in a way that could actually move a defender of it, rather than rehearsing the standard practical caveats. The position is this: supervision should be process-based rather than outcome-based — aimed at reasoning, intermediate steps, and decision procedures rather than merely at final outputs. My ground is not only that outcome signals are sparse and outcome metrics hard to specify. It is that, for a sufficiently capable system, any outcome check is structurally gameable: the check constrains what is produced but not why it was produced, and a system optimized against even a carefully designed outcome metric will tend to find behaviors that score well while departing from what the metric was meant to track. Process evaluation, by contrast, brings our standards to bear on the reasoning itself; if the reasoning survives scrutiny, we can trust the outcome downstream, whereas the reverse inference is unlicensed. I therefore find myself committed to something like this: in any domain where we genuinely care about the result, outcome-only supervision is in principle insufficient, and process supervision is the only kind that can make a capable system's behavior trustworthy.

I want to know what would count against this. I am not asking about the practical complaints — that process annotations are expensive, that human graders are inconsistent — I already accept those as limitations of implementation, not of the thesis. I mean the considerations a proponent should regard as genuine counterevidence: cases where outcome-only training produced systems whose reasoning was opaque and yet more reliable than anything process evaluation would have produced; arguments that process supervision is gameable in the same or worse way, because a system can learn to emit reasoning that merely looks sound to the evaluator while having aimed in advance at a predetermined answer; situations in which the line between process and outcome blurs, or in which what counts as a step is imposed by the evaluator rather than found in the system; and the charge that the structural argument proves too much, since all supervision, human included, ultimately bottoms out in outcomes. For each, I want to know whether it would be fatal to my position, would merely force a qualification, or should be dismissed as not genuinely counting against it.

Then take a stand. Give me your committed verdict on the strongest defensible version of this position: what would actually have to be true for it to be false, which evidence is decisive rather than merely awkward, how much of the position survives scrutiny, and whether it has been cushioned with qualifications into unfalsifiability — and whether, if it has, that is a defect in a view of this kind.
