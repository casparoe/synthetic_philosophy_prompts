"Brandom's inferentialism is the thesis that the meaning of a sentence is entirely fixed by its inferential role: what a speaker would infer from the sentence and what they would infer it from. For example, 'Fido is a dog' means what it does because English speakers tend to infer 'Fido is an animal' from it and tend to infer it from 'Fido is a Labrador.' Since these inferential dispositions differ from speaker to speaker, meaning is ultimately idiolectal; no two speakers ever mean exactly the same thing by a word. Brandom accepts this consequence and concludes that the idea of a shared public language is a myth. He also concludes that fully objective truth is left behind: for him, calling a sentence true is just endorsing it relative to the rest of one's beliefs, so truth amounts to coherence with a speaker's other commitments."
