I have encountered a proposal for implementing ethical reasoning in a general-purpose AI that I would like analyzed as a mechanism rather than evaluated as a moral position. The proposal runs as follows. Any registered developer, auditor, or civil-society organization may propose a normative principle (for example, "do not use a user's disclosed vulnerability to increase engagement or revenue"). The proposer posts a bond, and the principle is listed on an internal continuous market where a subsidized market maker quotes prices from a foundation's endowment. Whenever the AI faces a morally significant action, its governing engine must assemble a portfolio of listed principles that, taken together, authorize the action; the action is permitted only if the total market price of that portfolio clears a threshold set in proportion to the expected stakes. At the end of each month, a hidden set of human-annotated test cases, partly sampled from the actual action log, is used to score each principle based on how well its prescriptions matched raters' verdicts. Tokens pay out on that basis, and proposals whose scores fall below a floor are delisted and their bonds forfeited.

Treat this as an institutional mechanism: what would it actually incentivize among principle proposers, token holders, the engineers who choose the threshold and assemble portfolios, users whose actions are authorized, and the raters themselves? How would it be gamed, colluded around, or captured? What would happen with cases that fall outside the hidden test distribution, with small-minority moral views that the raters reject, or with principles that do well precisely because they are vague enough to rationalize almost anything? And which of the resulting failure modes could be fixed without abandoning the market structure, and which are inherent to it?
