I've been rewatching The Iron Giant with my roommate and we got into an argument about why the ending works on us. We both know he's a cartoon robot, which is to say he never existed, and on top of that the movie actually shows him putting himself back together in the last scene, so the grief is undercut almost as soon as it happens. And still the moment where he says goodbye and flies up to meet the missile gets us every single time. I want to write something about this as a case study in the paradox of fiction, but I don't want the usual "why do we cry at movies" essay. What seems interesting to me is that the movie is itself making a claim that the Giant is a someone and not just a something: Hogarth insists on it, Dean comes around to it, and a good chunk of the plot is the military treating him as a weapon while the audience is being trained to treat him as a person. Does the film thereby take a position in the philosophical debate about whether our emotions toward fictional characters are appropriate or rational? I mean, is it saying that what makes something a fitting object of real feeling is something like mindedness or the capacity to choose, rather than being flesh-and-blood real — and if so, does that actually help with the standard problem? I can't tell whether that dissolves the paradox or just relocates it, becuase the Giant's mindedness is itself fictional.

I'd like a reading that takes both the film and the philosophy seriously. Point me to the scenes that do the most philosophical work (the deer scene, the choice to fly up, the reassembly at the end) and say where the standard moves in the debate — make-believe emotions, quasi-fear, thought theory, whatever — map onto what the movie is doing, and where the movie resists or complicates them. Also, does the ending, by bringing him back, change what we are even sad about? I'm not sure the movie wants us to think of him as dead in the final shot, and I'd like an account of what that does to the "we feel for the fictional" question. I don't need citations or a reading list; I want the argument.
