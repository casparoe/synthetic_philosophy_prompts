A colleague and I have been going back and forth about whether the internal structures that mechanistic interpretability methods find in language models — directions in activation space, sparse-autoencoder features, circuits, linear probes — should be treated as real components of a network's computation or as convenient, purpose-relative summaries. We seem to fall into two camps. The representational realist says that, at least for robustly replicated features, interpretability is discovering something: the feature direction carves out a genuine causal structure, and a good decomposition is more like a map than an arbitrary re-parameterization. The instrumentalist says this is too quick. Superposition and polysemanticity suggest that many different dictionaries can describe the same activations almost equally well, and that the choice among them tracks our vocabulary and our downstream goals rather than a determinate fact about the network. The instrumentalist also presses on what have been called circular features, where the clean decompositions we can verify are partly shaped by the assumptions we built in.

What I would like is the document those two positions would produce after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, in the following format: first, an agreed-claims section stating the mathematical and empirical facts both sides accept about what these interpretability objects do and do not explain; second, a disagreements section in which each side's position is phrased so that its own advocates would accept the wording; third, a cruxes section that sorts out which sub-disputes are merely verbal and which are substantive. For each substantive crux, give a concrete test or observation — for instance involving sparsity penalties, cross-layer causal mediation, or features that fail to line up with any human-comprehensible concept — that both sides would agree bears on the crux, and record each side's prediction in advance. Where you think part of the disagreement is verbal, say which part and why.
