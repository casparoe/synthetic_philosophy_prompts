Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration on Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals. The two camps are as follows. The conditional-probability camp holds Adams's thesis: the probability of an indicative conditional is the corresponding conditional probability, P(if A then B) = P(B|A). On their view, Lewis's proofs show that indicatives cannot in general express truth-evaluable propositions; the triviality results are a reductio of truth-conditional semantics for the indicative. The truth-conditional camp holds that indicatives do express propositions (context-dependent, but genuine bearers of truth), and reads the triviality results the other way around: Adams's thesis is the culprit, and can survive only as a restricted assertibility norm for simple, unembedded conditionals, not as the general semantics.

Write the document these two camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Structure it as follows:

(i) Agreed claims (numbered; about 450 words). State the technical facts and phenomena both sides accept: what the triviality proofs establish, what they leave open, and which data about ordinary and embedded conditionals are secure enough for both sides to build on.

(ii) Disagreements (a table; about 300 words). For each remaining disagreement, give a neutral statement and then each side's preferred wording of its own position. End this section by saying which disagreements, if any, are merely verbal.

(iii) Cruxes and predictions (about 400 words). I take the cruxes to include at least these three, though you may add more: (a) do embedded indicatives — negations, disjunctions, and conditionals in the consequent — have stable truth-conditional readings; (b) is Adams's thesis preserved under conditionalization on the consequent and its negation, the step Lewis's first triviality proof exploits; (c) how should a semantics treat conditionals whose antecedents have probability zero. For each crux, state a concrete case or argument that both sides accept bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts about it.
