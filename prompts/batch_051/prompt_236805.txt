I've been spending too much time reading AI safety reports, and there's a pattern I can't quite stop thinking about. When a lab says a model is "honest," they usually mean it scored well on a benchmark where it's asked factual questions with checkable answers, and it didn't fabricate sources. If you ask them about "deception," they usually point to cases where the model made a literally false statement: wrong date, invented paper, denied something it knew. But that leaves out the way humans most often deceive each other — by saying only true things. You can mislead someone by leaving out the one qualification that matters, answering a nearby question as if it were the one asked, or listing three true causes while quietly omiting the most important one. A friend at a lab told me they know misleadingness is a problem but it's hard to score automatically, which made me wonder whether the philosophy is following the measurement rather than the other way around.

So what does this evaluation practice presuppose about honesty and deception, exactly? Is it actually committed to something like "honesty = asserting truths; deception = intentionally asserting falsehoods"? Or is that just a scoring shortcut, compatible with richer views where honesty is about what you lead the other person to believe and deception can be done with true statements? Does the practice quietly rule out the idea that a system can be honest while saying something false (from justified error) or deceptive while saying only what's true? And what would evals have to look like if a lab instead thought of honesty as something like "managing the user's beliefs well" — would they need to test for selective disclosure, strategic reframing, the true-but-misleading caveat, and so on? I'm less interested in whether any particular lab is wrong than in laying out the philosophy the practice presupposes and where exactly it's underdetermined.
