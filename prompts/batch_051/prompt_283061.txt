An old objection to natural selection claims it is empirically empty: if fitness is identified with actual reproductive success, then the statement that the fittest organisms are the ones that survive and reproduce reduces to the claim that the organisms that survive and reproduce are the ones that survive and reproduce. The propensity interpretation answers by saying fitness is not realized output but a probabilistic tendency of an organism, genotype, or trait to survive and reproduce in a specified environment — a tendency that actual outcomes realize only imperfectly, because chance can produce cases in which the fitter organism dies young and the less fit organism flourishes.

Treat that answer as a partially testable claim rather than as a stipulation. Design a concrete study — real or idealized, but specific about the population, the trait or genotype, the environment, and the measurements — that would distinguish a fitness propensity that earns its keep predictively and explanatorily from a post hoc redescription of observed outcomes. The design should make clear how the propensity values are estimated or fixed in advance of the outcome data used to test them, so that a favorable result cannot come trivially from fitting the same observations. Say what pattern of results would support the propensity interpretation, what pattern would undermine it, and address the worry that even a pre-specified propensity estimate is still curve-fitting under another name.

Then state precisely what even a strongly positive result would and would not settle: what it would show about the empirical content of selection talk, and what residue would remain — for instance, whether propensities are real features of organisms or merely useful summary parameters, and whether the tautology objection can be fully discharged or only relocated to choices about model selection.

End by saying how confident you are in the test you propose and in your verdict about what it would settle, and identify the single point you are least sure of.
