Give me a working definition of moral guilt—the kind that makes it appropriate for someone to be blamed, to feel remorse, or to be the proper target of resentment—that gets all seven of the following cases right.

1. A driver who knows the light is red, accelerates through it anyway, and kills a pedestrian.
2. A woman born today in a country whose government committed a genocide decades before her birth; her family arrived long afterward and neither participated nor benefited.
3. The doctrine of original sin in its strong form: every human being is born guilty of Adam's first sin, before any act of their own.
4. An heir who discovers that the fortune she inherited was built on a fraud her grandfather committed, and keeps the money.
5. A citizen of a democracy that launches an unjust war; she voted against it, pays her taxes, and protests the war in public.
6. Another citizen of the same democracy who never votes, avoids the news, and prefers not to know what is being done in his name.
7. Someone who learns that the house she has lived in for years was stolen from a family long before she bought it, and quietly stays.

I find case 1 plainly guilty and case 2 plainly not; the other five I keep deciding differently depending on which feature of the case I hold fixed. Propose the definition, run it through all seven cases, and say explicitly where it gives a verdict you are tempted to abandon. Then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict.

I'd rather not have the definition end up as seven ad hoc clauses, one per case; if it does, I want to know that the concept comes apart. Finally, tell me what would change your mind about the definition: which case is doing the most work in pressure-testing it, and where your argument is most likely to fail.
