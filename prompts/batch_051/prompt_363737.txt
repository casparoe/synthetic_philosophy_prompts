I keep running into a move in arguments that I don't have a word for, and I'd like to know if there is one, and if there isn't, then I want the boundary of the thing drawn anyway. The move goes like this. Someone cites Gödel's incompleteness theorems to settle a question that has nothing to do with formal logic. A friend of mine insists that since no sufficiently strong consistent system can prove its own consistency, no worldview—religious, political, scientific—can justify itself from the inside, so all of them are ultimately on equal footing and the honest position is a kind of relativism. Someone online says Gödel proves AI can never match humans, because any AI would be a formal system and would have a Gödel sentence it cannot see is true, while we can see it is true. A third person says Gödel shows truth outruns any finite set of rules, so no legal code can ever be complete, and judges necessarily fill the gaps with something non-rule-governed. I get that the theorems are about formal systems subject to specifc technical conditions, but the people making these arguments know that too, so "they don't understand the theorem" doesn't feel like the full diagnosis. My question: does this pattern have an established name—the export of a formal result into a domain where its premises don't apply, in a way that carries rhetorical force but not much else? If there's no standard name, what would you call it? I'm not asking just for a label; I want the carving: what exactly fails in the transfer, whether there are legitimate uses of Gödel outside mathematical logic and what makes them different, and which of my three examples (if any) actually counts as this thing and which doesn't.
