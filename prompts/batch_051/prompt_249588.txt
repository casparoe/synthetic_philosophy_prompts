I cover science and ideas for a living, which means I spend a lot of time in arguments about law and history without being a lawyer or a historian. And theres a tension I keep tripping over that I'd like someone to actually work through rather than wave at. Every modern legal system treats the ban on retroactive criminal law as close to sacred: you can't be punished for an act that wasn't a crime when you did it, and a regime that starts inventing offenses after the fact is, by that very move, a tyranny. Yet almost nobody wants to say the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals were wrong to convict Nazi and Japanese leaders for crimes against peace and crimes against humanity, even though those categories weren't clearly on the books as crimes when the acts were committed. So we seem to hold two things at once: retroactive criminal law is one of the great injustices, and some of the most important trials of the twentieth century were retroactive and also right.

I know the usual move: the acts were so monstrous that no reasonable person could have thought them lawful, so the defendants had fair warning in a moral even if not a legal sense, and the Kellogg-Briand Pact had already renounced aggressive war. But that seems to me to cut both ways. If moral enormity plus a treaty renouncing war is enough, then any powerful tribunal can punish anything it finds abhorrent enough after the fact. And Tokyo makes the problem sharper: some of the judges there dissented on exactly this ground, arguing that the whole proceeding was victors' justice dressed up as law — especially since neither tribunal so much as glanced at what the victors had done, from the firebombing of Japanese cities to the Soviet invasion of Poland. So the asymmetry isn't just between past and present law; it's between what the winners did and what the losers did.

Here's what I actually want to know: is there a principle that would say Nuremberg and Tokyo were legitimate but would still forbid a government from inventing new crimes to jail its domestic opponents? Or is the honest answer that we make an exception when the defendants are monsters and the judges are the winners, and we should just admit that? Give me the strongest justification you can, test it against a nearby case — say, a future tribunal that prosecutes leaders for crimes not yet defined — and tell me whether we should keep the asymmetry, narrow it, or give it up.
