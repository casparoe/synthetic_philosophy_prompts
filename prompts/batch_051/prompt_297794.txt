A while back I got into an argument with a friend about Newcomb's problem, and he brought up Death in Damascus as if it settled something. His claim was that Death in Damascus shows causal decision theory can't be right, because when you're the merchant in Damascus and Death has already predicted where you'll be, your choice doesn't cause the outcome — you die in Damascus if you stay and in Aleppo if you flee — so CDT says you should be indifferent between staying and fleeing. But that's absurd, he said, because obviously you should flee Damascus; at least then you don't die in Damascus, and maybe Death's prediction isn't really infallible. He also said evidential decision theory gives the same indifference, so both are useless. I didn't know how to answer at the time, but it's been bugging me. Now I'm not even sure what the right question is. Is Death in Damascus actually a counterexample to CDT, or to EDT, or is it a case where you should just accept that rational choice has nothing meaningful to say? And is my friend's assumption that you should flee even an application of decision theory, or is he smuggling in an outcome difference (dying in Damascus vs Aleppo) that the problem as stated rules out? I keep going in circles.
