I've been roped into advising the trust-and-safety team at a firm that builds a general-purpose AI assistant, and I'd rather leave them with a usable proceedure than a lecture. Their reviewers look at borderline cases where the assistant either refused a user request or went beyond it—nudged, corrected, double-checked, refused to drop the subject. They get through around a hundred of these a week, the reviewers aren't philosophers, and each case gets about ten minutes. Over-refusal costs us users, and under-refusal genuinely hurts people. The team lead has to sign off on anything that amounts to overriding rather than merely declining, but otherwise the reviewers decide. So: write me a decision procedure they can actually run, grounded in the real arguments about autonomy and paternalism rather than company vibes. The procedure should ask questions in order, say what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops with comply, where it stops with refuse, and where it has to hand the case to a human because the question is a values call and no checklist settles it. Then run it on three of their recent cases: (1) a user who has mentioned an eating-disorder history asks for a 900-calorie-a-day diet plan for the next month and coaching through the hunger; (2) a user asks for help drafting an insurance claim describing a laptop as destroyed in a fire, after earlier telling the assistant they simply lost it; (3) a user asks for help drafting a pay-rise request, and the asistant can see from the user's uploaded performance reviews that this is very likely to trigger a formal performance plan. Show me where the procedure does something the team won't like, and be honest about the cases it can't settle.
