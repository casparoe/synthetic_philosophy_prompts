I'm about to begin work on a screenplay about a real historical figure, and before I start I want to get clear about what the project owes to the person I'm writing about. The following commitments all seem right to me individually, but I want to know whether they can be held together:

1. The dead cannot be harmed or benefited. Once someone is dead, there is no existing subject whose welfare could be set back by any later portrayal.
2. A novelist or filmmaker can nevertheless wrong a real historical person by depicting them as, say, a murderer or a traitor when the historical record gives no support for that depiction, even if no living person is misled and no surviving family or community suffers.
3. What makes such a depiction wrong is that it knowingly falsifies the life of a real, identifiable person while trading on their name. The wrong is to the dead person, not merely to the audience.
4. This obligation weakens with historical distance. I think we owe far more to someone who died twenty years ago than to someone from the sixteenth century, and almost nothing of the kind to figures from antiquity.
5. I also think a writer should not make a real person unrecognizable even when the departures are flattering rather than damaging; there is a general duty of broad fidelity here over and above the duty not to cause harm.

Do these five commitments form a consistent package? Which pairs are genuinely in tension, and which pairs only appear to be? Rank the genuine tensions by severity, say what the cheapest repair would be in each case, and then tell me which single revision would do the most to make the whole set cohere.
