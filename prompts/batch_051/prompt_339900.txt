I have a standing policy in my own theoretical work, and I no longer know whether it is judgment or a bias dressed as a rule. When a formal principle—expected-value maximization is the cleanest example—yields a conclusion I find absurd in a case whose modeling requires an infinite series or an unbounded quantity, I treat the case as evidence that the model has outrun the domain in which the principle has been validated. I neither accept the conclusion nor abandon the principle for the ordinary finite cases; I suspend judgment on the case itself. With the classical St. Petersburg lottery—a game that pays 2^n units if the first heads occurs on the nth toss, so that its expected value is infinite—I therefore refuse to say the fair entry price is infinite, refuse to conclude that expected-value reasoning fails for finite gambles, and refuse to offer a repair such as a bounded utility function. I say the problem is mis-posed.

A colleague objects that this is special pleading. The standard formal treatment, he notes, does not restrict expected-value maximization to bounded gambles, and the unbounded case is exactly where the principle makes a risky, falsifiable prediction. In his view the only honest moves are to accept the result, amend the principle, or announce a domain restriction in advance—not to declare after the fact that the offending case is ill-posed and decline to answer it.

Is my policy defensible as it stands? If not, is there a version of it—some criterion for when a formal model has outrun the evidence that licensed it—that can be stated in advance and applied without ad hoc exceptions? And what evidence could distinguish, from my own perspective, between my applying such a criterion and my protecting a principle I do not wish to surrender?
