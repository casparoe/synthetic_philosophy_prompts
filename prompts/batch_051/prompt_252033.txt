I'm a PhD student in epistemology, and I'm trying to work out what I actually think about higher-order evidence — evidence about what my evidence supports, or about the reliability of my own reasoning — and about whether epistemic akrasia (holding a first-order belief while also believing that one's evidence doesn't support it) is ever rational. My verdicts on particular cases don't obviously add up, and I want to know whether there's a principle behind them or whether some have to go. Here are the cases.

1. The impaired-state test. I take a well-validated test indicating, with high probability, that I'm currently in a cognitive state that makes deductive reasoning less reliable than normal. I then work through a proof of a claim in a paper. Every step looks valid, and I can name the inference rule each step uses. Verdict: I still believe the claim, but with noticeably less confidence than before the test. Suspending outright would be an overreaction to a merely statistical fact about my state.

2. The peer's specific objection. Same proof, no test. A colleague I regard as my epistemic peer — same training, same evidence, and roughly as good at this sort of reasoning as I am — goes through the proof and tells me she is fairly confident that step 7 is invalid, though she can't yet articulate the error. I recheck step 7, and it still looks valid to me. Verdict: I suspend judgment on the claim. Her judgment about this very proof outweighs my continuing impression that step 7 is fine.

These first two verdicts sit uneasily together. In both cases I have higher-order evidence that my reasoning has gone wrong, and in both cases the reasoning still looks good from the inside. Why defer in one case but not the other?

3. The topic-specific base rate. I'm evaluating an argument against a policy I've publicly supported for years. I learn from a rigorous study that people with my political background are markedly overconfident when evaluating arguments in this exact policy area, even when they're trying to be fair. I evaluate the argument carefully and find it weak. Verdict: I lower my confidence in that assessment somewhat, but I don't adopt the base rate as my own judgment. I go with my assessment and ask someone I trust to check it.

4. The automated proof-checker. I run the same proof through a reliable automated proof-checker, and it reports an error in line 12. I can't see the error myself, and line 12 still looks valid to me. Verdict: I drop the claim, or at least treat it as very likely false, even though I can't locate the flaw.

5. The targeted-impairment drug. A trustworthy source tells me I've been given a drug that specifically degrades my ability to evaluate modus tollens arguments. It leaves other inference patterns alone and leaves me feeling fine. I then evaluate a modus tollens argument and find it valid. Verdict: I'm not sure what to think. Part of me says this is just Case 1 again; part of me says it is closer to Case 4, since the higher-order evidence concerns the very kind of reasoning I'm doing. I keep going back and forth.

Is there a principle — or a small set of candidate principles — stated precisely, that captures my settled verdicts and tells me what to say about Case 5? Which of my settled verdicts, if any, does it force me to give up? If no principle captures all of them, which should I abandon and why? Please also state the principle's verdict on a further case: I later learn that the proof-checker in Case 4 was miscalibrated that day and had a high false-positive rate for errors in this kind of proof, though I don't know whether the particular report was a false positive. Finally, say what argument or consideration would most likely lead you to change your mind about the principle you propose, and where you take the reasoning to be most fragile.
