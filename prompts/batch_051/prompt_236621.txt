I have just become the research editor of a general-interest magazine that runs a great deal of long-form history, much of it about antiquity and the early medieval world. Our fact-checkers are bright generalists but not historians, and a typical claim about the distant past gets about twenty minutes of checking against whatever is freely available online before a section editor decides whether it runs. We are repeatedly burned by sentences like "the city was destroyed by fire in the same year the king died," "he was poisoned by his brother," "the population may have reached half a million," and "the defeated general fled into exile." This comes up several times a week; the stakes are corrections, reader trust, and occasional legal exposure when a piece mishandles contested cultural-heritage claims. I want a reusable procedure so decisions stop depending on who is on shift and how confident they sound.

Please write a step-by-step verification procedure for any factual sentence about the more distant past, grounded in what source criticism actually teaches. It should push beyond the crude primary/secondary rule. It should make a fact-checker ask, in order: What kind of claim is it — an event, a motive, a quantity, a causal claim? What is the nearest surviving source, and how many steps of transmission separate it from the event? Is that source best read as a witness to what it reports, or as evidence of its own time and purposes? How close in time and place to the events does it stand? Is there independent attestation, or merely several copies of one stream of tradition? What kind of source is it — annal, panegyric, sacred narrative, administrative or tax record, public inscription, tragedy, biography — and what was that genre for? And when the record falls silent, is that silence telling, given what we know was systematically kept and what is likely to have survived? Phrase the gate questions so a fact-checker can answer them without prior training in historiography.

Each check should end in one of four dispositions: run as fact; run, but attribute the claim to a named source or tradition; run with an explicit hedge; or cut, with a recorded reason. The deliverable should be a flowchart in words, or a short sequence of decision gates, that a non-historian can operate within the time we have. Include a small set of practice claims for the desk to train on, and work through at least three of them, including one where the procedure forces us to remove a vivid scene on which the piece's opening paragraph depends, and one where it lets a twice-told story run as fact. Be explicit about the points where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment and a section editor has to decide. And say plainly what the procedure cannot do — for example, it cannot turn weak evidence into strong, cannot dismiss a late source merely for being late, and cannot resolve a genuine dispute between qualified historians. I am not asking for a bibliography or for advice to consult outside experts; the procedure must work for the desk we actually have.
