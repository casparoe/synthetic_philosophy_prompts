I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on an essay about why it's so strange to say that a person—or a proof assistant—"knows" everything that follows from what it knows. The setup I want to write about is the problem of logical omniscience: standard epistemic logic often treats knowledge as closed under consequence, so that at minimum, if you know P and know that P entails Q, you count as knowing Q; on the stronger version, the entailment doesn't even have to be known. But obviously none of us knows all of arithmetic just because we accept a handful of axioms, and a theorem prover doesn't "know" every theorem it would accept if you gave it enough time. I want to open the essay with a sestina that makes this tension felt rather than explained. Could you write one?

Here's the constraint I have in mind, and it's the whole point: the six end-words should come from the vocabulary of epistemic logic—knows, follows, true, all, if, consequence, world, whatever you choose—and their recurrence has to do the philosophical work. A sestina is a closed system: the same six words permuted through a fixed pattern for six stanzas and then crowded into a three-line envoy. I want the poem to use that so the reader feels the gap between a consequence being "in" the closure and somebody actually having the thought before their mind. Each stanza is just a new arrangement of the same stock, the way closure under entailment gives you nothing beyond what was already implicit in the premises; the envoy is where I'd like the strain to show, the point where having all the consequences available and knowing them occurrently come apart. It dosen't need to name-drop or teach the formalism—but a logician should be able to recognize the problem in the bones of the poem, and a general reader should still find it a good poem. I'm less interested in it resolving the tension than in it leaving the tension open, since that's honestly where my essay ends up.
