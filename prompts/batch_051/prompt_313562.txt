I'm trying to write an essay about how people decide that their political opponents aren't arguing in good faith, and I keep noticing a pattern I don't have a name for. Two examples.

Last fall I was in a long facebook argument about a local zoning change. The other guy repeated the same three complaints after I'd already answered each one. So I told him: look, your not here to update on anything, you're just dug in, this is bad faith. He said the same back to me — I hadn't moved either. And I remember thinking that was absurd, because the difference is that my points are correct, so of course I haven't moved; he hasn't moved because he's dug in, I haven't moved because the evidence hasn't shifted. But I couldn't say, from the outside, what distinguishes my case from his.

Then a similar thing happened with a friend who watches a lot of cable news. She said the other side's pundits are obviously performing, they don't believe any of it, they're paid to say it. I asked how she knows. She said: because no one could hold those positions after hearing the counterarguments. And I realized that's the same move I'd made, but from the inside it felt completely different.

Here's the part I can't name. Whether I count someone's refusal to change their mind as evidence of bad faith seems to depend entirely on whether I already think their view is false. If I think their position is false, then holding it in the face of counterarguments looks like not reasoning. If I think my position is true, then holding it in the face of counterarguments looks like being right. So the evidence for "they're arguing in bad faith" is indistinguishable from the evidence for "we disagree," and the only thing deciding the reading is the very disagreement it's supposed to explain. That means there's no observation I could make that would show me I'm doing the thing, since every observation gets read through whichever side's standard. It's not just a double standard — I can catch a double standard and correct it. This is more like a self-sealing loop.

Does this configuration have a name? I don't mean generic labels like partisanship or bias, and plain ad hominem only names the insult, not the loop. If there's an established concept, how exactly does the described thing differ from the usual nearby suspects — like false consensus, the fundamental attribution error, or naive cynicism — and which one fits best? If nothing quite fits, what would you call it, and where are its boundaries: can one side ever genuinely be arguing in bad faith while the other isn't, and could this pattern even tell the difference?

You're a researcher who works on political psychology and the epistemology of disagreement. Please brainstorm candidate concepts first, rule out the near misses, and don't just hand me a label — I care about the carving: what the phenomenon is, what it isn't, and where its edges are.
