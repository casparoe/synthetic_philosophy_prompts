A friend in my decision-theory reading group kept insisting that risk-weighted expected utility isn't a real rival to expected utility theory, just EU with the probabilities shuffled around and the differences papered over. He laid out the argument like this, and I want to know whether it goes through:

(1) For any gamble, REU evaluates it as a weighted average of the utilities of the outcomes, with the decision weights obtained by applying the risk function to cumulative probabilities; these weights are always nonnegative and sum to one.
(2) A nonnegative weighting of the outcome utilities that sums to one is exactly what an ordinary expected-utility calculation does when you treat the weights as probabilities.
(3) So for each gamble, REU's value coincides with the expected-utility value of that same gamble under some probability assignment over its outcomes.
(4) But then REU and expected utility assign every gamble the same value; and two rules that assign every gamble the same value must make the same pairwise comparisons between gambles.
(5) Therefore REU must make exactly the choices EU makes, so it can't actually permit the Allais-type preferences it was designed to permit. Since that was the whole point of the machinery, REU is empty.

I have a feeling the slip is somewhere in the middle, but I cant pin it down, and a couple people in the group found it convincing. Is the argument valid, and if not, which step fails? Answer as exactly five numbered points, one per step, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
