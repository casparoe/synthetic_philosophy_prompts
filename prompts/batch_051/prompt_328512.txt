I am trying to settle what I actually think about whether randomness can be a property of a finite binary string, and I want to know what principle, if any, my case-by-case verdicts add up to. Throughout, take "a short program" to mean a program in some fixed general-purpose programming language that is much shorter than the string it outputs.

Here are my verdicts.

1. 000000000000000000000000 — not random.

2. 010101010101010101010101 — not random.

3. 011010011001011010010110 — not random. It is the first 24 terms of the Thue–Morse sequence, where the nth bit is 1 when the binary representation of n contains an odd number of 1s, and 0 otherwise.

4. 0000100110101111 — not random. This one troubles me, because the string is balanced in a very strong sense: read cyclically, every one of the sixteen four-bit words from 0000 to 1111 occurs exactly once, so each four-bit block has exactly the frequency (1/16) that a fair coin gives it in expectation.

5. 010110111000110101001011 — random, or at least I want to call it random; I got it by flipping a coin 24 times.

6. 010110111000110101001011 again, but this time I learn that it was actually printed by a short deterministic program and that I had merely misremembered the flips. My verdict now: not random, or at least I am no longer willing to call it random — even though nothing about the string itself has changed.

Is there a single account — or a small set of candidates, if no single one will do — of when a finite string is random that delivers all six verdicts? If not, which of my verdicts should I overturn, and why? Where your principle has parts, say which of my cases motivates each part, and be explicit about any of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.

Then apply the account to two cases I have not listed. (a) A 32-bit string produced by a seeded pseudo-random generator: it looks patternless to me and passes every statistical test I run, but I know the algorithm and the seed. (b) A fair coin that happens to produce 000000000000000000000000.

Finally, say whether the resulting account locates randomness in the string itself, in the process that produced it, or in some relation between the two, and what it implies about the question "can a finite sequence be random?" — in particular, whether any answer has to be relativized to a length, a threshold, or a class of admissible descriptions.
