I recently moved into a role building causal models for a subscription business and have found myself in the middle of a running argument between two colleagues that I suspect is a philosophical disagreement in disguise. One insists that all our data can ever show is association and regularity; he says that when we speak of mechanisms, interventions, or causal effects, we are only ever speaking of patterns that held under the conditions we happened to observe, and anything beyond that is untestable decoration. The other treats the fitted structural equations as if they name real processes in the world—real ways the churn rate responds to price changes—and dismisses the first view as a failure to understand what identification buys you.

I have, perhaps recklessly, started reading some philosophy around Hume's regularity theory of causation, the idea of constant conjunction, the claim that we never observe a necessary connection between cause and effect, and the so-called New Hume interpretation, on which Hume should be read as a skeptical realist who thinks causal power is real but hidden behind the regularities. I can see that this maps onto my colleagues' argument, but I cannot make the mapping precise.

I would like you to translate the main positions in this debate into the vocabulary I actually work in and am fluent in: structural causal models, structural equations and mechanisms, the do-operator, potential outcomes, identifiability, counterfactuals, and invariance across environments. Treat me as competent in the technical material but without any formal training in philosophy. For each position—the regularity theory, the New Hume or skeptical-realist reading, and the projectionist view that the mind supplies the necessity—restate it as faithfully as you can in those terms, marking what translates cleanly, what can only be expressed with distortion, and what cannot be said in that vocabulary at all. Then give the residue report: which elements of the philosophical debate have no counterpart in the modeling vocabulary, and whether that residue reflects a limitation in the framework, a limitation of the original position, or a sign that the debate was partly about something other than what I take it to be about. I want to know which of my colleagues is closer to a defensible view, and whether my own intuition—that identifying a causal effect is a different and more substantive achievement than estimating a conditional distribution—is itself one of these positions and, if so, which one.
