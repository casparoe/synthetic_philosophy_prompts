I sit on the grants committee of a private conservation foundation that funds island-restoration projects. At next month's meeting we must vote on an application to fund the first field evaluation of a CRISPR-based suppression gene drive targeting invasive Norway rats on a roughly 1,000-acre uninhabited island that hosts the largest remaining breeding colonies of two endemic burrowing seabird species. I'm a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, so I've been thinking about the decision in terms of expected value, option value, and the reversal test, and I'd like the memo to reflect that cast of mind without turning into an essay about rationality.

For the past decade the island has been managed with an annual aerial baiting program using the anticoagulant brodifacoum. The program suppresses the rat population long enough for most chicks to fledge in a typical year, but it never achieves eradication: rats rebound each winter from a small remnant, a few native scavenging birds die of secondary poisoning each season, and the cost keeps rising. The applicant consortium proposes instead to release a limited number of rats carrying a construct that biases inheritance so that offspring are overwhelmingly male, which is predicted to cause the population to crash within two or three years and leave the island rat-free. Baiting would continue only until monitoring confirms eradication, then stop.

The central difficulty is that the construct cannot realistically be recalled once released. There is a small but not cleanly quantifiable probability that rats carrying it leave the island on fishing gear or floating debris and reach other islands, or in principle a mainland, where rats are ecologically embedded; if the drive established in such a population, its effects would be effectively irreversible. The applicants emphasize that the drive is species-specific and that the island's rats are an introduced population of no conservation value, but several committee members keep making a point I find forceful: a field evaluation of a self-propagating drive is not a trial, because the first release is the whole intervention.

At the same time, I keep noticing an asymmetry in how the committee applies the concept of irreversibility. The status quo is not neutral. The baiting program is itself an ongoing ecological intervention with cumulative nontarget mortality and no institutional endpoint, and deferral does not preserve a clean option: the seabird population is declining toward extirpation, and other organizations have signaled that they may pursue a gene-drive release elsewhere within the decade, after which our decision will have been made for us. I also suspect some status-quo bias is at work: the committee tolerates a decade of accumulating, difficult-to-reverse poison effects while treating the drive as uniquely irreversible because it is novel, discrete, and self-propagating. I want the memo to take that suspicion seriously rather than dismiss it or treat it as settled.

I lean toward approving the grant with conditions, but I want the memo to build the strongest case for rejection or deferral before setting it aside. The memo should be roughly 1,000 words and should be something the committee can vote on: a clear bottom-line recommendation (fund, fund with conditions, or reject), the reasoning behind it, the concrete conditions or milestones if any, and a serious engagement with the strongest consideration on the losing side. If the memo invokes the precautionary principle, option value, or reversibility, it should say precisely which options exist, for whom, and under which scenarios they are opened or closed; those terms should do analytic work rather than serve as applause lights for a conclusion already reached. Please write the memo in American English.
