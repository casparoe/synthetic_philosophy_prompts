i'm in a course this term on value alignment / learning what people want from what they do, and we've spent a few weeks on inverse reinforcement learning. the pitch is roughly: don't ask people what they want, watch them choose, assume their behavior is (near-)optimal for some reward function, and then solve for the reward function that best rationalizes the behavior. i find the theory genuinely compelling — more then i expected to. it turns "infer preferences from behavior" from a slogan into a well-posed computation, it handles noisily suboptimal behavior by softening the optimality assumption rather than throwing it out, and it gives a clean story for why watching someone act can tell you things they wouldn't or couldn't articulate. even the fact that the reward is underdetermined — loads of reward functions make the same policy optimal — doesn't bother me much, because the standard fixes (max-entropy, penalizing complicated rewards, feature matching) sound like what any inference worth doing has to do anyway: pick the least committal or simplest explanation.

but i have an intuition i can't shake. the cases where i actually learn the most about what someone values tend to be the ones were their behavior and their preferences are pulling apart rather than running together: the friend who eats the dish she hates to be polite, the uncle who keeps gambling even though he says he wants to stop, the person who caves to social pressure and then quietly regrets it, the person who is confused and does exactly what they didn't want. IRL as i understand it has to redescribe these as optimal-given-the-real-reward — the politeness was the all-things-considered preference, or the addict's real reward is the next fix no matter what he says. that move feels too easy. it makes revealed preference unfalsifiable, and it hides a judgment about which behaviors are preference-revealing rather than evidence of constraint, compulsion, confusion, or fear. my intuition is that behavior doesn't wear its motivational status on its sleeve: sometimes action reveals preference, sometimes it reveals a preference system being thwarted, and you can't tell which from the outside without already having a theory of the person.

so here's what i want. don't just defend IRL against my intuition and don't just tell me to trust the intuition. treat it as a negotiation: what would it take for my intuition to be the thing that goes, i.e. what would a successful debunking of it actually have to show? and what would it take for the IRL picture to give ground — can the theory stay intact if it treats the "which behaviors count as preference-revealing" filter as an explicit, separately justified part of the model, or does that concession already change what the theory is claiming? is there a stable middle reading where IRL is a fine way of recovering the preferences that rationalize a choice set, but it's a mistake to call those the person's actual values, with the person-level story doing independent work? i want you to actually reach a verdict at the end about which move i should make and what i'd be paying for it. and if the answer is "it depends on the use case," tell me exactly what it depends on, because that's the part nobody in the room is saying out loud.
