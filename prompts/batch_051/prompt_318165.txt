I'm an engineer on a team building a content moderation classifier, and I keep bumping into something that might be a philosophy question. Our model was trained on a huge corpus of natural language; it wasn't given a list of rights or a rulebook, but it has read an enormous amount of human text, so it has absorbed the way people talk about rights and justice. When we show it a new post, it often says things like "this violates the user's right to X" with very high confidence. But when we ask it why, it gives fluent justifications that don't always match any principle we can reconstruct from the model weights.

I was reading Mill's Utilitarianism, Chapter V, and hit this passage:

"In the case of this, as of our other moral sentiments, there is no necessary connexion between the question of its origin, and that of its binding force. That a feeling is bestowed on us by Nature, does not necessarily legitimate all its promptings. The feeling of justice might be a peculiar instinct, and might yet require, like our other instincts, to be controlled and enlightened by a higher reason."

Suppose we treat the model's learned "feeling of justice" as a stand-in for the natural instinct Mill is talking about. That seems like a case he didn't foresee, but the form of the claim is similar: the origin of the model's sense of justice is just statistical exposure to human text, so its binding force on us—or on the model's own classifications—is an open question. I want to know how far we can push his point before it breaks. Is it fair to say that we should not treat the classifier's rights verdicts as legitimate just because they are produced with high confidence, and that we need some separate "higher reason" to check them against? Or does Mill's argument only make sense for creatures with actual feelings, so applying it to a model is an analogy that misleads? And what would it mean, operationally, for a model to have a "feeling of justice" that is "controlled and enlightened" by a higher reason rather than merely overridden by a human reviewer?
