I've been volunteering with a mountain search-and-rescue team for a few years, mostly running the map while other people do the actual hiking. Last month we were looking for a hiker whose emergency beacon kept getting garbled by the weather, and the only fragment the software could decode had an if-then shape: if she's in the north valley, then she is above three thousand feet. The search area splits into two valleys, north and south, and each of those has an upper rocky section and a lower meadow section. Before the ping came through we treated the four sections as equally likely, since we had nothing better. The north valley's lower meadow is the one part of the whole area that sits below three thousand feet, so the decoded message basically tells us she isn't in "north valley plus low meadow."

My friend who runs the computer says we should just condition on that sentence, and when he does, her chance of being in the north valley at all drops from 50% to about 33%, so he wants to shift people south. That feels wrong to me and I can't quite say why. The sentence is an if-then about the north valley; it feels like it should only tell us that *if* she's up north, we should look in the high section, not give us evidence that the north valley itself is less likely. Me and my friend keep going back and forth: I say the software is treating a conditional as a flat fact and accidentally making "she's somewhere else" look more probable, he says my gut is just refusing to update. Is there a principled way to say when an if-then piece of information should move your confidence about the if-part itself? Or is he right that a rational update really does push us south?
