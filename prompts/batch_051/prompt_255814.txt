I have a set of verdicts about when reflective equilibrium is being done well as a method, and I want to know whether a single principle captures all of them, or whether some of my verdicts have to go.

Here are the cases.

(1) Someone accepts the principle that promises generate obligations. Asked about a casual promise to attend a friend's party, they initially judge that it is permissible to break it because no serious harm would follow. On noticing that their principle contains no harm threshold, they revise this judgment and conclude that the promise is binding. I regard this as the method working properly: the judgment yields to the principle.

(2) Someone accepts the principle that lying is always wrong. Confronted with the case of deceiving a would-be murderer, they reply that it is not lying because the murderer has no right to the truth. Confronted with misleading advertising, they reply that it is not lying because nothing is asserted. Confronted with a polite falsehood, they reply that it is not lying because nobody is deceived. I regard this as gerrymandering the concept to preserve the principle, not reflective equilibrium. But I cannot state exactly why (2) differs from (1), since both involve adjusting something to keep a principle intact.

(3) Someone initially judges that a paternalistic public-health measure is acceptable because it protects people from their own poor choices. They then come to accept a principle, supported by a broader theory about respect for persons, that competent adults may not be treated merely as means to their own welfare. Seeing the conflict, they reject their initial judgment about the measure. I regard this as legitimate: a widely shared and confidently held judgment can be overturned by a principle, particularly when the principle is supported by background theory.

(4) But I also think the method needs anchors. Someone declares that they are willing to revise any judgment whatsoever to fit their favored principle, including the judgments that gratuitous suffering is bad and that persons have moral standing. I regard this not as reflective equilibrium but as the protection of a theory by a person who has, in effect, decided the outcome in advance. Yet I cannot say which judgments are fixed points, or why those rather than others.

(5) I think the method is person-relative. Two people can arrive at different, internally coherent equilibria because their initial considered judgments differ, and neither need have made a methodological mistake. Reflective equilibrium does not promise convergence.

(6) But I also think that not every equilibrium is justified. Someone who reaches a stable fit between a principle and their judgments while considering only cases that support the principle, and who declines to consider alternative principles or background theories, has not thereby gained justification. I want to distinguish a merely stable equilibrium from one that actually confers justification.

Is there one account of reflective equilibrium as a method that delivers all six of these verdicts? If not, which verdicts should I overturn, and why? Please state the resulting principle, or small set of candidate principles, precisely, indicating which clause is motivated by which case, and say what it predicts for a case I have not listed: someone who begins with a fixed conclusion about a policy, then adjusts principles and judgments until everything coheres with that conclusion, while insisting throughout that they are in reflective equilibrium. Also say how your principle would classify a person who responds to every conflict by revising the principle rather than the judgment, until the principle has become too vague to conflict with anything.
