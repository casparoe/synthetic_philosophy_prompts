I'm designing a thought experiment about epistemic institutions for a course on social epistemology. Consider the following criterion, which draws on David Christensen's argument in his 2007 paper "Epistemology of Disagreement: The Good News." Christensen gives the example of you and a friend, both competent at arithmetic and both looking at the same restaurant bill and tip, who independently arrive at different figures for one person's share. He argues that you should become less confident in your answer as soon as you learn that your friend disagrees, even before you locate any arithmetic mistake. For present purposes, accept a rough conciliationist criterion: when you learn that an epistemic peer disagrees with you about a proposition, and you have both considered the same evidence with comparable care, you should move your credence toward theirs, absent special reason to discount them.

Now suppose a research funder proposes the following mechanism. A conciliation panel is formed for each disputed empirical question in a field. The panel identifies any two researchers who (i) are certified as epistemic peers by the panel's own calibration data, (ii) have independently reviewed the same evidence, and (iii) report credences about the same proposition that differ by more than 10 percentage points. Each researcher then privately submits a pre-disclosure credence, after which the two are told only that they disagree. Each then submits a post-disclosure credence. If their post-disclosure credences still differ by more than 10 points, the panel publishes, as the community's official credence, the simple average of the two original pre-disclosure credences. The original pre-disclosure reports are destroyed, and the panel will not say whose credence moved. The panel's official credence is then used by the funder as the prior for all subsequent meta-analyses, and researchers who publicly defend a credence more than 10 points from the official one lose their peer certification for one year.

Walk through this mechanism carefully. What does it incentivize among researchers before the panel, during the private submissions, and after publication? Where does it get gamed first? Does it tend to select for honest belief aggregation, for strategic positioning, or for something else? What happens to genuinely new minority views, and does the threat of losing peer certification have predictable epistemic costs? Finally, identify the most likely failure mode and say whether any small design change could fix it without reintroducing the original problem the mechanism was meant to solve.

Finish with a one-sentence summary of your overall assessment.
