I am a novelist. In the novel I am drafting, a marine ecologist is charged under a state endangered-species statute whose operative clause provides: “It is unlawful to take, possess, transport, or destroy any individual animal of a listed species, and each animal so taken, possessed, transported, or destroyed constitutes a separate violation.” The drafters plainly imagined a countable, single-bodied animal: one goshawk, one otter, one turtle. The act at issue, however, is this. The defendant collected a single Portuguese man o’ war—a colonial siphonophore composed of hundreds of physiologically connected zooids, each specialized and unable to survive alone, but each homologous to a whole polyp or jellyfish. It died in the collecting bucket. The penalty provisions make the question determinative: she faces either one count or several hundred. I am less interested in what a court would actually decide than in what it would take for the phrase “individual animal” to reach this creature, and in what the text, the statute’s evident purpose, what the drafters would have said if the question had been put to them, and what a reasonable reader takes the words to mean each support. Please answer in American English.
