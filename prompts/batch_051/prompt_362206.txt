My town has a recurring, dumb problem. People are constantly making informal promises to each other — I'll fix your fence before winter if you split your firewood with me, or I'll lend you my truck now and you'll give me half the harvest — and then somebody dies before holding up their end of the deal. The survivor just eats the loss and it makes everyone bitter. So the council is proposing an ordinance: for exactly one year after a person dies, the town will treat them as if they were still alive and still bound by their promises. The estate gets a "proxy" (an heir, or someone the council appoints if the heirs fight), who can collect what the dead person was owed and must pay what they owed, but only out of the estate's assets. The proxy is supposed to work out "what the deceased would have wanted" from their texts, a one-page statement they filled out, and whatever family members will say. The preamble goes out of its way to admit this is a legal fiction: everyone knows the person is dead, the law is just going to pretend otherwise for this limited purpose.

I have to speak at the council meeting in two weeks, and I don't want to argue about whether it's ghoulish. I want to know what actually happens if it passes: who shows up to claim a promise, how heirs and proxies game it, what it does to who people pick as their proxy, whether neighbors stop making promises at all, and where the pretense does real work versus where it's just moving losses from one neighbor to another. Walk me through how this would play out.
