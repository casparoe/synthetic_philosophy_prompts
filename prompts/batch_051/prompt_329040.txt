I cover constitutional theory and the behavioral sciences for a general-interest magazine, and I am working through a view that is treated in much of that literature as almost settled: that constitutional devices—entrenched bills of rights, supermajority rules, an independent judiciary—are best understood as a society's version of a Ulysses contract. On this view, a polity that entrenches a bill of rights is doing what Ulysses did when he had himself bound to the mast: it is one and the same agent, at time one, binding its own future agency against a predictable failure of judgment, so that the considered commitments of the polity survive the panic or the temporary appetite of the moment. The polity both ties the ropes and assigns the sailors.

I want you to play this position straight and then map exactly where it could give way. What would have to be true for the position to be false, or at least misleading enough that constitutional theory should stop treating it as the default description of what a constitution is doing? Work through the assumptions the analogy needs—that binder and bound are the same agent in the relevant sense; that the future episode really is a lapse rather than a legitimate collective change of mind; that the precommitment mechanism is likelier to encode the polity's considered judgment than the interests or errors of those who write and enforce it; that the danger is temporary and the bonds are revisable once it passes; and whatever else you identify. For each assumption, say what would have to fail, in the world or in the argument, for the position to be undermined, and which of those failures are live in actual constitutional orders rather than merely logically possible. Distinguish objections that would be decisive from those that would merely force defenders to qualify the view. Include the concessions a sophisticated defender should themselves make even while retaining the position. Then give your honest assessment of how exposed the position actually is—and if it turns out to be well insulated, say whether that insulation speaks well of the view or poorly of the way it is typically defended.

Please organize your response in titled sections, in this order:

1. The position stated (about 120 words): restate the view in its strongest, least straw-manned form, including what it would lead one to expect of constitutional constraints.
2. Failure points (about 400 words): take the assumptions of the analogy one by one and specify what would have to be true for each to fail, and what a constitutional order would look like if it did.
3. Decisive versus awkward pressure (about 250 words): separate objections that would refute or seriously wound the position from those that would only require qualification.
4. What defenders should concede (about 150 words): the admissions a proponent could make without giving up the core analogy, and whether they are cheap or costly.
5. Bottom line (about 150 words): state how exposed the position is, and whether its resilience is a feature or a problem.
