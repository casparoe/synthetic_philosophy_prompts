So I got into an argument in my gov club that I can't quite get out of, and it feels like the reasoning basicaly proves too much. My friend Sam was defending term limits for Congress, and he had this whole thing about career politicians being the root problem. I pushed him on it, and by the end we'd landed somewhere neither of us actually wants to be. Here's the cleaned-up chain:

(1) The whole point of term limits is that career politicians get entrenched: they build donor networks, lose touch with normal life, and start serving themselves instead of the public.
(2) If that's the problem, then the strength of a fix depends on how much it breaks up the career-politician class — the more it reduces careerism and entrenchment, the more justified the fix is.
(3) Sortition (picking the legislature by random citizen lot, like jury duty) would reduce careerism and entrenchment way more than term limits, because the people picked never become politicians at all.
(4) So if term limits are justified, then replacing elections with sortition is justified too — actually even more justified.
(5) But replacing elections with a lottery is absurd. Almost nobody thinks we should scrap voting and pick representatives by lotto.
(6) So the reasoning for term limits proves too much, and term limits must be unjustified after all. Unless (5) is wrong, which would be a whole different problem.

I really want to keep (1) and (5). Sam says that forces me to reject either (2) or (3), but I can't tell which step is actually the bad one, and neither of us can say what rejecting it would cost. Which step goes wrong, and why did it look so right on the way in?
