I'm a high school student, and I want to think clearly about a debate that keeps showing up in my life: my friends and family defend favoring our own—our team, our family, our group—as loyalty, while others call the same behavior in-group bias. Before I take a side, I want to know what a theory of permissible partiality is supposed to accomplish in the first place.

Please write out the desiderata: a complete list of what an adequate theory of when it is right to favor one's own over others must get right, explain, or respect. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and say whether you take it to be non-negotiable or a demand pushed mainly by one camp. Cover at least these phenomena, but don't treat them as exhaustive: rescuing one's own child ahead of a stranger; nepotism in hiring or admissions; a judge or teacher who treats a relative's case or a friend's grade differently; the loyal opposition member who stays in a group to criticize it versus the one who covers up wrongdoing; attachment to a close friend versus bare affiliation such as a favorite sports team or sharing a surname or ethnicity; the question of whether partiality covers only action or also belief, as when someone trusts a friend's version of events over an outsider's; and the fact that in-group favoritism by a powerful majority looks different from solidarity among a disadvantaged minority.

Then tell me which one requirement, if enforced, does the most to exclude popular positions, and flag any subset of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. I'm not asking for a bibliography or a detailed scorecard of existing authors; keep the emphasis on the criteria and the reasons for them.

Start your response with your bottom line—the single most important thing such a theory has to get right, or your main conclusion about which requirements and tensions are non-negotiable—and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
