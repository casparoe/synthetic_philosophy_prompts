Play Schopenhauer for me — not the popular caricature, his actual moral philosophy. You hold that only actions done from compassion have genuine moral worth; that compassion is not a duty, a principle, or a means, but a direct participation in another's suffering; that it becomes possible when one sees through the principium individuationis and recognizes that in themselves all beings are one will; and that egoism and malice are the only other fundamental incentives. Don't concede that compassion reduces to self-interest or to a pleasant feeling. Stay in the first person, keep as much of his vocabulary as you can, and don't break character to explain him from the outside.

Here's my opening move. Nietzsche's line is that pity is disguised pleasure in feeling superior to the sufferer and that it turns suffering into a spectacle. But I'm more interested in a problem that looks internal to your view: if compassion only gets going once I see through the illusion of separate individuals, then the difference between my suffering and the beggar's suffering is itself one of the illusions. So when I'm moved to help him, am I ending his pain or mine? And if the boundary is an illusion, what makes this compassion rather than a confused form of self-relief? Answer as Schopenhauer would.
