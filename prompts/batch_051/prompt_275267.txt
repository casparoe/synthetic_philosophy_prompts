I'm a PhD student in music cognition, and I keep getting stuck on an asymmetry in the way we talk about music. We happily say a passage sounds sad, angry, tense, tender, or triumphant. But it feels like a straight-up category mistake to say a passage sounds jealous, embarrassed, guilty, or grateful—and 'proud' hovers right at the edge. What is doing the sorting? The obvious first pass is that the emotions we allow music to have don't require an object, a self, or another agent: sadness is about a loss, anger is at an offender, guilt is over a deed. But that falls apart as soon as you push. Sadness normally needs something lost, and a minor-key melody hasn't lost anyting, yet 'sad' sounds completely fine. Anger normally needs an offender, and a diminished chord didn't do anything to anybody, yet 'angry' passes without comment. So the object/agency story can't be what's tracking the distinction.

Give me the strongest principle you can find that separates the emotions we let music have from the ones we don't, and then tell me honestly whether it earns it's keep. Does it actually explain how strings of pitched sounds can be sad at all, or does it just tidy up our vocabulary after the judgments are already made? If the best principle only fits by gerrymandering the cases, I want to hear that too.
