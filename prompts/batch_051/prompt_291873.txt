You are a political philosopher who works on distributive justice and the ethics of risk. Please think carefully, start by brainstorming a couple of ways a committed luck egalitarian could respond, and write your answer in British English.

Dworkin-style luck egalitarians draw a line between brute luck and option luck. Brute luck is what lands on you without any choice: your genes, the family you were born into, a tree falling on your parked car. Option luck is the deliberate gamble: you open a restaurant, it goes under, and you eat the loss. The core thought is that inequalities that trace to brute luck are the ones society should compensate, while inequalities that trace to option luck can in principle be left where they fall, because the person chose the risk. The well-known objection to this is the abandonment objection: someone who voluntarily takes a risk and ends up ruined seems to be told by the view that the whole disaster is theirs and they're owed nothing, which can look gratuitously cruel. The standard luck-egalitarian reply is that a risk only counts as option luck if the person had adequate opportunity to foresee it and to insure against it, so the view doesn't actually abandon people who couldn't have known or hedged.

Here's the development I want you to think through. Suppose personalised, algorithmic risk scores become cheap and ubiquitous. Before making a choice, a person can be shown a fairly accurate, individually tailored probability that a bad outcome will follow — job loss, illness, an accident — based on data from millions of people with a similar profile. But the scores are produced by black-box models: nobody, including the scored person, can explain why the number is what it is; the score is often wrong in the individual case (it tells you "a 23 percent chance" and can't tell you wether your the unlucky one); and the institutions using the scores understand them much better than the people being scored. An employer might not hire someone after seeing a high risk score, and the state might later treat a bad outcome as something the person had "chosen".

What I'd like you to work out is whether the luck-egalitarian argument survives this development, needs repairing, or whether the brute luck/option luck distinction itself starts to fray. Does the option-luck category require not only that risk information was available but also that it was reasonably comprehensible and something the person could genuinely act on as an agent? Can someone be said to have chosen a risk when they were shown a probability they couldn't interpret, from a model they couldn't interrogate? If the distinction frays, what should the view become — a view about the fairness of risk-management institutions rather than about which risks individuals own? Try to identify the exact premise the development bears on, say how a defender would repair the view, and then assess whether the repair actually works or just relocates the problem.
