I’m a PhD student in political philosophy, and I keep going back and forth on how to read Mill’s attack on appeals to “nature” in The Subjection of Women. It seems to me that there are two incompatible readings, and they lead to pretty different feminist positions. I’d like a dialogue between two characters who each defend one of these readings. Use this passage from Chapter 1 as the anchor — John Stuart Mill, The Subjection of Women, Chapter 1:

“So long as an opinion is strongly rooted in the feelings, it gains rather than loses in stability by having a preponderating weight of argument against it. For if it were accepted as a result of argument, the refutation of the argument might shake the solidity of the conviction; but when it rests solely on feeling, the worse it fares in argumentative contest, the more persuaded its adherents are that their feeling must have some deeper ground, which the arguments do not reach; and while the feeling remains, it is always throwing up fresh intrenchments of argument to repair any breach made in the old.”

The two readings I have in mind are roughly: (1) Mill’s point is epistemic — we can’t safely infer women’s nature from how women behave under subjection, because the conditions are artificial, so the appeal to nature, while possibly coherent, is just ungrounded. (2) Mill’s point is more radical — nature-talk in this context is not merely ungrounded but ideological: a recycled rationalization whose function is to protect entrenched feeling, and the passage above is evidence that he thinks arguments from nature are post-hoc repairs rather than genuine discoveries. Let the characters push each other on whether the passage actually supports (2), whether (1) and (2) really come apart when applied to Mill’s own examples, and whether Mill himself ends up needing some conception of human nature when he says equal liberty will allow women’s capacities to develop. Make it a real fight, no strawmen: each character should hold a strong version of their side, respond to the other’s actual moves, and not talk past each other. Please have one of them come out ahead by the end — I want a committed take, not a survey of options — and then add a short note saying which reading you think is closer to Mill and why.
