I keep going back and forth on something in the moral testimony/moral deference debate, and I don't want to be told which side wins. The view I'm drawn to is basically continuity with ordinary epistemic deference: elsewhere, if someone has thought about a question much longer and more carefully than I have, I shouldn't trust my hasty reasoning over theirs, and accepting their answer can even be a way of coming to know. I have a hard time seeing why moral questions should be the one place where that never works. There are people dramatically better at moral reasoning than I am, and my own snap moral reasoning is unreliable in predictable ways (post-hoc rationalization, status quo bias, the lot). So the tidy principle is: when the balance of epistemic considerations favors someone else's moral judgment over mine, I should defer, just as I would in science or law.

And yet I reread Kant's "What Is Enlightenment?" recently and got stuck on this passage:

"It is so easy not to be of age. If I have a book which understands for me, a pastor who has a conscience for me, a physician who decides my diet, and so forth, I need not trouble myself. I need not think, if I can only pay - others will easily undertake the irksome work for me."

It lands with me in a way I can't reconcile with the deference view. It isn't that I think other people's moral views are never useful: obviously they can shake me out of self-serving reasoning. But the "pastor who has a conscience for me" seems different from the physician who decides my diet. The physician delegates a technical judgment I can then hold on good authority; the pastor is doing something I'm not sure I can outsource, because the act of judging what I ought to do feels like its mine in a way my diet isn't. If I simply adopt someone else's verdict as my own, it feels less like I've gained moral knowledge and more like I've stopped being the person asking the question.

So which should give? What would it take for the anti-deference intuition to be the thing that goes — say, it reduces to an autonomy-flavored self-image, or to the uncontroversial thought that I shouldn't defer lazily? And what would it take for the theory to need amending — maybe testimony can supply moral premises but not the concluding judgment, or it can transmit knowledge without transmitting the right to act on it? Or is there a reading on which both survive, where I genuinely defer about the moral facts and still can't delegate the moment of "this is what I'll do"? What I want is the cost of each move, not a verdict.
