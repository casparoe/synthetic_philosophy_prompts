Analyze the 1997 Deep Blue–Kasparov match as a case study in the intellectualism/anti-intellectualism debate about knowledge-how. On the intellectualist view, knowing how to do something is a species of knowing that: the agent stands in the knowledge relation to a proposition concerning a way to do it. On the Rylean anti-intellectualist view, knowing how is a distinct practical capacity, exercised in performance and not reducible to a stock of propositions. The salient facts: Deep Blue, an IBM program, lost to Kasparov in 1996, then in May 1997 defeated him in a six-game match under regulation time controls. Its strength came from a prepared library of opening lines and endgame tables, an evaluation function, and brute-force search over hundreds of millions of positions per second; it did not possess anything like human understanding of chess.

Produce a disciplined mapping of the episode onto the theory rather than a eulogy for either the machine or the human. (1) State each view precisely enough to use it. (2) Under each view, say whether Deep Blue knows how to play chess, merely executes a procedure, or satisfies some test of know-how, and locate exactly what the difference consists in; say what counts as the knower in each analysis. (3) Identify which features of the match each view explains well—the machine's strength without understanding, its inhuman style of play, its errors of judgment, Kasparov's skilled performance even in defeat—and which features each view leaves out or misdescribes. (4) Run the analysis in the other direction: is the match evidence for intellectualism (because a system whose excellence consisted in stored, proposition-like information and search outperformed expert human skill), evidence for anti-intellectualism (because the machine lacked precisely the practical capacity it appeared to exhibit while the defeated human retained it), or a case that shows the debate needs redrawing—for instance, by distinguishing the attribution of know-how from the generation of expert performance? (5) Finally, say what the later history of chess engines, now decisively stronger than any human player, does or fails to do for the philosophical point.
