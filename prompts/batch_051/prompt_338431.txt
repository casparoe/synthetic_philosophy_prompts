Three weeks ago I was called for jury duty, and during selection I was struck by how little the system asks of the people who will decide whether a defendant loses years of freedom: they are drawn by lot, need no legal training, and need not give reasons for a verdict. Later, at a city meeting, I watched residents fiercely oppose a proposal to seat an advisory council by lot, insisting that governance belongs only to people who have stood for election and can be turned out at the next vote. Moments later, several of those speakers denounced the sitting councillors as career politicians, as though their years of experience counted against them. I shared all three reactions, but I cannot state the principle behind them. Ordinary practice treats a randomly selected citizen as fit to exercise the state's most coercive power in a jury box yet unfit to serve in a legislature, and treats long experience in politics as corrupting while treating long experience on the bench or in an operating theatre as a mark of competence. What, if anything, justifies these asymmetries? Give me the strongest principle you can find, and test it against a case in which the available elected candidates are plainly worse than a random draw. If no single principle survives, say which of the three judgments I should give up.
