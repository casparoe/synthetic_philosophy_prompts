I teach a seminar on personal identity, and last week a student brought me the membership agreement from the uploading company her aunt signed up with. We had been going through the standard gradual-replacement thought experiments, so the student noticed something about the contract I probably would have missed: it seems to assume uploading happens all at once. The clause at issue is Section 5, and it reads in full:

"Upon the Member's clinical death, the Service shall create a whole-brain emulation of the Member and instantiate that emulation on a suitable non-biological substrate, taking all reasonable measures to preserve the continuity of the Member's personal identity. Upon successful instantiation, the Member shall be deemed an Uploaded Member and shall be entitled to all rights and benefits appertaining thereto."

Now the case. The aunt is enrolled in an experimental program that replaces her neurons gradually, in small batches, over the course of about two years. At no point is she unconscious for more than a few hours, and she is never clinically dead; her memories, personality, plans, habits — the lot — just carry through the way they do across any ordinary stretch of years. By the end, her brain is entirely synthetic. There was no scan, no emulation, and no moment at which some separate software copy was "instantiated"; the old neurons were simply swapped out a few at a time while the system as a whole kept running.

She now argues that she is exactly what Section 5 was trying to produce — a continuously preserved mind running on a non-biological substrate — and she wants the company to treat her as an Uploaded Member, with the maintenance, backups, and connectivity that status carries. The company says the clause never triggered: no clinical death, no emulation, no instantiation, and therefore no reason to treat her as anything other than a biological member with a bunch of warranty-voiding implants.

We're not lawyers, and I'm not asking for legal advice. I want to know what Section 5 should be taken to require in this case, as an exercise in interpretation. Please go through each of the standard lenses: the ordinary meaning of the words, the purpose of the clause, what the drafters would have said if they had been asked about in situ gradual replacement, and what a reasonable member would have understood at signing. Where do those lenses point, where do they come apart, and which should carry the day here? Finally, bracket the contract and tell me wether the metaphysics of gradual replacement pushes one way or the other: is the aunt already an upload in every sense that matters, or does the contract's trigger condition do real work that continuity talk cannot dissolve?
