I spent my career as a physical chemist, and I have been reading metaphysics seriously only since retiring. One of the first things that struck me is that a habit of mind natural to a chemist — that what a thing is and how it behaves are settled by its composition and structure — begins to wobble the moment one asks how a clay statue is related to the lump of clay from which a potter shapes it. The literature on material constitution, as I have so far encountered it, tends to proceed case by case: one paper presses the statue and the lump, another presses a cat that loses its tail, a third presses a ship rebuilt plank by plank, and each author reaches for a different piece of machinery. Before I read any further, I want the specification first: what, exactly, must a theory of material constitution get right in order to count as adequate — adequate, that is, for a metaphysics of ordinary material things that can underwrite our ways of counting, reidentifying, and reasoning about them without quietly changing the subject?

Please write out the desiderata systematically. I want the cases any such theory must handle, with the consideration behind each; the distinctions it may not collapse, such as that between being made of the same matter and being the same object, or between spatiotemporal coincidence and numerical identity; the explanatory demands it must meet, including the worry that if two objects share all their parts and matter, nothing seems left to ground their alleged differences in kind or in what they could survive; the logical principles it must respect or explicitly explain away; and the ordinary counting and survival judgments it must preserve or give a principled reason to reject. For each desideratum, indicate whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp. Then say which subsets of these desiderata look jointly unsatisfiable — I suspect at least one, built from the indiscernibility of identicals, the apparent modal differences between the statue and the lump, and the claim that they are nevertheless one thing — and how the main families of views fare against the list: a one-object or identity view, a view on which constitution is not identity, four-dimensionalism, and some form of eliminativism or hylomorphism. Keep that scorecard brief; the criteria are what interest me. Above all, tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude candidates, and whether that requirement is genuinely mandatory or is itself a piece of philosophical prejudice.
