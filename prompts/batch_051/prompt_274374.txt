Below are my verdicts about which public statements about a person or a company should be actionable in defamation under United States law. I want to know what principle, if any, captures the pattern, what that principle says about some further cases, and which of my verdicts it would force me to abandon.

My verdicts:

1. A newspaper calls a landlord the worst in the city. I think this is not actionable, even if the landlord is, by most reasonable measures, ordinary.

2. A credit rating agency issues a report assigning a company a D rating because, it says, the company's current ratio has fallen below 0.5 and it has missed two bond payments in the past six months. If the current ratio is actually above 1.0 and no payments were missed, I think the report is actionable, even though a letter grade is an evaluation.

3. An analyst publishes a Sell rating and a twelve-month price target of $10 for a stock trading at $50. The stock later rises to $80. I think this is not actionable merely because the forecast turned out wrong, since it was presented as a predictive judgment.

4. The same analyst writes instead that the stock will be at $10 by year's end and that this is not a prediction but something he is certain of. I lean toward saying this is also not actionable, but I am uneasy about why mere certainty should change anything.

5. A food critic describes a restaurant's food as inedible and gives it one star. I think this is not actionable.

6. A professor-rating website publishes: Avoid Professor Y; his lectures are worthless. I think this is not actionable.

7. The same website publishes: Professor Y faked his teaching evaluations. I think this is actionable, because it states a concrete fact about what happened.

8. A municipal bond analyst writes that a city will default on its general-obligation debt within two years, based on contestable assumptions about the city's tax revenue. I think this is not actionable.

9. A data analytics firm gives a hospital a letter grade of F for safety and publishes no methodology and no underlying facts. The hospital says the grade is false and devastating. I lean toward saying this is protected, but I cannot say why case 9 differs from case 2, where I did find liability.

10. A think tank publishes that State University gave Professor Z a teaching award she did not merit. I am genuinely torn. It reads like an evaluation, but it also implies that her teaching was not good enough, which feels factual enough to be checked.

I can see that my verdicts roughly follow the slogan that only statements of fact can be defamatory while opinions are protected. But I cannot make that line precise. Ratings and forecasts are neither simply statements of fact nor simply expressions of taste: they can be wrong, they can be checked later, and they can do real reputational damage while hiding behind an evaluative or forward-looking form.

So my question is this: is there a defensible principle that yields most of these verdicts? Please state the principle precisely, show which of my verdicts motivate each clause of it, apply it to two cases I keep going back and forth on, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and whether I should overturn them. The two further cases are:

11. A published customer review gives a roofer one star with the comment: sloppy work and dangerous wiring.

12. A real-estate data firm states that crime near a particular apartment building will rise sharply over the next year.

Please write your answer in American English.
