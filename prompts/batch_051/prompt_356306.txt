Consider a counterfactual change in research governance. Beginning next year, every major public and philanthropic funder of animal behavior research, together with the field's leading journals, adopts an unusually strict version of the methodological rule usually associated with C. Lloyd Morgan. Under the policy, no study may attribute a cognitive state — a belief, an intention, a plan, an episodic memory — to a nonhuman animal if the same behavioral data can also be accounted for by associative learning, reinforcement history, or any other process ranked lower on the usual scale of psychological sophistication, and the investigator bears the burden of showing that no such lower-level account is available. Predict, with visible reasoning, what this does to comparative psychology, animal welfare regulation, and the building of artificial systems modeled on animal cognition over the next twenty-five years. Answer in exactly five numbered items, with no introduction or conclusion, each item one or two sentences. Each item should state one predicted effect, the mechanism that produces it, and a brief confidence judgment indicating whether the effect is robust or depends on how researchers, regulators, or engineers actually adapt. Cover at least one effect in each of the three domains named.
