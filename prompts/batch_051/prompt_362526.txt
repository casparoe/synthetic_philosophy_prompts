You are a moral and political philosopher working in applied ethics; think carefully and reason from first principles. I teach a course on paternalism, and before I present any candidate theory I want the specification such a theory must satisfy. Write the desiderata for an adequate account of when a competent adult's choices may be overridden, taxed, or steered for that adult's own good. The account must let my students evaluate real interventions: mandatory seatbelt laws, retirement-savings defaults, soda taxes, graphic warning labels, a physician withholding a grim prognosis, cooling-off periods for payday loans. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and mark which requirements are constraints on any adequate theory and which are merely commitments of particular camps. Among the distinctions I need adjudicated: hard versus soft paternalism; paternalism about ends versus about means; autonomy as sovereignty versus as competence; reversible versus irreversible interventions; and whether nudges are different in kind from mandates. Also tell me whether findings from behavioral economics — status quo bias, present bias, reactance — constrain every candidate or only welfare-based ones. Flag any pair of desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, say which single requirement does the most to exclude the familiar positions (the harm principle, libertarian paternalism, strong autonomy views), and close with a brief scorecard of those three against the list. Start by brainstorming the field of cases before you settle on the final list, and be honest about the point at which stating a requirement becomes importing a value.
