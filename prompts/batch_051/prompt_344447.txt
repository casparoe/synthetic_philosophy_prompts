I've been developing a view I call the Novelty Budget Account of the hedonic treadmill, and I'd like a candid assessment rather than encouragement. The central claim is this: the hedonic treadmill is not a defect in our capacity to enjoy stable goods; it is a consequence of the fact that affect tracks prediction error, the mismatch between expected and actual outcomes. We experience something as felt-good, on this account, only for as long as it continues to outperform expectation. Once a good is fully anticipated, its felt charge decays—not because we have failed to appreciate it, but because the contribution of novelty to that experience has been used up. Novelty is therefore not merely an instrument for refreshing our appreciation of stable goods, as in the standard advice to vary one's routine; it is a non-derivative component of affective well-being, roughly analogous to a nutrient that must be continually resupplied. A fully predictable good life, one in which every joy arrives exactly as expected, would be affectively flat, and the flatness would not be a failure of appreciation on the subject's part but the absence of the very ingredient that makes felt appreciation possible.

Examples: the music lover for whom a beloved piece slowly loses its felt charge unless she discovers new performances or layers; the long-term traveler who is not running from anything but for whom continued movement is itself the good; the retiree for whom comfort without variation begins to register as a quiet absence rather than a state of ease.

I can already see several objections. First, the account may confuse the trigger of an affective state with its content: prediction error might be what causes felt appreciation without being what the appreciation is about. Second, it may pathologize lives built on repetition, ritual, and stable attachments, which many people report as among the deepest sources of well-being. Third, it may simply restate the hedonic treadmill in computational language rather than explain it. I'd like to know whether the account is coherent as stated, whether it already exists in a more careful form under another name, and where it breaks—what it wrongly counts as impoverished, or what it fails to explain. If one of the three objections is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable, I'd rather hear that than be told the view is interesting.
