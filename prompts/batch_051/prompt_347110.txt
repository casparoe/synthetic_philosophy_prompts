I am a retired physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy seriously, and I convene a reading group of fellow retired scientists and engineers with no prior philosophical training. We have been working through Kant's Groundwork, and I am to lead the session in which the group first encounters the Formula of Universal Law. The members are at ease with multi-step deductive argument, but their instinct will be to recast any moral principle as a rule for predicting and evaluating outcomes, and they will want a procedure they can apply mechanically.

I would like a plan for teaching the universalizability test in that one ninety-minute session, not the explanation itself. Spell out the order in which the pieces must be introduced — the maxim, the act of universalizing it, and the distinction between a maxim that cannot even be conceived as universal law and one that can be conceived but not rationally willed — and make explicit what depends on what, so that the test does not present itself as a more elaborate way of asking what would happen if everyone did that. Choose the single worked case that should carry the session, and specify the features that make a case suitable here and the features that would make it a poor choice. Then discuss the misconceptions I should expect from technically trained adults who are new to philosophy — judging the maxim by its likely consequences, treating the two failure modes as differing only in how badly the resulting world would go, and the like — and say where in the sequence each is best defused before it hardens. Finally, give me a closing question that would show whether the idea has actually landed, and state what I should explicitly set aside for later rather than paper over.
