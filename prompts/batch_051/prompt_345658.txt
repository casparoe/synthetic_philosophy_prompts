I am trying to find a principled account of biological individuality that fits my case-by-case judgments, and I would like you to extract and stress-test that principle. Here are my verdicts.

1. Portuguese man o' war. I take a siphonophore of this kind to be one organism, even though it is composed of zooids that arise by budding from a single zygote and are specialized for flotation, feeding, defense, or reproduction.

2. Ant colony. I do not take the colony to be an organism, even though it shows reproductive division of labor, sterile workers, and colony-level coordination. I take each ant to be an organism and the colony to be at most a higher-level unit of selection rather than a superorganism.

3. Clonal aspen grove. Where genetically identical trunks remain connected by a common root system, I take the whole grove to be one organism, rather than a population of separate organisms corresponding to the trunks.

4. Lichen. I do not take a lichen to be an organism, even though the fungal partner and the photosynthetic partner form a structurally intimate and physiologically integrated composite. I treat it as an association of two organisms.

5. Human plus gut microbiome. I take the human to be the organism and the gut microbes to be resident symbionts rather than parts of the organism, holobiont terminology notwithstanding.

6. Dictyostelid slug. When free-living amoebae aggregate into a motile slug and then differentiate into a sterile stalk and reproductive spores, I count the slug as a temporary organism, even when the amoebae come from genetically distinct clones.

Please tell me what principle, or small set of principles, best captures these six verdicts. State the principle precisely enough that it yields definite verdicts on cases I have not listed, and apply it to at least two such cases. Then say which of my six verdicts, if any, the principle cannot accommodate, and whether a more complex principle that saves all six is worth its costs.
