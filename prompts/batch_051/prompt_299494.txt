Write a philosophical dialogue of roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words between two equally able interlocutors who disagree about what follows from Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" for the justification of deduction. One interlocutor, a regress theorist, maintains that the dialogue exposes a genuine impasse: if a rule of inference such as modus ponens must be supplied as an additional premise before an inference is licensed, then justifying any deduction requires an endless chain of further premises; but if the rule is not supplied, the inference appears to be made on no authority at all. The other interlocutor, a practice theorist, maintains that the impasse is an artifact of a category confusion: a rule of inference is not a premise at all but a norm, permission, or capacity exercised in the practice of reasoning, so the justification of deduction bottoms out in our competence with that practice rather than in any further proposition. The dialogue should be a genuine exchange in which each character responds to the other's strongest formulations, and neither side should be a strawman. Include the following beat: have the practice theorist concede that the conditional corresponding to an inference—for example, if p, and if p then q, then q—is something one can state and accept, while denying that accepting it adds a distinct entitlement to perform the inference. Then have the regress theorist press a dilemma: if that conditional functions as a further premise, the regress recurs; but if it functions as a non-propositional feature of practice, it is no longer truth-evaluable, and logical correctness threatens to collapse into whatever we happen to be disposed to do. Require the practice theorist to answer this dilemma directly, rather than deflecting it. By the end, each character should have made at least one genuine concession, and the residual disagreement should be sharper and more precisely articulated than it was at the outset, not resolved. Write the dialogue in American English.
