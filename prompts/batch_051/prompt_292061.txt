You are an expert metaethicist with a formal, precise writing style. I am a PhD student in moral philosophy, and I have spent the past year facilitating an adversarial collaboration between two participants who hold sharply opposed views. Before you draft anything, spend time brainstorming candidate formulations: for each central agreement and disagreement, write a version that is too generous to one side, a version too generous to the other, and a version both can sign. Use those drafts as scaffolding, but include only the final formulations in the finished document. Think carefully about which disagreements survive precise wording and which collapse into merely verbal differences. Do not include citations or bibliographies; the parties want a statement of views, not a survey of the literature.

The two positions are as follows.

Robust non-naturalist moral realism (R): moral judgments are beliefs, some such beliefs are true, and their truthmakers are robust, stance-independent, non-natural moral facts. These facts are irreducibly normative and are not identical with any descriptive natural property.

Moral error theory (E): moral judgments are beliefs, and they carry a non-negotiable commitment to robust, non-natural, categorically reason-giving facts. Because no such facts exist, positive first-order moral judgments are systematically false. Error theory is therefore a form of moral anti-realism.

Both positions accept that moral discourse is cognitivist on its surface, so the collaboration is not about whether moral talk aims at truth. It is about whether that talk ever succeeds, and what its failure or success would consist in.

Write the document this collaboration should have produced, in exactly the following format.

Section 1. Numbered agreed claims. Write between six and ten substantive, precisely worded claims that both R and E would sign without private reservation. They should not be tautologies. Cover at least: the surface cognitivism of moral discourse; what ordinary moral thought commits itself to, including the commitments an error theory must attribute to ordinary speakers; the platitudes connecting moral judgment to motivation and action in the ordinary case; the phenomena of moral disagreement and moral agreement; and a joint specification of what any metaethical account of morality must explain.

Section 2. A disagreements table. Use these four columns: neutral statement of the question; R's answer in wording R would own; E's answer in wording E would own; and a judgment about whether any element of the disagreement is verbal. Include at least the following rows: whether irreducibly normative facts exist; what, if anything, makes moral beliefs correct; whether the action-guiding character of moral judgment is better explained by normative truth or by projective error; and how much weight should be assigned to the theoretical cost of positing non-natural facts.

Section 3. Cruxes. Identify three to five live cruxes: sub-questions such that a settled answer would rationally move, though not necessarily convert, at least one side given everything else that side accepts. For each crux, state (a) the sub-question, (b) a concrete test, argument, or case that both R and E agree bears on it, (c) each side's prediction about it, recorded in advance, and (d) a precise conditional each side signs indicating exactly how it would revise its view if the evidence came out decisively the other way. The tests or cases should be specific enough to be run or formulated, not gestures at further discussion.

Section 4. Partly verbal disagreements. If, in your judgment, part of the dispute between R and E is verbal, state exactly which part and explain why. If none of it is verbal, say so and defend that verdict.

Keep the document formal throughout. Do not drift into a dialogue, a verdict on who is right, or a diagnosis of why the debate is stuck. The finished statement should be one that both participants could publish jointly as the output of their collaboration.
