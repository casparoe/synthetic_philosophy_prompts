I am a high school student working on an independent study of J. R. Lucas's 1961 paper "Minds, Machines and Gödel." The section I keep coming back to is the one in which Lucas says, in effect, that Gödel's theorem lets us construct, for any consistent machine, a formula that the machine cannot prove but whose truth we can see. He takes this to show that no machine can be a human mind. I have tried to turn that section into a numbered argument, and a friend says it proves too much. Here is the argument.

(1) For any consistent formal system S rich enough to express arithmetic, there is a sentence G(S) such that S can prove neither G(S) nor its negation, but a human mathematician can see that G(S) is true.
(2) Suppose a human mind is identical with the operation of some consistent formal system H.
(3) By (1), the human mind can see that G(H) is true.
(4) But H cannot prove G(H); so if the mind really were identical with H, then the mind would be producing as true a sentence that H cannot prove, which is impossible.
(5) Therefore the human mind is not identical with any consistent formal system.
(6) The same reasoning shows that no physical computing device is identical with any consistent formal system either: for any device running a formal system C, a human can see that G(C) is true while C cannot prove it.
(7) But ordinary digital computers are physical computing devices whose operation is described by formal systems.
(8) Contradiction. So something must have gone wrong, even though each step looked plausible.

Where does the reasoning actually break? I especially want to know whether rejecting (6) would require giving up Lucas's conclusion, and what the cost of that rejection is. Please answer in American English.
