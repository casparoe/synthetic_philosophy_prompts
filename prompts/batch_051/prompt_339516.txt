Reading through a translation of Leibniz's Monadology, and the bit in section 9 where he talks about there being no two perfectly like things in nature because every difference between substances has to be grounded in an internal difference—that bit has stuck with me. People around me have turned it into a slogan: no two things are exactly alike. But I keep hearing it doing very different jobs. A friend used it to push back on the idea that kids are interchangeable in large families. A physicist in my group used it to say that Leibniz's principle can't survive quantum mechanics because two electrons in the singlet state share every intrinsic property and yet are two. And someone online used it as if it just meant "if you can't tell them apart, there's only one of them," which sounds obviously false for identical twins. I get the sense this slogan is actually a bundle of different claims with different strengths, and I don't know wich one to push back on. Can you untangle the precise claims someone might be making with "no two things are exactly alike": which are trivial, which are substantive but defensible, which are actually false, and which reading is needed when someone uses it to argue that quantum particles violate the identity of indiscernibles? I'd like a menu with verdicts, not just the most charitable reading. At the end, give me a one-sentence summary as the last line, prefixed with "Summary:".
