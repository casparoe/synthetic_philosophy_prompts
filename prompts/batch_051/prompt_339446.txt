I keep returning to the end-of-physics debate, but from an angle that leaves me unsure how to proceed. Suppose physicists reached what is sometimes called a final theory: a theory that unifies the fundamental forces and particles, reproduces everything currently known, makes several novel predictions that are later confirmed, and survives every precision test we can devise. The claim I want to understand is the distinct, stronger claim that this theory is final—that it will never be replaced, and that the project of fundamental physics is in principle complete. My question is: how would one test that claim? I would like a concrete experimental or observational design aimed specifically at distinguishing "the best theory so far" from "the final theory," and an account of what result would favor each reading. I can see how an anomaly or a failed prediction would count decisively against finality; it is much less clear what positive result could count in favor of it, short of continued non-falsification.

Then I would like you to say exactly what a positive result would and would not settle. Which part of the claim that a theory is final can an experiment or observation reach, and which part does operationalization inevitably leave out? Is that residue merely a practical limit on testing—something future experiments could narrow—or does it point to a conceptual feature of the idea of a final theory itself, for instance that "final" encodes a modal or explanatory claim about all possible future evidence rather than a claim about any particular measurement? I am less interested in a verdict than in a careful account of how the empirical and the conceptual pieces of finality separate, and where they touch.
