In debates over labor law and the legal privileges of unions, one commonly encounters the view that a labor union is at bottom a cartel of workers, and that the analogy is not loose but exact. The argument runs this way. A cartel is an agreement among competing sellers to hold the price of a commodity above the competitive level by restricting supply; that is why antitrust law treats cartels as the paradigm of an unlawful restraint of trade. A union, on this view, is an agreement among competing sellers of labor to hold the price of labor above the competitive level by restricting the supply of work—above all through the strike, the picket line, the closed shop, and the bargaining monopoly. It follows, so the argument concludes, that the legal protections labor organizations enjoy are an anomaly: the right to organize is the right to form a price-fixing conspiracy, and the law's tolerance of unions is a standing exception to the principle that collusion is condemned wherever it appears.

Audit this analogy rigorously. Begin with an explicit correspondence table: what corresponds to the firms, the commodity, the price, the output restriction, the cartel agreement, the consumers, and the deadweight loss? Then set out the respects in which the mapping breaks. In doing so, separate the disanalogies that are merely incidental from the disanalogies that matter for the argument the analogy is being asked to carry. Consider in particular the features of a commercial cartel that the analogy quietly imports into labor markets, and the features of wage labor that the commodity picture must suppress: that the thing sold is the hours and capacity of a person who must be present for the sale, that the buyer typically stands in a relation of authority over the seller during production, that the buyers may themselves possess market power, that the workers who withhold their labor bear the direct cost in foregone wages rather than recouping it through a higher price on remaining sales, and that a union is a continuing membership organization that supplies representation, voice, and other goods that a price-fixing ring does not. Then ask what would need to be true for the argument to succeed: does the conclusion that union protections are an anomalous privilege survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or does the argument derive its force from the premise that the labor market is just a market for a commodity like any other—the very point the analogy was introduced to establish? Finally, state whether a better analogy is available, and what its own limits would be.
