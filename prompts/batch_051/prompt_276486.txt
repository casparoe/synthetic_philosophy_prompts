You are a professional philosopher of physics with a strong background in the logic of supertasks, and you are used to distinguishing physical possibility from mere mathematical definability. Think slowly. Before settling on a verdict, brainstorm the different ways a lamp could come to be on or off at an instant after infinitely many switches, and consider the strongest and weakest readings of each step.

Consider the following case. A lamp with a two-position switch is off at exactly 12:00. At 12:00:30 someone switches it on; at 12:00:45 off; at 12:00:52.5 on; and so on, each switch occurring halfway through the time remaining before 12:01. Call the state of the lamp at exactly 12:01, after the entire infinite sequence, its terminal state.

Here is an argument that the terminal state is impossible:

(1) If the lamp is on at a given time, then there is some earlier time at which it was switched on and after which it was not switched off before that given time.
(2) In the supertask described, every switching-on before 12:01 is followed by a later switching-off before 12:01.
(3) Therefore there is no earlier time before 12:01 at which the lamp was switched on and after which it was not switched off before 12:01.
(4) By (1), the lamp is not on at 12:01.
(5) By parallel reasoning with “on” and “off” exchanged, the lamp is not off at 12:01.
(6) Any working two-position lamp is either on or off at every instant.
(7) So the lamp at 12:01 is neither on nor off (from (4) and (5)) and is either on or off (from (6)): a contradiction.
(8) Hence the described supertask cannot be completed by any lamp governed by these rules.

Assess the argument. Is it valid as it stands? If not, identify the first step that fails and give the exact reading under which it is false; also give the most charitable reading under which it is true, and say whether, on that true reading, step (8) still follows. If the argument is valid, say whether the conclusion should be accepted for a real physical system.
