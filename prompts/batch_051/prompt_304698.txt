I chair the membership committee of a 96-unit housing cooperative. Our members pay monthly carrying charges and are required to contribute a set number of maintenance hours each year. In practice, a handful of members pay their carrying charges but never show up for maintenance, yet they use the common workshop, the guest rooms, and the garden, and they vote on budgets. Our governing agreement says every member must carry a fair share of the burdens of cooperation, but it does not define when a pattern of non-contribution becomes chargeable free-riding rather than a legitimate private arrangement or a temporary hardship.

The intellectual anchor I want the procedure to respect is the passage in Hume's An Enquiry concerning the Principles of Morals where he introduces the figure he calls the sensible knave. As I understand it, the knave is a rational person who grants that honesty and justice are, as a general matter, the best policy, but who also believes it can be wise to take advantage of particular exceptions—cases where breaking the rules would bring a large personal gain while the damage to the social fabric would be small or hard to notice. I do not want the procedure to simply paste Hume's own answer onto our committee; I want you to translate the principle behind that worry into a decision procedure we can actually run.

Please write a reusable procedure for the committee to use when a member is reported as a free rider. It must tell us: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops with no action; where it hands the decision to the full membership because the question is one of values rather than facts; and which cases it cannot settle. We have no philosopher on the committee, and we need to be able to get through a case in one evening meeting. The committee makes a recommendation, the board approves it, and the member can appeal to the full membership.

Then apply the procedure to these three cases, and say explicitly what it gives and where it has to stop. (1) A software developer pays full carrying charges and never attends maintenance days, saying his hourly rate makes it rational for him to work his job rather than sand floors, and that the co-op is still better off for having his dues. (2) A member who did her full share for years stopped contributing maintenance hours after a serious illness and has not resumed two years after being cleared to return, though she still uses all the common facilities. (3) A member contributes no general maintenance hours but spends a comparable number of hours each year rewiring and repairing electrical systems, work the co-op would otherwise have to pay a contractor to do. Include among these at least one case where the procedure gives an answer the committee will not like, and say why the procedure gives it.

Please give a committed answer: make definite choices and defend them, rather than laying out options for me to select from.
