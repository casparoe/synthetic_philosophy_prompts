At a reading group I sat in on, two members, Priya and Tom, got into an argument about a pair of glass vases. The first vase was packed in a crate lined with shock-absorbing foam; when the crate was dropped from a balcony, the vase survived intact. The second vase had been sprayed with a shear-thickening coating that is chemically bonded to the glass and hardens almost instantly under the stress of an impact; when it was dropped from the same height, it also survived. Priya said that both vases are still fragile, and that the simple analysis of dispositions — roughly, that something is fragile just in case it would break if dropped — fails in both cases, but for different reasons. In the first case the fragility is masked: the glass remains fragile, but the foam blocks the manifestation. In the second case the fragility is finkish: the impact itself makes the object lose its fragility at the very moment it would break, because the coating stiffens. Tom replied that this is too convenient: if a vase can be called fragile even though it would not break when dropped, then the word 'fragile' stops making predictions. He added that a mask and a fink are less different than Priya claims, since in both cases the drop fails to break the vase and a bystander who knew only the outcome could not tell which explanation applied. Priya answered that the two cases can be told apart by examining the intrinsic structure of the glass and the causal pathway of the impact, not just the outcome: the foam leaves the glass untouched, whereas the coating alters the object in response to the very stimulus that is supposed to reveal the disposition. Tom came back with the point that this makes 'fragile' an unobservable posit, and that if the coating is genuinely part of the object, the right conclusion may simply be that the coated vase is not fragile, rather than that it is fragile-but-finked. Neither would budge, and I was asked to referee, but I could not untangle it.
