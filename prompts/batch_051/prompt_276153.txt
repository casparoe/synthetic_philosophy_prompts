I have been trying to work through something on my own, without much background in philosophy, and I would like a definite judgment from you rather than a survey of possibilities. Suppose someone takes seriously the view that there is something it is like to be even the basic constituents of the physical world—the sorts of entities fundamental physics describes—and not only to be a brain or a whole animal. It seems to me that a great deal turns on whether the experience attributed to these fundamental entities is bare and neutral, a kind of faint is-ness with no felt quality of going well or badly, or whether it includes something like valence: a dimension along which states can be better or worse, painful or pleasant. If the higher-level suffering we care about is somehow built out of, or mirrored by, valenced states at the bottom, then what looks like a fanciful metaphysical picture becomes an ethical claim about how much of the universe has a stake in how things go. My question is this: what do you regard as the single most important implication of this view—if it were taken seriously—for how we should think about suffering and moral concern, and why is that implication more important than the others one might draw? Please take a clear, committed position and argue for it rather than laying out options and leaving me to choose. Write your answer in American English.
