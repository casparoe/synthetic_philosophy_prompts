I've caught myself running a fairly strong epistemic policy in my own research, and I can't tell whether it's defensible or just a bias with a nice story. I'm a neuroscience PhD student, so causal talk is everywhere: a colleague will say a pathway "really produces" a certain behaviour, or that a manipulation failed "because the mechanism wasn't engaged," and I find myself insisting that none of that is allowed to update my beliefs until it's restated as a projectible regularity under interventions. My working rule is that causal claims just are claims about stable, projectible regularities and counterfactual dependence; references to powers, dispositions, or necessary connection are decorative. They can go in the discussion section, but they can't carry inferential weight. The habit keeps me from overinterpreting results and from telling mechanism stories after the fact, but it starts to feel strained when I have to extrapolate outside the range of conditions I've actually tested — regularities get thin there, and everyone, me included, reaches for richer causal talk.

What I want to ask is whether this policy is actually warranted, or whether it's a piece of metaphysics dressed up as methodological hygiene. I know there's a live debate in the Hume literature between a regularity reading of causation and the so-called New Hume reading, where necessity is something real in the world but unknowable in experience. I don't need exegesis of Hume, but I do want to know whether taking that debate seriously would change anything about how I act in the lab: if there were such a thing as unobservable necessary connection, could it make a practical difference to inference, prediction, or experimental design, or would it be empirically inert? And from the inside, how would I tell whether the rule is keeping me intellectually honest or just lowering my cognitive load by refusing to think hard about mechanisms? Please separate what's roughly settled among people working on causation from what's genuinely contested, and don't present one side of the New Hume debate as the consensus. British English, please.
