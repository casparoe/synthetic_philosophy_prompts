Last week we covered the ethics of belief debate in my philosophy class, and I went back over James's "The Will to Believe" and Clifford's "The Ethics of Belief" with a friend. Afterwards my friend—who gets most of his philosophy from a popular book on pragmatism—insisted the whole thing boiled down to this: "Clifford's view is that it's always morally wrong to believe something on insufficient evidence, because you can never be sure a sloppy belief won't lead someone else into harm, so the only responsible policy is to suspend judgment on anything you can't fully prove. James replies that when the evidence can't settle a question, you're entitled to believe whatever makes your life go best, since the emotional and practical benefits of a belief are themselves a kind of evidence. So basically James is defending wishful thinking, and all he really does is lower the standards of evidence because he wants religious faith to be respectable." I have a feeling this is unfair, especially because our instructor treats James as making a much narrower argument then "believe whatever feels good," but I don't trust myself to pinpoint the mistakes. Can you audit my friend's summary: which parts would a careful reader of Clifford and James accept, which are subtle distortions or conflations, and does the closing charge about wishful thinking actually follow from what James argued?
