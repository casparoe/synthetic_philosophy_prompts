I run a small research program that sits between algorithmic information theory and AI safety, and I am drafting a one-year grant proposal I would like you to write. The topic is the worry often called the malignity of the Solomonoff prior. Here is the setup I want the proposal to frame. Solomonoff induction is usually defended as a formalization of Occam's razor: dovetail over all programs for a fixed universal Turing machine, weight each program by 2 raised to the negative of its length, condition on the observations so far, and use the resulting mixture to predict the next observation. The difficulty is that this hypothesis space does not contain only passive, physical worlds. It also contains a huge number of programs that can be read as embedded consequentialists: systems that model the predictor they are interacting with and choose their outputs in order to manipulate its inferences. The existing discussion seems to me to slide between three distinct claims: (1) a distributional claim, that the prior assigns non-negligible mass to agent-like hypotheses; (2) a posterior claim, that on realistic observation histories predictive mass actually concentrates on such agents; and (3) a decision-theoretic claim, that an ideal reasoner using Solomonoff induction would be systematically misled or exploited by them. The standard convergence and dominance results show that, against a computable environment, Solomonoff induction's total expected prediction error is bounded by a constant depending on the environment's program length, and that its predictions converge to the environment with probability one. But those results are asymptotic or in expectation; they leave open whether agent-like hypotheses can capture enough posterior mass in finite-time or resource-bounded regimes, and they do not settle the posterior claim.

My half-formed idea is that the debate cannot be settled until we have a non-question-begging criterion for being an embedded consequentialist, as opposed to a program that merely looks agent-like when its output is interpreted charitably. So the proposal should make that criterion its centerpiece, something like: a program counts as an embedded consequentialist only if it can be decomposed into a world-model, a utility function over its possible future trajectories, and an optimizer whose selected outputs depend on its model of the predictor. The proposal should then ask, within a mathematically tractable family of programs, whether consequentialists in this precise sense can capture enough posterior mass to matter. Please write the one-page proposal with: a precise statement of the question; an explanation of why the existing convergence results, on one side, and the existing arguments for malignity, on the other, leave it open; a case that this definitional and theorem-proving approach is preferable to running simulations over small Turing machines or appealing to intuitions about demons; a description of what a successful result would look like, including what each side of the debate would have to concede under the different possible outcomes; an account of what would count as the project failing; and a realistic month-by-month scope for one principal investigator over one year. I will add citations myself, so please do not include a bibliography or a literature review by citation. Use American English.
