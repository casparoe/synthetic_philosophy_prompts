Act as a professional moral philosopher. Think carefully and work through the issue rather than reaching for a canned position; begin by brainstorming candidate justifications before settling on the strongest one.

Here is an asymmetry in ordinary moral judgment that I want you to examine. We tend to admire the personally frugal person: someone who mends instead of replacing, saves a large share of a modest income, and wastes little is praised as disciplined, humble, and virtuous. But a person who puts the same effort into making others frugal — campaigning for consumption taxes, organizing repair or buy-nothing initiatives, or pressing friends to consume less — is often regarded as meddling, preachy, or stingy. Being frugal is admired; promoting frugality is not, even when the stated aim is simply that more people live the way we claim to admire.

Does anything actually justify this asymmetry? Consider the best deontological case for it (for example, frugality as a self-regarding virtue or as a value partly constituted by being freely chosen, so that imposing it would undermine it) and the best consequentialist case against it (if frugality is good because of its effects, the effects are larger when it is promoted). Then give the strongest principle you can find that draws the line between the two stances, test it against nearby asymmetries such as personal honesty versus campaigning for honesty or personal kindness versus pushing others to be kind, and give an honest verdict: should the asymmetry be kept, reversed, or given up?
