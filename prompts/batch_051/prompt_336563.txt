I write about probabilistic reasoning and legal institutions for a general-interest magazine, and I have been trying to work out what I actually think about statistical evidence. My verdicts in particular cases feel firmer than any principle I can find to explain them, so I want you to construct the principle—or tell me which of my verdicts cannot survive.

1. The bus, market-share version. A pedestrian is hit by a bus that flees the scene. The only evidence connecting any company to the injury is that 80 percent of the buses running on that route at that hour are operated by Blue Bus, and the rest by Red. There is no witness, no paint fleck, no dispatch record. The probability that the injuring bus was Blue’s is 0.8, and the civil standard is only the balance of probabilities, yet I would not hold Blue Bus liable.

2. The bus, eyewitness version. Same accident, same route, but this time a single bystander testifies that the bus was blue. The bystander is known to be reliable about color under those lighting conditions 70 percent of the time. Here I would find Blue Bus liable. The probability is lower, but the testimony seems to change the kind of evidence, not just the quantity, and I cannot say exactly why.

3. The gatecrasher. A promoter sells 1,000 admissions to a rodeo; 499 people paid and 501 entered without paying, but the ticket stubs were lost. From a photograph of the crowd, the promoter sues one attendee, selected at random, for the price of admission. The chance that this particular attendee gatecrashed is 0.501. I would not find for the promoter.

4. The prison yard. One hundred prisoners are in an exercise yard when ninety-nine of them kill a guard. The state charges one prisoner, selected at random. The probability of guilt is 0.99. I would not convict.

5. The recall. A health agency is deciding whether to recall a brand of bagged salad after an outbreak. Eighty percent of the people who became ill report having eaten that brand in the days before, although the brand accounts for only a fifth of the packaged-salad market. Investigators have found no pathogen in the company’s facilities and cannot trace the contamination beyond the epidemiological association, so the agency would be acting on a bare class-level distribution. I would support the recall even though I would not award damages in a tort suit against the company on the same evidence. I do not know whether this reveals a principled difference between regulation and adjudication or simply an inconsistency.

The only distinction I have managed to articulate is something like a difference between evidence that reaches the particular event and evidence that merely classifies it, but I cannot make that distinction precise, and it may not survive case 5. Is there a single account—or a small set of candidates—that captures all five verdicts? Please state it precisely, indicate which case motivates each clause, tell me which of my verdicts it requires me to revise or abandon, and then apply the account to a case I have not been able to settle. In that case, a stylometric system identifies an individual as the likely author of a series of threatening letters with 96 percent accuracy on a validation set, but the system offers no articulable feature as the basis for its classification. Should this evidence be treated more like the eyewitness, more like the market-share evidence, or more like something else, and why?
