A local land-use dispute has me stuck, and I would like your advice. Our town abuts a seasonal marsh that floods each spring and shrinks to a few muddy pools by late summer. A developer has applied to fill it and build a distribution warehouse—a project I have no independent reason to support, and which the conservation group I volunteer with is fighting largely on ecological grounds: the marsh is a breeding site for native frogs, salamanders, and waterfowl, it filters runoff, and it is one of the last undeveloped parcels in the area. But a close friend of mine, who has been an environmentalist and a vegan for years, has been quietly arguing that we have this backwards. Every spring, he points out, the marsh produces a vast number of animals—tadpoles, insects, small fish, ducklings—and the overwhelming majority of them die painfully before the year ends, from starvation as the pools dry up, from disease, or from being eaten. The lives they have are, at best, short and punctuated by fear and pain. If the marsh is filled, those future animals never come into existence; he says that cannot harm them, since there will be no one to be deprived of anything, and it prevents an enormous amount of misery. He is not proposing that we kill the animals now living there—only that we should not oppose a project that would prevent next spring's generation from coming into being. The adults that currently use the site would mostly move elsewhere, and he thinks that harm is small compared with the suffering that would be prevented year after year.

I find it disturbingly hard to refute him, yet I recoil at the thought that a conservationist should quietly accept the paving of a wetland. Is his argument sound? If not, where exactly does it go wrong? And if you were in my position, what would you argue at the next conservation-group meeting?
