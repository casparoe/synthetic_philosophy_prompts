Exam Question:

Read the passage below from David Hume's essay "Of National Characters":

The human mind is of a very imitative nature; nor is it possible for any set of men to converse often together, without acquiring a similitude of manners, and communicating to each other their vices as well as virtues. The propensity to company and society is strong in all rational creatures; and the same disposition, which gives us this propensity, makes us enter deeply into each other's sentiments, and causes like passions and inclinations to run, as it were, by contagion, through the whole club or knot of companions. Where a number of men are united into one political body, the occasions of their intercourse must be so frequent, for defence, commerce, and government, that, together with the same speech or language, they must acquire a resemblance in their manners, and have a common or national character, as well as a personal one, peculiar to each individual.

Reconstruct the argument Hume gives in this passage for the existence of a national character, and explain how it is meant to avoid the "undistinguishing judgments" he criticizes earlier in the essay. Then evaluate whether the mechanism of imitation and contagion can justify generalizations about a nation's character, or whether it leaves us only with weaker, statistical claims about individuals. In particular, discuss whether the nation, rather than other kinds of association, is a privileged unit on this account.

Student Answer:

In this passage Hume explains national character through social contagion rather than physical causes such as climate. People who converse frequently imitate each other, and this imitation propagates similar passions and manners through the whole "club or knot of companions." When the companions are members of one political body, their frequent intercourse for defense, commerce, and government, together with a common language, produces a resemblance in manners. Hume calls this a "common or national character," while allowing that each person also has a distinct personal character. This lets him reject undiscerning generalizations that treat every member of a nation as knavish or cowardly: the relevant claim is only about tendencies that are more frequently met with among one people than another.

I think the mechanism is plausible, but it has limits. Imitation and contagion also operate within professions, cities, and families, so the nation is only one of many groups in which shared manners can arise. Hume does not give a criterion for deciding when a set of shared manners amounts to a national character rather than an urban or professional one. Moreover, the fact that a quality is more frequently met with among one people than another does not by itself entitle us to attribute a character to the nation as a whole; that would move from a distribution over individuals to a property of the collectivity. Still, if the generalization is kept probabilistic, Hume's account avoids the worst kind of national stereotyping.
