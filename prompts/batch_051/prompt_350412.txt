I am a PhD student in political theory, working on institutional memory and the justification of inherited rules. Consider a legislature that adopts the following standing order: no existing regulation may be repealed unless the repeal's sponsor first files, and a committee accepts, a written account of the specific problem the regulation was originally enacted to solve; if no accepted account is produced within a fixed period, the regulation is retained automatically. The rule is an institutionalized version of the idea that one should not tear down a fence until one knows why it was built. Treat this as a mechanism to be analyzed rather than a position to be defended or attacked. Walk me through how it would actually behave: what it incentivizes among legislators, agencies, lobbyists, and the regulated public; how it would be gamed; what happens to rules whose original rationale is obsolete, mistaken, factional, or fabricated; whether it would mostly preserve accumulated wisdom or the rules with the best-documented histories; and where it would fail first. Can its failure modes be fixed without gutting the rule?
