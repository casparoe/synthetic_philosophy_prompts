After a long career in the natural sciences, I have recently begun sitting in on university philosophy courses, and I have been asked to help grade an examination in an introductory philosophy of mathematics course. I have not done much philosophical grading before, and I would prefer to base the grade on specific points rather than a general impression. The exam question read as follows: Explain Hilbert's Hotel and assess what, if anything, it shows about the existence of actual infinities; in doing so, be sure to address the finitist objection that a completed infinite totality is impossible.

The student's answer read as follows: Hilbert's Hotel has one room for every natural number and is fully occupied. When a new guest arrives, the manager moves the occupant of room 1 into room 2, the occupant of room 2 into room 3, and so on, thereby freeing room 1 without evicting anyone. After the move, the occupied rooms are rooms 2, 3, 4, and so on, which form a proper subset of the rooms but can be placed in one-to-one correspondence with the original occupancy. This is often taken to illustrate Cantor's theorem that an infinite set is one that can be matched one-to-one with one of its proper subsets. The example also shows that infinite cardinal arithmetic differs from ordinary finite arithmetic, since adding one to an infinite cardinal leaves it unchanged. Proponents of actual infinities read the hotel as showing that an actually infinite collection is logically coherent, since the story can be described without contradiction. Finitists reply that this is too weak: the scenario merely assumes the finished hotel, and the instruction to move the guest in room n to room n+1 is only a finite rule that never produces an actually infinite totality. For a finitist, the natural numbers exist only as a potential sequence, so the premise of a fully occupied hotel with countably many rooms is exactly what is in dispute. The right conclusion is therefore that Hilbert's Hotel illustrates how an actual infinite would behave if there were one, rather than proving that one exists. Since every operation in the hotel is carried out on countably many rooms, the thought experiment also establishes that any actual infinite collection would have the same size as the natural numbers, which is why the only serious candidate for an actual infinite is the countable one.

Assign a grade on an American A–F scale, using pluses and minuses if helpful, and give point-by-point comments that identify which parts of the answer are correct, which parts are incorrect or unsupported, and why. Confine the assessment to the answer as written, without speculating about the student's intentions or effort, and write the comments in American English.
