I was arguing with a friend who's in physics about whether history could ever be a real science. He laid out what he called his four non-negotiable criteria: a science has to use mathematical models; it has to make quantitative predictions that can be falsified; it has to posit underlying mechanisms that can be tested against data; and it has to be able to forecast out-of-sample, not just retrodict the curve it was fit to. He said history fails all four and called it a day. I brought up cliodynamics—the line of work associated with Peter Turchin and his secular cycles stuff. The picture there is that premodern agrarian states go through multi-century cycles where population growth, elite overproduction, state fiscal strain, and falling real wages feed into political instability, and that you can write down differential equations for these variables and fit them to historical datasets. Then I posed him a case: imagine a cliodynamics paper that fits a Turchin-style model to data from, say, medieval France and Ming China, estimates the model's parameters, and then uses it to issue a probabilistic 40-year forecast that a particular modern country will enter a disintegrative phase around 2060, with a stated confidence interval. It also lists the causal mechanisms and says which historical data would count against the model if things don't happen as predicted. Is this thing a science by his four criteria? Go through each criterion and say where the case clearly qualifies, clearly fails, or lands in a gray zone. And then tell me whether the gray zones mean the criteria are too strict, too loose, or just about right—basically, should the definition of science bend to fit this kind of work, or should this kind of work bend to fit the definition?
