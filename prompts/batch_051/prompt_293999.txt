I am a PhD student in programming languages, writing a thesis chapter on the semantics and cognitive ergonomics of mutable state, and the chapter keeps drifting into philosophy. I have a theory I would like checked before it goes in. The observation behind it is that mutation is disproportionately hard to reason about: the change itself can be trivial, but once a program mutates something, the number of things a competent reasoner must hold in mind seems to jump. My theory is that ordinary reasoning about programs runs on a single, undifferentiated notion of 'same thing,' one that actually covers two relations that immutable code lets us conflate. I call them value continuity and location continuity. Value continuity holds between two points in a computation when some expression denotes the same value at both; location continuity holds when the same storage location — a variable binding, a field, a list cell, whatever — persists across the two points. In an immutable setting these two relations mostly travel together: if a name still denotes the same value, there is often nothing else worth tracking. Mutation, on my account, is the operation that drives them apart. Updating a field keeps the location continuous while making the value discontinuous; aliasing and copying can keep the value continuous while making the location discontinuous. The difficulty of mutation is then the cost of maintaining two bookkeepings — for where a thing is and for what it is worth — under a concept of sameness that only budgets for one. I think this explains, with a single mechanism, a scattered family of phenomena: novices reading 'x = x + 1' as a contradiction; the stubborn tendency to call an object 'the same object' after every one of its fields has changed; the difficulty of loop invariants and induction over mutable structures; and the fact that debuggers and execution traces always seem to be showing both an identity and a current value at once.

I know there are standard treatments of identity versus equality, of l-values versus r-values, and of denotational accounts of state, and I do not think my distinction simply is one of those. What I want is an honest assessment. Is this a real explanation, or have I just renamed the phenomenon? Has something close been worked out under another name — and if so, where does my version go beyond it? Most importantly, where does it break: can you give a case where mutation is hard even though the two continuities do not come apart, or one where they come apart and reasoning stays easy? Give me the three strongest objections you can and say whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable.
