I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and next month I am giving a short talk to a university philosophy department about an essay I am developing on Richard Rorty's antirepresentationalism. My thesis is that Rorty's antirepresentationalism is better read as a normative claim about the conditions under which we are entitled to treat a vocabulary as answerable to the world, rather than as the metaphysical claim that no vocabulary represents the world. On this reading, Rorty escapes the charge of relativism, because he is not offering a theory of what truth consists in. But the reading has a price: it cannot explain why the vocabulary of the natural sciences has been so uniquely successful at prediction and control, except by conceding that scientific vocabulary tracks the world better than other vocabularies do — and that concession looks exactly like the representationalism Rorty wanted to abandon.

The audience is a department colloquium of mostly analytic philosophers. There is an epistemologist who works on truth and realism, who will care most about whether my reading is internally coherent; a metaphysician sympathetic to truthmaker theory, who will ask what makes claims true if not representation; and a philosopher of science who is a scientific realist, who will press the success-of-science objection hard. There are also two faculty sympathetic to pragmatism who think Rorty was right to reject representation but wrong to give up on objectivity, and they will suspect I have made Rorty too tame. I am an outsider to the discipline, and this audience cares more about argumentative force than about textual fidelity for its own sake.

Act as a professional philosopher who knows Rorty's work and mainstream analytic epistemology and metaphysics well.
