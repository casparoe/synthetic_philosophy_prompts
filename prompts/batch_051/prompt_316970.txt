I’ve been in an informal reading group working through David Lewis’s essay Scorekeeping in a Language Game, and there is a passage near the beginning that has been bothering us. Lewis asks us to think of a baseball game with an official scoreboard. His point, as I understand it, is that the score is not just a passive record of the physical events on the field: the scoreboard has a kind of authority, and the score can be updated by certain moves in the game itself, without anyone going back to check the events. A friend in the group has taken this passage and run with it. He says presupposition in conversation works the same way: when someone says something like Even John’s children are here, the fact that John has children gets added to the conversational score automatically, and from then on everyone is entitled to take it as established, just as a run appears on the scoreboard and becomes part of the state of the game.

I want to know how far this scoreboard analogy can actually be pushed. What in a conversation plays the role of the scoreboard, the official scorer, the rules of baseball, the fans, and the events on the field? Where does the mapping start to break down? For example, what happens in the analogy when the scoreboard is wrong, or when different people at the game keep different scoreboards, or when an update happens without anyone noticing? Does the analogy quietly import features of baseball that conversation lacks—maybe an agreed-upon official, or a single public board—and if so, does my friend’s conclusion about how presuppositions get created still hold once those disanalogies are on the table? I would like a detailed audit, and if the scoreboard analogy fails, I want to hear what a better analogy would be. Along the way, please say how confident you are in each main part of your answer and identify the specific point you are least sure about.
