You are an expert in decision theory and the philosophical foundations of expected value reasoning, comfortable with formal distinctions and with the kind of case-based thinking that shows up in seminar rooms. I run an advanced reading group on longtermism, existential risk, and rational choice, and I want to open the term with a "which position do you actually hold?" instrument about expected value fanaticism. Please design it.

The deliverable should be either a decision tree or a short scored quiz (8–10 items, with a scoring key) that sorts a participant into a leaf position on the strength of what they say about concrete cases — never by which label they already like. Every diagnostic question should be a situation they can answer without knowing the positions: a Pascal's-mugging-like interaction, a lottery with a minute probability of an astronomically good or bad future, an outcome involving infinite value, a case where the payoff is enormous but the participant's action has no detectable effect, a case where the probability estimate comes from a demonstrably unreliable source, and so on. Each question should leave a route for someone who refuses the forced choice or says "it depends," rather than silently assigning them to a leaf.

Include at least these leaves, under the clearest ordinary-language names you can give them: (1) the wholehearted expected-value maximizer who accepts the fanatical verdicts, holding that a small enough probability of a large enough good can rationally require enormous present sacrifice; (2) the bounded-utility view, which denies that value is unbounded and holds that there is a ceiling near enough that fanatical verdicts do not follow; (3) the probability-threshold view, which treats prospects below some minute probability as no prospect at all; (4) the higher-order-uncertainty view, which holds that our uncertainty about extremely small probabilities themselves is so severe that fanatical calculations collapse on inspection; (5) the difference-making view, which says the relevant quantity is what the participant's action changes in expectation, not the raw payoff attached to the option; (6) the lexical or non-Archimedean view, on which some goods or bads outrank others no matter how much the probability gap between them widens; and (7) the infinity-agnostic, who suspends judgment when infinite values enter because standard expected value reasoning breaks down. Add further leaves if the logical space demands them, but keep the total between six and eight, and define every technical term in one sentence within the instrument.

Build from the logical space first rather than from the list of names. Before writing the final questions, work out which branching claims genuinely separate the positions — for example, whether value is bounded, whether all nonzero probabilities count, whether the estimate or its precision can be trusted, whether the quantity that matters is raw payoff or expected difference, and what to do when infinite values appear. Lay out the combinations of answers that are possible, locate the leaves in that space, and mark any combinations that no one occupies; for each empty combination, say in one sentence whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Then write the diagnostic questions so that each one really separates positions rather than paraphrasing a label, and phrase every question so a bright non-specialist can answer it without training in decision theory.

I need the instrument to work for people who call themselves longtermists but may never have tested how comfortable they are with the extreme verdicts. So include at least two cases where the cost is real and immediate — money, time, career, a painful medical procedure — and the expected benefit is astronomically uncertain and far in the future; at least one mirror-image case involving a tiny risk of an astronomically bad outcome; and at least one case in which the probabilities are explicitly stipulated to be exact, so that the higher-order-uncertainty view cannot get its grip by saying the estimate is unreliable.

Do not include citations, bibliographies, or named-author exegesis; the instrument must stand on its own. Keep the whole thing under about two pages of dense prose.

After the instrument, add a short confidence and limitations section that (a) flags any pair of leaves the instrument cannot genuinely separate and says what extra case or commitment would separate them; (b) notes which leaf, if any, is a defensible position that nonetheless sounds absurd when its verdicts are stated plainly; and (c) states how confident you are that this taxonomy carves the space at the joints rather than at the labels.

One more thing: if this request rests on a confused premise — for example, if you think expected value fanaticism is not one position but a family of views no single tree of this size can sort, or that some of my "leaves" are not positions but stances or moods — say so plainly in a preliminary note, correct the framing, and then do the most useful version of the task rather than politely pretending the tree is fine.
