In a seminar on bounded rationality, my professor has been summarizing Simon's critique of rational choice theory with the slogan "people don't optimize, they satisfice." He uses the line to do several different jobs: to describe how real deliberation actually proceeds, to explain why aspiration-level stopping rules and fast-and-frugal heuristics sometimes outperform more extensive optimization, and to argue that expected utility maximization is not an appropriate normative ideal for computationally bounded agents. The slogan seems to me to mean something different in each of these uses. Could you separate the precise claims that "people don't optimize, they satisfice" might be asserting, ordered from the weakest to the strongest? For each reading, say whether it is a near-trivial descriptive point, a substantive and defensible claim, or something probably false, and indicate which reading, if any, would support the conclusion that expected utility maximization is the wrong normative standard for a bounded agent.
