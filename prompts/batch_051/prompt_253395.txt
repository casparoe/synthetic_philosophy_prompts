In my ethics course, the professor summarized Kant's Formula of Universal Law as follows. An action is permissible only if one can will that everyone act on the same maxim. Lying promises fail this test because, if everyone made promises they did not intend to keep, the practice of promising would collapse and no one could gain by a false promise, so the policy of false promising is self-defeating. Suicide fails the test because a world in which everyone in unbearable pain took their own lives would end the species, and no one can will that outcome. Neglecting one's talents fails because, although a world of idlers is conceivable, a rational being cannot will universal idleness, since reason necessarily wills the development of its own capacities. He added that the Formula is really just the Golden Rule made rigorous.

Something about this summary seems off to me — especially the Golden Rule remark and the suicide example, which sounds like a prediction about outcomes rather than a contradiction.
