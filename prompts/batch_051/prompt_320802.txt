Here is an argument I have been turning over about determinism and predictability. I would like a careful, step-by-step verdict on it.

(1) A universe is deterministic if and only if the complete state of the universe at any one time, together with the laws of nature, fixes exactly one complete state for every later time.

(2) An event is predictable in principle if and only if some possible reasoner — no matter how powerful, but working within nature and within time — could, before the event occurs, derive a correct specification of the event from the present complete state and the laws.

(3) Now imagine a completely deterministic universe and a reasoner R who lives inside it. At time t0, R sets out to predict R's own action at a later time t1.

(4) To succeed, R must produce, before t1, a specification of its action at t1 that matches whatever action the laws single out for t1.

(5) But R's own activity between t0 and t1 — in particular, the very prediction R forms, and any effect that forming it has — is part of the course of events from which the laws single out the action at t1. After R forms the prediction, the state of the universe already contains that prediction.

(6) Consequently, to work out what it will do at t1, R must first work out what prediction it forms before t1; and to work out what prediction it forms, R must work out what prediction it forms before that; and so on without end.

(7) No thought process, however powerful but finite, can complete an endless chain of this kind, in which the result of a computation is required as an input to that very computation.

(8) Therefore R cannot predict, in principle, what it will itself do at t1.

(9) Hence determinism does not entail predictability in principle: a fully deterministic universe can contain events — its inhabitants' own future actions — that are not predictable to those inhabitants themselves.

I half-suspect the argument is too quick, but I cannot locate the exact place where it goes wrong, or even say confidently whether it goes wrong at all. Please adjudicate. Is the argument valid? If not, which is the earliest numbered step that fails, and under what precise reading? Give the strongest version of any contested premise and say whether the argument still goes through on that version. If the argument is valid, state exactly what conclusion it supports and whether (9), as worded, follows from it.

Please write your answer in American English.
