In my English class we read this essay by David Hume called Of Tragedy, and he has this whole theory about how the painful things in tragedy get kind of converted into pleasure because they're mixed with eloquence and artistry. There's a passage where he says something like: the force of imagination, the energy of expression, the power of numbers, and the charms of imitation are all naturally delightful to the mind, and when the object presented lays hold of some affection, the pleasure rises on us by the conversion of that subordinate movement into that which is predominant. That sounds nice but I can't figure out how you'd ever check it. Like if someone said no, actually sad art is pleasurable because it gives us a safe space to practice coping with loss, or because we feel superior to the characters, or because the sadness triggers endorphins, how would Hume's account make a different prediction? My question is what kind of test or observation could even tell these apart, and what would a positive result actually settle — or is this just the kind of thing where the explanation sounds good after the fact but there's no real way to put it at risk?
