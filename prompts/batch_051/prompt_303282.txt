I keep running into a style of historical explanation where the real causes are never individual decisions or big events, but slow, almost static background conditions: coastlines, river systems, soil, crop regimes, climate. So a historian will say that the shape of the Mediterranean world didn't come from Rome or from the battles, but from things like the limits of the olive and the vine and the difficulty of moving grain over land; or that industrialisation happened where it did because of coal seams and navigable water, not because of any particular inventor. I don't mean simple environmental determinism — these accounts do let institutions, prices, and the occasional event matter, but only as the froth on top a much slower current. Sometimes the slow current is treated as the real explanation across centuries; other times the same move gets used to explain a punctual thing (a plague, a price shock). My question: does this style of explanation have an established name or a more precise articulation? I'm not just after a label — I want to know how it differs from environmental/geographic determinism, from 'big history', and from a bare claim that long-run continuities exist. Where are its edges: what counts as a slow structure and what doesn't, and when does an event stop being froth? Also, is the second use (slow structures explaining a punctual event) the same explanatory move as the first, or is it actually a different kind of explanation being smuggled in under the same intuition?
