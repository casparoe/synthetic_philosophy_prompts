I'm running a small philosophy-of-mathematics reading group and we've been on Lakatos's Proofs and Refutations for two months. The same argument breaks out every meeting and nobody has moved in weeks. One camp thinks the book shows mathematics is quasi-empirical: you start with a conjecture like Euler's polyhedron formula, a counterexample turns up (the picture-frame thing, the nested cube), and people respond by patching the proof or quietly changing what counts as a polyhedron, so mathematical knowledge grows by trial and error rather than deduction from fixed axioms. The other camp says that is just the sociology or history of mathematics. Once a proof is formalized and checked, it is deductively certain in a way no empirical result could be, so calling mathematics quasi-empirical is a category mistake. The weird part is that everyone agrees on the historical examples and on the final formal proofs; they disagree about what those examples show. I've started to suspect the two sides are answering different questions, one about how mathematical knowledge is produced and the other about what justifies it, but the Lakatos side says the whole point is that you cannot separate the two. I'd like a diagnosis rather then a survey of the two readings. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, a disagreement about what counts as evidence, or something else altogether? And given your diagnosis, is there anything that would actually move the debate — a distinction, a formal result, a better-posed question — or is this just how the argument goes?
