You are a professional epistemologist of mathematics, with expertise in the division of cognitive labor and the epistemology of computer-assisted proof. Before giving your verdict, please brainstorm the strongest considerations for and against the policy I describe below, and then evaluate it as a standing policy: where it is reliable, where it fails, and whether it can be restated to keep its benefits without its costs.

Last night I had a long argument with a close friend about the four-colour theorem and the Kepler conjecture. He holds that once a proof by exhaustive computer search has been refereed and its code checked, the resulting theorem deserves exactly the same unqualified acceptance as a theorem with a human-surveyable proof. He says that when I withhold full acceptance merely because the checking was done by a machine rather than by a person, I am committing a genetic fallacy and displaying an irrational preference for the familiar.

My standing habit is this: when a theorem is established only by a computational search far too large for any human being to survey step by step in a lifetime, I do not grant it full mathematical certainty. I treat it as reliable enough for practical and engineering purposes, and I accept it for the purposes of ordinary mathematical life, but I keep a small permanently open doubt and I avoid using it as an unexamined lemma in my own reasoning about adjacent matters. I do this even for the four-colour theorem and the Kepler conjecture, which are as well-established as computational results get.

My friend pressed me on an apparent inconsistency. He pointed out that I cannot survey many classical human proofs either, and that human surveyability is a matter of degree; so, he argued, my acceptance of either kind of proof is ultimately based on testimony and peer review, and the real epistemic work is the same in both cases. My reply was that there is a difference in kind. A human proof, even if I have not checked it, is in principle the sort of object a competent mathematician could check step by step and grasp as a whole, whereas a proof that turns on thousands of enumerated cases verified only by code is opaque even to its own authors: the survey has been outsourced to a process that can fail in ways no one is in a position to anticipate. He responded that I am laundering a bias against computers as a methodological principle.

So I want to know whether my habit is defensible. Specifically: (1) Is there a defensible epistemic difference between humanly unsurveyable because long and difficult and humanly unsurveyable because only a quasi-independent computational process has checked it, or am I simply trusting what feels like understanding? (2) Under what conditions should the permanently open doubt be so small as to be negligible, and are those conditions satisfied in the cases of the four-colour theorem and the Kepler conjecture? (3) Can my policy be restated so that it survives his inconsistency charge but still captures my real worry, and would that restated policy still treat the two famous computer proofs differently from the way most mathematicians currently treat them?
