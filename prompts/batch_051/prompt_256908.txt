I'm TAing an upper-level ethics course and Ive got to grade this exam answer. The question was:

"Explain the wrong kind of reasons problem for fitting-attitude accounts of value, and assess one way of drawing the right-kind/wrong-kind distinction."

The student wrote:

"Fitting-attitude accounts say that something is valuable when it is fitting to have a certain pro-attitude toward it, e.g. something is admirable when admiration is fitting. The wrong kind of reasons problem starts from cases where you have a reason to have an attitude even though the attitude doesn't fit the object. The standard example is an evil demon who will punish you unless you admire him. You have a reason to admire the demon, but the demon isn't admirable, and your admiration wouldn't be fitting. So if the account defines value in terms of what there is reason to value, the demon comes out admirable, which is wrong. The standard solution is to restrict the account to right-kind reasons, which are reasons that bear on the object itself rather than on the benefits of having the attitude. But this looks circular unless we can say what makes a reason right-kind. I think the best proposal is that a reason is right-kind just in case it would still be there even if the attitude had no consequences for you. Since the demon's reason disappears once the threat is removed, it's wrong-kind; reasons like 'she is generous' survive the test, so they're right-kind."
