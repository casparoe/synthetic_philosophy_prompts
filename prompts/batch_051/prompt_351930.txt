In my high school math circle, two members got stuck in an argument, and I have not been able to settle it myself. We had just seen the topological definition of continuity—a function is continuous when the preimage of every open set is open—and someone had asked why that should count as the "right" definition, when the epsilon-delta definition already works perfectly well in the metric spaces most students ever use. Here is the argument as it unfolded over several rounds.

Daniel opened. "The right definition is the one that generalizes furthest while keeping the theorems we care about. The open-set definition wins precisely because it lets us state and prove the same results—continuous images of compact sets are compact, connectedness is preserved, the intermediate value theorem—in spaces that don't have a metric at all. It's the same with groups: we deliberately leave out commutativity so the definition covers permutations, symmetries, matrix groups, all of it. The goal is maximizing generality without losing the theory."

Priya disagreed. "That gets the order backwards. You don't choose a definition to make a theorem keep working; that's too easy, because you could cook up an artificial condition to preserve any single theorem. The open-set definition is right because it removes what wasn't doing the work in the epsilon-delta definition—the metric. Continuity is really about how sets sit around points, not about distances. Generality is a side effect of isolating the actual idea. And we don't omit commutativity from the group axioms out of a thirst for breadth; we omit it because the shared structure of invertible operations simply doesn't require commutativity. Many of the central examples happen not to be commutative."

Daniel pushed back. "I think 'isolating the actual idea' is a story told after the fact. There are lots of ideas you could isolate from the epsilon-delta definition, and most of them wouldn't generalize well. Mathematicians settled on open sets because the resulting theory is productive: the same theorems come out cleanly in a much wider range of settings. That is a test the definition passed. It isn't some free-floating essence."

Priya replied. "Productivity matters, but it's evidence, not the criterion. If producing theorems were the ground, the best definition would simply be the broadest one, and that can't be right—we don't define a group by dropping all the axioms, because then no structure remains and no theorems survive. The open-set definition and the group axioms sit between those extremes: strong enough to keep the structure, weak enough to catch the examples that genuinely share it. That middle position is the concept, not a theorem count."

Daniel answered. "Then why did it take so long for the open-set form to appear? The epsilon-delta idea existed for a long time before topology settled into the open-set framework. If the definition just named the concept, it should have been obvious. It arrived late because it's a retrospective choice: once you see which theorems you want, you go back and pick the definition that makes them tidy."

Priya countered. "Clarifying a concept can take time without being mere retrospective justification. The open-set definition didn't replace the epsilon-delta definition; in metric spaces they pick out exactly the same functions. It showed why the metric-based story worked and identified what was load-bearing in it. That's clarification, not a post hoc patch. If it were only about tidiness, any equivalent restatement would do just as well."

Neither of them budged, and I genuinely cannot tell which of the specific claims each one made actually withstand scrutiny. Also address whether this is ultimately one disagreement or two—one about how definitions actually get chosen, and one about what makes a definition correct—and state what, if anything, both of them are missing.
