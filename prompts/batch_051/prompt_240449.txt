I'm a novelist on the research committee of a small foundation that funds work on governing emerging technologies — I only got asked on because my last book was about a temporary emergency government that never quite hands power back, so they think I know how the story ends. We're deadlocked over a funding bid, and I need to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's meeting.

The application is from a respectable policy institute, for a two-year working group called something like 'Deep-Future Stewardship and the Limits of Present Consent'. The core claim, as I understand it, is that once you take the likely scale and duration of the deep future seriously, the present starts to look phenomenally small: so ordinary democratic constraints — electoral accountability, individual rights, local consent — can seem parochial next to the risk of closing off the future entirely. On that basis, the group wants to work out criteria under which an appointed future-generations commission could lawfully override otherwise-binding decisions: vetoing elected legislation it judges to raise existential risk, pushing through urgent infrastructure against local opposition, that sort of thing. Half the commitee hears this and thinks we'd be bankrolling a philosopher's manual for authoritarianism; the other half says refusing to study it openly is timid, and that the only real safeguard is to get the criteria debated in public.

Draft the decision memo I'll circulate — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with a clear bottom line, reasoning the losing side could actually engage with rather than shrug off, and an honest treatment of the strongest point on the losing side. And at the end, say how confident you are in the recommendation, and single out the point in you're own reasoning you're least sure of; I'd rather know where the memo is borrowing trouble than have it sound more settled than it is.
