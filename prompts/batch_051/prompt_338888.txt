I am a novelist, and I have been trying to understand a philosophical thought experiment that a screenwriter friend insists would make a good scene, though I suspect he has compressed its point rather hard. He summarized what he called the toxin puzzle roughly like this. An eccentric billionaire offers you one million dollars if, at midnight tonight, you genuinely intend to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon. The toxin will make you painfully ill for a day but has no lasting effects. The money will already be in your bank account before the time for drinking arrives, and you are free not to drink once you have been paid. My friend then drew two lessons. First, you cannot rationally form the intention at midnight, because you already know you will not drink when the time comes. Second, this shows intentions are not the sort of mental state that can simply be summoned for a reward, since rationality prevents the mind from intending an act it has no reason to perform. He added that the puzzle therefore shows self-binding resolutions are irrational whenever the future action would be painful and pointless at the moment of performance.

I would like an honesty check on his interpretation. Please go through his claims one by one, separating what a careful philosopher would accept from what is overstated, subtly conflated, or just wrong. In particular, I want to know whether the puzzle really licenses the sweeping conclusion about self-binding, or whether that rests on a slide between reasons for intending and reasons for acting. I am not asking for a bibliography or a history of the debate; I want the conceptual audit itself, precise enough that I could use it to write a character who tries to make a costly resolve credible to himself and to others.
