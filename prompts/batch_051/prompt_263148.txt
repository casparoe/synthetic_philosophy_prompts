For the last two years I've been sitting in on a hospital ethics committee where two members have been arguing past eachother about advance directives. One of them holds what you could call the earlier-self-authority view: a competent person's informed and settled wishes should keep governing her care even after she's cognitively transformed and no longer endorses them, and honoring the old directive is itself a way of respecting her as the same person. The other holds a later-self-welfare view: once someone's values and capacities have changed that radically, the person in front of you right now should be the main object of concern, and the directive can be overridden when following it would make the later self worse off in the ways she can still experience or care about. They've finally agreed to stop trying to win and to produce an adversarial-collaboration statement they both can sign. Write that document for them.

Please give it this structure: (1) a numbered list of claims both parties genuinely accept, stated strongly enough that they're not empty; (2) the remaining disagreements stated in wording each side would accept as fair to its own view, without slanted verbs or scare quotes; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of them; and (4) for each crux, at least one concrete case or observation both would accept as bearing on it, plus each side's prediction about it recorded in advance. You can use this recurring example from their meetings: a woman signed a directive at fifty-five refusing antibiotics for a future state of advanced dementia, and now, in roughly that state, she smiles at visitors, seems to enjoy music, and gives no sign of wanting the treatment — while the document points the other way. I suspect one crux is about what gives an old directive its authority in the first place (a promise-like commitment, a permission from the earlier self, continuing identity, or something else), and another is whether the later self's apparent contentment should count as her current wellbeing or as a misleading snapshot of a life that no longer has the shape she cared about. If part of the dispute looks verbal rather than substantive, say which part and why.
