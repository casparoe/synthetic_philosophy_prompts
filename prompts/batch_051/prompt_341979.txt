I work as a software engineer on the ranking stack of a large social platform. My team treats observed user behavior—opens, dwell time, click-throughs, whether a session ends immediately after a recommended item—as evidence about what users actually want. We fit reward models from this behavioral data using inverse reinforcement learning, then train policies to maximize the fitted reward. I have no formal background in philosophy, but I have been trying to get clear on the assumptions this whole pipeline rests on.

I recently read Andrew Ng and Stuart Russell's paper Algorithms for Inverse Reinforcement Learning. The passage that stopped me is the one where they show that recovering a reward function from an observed policy is underdetermined: for a given policy, many reward functions are consistent with it as optimal, including degenerate ones that assign zero reward everywhere or that differ only in states the policy never visits. So observed behavior alone does not pick out the reward function that generated it without strong additional constraints.

Our production system, however, behaves as if the inverse problem is basically solvable. We take short-term engagement behavior as revealed preference and treat the recovered reward function as a legitimate objective for deciding what users should see next. I want someone to reconstruct the philosophical commitments that this practice presupposes. In particular, does it presuppose that human choice behavior under conditions of limited attention, variable self-control, and algorithmic manipulation reliably reveals a well-defined preference ordering? Is the underdetermination point from Ng and Russell a technical problem that disappears once we add the right regularizers, or does it reappear as a deeper problem about the difference between what people do and what they value? And if we took that underdetermination seriously, what would have to change in how we design the objective, evaluate the system, or describe what it is doing to users?
