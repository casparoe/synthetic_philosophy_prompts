Right, so I'm giving a presentation next month to the clinical ethics forum at a big teaching hospital — mostly genetic counsellors and clinical geneticists, plus two patient reps from a disability advocacy group, one paediatrician, and a couple of academic bioethicists. The talk defends a moderate version of procreative beneficence, roughly: in IVF, when a family is already choosing among several viable embryos, they (and the clinician advising them) have a pro tanto reason to prefer the embryo with the best expected life — lowest risk of serious disease and the best projected overall wellbeing — and this is compatible with thinking society still owes disabled people real support, and with parents having real latitude about non-disease traits. I want to argue this is not the bad sort of eugenics and that the taboo on any selective preference is overblown.

My rough argument, so you know what they'll be aiming at: (1) if you can choose between two children, neither of whom exists yet, and one can expect a life with considerably less pain, more options, and better health at no extra cost, it's odd to say the choice is morally neutral; (2) we already accept lots of selection-adjacent practices (prenatal screening, choosing a younger sperm donor, not conceiving during a rubella outbreak) and most people think those are fine; (3) what makes eugenics bad is coercion, state control, and a ranking of which lives are 'worth living', none of which my view needs; (4) refusing to select doesn't avoid value judgements, it just quietly makes one about not caring about the expected wellbeing of the child you could have.
