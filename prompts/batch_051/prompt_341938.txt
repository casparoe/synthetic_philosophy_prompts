I'm a novelist, and I've been fiddling with a collection about subtext — all the stuff a conversation communicates without anyone actually saying it. I keep circling back to this Gricean distinction between sentence meaning and speaker meaning, what a sentence literally says versus what a speaker implicates by saying it. Classic move: a recomendation letter that says "He was always punctual and his penmanship is lovely" — literally true, literally positive, but what it implicates is that there's nothing better to say, so the guy's a dud. Same words, same literal content, wildly different message.

Write me a short story (roughly 1,200 words) where the whole plot runs on that gap between the said and the implicated. I want a reader who works through the subtext to get the real story, and a reader who takes every line literally to get a much thinner, mostly wrong one. A few things I'd like it to pull off, as unobtrusively as possible:

- A scene where a character flouts one of Grice's maxims on purpose to generate an implicature — answering "How was the play?" with "The costumes were... detailed," or replying to a serious question with an obvious non sequitur. That flouting should do actual narrative work, not just sit there being clever.
- A particularized conversational implicature that only lands because of something very specific in the world of the story, so the same line outside that context wouldn't carry the same sting.
- A moment where somebody cancels an implicature — the textbook Gricean test is that conversational implicatures can be canceled ("...not that I'm saying she's lying") — and show how the cancellation doesn't really erase the inference, just leaves it hanging there awkwardly.

Don't let any character explain Grice or break the fourth wall; I want the machinery completely below the surface. And please write it in American English.
