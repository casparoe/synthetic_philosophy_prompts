I've gone down a bit of a rabbit hole lately about politicians who have to make genuinely awful choices—not cartoon villains, but people who think they're doing the best thing for the most people and still feel sick about it. My sister keeps saying that if it was truly the right call, then guilt is irrational: you can't have it both ways. But that seems to clean to me. I have this half-formed idea that a mayor can be required to do something, and it can still be wrong in a way that stays on them—like a stain that no good outcome can fully wash off, regardless of weather it feels gross to talk about guilt and duty at the same time.

So I'm trying to build a single realistic story where these three stances come apart on the same facts. The case should be ordinary politics: an elected mayor or similar, no ticking bombs, no sci-fi or perfect foresight, and doing nothing should be a real decision with real costs, not a way to keep your hands clean. The three positions I want to put tension on are: (1) an act consequentialist type, who says once the costs and benefits come out right, the mayor did the right thing and any leftover guilt is just emotional noise; (2) a strict deontologist type, who says the act was wrong full stop and you don't do evil so good may come; (3) a dirty hands person, who says the mayor should have done it but should still carry the wrong—feel guilt, apologize, maybe owe something to the victim's family. Make sure the case earns the dirty hands verdict instead of just stipulating it: the person harmed should be wronged in a way that doesn't vanish once the benefits are counted up.

After you sketch the story, walk through the reasoning each position gives. Then be honest about which structural feature is doing the real work—my guess is it's something about harm being used deliberately as a tool rather than coming along as a side effect, but I could definitely be wrong. Then tell me what happens if you remove that feature: does the case collapse back into something where everyone roughly agrees? And finally, play the act consequentialist's advocate: how would they argue the dirty hands person is just doing double-entry bookkeeping to avoid admitting the decision was flat-out right, and is there anything to that charge?
