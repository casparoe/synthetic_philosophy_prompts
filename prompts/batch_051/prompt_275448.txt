I write about science and ideas for a general audience, and I have been trying to find a principle behind the way long working hours are judged, and failing. Two people work seventy-hour weeks for a decade. One is a startup founder, a surgical resident, or a laboratory scientist; she is described in terms of vocation, grit, and dedication. The other is a warehouse worker, a call-center agent, or a garment worker on mandatory overtime; he is described in terms of exploitation, burnout, and labor abuse. The health costs and the strain on family are comparable. Most people hold both descriptions at once and see no clash: they would celebrate the first person and support regulation that caps the second person's hours. The surrounding culture points three different ways at once. The Protestant work ethic treats devotion to worldly labor as a moral calling. A statutory 35-hour week treats long hours as a harm to be limited whatever the worker says. And the category of karoshi treats prolonged overwork as a systemic failure when it ends in death. I am not looking for a sociological explanation of why these judgments coexist. I want the strongest normative principle that could generate all of them. What feature of the two workers, if any, does the real justificatory work: their consent, the quality of the work, who appropriates the gains, whether the hours express the worker's own projects? State the principle precisely, test it against the hardest cases for it, such as coerced "voluntary" hours in banking or a self-exploiting artist who has no employer to blame, and give an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, revised, or given up.
