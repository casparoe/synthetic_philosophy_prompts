A colleague of mine works on the ethics of historical injustice, and I've been trying to reverse-engineer the rule under her verdicts. She's settled on these: yes to U.S. reparations to living Japanese-American internees; no to reparations from the U.S. government to all present-day African Americans for slavery, as a group claim; yes to land-back style reparations to Native nations for treaty violations and broken land agreements, even though the original victims are long dead; no to descendants of people enslaved in ancient Rome claiming against the modern Italian state; yes to a university paying reparations to descendants of people that same university enslaved and sold, on the grounds that the institution, it's records, and its assets are continuous and the descendants are identifiable. The next case before our working group: a still-existing insurance company that in the 1850s insured slave ships and enslaved people as cargo is being asked to pay into a compensation fund for descendants of the people it insured, even though the individual policies can't be matched to named individuals today. Predict how she'll come down, what rule or rules could explain her whole pattern, and say which further case would most cleanly decide between the remaining candidate rules.
