I'm not a philosopher, just someone who fell down a rabbit hole about Zeno's arrow. In the part of Aristotle's Physics where he talks about Zeno's arrow, Aristotle reports the argument roughly like this: at any instant the arrow occupies a place equal to itself, and anything that occupies a place equal to itself is at rest, so at any instant the arrow is at rest. His comeback is that time is not made of instants, so being at rest at every instant doesn't mean being at rest over a span.

What I want to know is whether that comeback still works if we leave physical time alone but change the assumption about experience. Suppose our conscious experience of motion is built up from discrete perceptual moments, like the brain samples the world in snapshots with gaps that get smoothed over. Then at each moment of experience, the arrow would be in a place equal to itself and not moving, yet we still experience smooth motion. Does Zeno's puzzle come back as a real problem about experience rather than about time? Or does Aristotle's point still kill it, because those perceptual moments aren't durationless instants and the whole issue is just about how durationful samples get stitched together?

I'd like an essay on that: does Zeno's arrow survive if perception is discrete? Weigh the ways someone might say yes and no, but don't end with it could go either way. Defend a verdict, even if it's a conditional one. Also say which premise of the original argument is doing the work now, and whether the new version is really Zeno's argument anymore or just a different problem wearing the same clothes.
