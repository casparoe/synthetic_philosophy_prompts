Consider the following chain of reasoning about the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals. Each step, taken alone, seems plausible to me, but the conclusion seems unacceptable. I need a diagnosis: which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost?

(1) A criminal conviction is legitimate only if the defendant violated a legal prohibition that existed, in recognizable form, at the time of the conduct.

(2) Several of the offenses for which people were convicted at Nuremberg and Tokyo — most clearly crimes against peace, and on some accounts parts of crimes against humanity — were not established as individual crimes under any positive law at the time the acts were committed; the categories were consolidated in the very charters that created the tribunals.

(3) Therefore, if (1) is right, those convictions were retroactive and to that extent illegitimate.

(4) The only way to avoid that verdict, if one grants (2), is to hold that sufficiently heinous conduct may be punished despite silence or uncertainty in the positive law, because the conduct was manifestly wrong and the actor could have known as much.

(5) But every victor can say that about the defeated. The principle in (4) contains no internal constraint that would stop any subsequent conqueror from punishing whatever it chooses to call manifestly wrong.

(6) A principle of legality with no internal constraint against such use is not a legality principle at all; it is the accuser's moral vocabulary applied retroactively.

(7) So either the Nuremberg and Tokyo convictions were unjust, or the idea of legality provides no real constraint and every victor's tribunal is, in principle, on the same footing as every other.

I want to go on believing both that the post-war trials were just and that victors' justice is a genuine defect. Which step, if any, do I have to give up, and what do I lose by giving it up?
