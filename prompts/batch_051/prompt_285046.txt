I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but I've drifted into a running dispute with a friend about petitionary prayer and would like a careful assessment of the argument below. I'm not attached to any particular conclusion; I just can't locate the error, and each premise seems individually hard to deny. (1) God is omniscient, so before I ever pray God already knows whether I will pray and exactly what I will ask for. (2) God is immutable, so God's will and God's responses are fixed from eternity and cannot be changed by anything that happens in time. (3) If God already knows what I will ask and God's response is fixed from eternity, then whether I pray cannot make any difference to what God does. (4) If an action cannot make any difference to what the other party does, then performing that action with the aim of getting the other party to do something is pointless. (5) Therefore, petitionary prayer — asking God for things, as opposed to praise or thanksgiving — is pointless.

Thinking of it as a request sent to a service whose complete state and output are already determined only makes the conclusion feel stronger: in such a system, a client request would be a no-op by definition. Yet the conclusion seems too clean, and the practice it dismisses has been treated as central by many careful and intelligent people over centuries. So: is this argument valid? If it is, I would like to know which premise I should find most unsettling and why. If it is not, which numbered step is the first to fail, and what exactly does it get wrong rather than merely needing qualification?
