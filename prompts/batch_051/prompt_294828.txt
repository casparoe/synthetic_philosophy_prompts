I'm a journalist preparing a long piece on whether personal data is property, and before I write it I want to know what principle actually captures my own judgments about specific cases. Act as a professional philosopher specializing in property, privacy, and information ethics. Work carefully rather than reaching for a slogan: begin by briefly brainstorming three or four candidate principles, test each against every case below, and then state the proposal you think survives, indicating which case motivates each clause and making explicit where it rejects or revises one of my verdicts.

My judgments:

(a) A fitness app records my heart rate and sleep data and shares them with my doctor because I turned that feature on. Fine.

(b) The same app's terms of service, which I accepted without reading, let it sell my location history to a data broker that resells it to advertisers. Not fine — I never meaningfully agreed to that.

(c) A newspaper uses public court records to report that I filed for bankruptcy last year, without asking me. Fine, or at least not a taking of mine.

(d) A hospital sells de-identified versions of my treatment records to researchers studying disease outcomes, without asking me. I keep going back and forth on this one.

(e) A genetics company sells my anonymized DNA sample to a pharmaceutical company for drug development. Not fine — it came from my body even though my name was removed.

(f) A supermarket builds a model from millions of shoppers — including my purchases — that predicts household buying patterns and then sells that model. Fine; the pattern isn't about me anymore.

(g) A large language model company scrapes a poem I posted on a public blog and trains on it, sometimes paraphrasing my lines. I lean fine, because I made it public, but I'm not fully comfortable.

(h) A facial-recognition firm scrapes photos of me from the open web and trains a system that can pick me out of any crowd. Not fine, even though every image came from a public source.

Two pairs bother me. Cases (d) and (f) both repurpose my information after stripping out my identity, yet I lean toward different verdicts. Cases (c) and (h) both rely only on information I or others made public, yet I judge them differently. Is there one principle — about consent, control, identifiability, public space, harm, labor, or something else — that gets all eight verdicts right? If not, which of my verdicts should I overturn and why? Finally, apply your principle to a case I haven't decided: a lender buys a repayment score computed from my social-media posts and purchase history, where the inputs are never shown to me. Is that closer to the de-identified hospital records, the supermarket model, or the facial-recognition system — and does the principle you've defended actually deliver that answer?
