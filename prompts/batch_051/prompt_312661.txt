I'm trying to get a handle on the debate about whether quantum indeterminacy actually helps free will, specifically the side that says it doesn't. So far I've collected these arguments, and I'm pretty sure they're the standard ones, though I could be missing something and they're distinct enough to count as seperate entries:

1. The randomness objection: a chancy event isn't controlled by the agent. If the final fork in my decision process is a genuinely random quantum event, then the outcome is less attributable to me, not more. A random fork severs the link between my reasons, my character, and what I actually do.

2. The scale objection: quantum effects are at the particle level and wash out by the time you reach the neural or behavioral level. The brain is a hot, wet, macroscopic system, so our decisions aren't where quantum indeterminacy shows up, and the whole appeal is moot.

3. The wrong-question objection: free will never needed an indeterministic break in the causal chain. What it needs is that the agent acts from their own reasons and character, free of coercion and compulsion. So quantum randomness answers a question the compatibilist already showed we didn't need to ask.

4. The wrong-level objection: actions and decisions are coarse-grained, person-level events. Even if the microphysics underneath is chancy, the action itself is a higher-level pattern, and free will is stated at that higher level. Indeterminism at the level of particles never shows up in the vocabulary that matters.

5. The luck or desert objection: suppose the decision itself is indeterministic. Then which of two actions you perform is, relative to everything about you beforehand, a matter of luck. That makes you less praiseworthy or blameworthy for the outcome, not more. Chance doesn't secure moral desert; it erodes it.

I want one more argument for the same conclusion that is not on this list and not a rewording, special case, or combination of these five. It also can't rely on studies show or on citing some author. Give me the argument as plainly as you can, and then tell me honestly whether it's any good — whether it lands a genuinely independent blow, or just repackages one of the five.
