I help run a community foundation, and I keep having to referee what I've only ever heard called 'moral trade' proposals: a donor or applicant wants us to accept that a program will do real harm to one group because, bundled with it, some benefit will be delivered elsewhere — lost local jobs traded against lives saved in another county, privacy given up against disease prevented, one neighborhood's disruption against another's gain. Some of my board says a good outcome is a good outcome; others say certain harms can't be bought off no matter what is offered on the other side. We decide case by case, and the side with the most vivid story wins. The recurring question I want help with is when a harm is genuinely compensated or offset, and when it is merely excused by pointing at some unrelated good.

Please write a reusable decision procedure my board could actually run in a single one-hour meeting, with no professional philosopher in the room and no special terminology. In your response, use these sections in this order:

- 'The checklist' — the steps in order, written so a board member can literally ask and answer them, with a pass, fail, or pause at each step. (~400 words)
- 'What each step is doing' — in plain words, what mistake or failure mode each step exists to catch, and which of my two board factions it restrains and why. (~300 words)
- 'Three worked applications' — run the checklist on: (1) a telehealth rollout that will shorten waits for thousands of patients but will close the only in-person clinic serving a rural, mostly older community; (2) a funder who will pay for a school in one district only if we also administer the demolition and partial 'compensation' of a block of low-income apartments in another district; and (3) a third case of your own choosing that the checklist rejects even though you think most boards, including mine, would vote to approve it — and say plainly that this is the uncomfortable one. (~650 words)
- 'Where the checklist goes quiet' — the points at which no check mark removes the need for a value judgment, the common ways the procedure will misfire or be gamed, and what the board should do when the steps return no answer. (~250 words)
