I'm putting together a unit on concepts and abstraction for a course, and I want to use Borges's Funes the Memorious together with Bongard problems, but I'm still not sure how to frame the discussion. Bongard problems are those little visual puzzles where you get a row of examples on the left and a row on the right, and you have to say what rule or property separates the two groups — all the left ones contain a triangle, say, or all the right ones are convex. The odd thing is that people reliably find the rule after seeing just a handful of examples, even though in principle infinitely many rules fit those examples. Funes feels like a thought experiment about exactly that capacity. After the horse-riding accident, Funes can't forget anything and perceives every detail so exactly that he finds our ordinary general words absurd; he complains that a dog seen from the side at one moment and the same dog seen from the front a moment later are treated as the same dog, because to him they are two entirely different objects. The narrator suggests, roughly, that thinking depends on overlooking differences — that abstraction is a kind of useful forgetting, and that Funes is almost incapable of real thought because he cannot do it.

I'd like a reading of the story through the lense of Bongard problems and few-example concept learning. Does Funes dramatize the idea that abstraction is an achievement rather than a loss, or does the story cut the other way — would a perfect memory of every example actually make Bongard-style rule-finding easier, with the narrator just romanticizing forgetting? Where does the lens illuminate the story, and where does the story resist it? I don't need plot summary or quotations, and I'd rather you not reduce the story to a mere illustration; I want a reading that takes both Borges and the cognitive puzzle seriously and can say what the story adds that the puzzles don't.
