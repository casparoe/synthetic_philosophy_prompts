I keep turning over a parallel between the knowing-how/knowing-that distinction and the way a trained neural net holds its competence. A chess rulebook sits on the knowing-that side: explicit, inspectable propositions, each one something you could put on a flashcard. The weights of a chess engine sit on the knowing-how side: they can win games, but there is no sentence inside the weights saying how they do it, and you can't extract the policy without actually running the thing. That looks a lot like Ryle's point that competent performance doesn't require an inner shadow-performance of rules being consulted — the weights don't consult the rulebook, they just produce the moves. But maybe I'm only re-dressing Ryle's own examples in new clothes, or letting the word "implicit" do too much work. I want to know whether the mapping is deep or superficial. Concretely: is the know-that/know-how distinction really coextensive with the rulebook-versus-weights distinction? To stress it, give me one case from each direction where they come apart — something that is obviously knowing-how but couldn't be stored in weights the way we normally understand them, and something weights encode that we'd still want to call knowing-that. And if they do mostly line up, does the neural-net picture give Ryle any new ammunition against the intellectualist, or is it just his argument moved from the lecture hall into the machine room?
