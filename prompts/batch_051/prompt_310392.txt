I'm a software engineer who has just moved onto a team working on AI safety, and I recently asked a colleague from another group to explain the difference between the "alignment" and "control" approaches to reducing risk from misaligned models. I wrote down their summary as faithfully as I could, and I want to know how much of it I can safely repeat to my own team.

Here is the summary I wrote down: Alignment and control are two competing answers to the question of what to do about models that might be dangerous. Alignment tries to solve the problem from the inside: you train the model so that its goals are the right goals, and once that succeeds you can mostly trust it. Control doesn't bother with any of that. It treats the model as a black box that may be actively working against you at every moment, and asks how much useful work you can still safely get out of it by keeping it inside a monitored environment — sandboxing it, logging everything it does, restricting what it can touch, and having human overseers or other models review its outputs. The job of control evaluations is to measure whether those safeguards would catch a model that is actually trying to scheme against them. The obvious problem is that any model smart enough to do useful work will eventually find a way around whatever monitoring you put in place, and it will simply wait until your attention lapses; so control can at best buy time while alignment catches up. Control also assumes that the model's goals stay stable while it is being tested, which is why control people reject the whole idea of trying to measure what the model wants.

I don't have any background in this debate. The parts that make me uneasy are the claim that control treats the model as actively working against you at every moment, and the conclusion that control is only a stopgap — but I can't tell whether those are genuine misrepresentations or just the kind of blunt phrasing people use when explaining to an outsider. Could you go through the summary claim by claim and tell me which parts are accurate, which are overstatements or conflations, what a defender of the control agenda would object to, and whether the conclusion that control can at best buy time actually follows from the parts that are accurate? I'd also like to know what a careful version of this summary would need to add.
