I retired from a career in the earth sciences a few years ago and have been reading philosophy of probability and judgment under uncertainty ever since; I sometimes get invited to sit in with humanities seminars. A colleague who teaches comparative history has asked me to lead a ninety-minute session for her graduate students — about fifteen of them, serious historians with no statistical training — on the outside view and what it does to the study of long-lived and collapsed states. They are likely to arrive suspecting that a forecasting heuristic has little to teach historical scholarship.

I do not want the lecture itself; I want the teaching plan. Give me the order in which the components must be introduced and the dependencies between them — what has to be in place before what. Pick the single historical case that should carry the whole session and tell me what features it must have and what it must lack. Map the misconceptions this audience will predictably bring or form — I expect at least these: that the outside view is just base-rate numerology with no room for detail; that a unique event has no usable reference class; that the outside view replaces case-specific knowledge rather than serving as a prior to be updated by it; and that choosing the reference class is a mechanical empirical act rather than a contestable similarity judgment. Indicate the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off rather than corrected afterward. End with a question I can put to them to check whether they have grasped the conceptually hard step — reference-class selection — rather than merely learned to compute a survival rate, and tell me what I should explicitly decline to cover at this level.
