Consider the MU-puzzle, a formal system with alphabet {M, I, U}, the single axiom MI, and four rules: (i) from a string ending in I you may append U; (ii) from Mx you may derive Mxx; (iii) any occurrence of III may be replaced by U; (iv) any occurrence of UU may be deleted. The question is whether MU can be derived. The standard solution notes an invariant: every derivable string has a number of I's that is not divisible by 3, whereas MU has zero I's. This move is often called jumping out of the system, or JOOTSing. Here are three things that description might mean in this case. First, a change of vocabulary: one abandons strings and rules and states a property in arithmetic instead. Second, a metatheorem: one proves, by induction over the rules, that a property holds of every derivable string, making a claim about all possible derivations rather than about any particular string the object rules can produce. Third, a semantic shift: one interprets the strings so that the axiom is true and all four rules preserve truth while MU comes out untrue, thereby converting the question from proof to interpretation. Is there a fourth thing that jumping out of the system could mean here, genuinely distinct from these three and not a rewording, special case, or combination of them? If so, state it and say what it adds; if not, say which of the three is doing the essential work and why the others collapse into it.
