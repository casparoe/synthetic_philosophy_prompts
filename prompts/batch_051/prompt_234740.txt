In this exercise you will compose a philosophical dialogue of roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words on the question whether there is a morally significant difference between lying and merely misleading.

Your two interlocutors should be equally competent moral philosophers who meet after a public lecture and discover that they disagree about a single concrete case. The case is this: at a press conference, a cabinet official is asked whether a foreign government carried out a recent cyberattack. She answers, "I have seen no evidence that Country X was responsible." The sentence is true, because the documentary evidence is classified and has never been shown to her, but she knows from a classified oral briefing that Country X was responsible, and she expects the audience to take her answer as a denial. The two philosophers disagree about whether her answer is morally on a par with an outright lie.

One character should defend the separationist view: lying has a distinctive moral character because the liar asserts what she believes to be false and thereby violates the trust placed in her word in a way that merely inviting a false inference does not. Misleading may be wrong, but it is wrong through a different mechanism and is often less serious. The other character should defend the unificationist view: the distinction carries no independent moral weight, because what matters is that the speaker intends to induce a false belief and predictably does so; any asymmetry between the two practices must be explained by downstream factors such as harm, likelihood of detection, or the speaker's ability to escape responsibility.

The dialogue should contain genuine back-and-forth, not set speeches. Each character should present the other's strongest argument, respond to the actual points the other makes, and concede or revise when the argument requires it. At some point the dialogue should address all of the following:

- whether the wrong of either practice lies primarily in the false belief it produces or in the way the speaker relates to the hearer;
- whether the separationist position inadvertently licenses carefully worded evasion as a loophole;
- whether the unificationist position can explain why a bald-faced lie, which deceives no one, still seems wrong;
- whether misleading is sometimes worse than lying because the speaker can later say, truthfully, "I never said that," and what this means for each character's view;
- how much responsibility the hearer bears for drawing an invited inference;
- whether the fact that the speaker is a public official addressing the public affects the moral assessment.

The dialogue need not end with a declared victor. It should sharpen the underlying disagreement and identify any point on which the two characters converge.

Write your answer in American English.
