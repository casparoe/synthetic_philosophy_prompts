I'm a philosophy PhD student working on reproductive ethics. My advisor also reviews embryo-selection cases for a hospital ethics committee, and I've been trying to reverse-engineer the principle behind her judgments. From the cases I've observed so far:
- She considers it not only permissible but clearly right to discard embryos carrying a mutation for a fatal, early-onset disease.
- She judges it impermissible to implant an embryo with a variant that virtually guarantees a serious, painful chronic condition when a healthy embryo is available.
- She treats selecting an embryo's sex purely to balance a family as permissible.
- She has approved of couples who choose, among several viable embryos, the one with the best predicted long-term health and cognitive prospects.

A new case has come to the committee: a couple must choose between two healthy embryos, both expected to have good lives, but one has a genetic profile associated with somewhat higher expected well-being across life. The couple wants the other embryo because it is female and they want a daughter. What I'm trying to work out is which principle—or small set of principles—best rationalizes her earlier judgments, what each plausible principle would say about this new case, which verdict I should predict she will give, and what further hypothetical case would cleanly distinguish the contending principles.
