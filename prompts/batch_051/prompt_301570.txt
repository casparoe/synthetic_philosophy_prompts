I am a local historian working through the parish, court, and poor-relief records of a market town over roughly two centuries, and I keep noticing a pattern I have no precise name for. The people I can reconstruct in greatest detail are those who fell into the documentary net of institutions: debtors summonsed, defendants tried, paupers granted relief, women examined for settlement, orphans placed under guardianship. Each arrives with a thick file, while the ordinary, lawful, self-supporting majority of the town — people who simply lived there year after year — leave barely a trace. The result is an odd inversion: the archive does not merely omit the unrecorded; it promotes the recorded into the foreground, so that institutional contact begins to stand in for historical importance. I have caught myself writing a chapter in which the court calendar practically serves as a census, with the disorderly and the dependent cast as the town's main characters and everyone else as background.

A second instance shows the same thing more sharply. Often a person appears in my records only because she was prosecuted, or only because her household received relief, and the clerk has already fitted her with a category — vagrant, pauper, recusant, lunatic. The category is the price of visibility. After enough pages, those administrative categories start doing my thinking for me, as if the people most visible to the parish officers were the most representative of the town.

I know this lies near survivorship bias and selection bias, but both feel too general: what survives here is not a random or luckily preserved sample; it is systematically selected by coercive institutions, and the bias concerns not only which evidence survives but whose lives get narrated at all. I have also heard historians speak of silences in the archive, but as I have encountered that phrase it usually means people or topics left out or erased. Here the silence sits with the unrecorded majority, while the surviving records are noisy with the punished and the poor — almost the obverse. Is there an established concept for this particular inversion, where documentary richness tracks institutional coercion rather than ordinary significance and then presents itself as a portrait of the town? If no concept fits exactly, coin one and define it. Either way I need the boundaries: how the thing differs from survivorship bias, from selection bias, from archival silence, and from the stock advice to read against the grain — and which neighboring cases it should not be stretched to cover. A bare label will not satisfy me; I need the carving.
