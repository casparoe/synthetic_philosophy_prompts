ok so ive been going down a rabbit hole on decision theory (long story, my econ teacher mentioned something about it) and this blog post explained the Ellsberg paradox like this:

"Take two urns. Urn A has 50 red balls and 50 black balls. Urn B has 100 balls, but you have no idea what the mix of red and black is. When people bet on drawing a red ball, most pick Urn A, and when they bet on drawing a black ball, most also pick Urn A. This is irrational because no single estimate of Urn B's red-to-black mix can make Urn A better for both colors, so people are violating expected utility theory. Ellsberg proved that ambiguity aversion—caring about whether the probabilities are known or unknown—is a cognitive bias, basically the same failure as the Allais paradox, and the upshot is that a smart decision maker should ignore whether uncertainty is known or unknown and just assign their best probabilities."
