Read the afterlife points system in The Good Place through the lens of reputation systems and karma economies. The usual moral people draw from the show is something like: quantifying moral desert poisons it. Tahani does good works for the ledger rather than for the people she's helping, Doug Forcett turns his entire life into a miserable attempt to game the score, and the whole apparatus turns out to be so broken that no one has gotten in for centuries. I want a reading that goes deeper than that. Use this distinction, which I find more helpful than the usual points-corrupt-motives line: a reputation system can be operating as (i) an epistemic signal, evidence about how someone will behave in a future interaction; (ii) a sanctioning system, a way of rewarding and punishing so that behavior actually changes; or (iii) a status good, where the score stops being a proxy for anything and becomes the thing people want for its own sake. Which of these three is the Good Place system actually instantiating, scene by scene? Does the shows critique land on all three, or mainly on (ii) and (iii) while leaving (i) untouched? And where does the show stack the deck: real reputation systems have context-sensitivity, decay over time, disputes, appeals, and the option to walk away, whereas the afterlife ledger has none of that. So is this really a critique of karma economies as such, or just of a badly designed one? I'd like a fair reading in both directions: one that doesnt reduce the show to an illustration of the theory, but also doesn't let the show claim a win over every real reputation system if it has only hit a straw man.
