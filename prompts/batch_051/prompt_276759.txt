I've been drafted onto my town’s “history and reconciliation” committee, which is rewriting the interpretive panels for the old fort and the land the town now sits on, and im realising the whole thing turns on a double standard I would have shrugged at before. The written sources — the garrison commander’s letters, the survey plans, the land-office records — get treated as basically neutral evidence: they were made at the time, so they go into the exhibit as “the historical record.” But the oral accounts passed down in the families that were pushed off the land — where the graves are, what the commander actually said when he read the eviction order — keep getting called legend, or “interesting, but we cant verify it.” Both sets of sources come from interested parties. If anything, the commander had more reason to put a flattering version on paper than the descendants had to keep a memory straight. So the practice is: contemporaneous and written counts as evidence, even when the winners wrote it; remembered and passed down counts as something less than evidence, even when it’s the only trace left by the losers. I want to know wether there’s a real principle under that distinction, or just the winners’ version winning twice.

Part of why I cant let this go is something I read in Walter Benjamin’s Theses on the Philosophy of History (also called On the Concept of History): “To articulate what is past does not mean to recognize ‘how it really was.’ It means to take control of a memory, as it flashes in a moment of danger. … The danger threatens the stock of tradition as much as its recipients. For both it is one and the same: handing itself over as the tool of the ruling classes.” Benjamin seems to think the whole “just establish how it really was” standard is already on the victor’s side, and that history is precisely the act of seizing a memory at the moment it’s about to be lost. Does that give me a principle that would justify the committee’s asymmetry — say, that contemporaneous records and later oral memory are genuinely different kinds of evidence — or does it show the committee is just treating the victors’ papers as neutral by default? If Benjamin is right that the danger threatens tradition and its recipients alike, should we reverse the thing and prefer the families’ memory over the commander’s letters? Or is that just swapping one interested account for another? Give me the strongest principle you can find for why people feel fine treating these two sources so differently, apply it to my fort case, and give me an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
