I am a member of our state's Historical Reconciliation Commission, which advises the governor on whether the state should make any reparative commitments, and we have one case to resolve before the coming legislative session. In 1883 the state militia cleared a valley to make way for a rail corridor; the state's own contemporaneous survey records 212 settlers killed, and the enabling acts, which were never repealed, transferred the land to the railroad at legislatively fixed prices. No one who planned or carried out the clearing is alive, and the last direct witness died in the 1940s. But the valley's present population is largely descended from the evicted families, and the railroad — now publicly traded, with most shareholders having bought in within the last thirty years — still holds title to much of the corridor. A petition asks the state to (a) issue a formal apology naming the clearing as a wrong, and (b) create a fund, financed by a modest surcharge on the railroad's in-state freight revenue, to support school construction and land-repurchase grants for descendants in the valley. The commission is split. Some members say guilt attaches only to persons and only to what they themselves have done, so the present state cannot be guilty of acts committed 140 years ago, and a surcharge would punish shareholders and customers who did nothing. Others reply that the state is a single continuous institution that has never repudiated the clearing and has quietly benefited from the corridor and its tax base, and that valley families still live under the concrete disadvantages the clearing created, so the apology and the fund are repair rather than punishment.

Draft the formal written opinion I will submit to my fellow commissioners. It should state a clear recommendation on the petition (grant it in full, in part, or not at all), give the reasoning, and deal honestly with the strongest consideration on the losing side. Keep it under 750 words, and write in American English.
