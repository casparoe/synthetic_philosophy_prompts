I've been thinking about the sphex wasp, which follows an extremely rigid routine when storing prey: it paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the burrow entrance, goes inside to inspect the burrow, comes back out, and only then drags the cricket in. If an experimenter moves the paralyzed cricket a few inches during the inspection, the wasp repeats the whole routine from the start instead of simply dragging the cricket from its new position. The lesson people sometimes draw is that what separates us from such creatures is flexibility — the ability to alter our behavior when circumstances change — and that this flexibility is what free will really amounts to. I have never understood how that follows. Please argue that flexibility of this kind cannot, by itself, amount to free will, and that the contrast between rigid routine and adjustable behavior cannot carry the weight it is usually given in discussions of free will.
