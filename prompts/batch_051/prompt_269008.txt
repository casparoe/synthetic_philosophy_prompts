I work in human rights documentation, mostly on post-conflict settings — South Africa, Argentina, Rwanda, that sort of thing — and I've noticed I run on an epistemic rule I've never actually defended. The rule is about whose word I'm willing to accept about past atrocities. When a perpetrator gives an account of what happened, especially under an amnesty process like South Africa's, where amnesty was conditional on making full disclosure, I treat the statement mostly as evidence of the speaker's incentives and self-presentation, not as evidence about the events themselves, unless it's independently corroborated by victims, documents, or forensics. I apply the same discount to memoirs by former officials and to testimony offered in exchange for reduced charges. A confession, however detailed, basically never counts on its own.

The part that nags at me is the asymmetry. I dont hold victims to anything like that standard. I'll accept uncorroborated victim testimony fairly readily — partly because victims have less to gain from lying, partly because the paper trail was usually produced by the perpetrators and the institutions they controlled. So my working default is: perpetrator statements are evidence of incentives, victim statements are evidence of events, until something forces me to revise that.

I know this is exactly the kind of rule that can be a bias for the underdog dressed up as an epistemic policy. Victims in these processes aren't disinterested either; they're fighting for recognition, reparations, sometimes political power, and the split between victim and perpetrator can be too clean for real cases. But ditching the rule entirely seems to leave me with no way to weigh testimony that was produced inside a bargain.

And am I entitled to a rule this lopsided, given that I'm usually working years later and far away, with no real way to corroborate most of what I read?
