The debate over whether 'meme' denotes a real replicator in cultural evolution or is at best a loose metaphor has, as far as I can tell, been running for decades without convergence. One side holds that cultural variants are copied with enough fidelity, fecundity, and longevity to count as replicators in something close to Dawkins's original sense, so that the Darwinian trio of variation, selection, and heredity applies to culture literally rather than merely analogically. The other side holds that what moves between minds is not copied but reconstructed: people infer, blend, and repair cultural material against a background of pre-existing cognitive attractors, and transmission is too low-fidelity and too biased for a discrete unit to persist the way a gene does. The puzzle is that the two sides now largely accept the same empirical facts about noisy transmission, yet neither moves. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is partly verbal — the two sides appear to operate with different standards for what counts as a 'replicator' — and partly substantive, turning on what Darwinian population thinking actually requires in a domain without particulate inheritance. Diagnose the disagreement along those lines. How much of it is a dispute about the word 'replicator', how much a genuine disagreement about the conditions for selective explanation, and how much a difference in explanatory standards about what it would take for a Darwinian model to explain culture? Given the diagnosis, say what would count as progress: a formal characterization of replication, a new empirical finding about transmission fidelity, a shift of question, or something else.
