My sister went vegan six months ago and last weekend she cornered me with an argument I still can't shake. It's not about meat exactly; it's about the insects killed by ordinary farming and pest control. She admitted up front that she doesn't know whether insects can suffer, and then turned that into the whole point. Her chain went like this.

1. For any given insect, I should assign some positive probability p to its being capable of genuinely bad pain; certainty that they're all insentient would be overconfidence.
2. If an insect can suffer, then its pain counts in the same basic moral currency as a dog's or a pig's pain, scaled by intensity and duration.
3. Ordinary actions—growing crops, spraying for pests, mowing—kill an astronomical number N of insects.
4. Under expected-value reasoning, those actions produce expected suffering proportional to p times N times the average badness of an insect death if they can suffer.
5. For any fixed p greater than zero, no matter how small, an N can be found (and actual agriculture seems to provide one) that makes the expected suffering enormous, far beyond harms I'd call clear moral catastrophes.
6. There's no non-arbitrary p-threshold where expected-value reasoning shuts off, so the only way out is to set p to zero.
7. But setting p to zero is claiming certainty I don't have, and the same move would let me set zero probability on any sufficiently large-scale uncertain harm—AI systems, fetal pain, maybe fish if I got skeptical enough.

So either I treat normal agriculture as a moral emergency, or I become certain insects feel nothing, or I reject part of the argument. I'd most like to keep the expected-value machinery and keep my uncertainty about insect pain, but those two seem to be what's doing the damage. Walk me through which premise actually gives way, and what I'd have to accept about the other cases if I reject it.
