Lately I have been thinking about a problem one of my colleagues calls fun theory: not whether a future can be made to score well on some set of measures, but whether it would actually be good to live in. The optimiser's position is that the question is empty, because challenge, novelty, risk, even a controlled amount of dissatisfaction, can all be priced into the objective function; a future that then maximises it is good by construction. The sceptic's reply is that this misses the point. On the sceptic's view, the standpoint from which we judge what makes a life good is itself constituted by conditions — open-ended uncertainty, scarcity of meaning, the live possibility of failure — that a thoroughly optimised future would alter or eliminate; so there may be no stable ex ante standpoint from which to specify what such a future would be like from the inside.

I want a committed answer here, not a balanced survey. Take the sceptic's claim in its strongest form and turn it into something testable. Design the best test that could in principle be run: what would be measured, in whom, over what timescale, and against what comparison conditions; which result would count in the sceptic's favour and which would drive us back to the optimiser; and then be precise about what the test would not settle even if it came out cleanly. In particular, would a positive result show that 'good to live in' is irreducibly first-personal, or merely that our current welfare measures are too crude to price in the relevant goods? If neither, say what it would show.
