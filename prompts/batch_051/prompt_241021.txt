Working title: "Inert and Market-Mediated Byproducts: Why the Waste Argument Is Not Always an Answer"

Freeganism, as I use the term, is the practice of reclaiming and using goods — especially food — that would otherwise go to waste. The paper is about a narrower question: when, if ever, a person committed to opposing animal agriculture may use animal products obtained without paying for their production — dumpster-dived meat and dairy, leftover animal products from another person's plate, eggs from rescued hens, roadkill, and slaughterhouse byproducts used as pet food. I argue that the familiar debate between a demand-based view and a complicity-based view misses a distinction that should bear much of the weight.

The demand view says that what makes purchasing animal products wrong is that it sends a market signal sustaining production; consuming something that would otherwise be discarded sends no such signal, so it is permissible, at least considered in itself. The complicity view says that using animal products treats animals as resources available for human benefit, and thereby expresses acceptance of the very practice we should oppose; on this view, even true waste is not free for the taking, because the act of taking is itself a small endorsement of the system that generated it.

My central move is to separate two questions these views tend to run together.

First: Does my act of consumption causally encourage the production of the good?

Second: Is the availability of this byproduct itself sensitive to the existence of demand for that byproduct?

I distinguish inert from market-mediated byproducts. A byproduct is inert when its existence and availability are fixed independently of whether anyone consumes or values it: the carcass of a deer hit by a car, the half-eaten meal a guest leaves behind, produce a supermarket has already discarded. A byproduct is market-mediated when it is produced in quantities — or produced at all — only because a market exists for that byproduct itself, so that even a secondhand or no-cost acquisition contributes, in aggregate, to a reason to keep producing it. Slaughterhouse offal is the clearest case: it can be rendered into pet food or fertilizer, and the income from those uses makes the slaughter operation more profitable and more stable, even though the offal is waste from the perspective of the meat buyer. Leather and gelatin work similarly. I claim, with some hesitation, that eggs from rescued hens can also be market-mediated in a dispersed way: not through the individual hen, but through a rehoming system that maintains a larger pipeline of rescued hens partly because adopters are expected to use the eggs. If that expectation keeps more hens moving through the rescue-and-adoption system, then the egg is not inert.

I then argue that even inert byproducts can become causally live through two channels. The first is signaling: eating the leftover meat on a friend's plate may reduce their felt waste, and with it one psychological cost of ordering meat in the first place, leaving them marginally more willing to over-order in the future. The second is the displacement of substitutes: an egg from a rescued hen fed to someone who would otherwise buy eggs from caged hens may be inert in itself, but its use displaces a purchase that would otherwise have gone to the industry. I treat these as second-order complications rather than reasons to abandon the distinction.

The practical upshot is a principle: one may consume an animal byproduct only when its supply is inert with respect to one's own and others' prospective consumption, and when one's consumption would not foreseeably alter anyone's future production or purchasing through signaling, normalization, or the displacement of substitutes. I concede that this is hard to apply, because inertness is an empirical property of a whole production and distribution chain, not something visible at the dumpster. I close by distinguishing rescue from reclamation: rescuing an animal can be justified by the animal's own interest in leaving a harmful system, while the question whether to use that animal's byproducts is a further question that must be settled by the elasticity of the byproduct's supply, not by the virtue of the rescue itself.
