I keep seeing referendums attacked or defended by pointing at one feature — turnout, money, voter knowledge, whether the result was really about the question — and I want the full specification before those one-feature arguments can be run. What would a theory of when a referendum is a legitimate way to settle a contested political question have to get right? Take the purpose to be guiding a constitutional designer in deciding which questions may be put to a direct popular vote at all and when the result should bind. Give me the desiderata: the cases it must handle, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the empirical regularities about voting and campaigns it cannot contradict, with the reason each is a requirement. Use at least these raw materials and say which are genuine requirements and which are merely one camp's prejudice: a well-attended but low-information vote to authorize an open-ended change (leaving the EU); a low-turnout vote on an irreversible matter; an omnibus ballot initiative of the kind associated with California that packages many provisions into one question; a routinized national practice of frequent referendums on ordinary legislation (Switzerland); a plebiscite called by an autocrat to ratify his own authority; a popular vote that strips an existing right from a minority; and the regular findings that most voters are rationally ignorant and rely on party cues or heuristics, and that wording and question order move outcomes. Mark the distinctions the theory cannot collapse, such as between authorizing an open-ended process and ratifying a fixed legal text. Then say which desiderata are negotiable and which are fixed, whether the full set is jointly satisfiable, and which single requirement does the most to exclude the stock defenses of referendums. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring named theories.
