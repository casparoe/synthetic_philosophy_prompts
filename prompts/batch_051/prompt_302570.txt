I've been chewing on a version of Williams's Makropulos argument that a friend built, and it bothers me because the conclusion feels way stronger than anything Williams actually wanted. Here it is:

(1) A life worth going on with has to be animated by at least one categorical desire—a desire for something in the world that isn't just conditional on already being alive. (2) Over an unending life, any particular categorical desire, and any finite stock of them, would eventually be fulfilled, abandoned, or otherwise exhausted. (3) So an endless life would either settle into going through motions you no longer endorse—tedium—or require an endless supply of fresh categorical desires. (4) But categorical desires can't be manufactured on demand: if you could switch one on whenever you were getting bored, it would be conditional on your wanting to stave off boredom, not categorical. (5) And if the desires did get replaced wholesale forever, the later person would share too little with you to count as you. (6) So immortality is a bad bargain: tedium on the one hand, self-annihilation under a new name on the other. (7) Which suggests that continuing to live isn't something you can coherently want for its own sake, and that death isn't really a misfortune—it just looks that way because we're picturing a finite extension of desires we currently have.

I'd very much like to keep (6), since it feels right, but (7) seems obviously false. But I can't find the exact step where the thing goes wrong, and it's possible the argument is just valid and I'm being sentimental. Can you tell me where it goes wrong, if it does—which premise to give up or which move fails—and what I'd be paying either way? Please answer in American English.
