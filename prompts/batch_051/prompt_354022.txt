I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have been reading on my own about the proposal usually called moral bioenhancement: the idea, associated especially with Ingmar Persson and Julian Savulescu, that humanity ought to use biomedical means—drugs, brain stimulation, perhaps eventually genetic or neural interventions—to make ourselves morally better, chiefly by strengthening attitudes such as altruism, fairness, and impulse control. The motivating thought is that our moral psychology was shaped for small, face-to-face life and has not kept up with technological powers that now allow a few people to harm vast numbers of others.

From my reading I have collected what seem to be the five standard objections. As I understand them:

(1) The freedom or authenticity objection. If a drug makes me behave more generously, the generosity does not pass through my own judgment and choice. The resulting behavior may be useful, but it is not an improvement of me as a moral agent. It is a way of being managed, and a virtue that has been administered rather than acquired is no virtue of mine.

(2) The contested-content objection. There is no agreed inventory of the attitudes to strengthen. Would we enhance impartial benevolence or group loyalty, anger at injustice or a disposition to forgive, deference to authority or readiness to disobey? Choosing among these is itself a moral choice, and a program of moral bioenhancement would have to impose someone's contested answer on everyone else—including, in effect, making dissenters less equipped to press their disagreement.

(3) The blunt-tool objection. The biomedical interventions actually available to us do not target morality at all. They alter broad emotional systems that are morally double-edged. There is no dial for fairness, and the same change that increases caring can also increase bias toward one's own group or susceptibility to emotional overload.

(4) The wrong-level objection. The dangers that motivate the proposal—climate change, weapons of mass destruction, global catastrophic risks—are produced by institutions and incentive structures, not primarily by individual viciousness. Making individuals nicer leaves those structures intact, and may make them harder to fix by creating the impression that something adequate is already being done.

(5) The consent objection. If enhancement is voluntary, there is reason to expect that the people who most need it will decline it. If it is compulsory or pushed on whole populations, then the state or the medical system is deliberately altering people's moral psychology without their consent—which is itself a moral wrong of just the kind the proposal hopes to eliminate.

These five seem to exhaust the objections I keep encountering. I would like one more, and I want it to be structurally different from all of them: not a restatement of any one, not a special case or application of one, and not a combination of two or more. State the objection as clearly as you can, and then tell me honestly how strong it is. In particular, does a defender of moral bioenhancement have to answer it on its own terms, or does it, under pressure, collapse back into one of the five?
