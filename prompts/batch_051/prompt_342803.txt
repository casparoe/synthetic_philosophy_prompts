I've been rereading the classic pragmatist papers on truth, and I keep getting stuck on a pair of passages that look, on the surface, like they're pointing in opposite directions. Peirce, in How to Make Our Ideas Clear, defines truth as something like the opinion which all investigators are fated to converge on in the long run, the settled belief reached at the ideal end of inquiry. James, in Pragmatism, says that truth is not that kind of static thing. Here's the exact line: "The truth of an idea is not a stagnant property inherent in it. Truth happens to an idea. It becomes true, is made true by events." So Peirce seems to say truth is what awaits the community at the end of inquiry, while James seems to say truth is something that happens to individual beliefs along the way as they prove their usefulness. Both of them call themselves pragmatists and think they are attacking the same mentalist/intellectualist picture, but I can't shake the feeling that these are two different definitions wearing the same trench coat. My question: can these two accounts be reconciled without turning one of them into a mere restatement of the other? Is James's "becoming true" just the process by which the community gravitates toward Peirce's final opinion, or is Peirce's "final opinion" exactly the sort of absolute, transcendent end-point James would call metaphysical? If they can't be reconciled, which one is actually being more faithful to the pragmatist maxim? Give me the strongest reconciliation you can, then tell me what specific part of the texts refuses to cooperate with it. Also, if you think one of them is really just special pleading, say so.
