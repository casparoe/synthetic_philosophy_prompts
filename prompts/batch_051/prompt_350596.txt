I manage a small product team at a mid-sized software company, and every quarter we end up in the same recuring fight about whether to kill a project or keep funding it. Everyone agrees the sunk cost fallacy is bad, but we cannot agree on what it actually rules out. For example, we prepaid for a year-long license to a design tool that turned out to be a bad fit. Some people say we have to keep using it to get our money's worth, others say that's textbook sunk cost and we should switch now. But then some of the sharper folks push back with cases where abandoning a project seems to matter for reasons that aren't just the money already spent: abandoning the half-built reporting feature after six months would signal to the rest of the company that this team doesn't finish things, which affects wether other teams will cooperate with us later. Or the longer we stay on a wobbly project, the more we learn about which projects will actually ship, so the spent effort isn't just gone even though it can't be recovered.

I've been asked to turn this into a single decision procedure our product managers can actually run. The stakes: we review roughly eight to twelve projects a quarter, each decision can involve hundreds of thousands of dollars of future spending, and the people running the procedure are not philosophers or economists. It has to be something a PM can work through in under thirty minutes before a go/no-go meeting, with finance signing off afterward. It should be grounded in whatever the philosophical and economic literature actually says about whether honoring sunk costs is ever rational, but only the parts that survive being turned into a tool for someone who will never read that literature. I especially need it to separate (a) costs that are genuinely sunk from costs that are recoverable or actually fixed-but-upcoming, (b) the decision you would make if you walked in fresh today from the question of what your past decisions say about you, and (c) the social and reputational reasons for continuing from the self-justifying ones that are pure rationalization.

Then run the procedure on three of our recurring cases: killing a half-built feature after user interviews show low interest, pulling out of an already-paid conference sponsorship whose product is now sunset, and abandoning a long data-migration project in a way that will be seen by other teams as proof we can't finish infrastructure. Include at least one case where the procedure tells us something we will not like to hear, and be explicit about the points where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment and someone has to decide. Keep the whole thing usable as a handout, not an essay, and finish with a single sentence starting "Bottom line:" that states the one test you would want a PM to remember if they forget everything else.
