Please argue that a serious commitment to longtermism — understood as the view that the far future may contain an enormous number of morally considerable lives and that positively influencing it is among the most important moral priorities, if not the most important — does not by itself license authoritarian political control. Engage the strongest version of the objection rather than a strawman: if the expected value of the far future dwarfs nearer-term considerations, and if reducing existential risk is therefore the dominant moral task, it can seem as though ordinary constraints such as democratic deliberation, respect for individual rights, and truthfulness should be brushed aside whenever they slow down efforts to safeguard the future. Show exactly which premise or inference in that line of thought should be rejected, and build the strongest available case that longtermism leaves standard political and moral constraints binding. In particular, assess whether the slide from the axiological claim that the far future dominates in moral importance to the deontic claim that maximizing expected long-run value is the criterion of right action is valid. Do not rest your argument solely on the claim that our forecasts about the far future are too uncertain to justify action: the challenge is to say why the constraints remain binding even when longtermists’ expected-value reasoning is granted for the sake of argument. The answer should be written in American English.
