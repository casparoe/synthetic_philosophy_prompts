Space agencies and many public research funders require proposers to locate their technology on a nine-point Technology Readiness Level scale: the low end means the underlying principles have been observed, and the high end means the system has been proven in operational flight. In the search for extraterrestrial intelligence and discussions of how to detect technological civilizations, the same habit of reducing technological sophistication to a single number is projected onto whole civilizations. The familiar Kardashev Types I, II, and III rank civilizations upward by the total energy they can command; a less common Barrow-type ranking runs in the opposite direction, giving higher status to civilizations able to deliberately manipulate smaller structures, from large artifacts down through genes, molecules, atoms, and ultimately spacetime itself. These two axes need not agree: a civilization that commands only the energy of one planet but can engineer spacetime would rank modestly on the energy axis and near the top on the small-scale axis.

What do these ranking practices presuppose about the concept of technological or civilizational advancement? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can. In particular, do they presuppose that advancement is one-dimensional; that greater command over nature always counts as advancement; that qualitatively different capabilities are commensurable on a single axis; and that the scales are merely descriptive rather than value-laden? Distinguish what the practices strictly presuppose from what they merely fit comfortably with, and say where they are philosophically underdetermined. Then explain how the practices would have to change if advancement were really a bundle of partly incommensurable capabilities, or if vast technical control could coexist with stagnation in judgment, coordination, or well-being. Finally, tell me which part of your reconstruction you are least confident in and what argument or consideration would change your verdict.
