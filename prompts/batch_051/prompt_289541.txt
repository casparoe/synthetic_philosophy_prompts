I'm trying to work out what I actually think about when it is rational to follow the crowd. My provisional principle is this: follow others whenever their choices might be based on private information that you lack. But my intuitive verdicts on a series of cases do not fit that principle.

In an unfamiliar city I see a long line outside one restaurant and an empty one next door. I join the line. I am confident this is rational, and that the crowd is doing genuine epistemic work for me.

In a village trying a new seed, farmers decide in turn, each after testing the seed on their own plot and each able to see what earlier farmers chose. The first two adopted. I ran my own test and it failed. I adopt anyway. I think that is rational: their adoptions carry information that probably outweighs my one bad test.

I have unusually good private evidence that a bank is solvent, but depositors are withdrawing, and a run itself could break the bank. I withdraw too. I think that is rational, but not in the restaurant-line way: I am not learning from the others, I am simply responding to what they are likely to do.

I am on a hiring committee that votes aloud in a fixed order. The first three members voted yes, and my own reading of the file turned up a serious red flag. I vote yes anyway, mainly because a lone no that turns out to be wrong would make me look sloppy. I know I am not truth-seeking here; I think the decision is individually rational but the opposite of what the first two cases are about.

A social-media accusation against a small business goes viral, and thousands pile on. I have direct knowledge that the accusation is false. I do not join in. I do not think I should treat thousands of shares as thousands of independent signals, the way I treat the restaurant line.

So the principle over-accepts: it would license the bank withdrawal and the committee vote, which I do not want to class with the restaurant line or the seed adoption. And it over-generates: it would treat each viral share as one more independent signal. Is there a coherent principle that captures all five verdicts, or nearly all of them? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop, and what does it predict about a case I keep going back and forth on: being the fourth person in a line of tourists all ordering the same unfamiliar dish, when the menu description sounds like something I usually hate?
