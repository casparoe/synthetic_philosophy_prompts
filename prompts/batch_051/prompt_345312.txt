I'm an SRE (site reliability engineer) by trade and I've been reading a bit of care ethics on the side, and I keep having the nagging feeling that the whole thing is just my job described in fancier language. I live in a world of monitoring dashboards, SLOs, on-call rotations, incident response, and blameless postmortems; a service is healthy when someone is watching it, someone owns the pager, someone can actually fix it when it breaks, and someone verifies that the fix actually helped rather than just quieting the alert.

The care ethics picture as I've picked it up goes something like this: care isn't mainly a warm feeling or a virtue you either have or don't; it's a practice with phases — noticing that a need exists (attentiveness), accepting that your the one in a position to do something about it (responsibility), actually doing the caretaking competently (competence), and then checking back with the cared-for that the care actually landed and was received (responsiveness). The picture also says caring relationships are asymmetric, built on dependency and vulnerability, and that the cared-for's response is partly what makes it care rather than something done to them.

I'd like you to do a systematic translation of care ethics into SRE/ops vocabulary — monitoring and alerting, service ownership, change management, feedback loops, incident review, toil, dependency graphs — as faithfully as you can. Then give me the residue report: which parts map cleanly, which parts you had to fudge or distort to get them to fit, and which parts have no counterpart at all in ops. Most importantly, commit. Tell me whether the thought I keep having — 'care ethics is basically on-call for people' — is basically right, or whether the parts that don't survive translation is exactly the parts that make it an ethics rather than a maintenance handbook. If the translation flattens it, say so plainly and tell me what I'd have to add to my mental model to not miss the point.
