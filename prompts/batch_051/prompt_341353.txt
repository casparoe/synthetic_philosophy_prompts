I'm a philosopher, and I've gotten pulled into an argument with a collegue in psychology that I keep going in circles about, so I'd like a sharp outside take. She runs a small honors seminar where students interrogate one of the big language models about arguments in the history of psychology — asking it to lay out a position, poke holes in it, notice flaws, that sort of thing. She's come to think the model genuinely believes the claims it makes: it sticks to commitments over a long conversation, revises when students show it textual counterevidence, even corrects a student who misattributes a view. My instinct is to say that's fine as a façon de parler, but 'believes' there is instrumental or metaphorical. The trouble is, I don't want to fall back on 'it's just a dumb autocomplete' either, because plainly it tracks something about the arguments: taking the as-if stance toward it is exactly what makes the seminar pedagogically useful. Sure, one could describe everything at the level of weights and next-token probabilities, but that wouldn't explain why it keeps the threads of a conversation straight.

The question we're facing is whether she may describe the course, in the catalogue, as one where students debate an AI that 'holds views' and 'revises its mind.' So: is one of us wrong, or is this really a dispute about what we're entitled to say rather than how the world is? And does the predictive usefulness of belief-talk settle it, or could it be useful and still not literally true? I'd rather not be told it's all just vocabulary unless that's genuinely the right answer, and if it is, I want to know why.
