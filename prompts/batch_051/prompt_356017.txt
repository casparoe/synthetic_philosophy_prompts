My training is in evolutionary biology, and the vocabulary I can think in comfortably is that of fitness landscapes, honest signals, the handicap principle, Fisherian runaway, kin and group selection, mismatch, exaptation and spandrels. I have no philosophical training, and I do not want an introduction to the literature or a reading list; I want a translation into the terms I already think in.

The debate I need translated is whether excellence can be good when no one benefits from it: whether a technically superb performance that no audience ever hears, an achievement no one learns of, or a capacity developed and exercised in perfect privacy is worth pursuing, or whether its value is exhausted by the good it does for others. The positions I have in mind are roughly these: (1) the excellent exercise of a human capacity is good in itself, final rather than instrumental; (2) such exercise is only as good as its downstream benefit to some sentient being, so that a private, unobserved excellence is at best wasted; (3) what gives an achievement its value is the difficult process of bringing skill to bear against resistance, so that the value lies in the doing rather than in any audience or product.

I am not asking for loose analogies. I want each of these three positions restated as faithfully as possible in my vocabulary, with every load-bearing term given a counterpart in fitness-and-signalling terms where one exists. For each position, say which parts translate cleanly, which parts you had to bend to make them fit, and which parts have no expression at all in my vocabulary. Then give me the residue report that matters to me: after the translation is done, does the sentence 'excellence is good for its own sake' survive as a substantive claim, or does it shrink to 'we have evolved to admire costly, difficult-to-fake performances'? If it shrinks, tell me whether that is because my vocabulary is missing something the debate is actually about, or because the original claim was doing less work than it appeared. And do not assume without argument that what selection has disposed us to admire is what we ought to value; if any translation needs that assumption, say so and flag it as an import, not a translation.
