I got into an argument about Leibniz and quantum mechanics, and I want a straight verdict rather than a survey of options. Here's the argument as I reconstruct it:

(1) Quantum mechanics says two electrons of the same kind are identical particles: permuting the particle labels in the wavefunction changes nothing observable (at most an overall sign).
(2) If exchanging two things changes nothing observable, then no property at all distinguishes them.
(3) By Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles, if no property distinguishes two things, then they are not really two things at all.
(4) But quantum theory doesn't treat them as one thing: the Pauli exclusion principle says two electrons can't be in the same quantum state, which would be meaningless if there were only one electron.
(5) So quantum mechanics is a genuine counterexample to the identity of indiscernibles: two things, zero distinguishing properties.

I half-suspect step (3), because it feels like its smuggling in a reading of 'property' that quantum mechanics is allowed to reject, but I can't pin it down. Is the argument valid? If not, say exactly which step fails, and whether the principle can be saved by being more careful about what counts as a property, or whether we should just give up on it. And commit: does the identity of indiscernibles survive quantum mechanics, or does it not?
