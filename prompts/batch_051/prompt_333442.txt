I'm the secretary of a small maritime preservation trust, and I've hit a genuine puzzle. Our founding deed says:

'The Trust shall at all times keep the vessel in good and substantial repair, preserve it in its original form and substance, and shall not alter, replace or part with any portion of it except where necessary for the preservation of the whole.'

Over the last sixty years the boat has been maintained almost continuously. Each year a plank or two, a rib, or some fittings were found rotten or corroded and were replaced like-for-like. It's only just occurred to me that, taken cumulatively, there's now probably not a single piece of original material left in her. The deed's authors clearly had ordinary repairs in mind, not the whole substance turning over bit by bit. So: have we kept 'the vessel' the deed refers to, or have we accidentally replaced her out of existence and put the trust in breach along the way? And weather the deed has anything sensible to say about its own blind spot.

I'd like you to act as a careful legal philosopher who thinks well about identity over time. Work through the question from four angles: the plain wording, the purpose of the clause, what the original trustees would say if we could put the point to them now, and what a reasonable outsider would understand 'preserve the vessel' to include. Then tell me which of these approaches should govern here, and why. Think it through before you settle.
