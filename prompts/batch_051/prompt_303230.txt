I recently read a popular book on consciousness whose chapter on the knowledge argument I keep coming back to. I would like to know how much of it I should trust, because I have already repeated parts of it in conversation. The book summarizes the argument like this:

"Frank Jackson's Mary's room argument is the modern locus classicus for the conclusion that physicalism about the mind leaves something out. Mary is a scientist confined from birth to a black-and-white room, where she nevertheless learns all the physical facts about human color vision — everything a completed neuroscience could say about the eye, the retina, and the visual cortex. When she is released and sees a ripe tomato for the first time, she learns what it is like to see red. Since no amount of physical information she already possessed could have told her what red looks like, what she learns must be a non-physical truth about the felt quality of the experience — a quale. The argument is therefore more than the complaint that science finds consciousness hard to explain; it is a proof that conscious experience contains non-physical properties. The standard physicalist response, David Lewis's ability hypothesis, allows that Mary changes but denies that she learns any new fact: she only gains abilities, such as the ability to recognize and imagine red. But this response is generally regarded as inadequate, because being able to recognize red is itself a matter of knowing that something is red rather than green, which is factual knowledge, not mere know-how. So the ability hypothesis merely relocates the mystery; Mary's room remains a demonstration that there are truths about experience that no physical account could state."

This sounded largely right to me, but I am aware that popular summaries often smooth over distinctions, and I want to know which parts would survive contact with the actual dialectic. Please go through the summary sentence by sentence and give me an accuracy audit: which parts would a philosopher working on the knowledge argument accept, which are fair simplifications, and which are subtly wrong, overstated, or conflated. In particular, I'd like to know whether it is right to call the argument a proof that physicalism is false, whether its treatment of Lewis's ability hypothesis is fair, and whether the final claim about truths that no physical account could state actually follows from the corrected version of the argument. Where the summary goes wrong, tell me what the more careful position is, and whether the mistaken parts are incidental or load-bearing.
