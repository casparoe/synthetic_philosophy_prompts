In my decision theory course we have been working through Newcomb's problem and the dispute between evidential and causal decision theory, and I have reconstructed an argument that seems to show that evidentialism commits us to obeying unbounded acausal threats. I find the conclusion absurd, but I cannot locate the step that fails, and I would prefer to keep the one-boxing answer to Newcomb's problem if I can.

(1) In Newcomb's problem with a highly reliable predictor, one-boxing is rational: conditioning on one-boxing, the opaque box probably contains the $1,000,000; conditioning on two-boxing, it probably is empty; and the fact that one's choice does not cause the box's contents is irrelevant to this calculation.

(2) The principle behind (1) is that the rationality of an act depends on its expected utility conditional on the act's being chosen, counting evidence about states the act does not cause. Call this evidentialism.

(3) Define an acausal threat as follows: an agent commits to punishing me iff its predictive model of me outputs defiance, and to not punishing me iff it outputs compliance, where my actual choice has no causal route to the model's output or to whether the punishment occurs—any correlation runs through information, not causation.

(4) Every acausal threat therefore reproduces the structure of Newcomb's problem: my choice is evidence about a causally insulated state (the model's output and, through it, whether I am punished), without being a cause of that state.

(5) Given (2), I should comply with an acausal threat whenever the expected utility of complying, conditional on complying, exceeds the expected utility of defying, conditional on defying—for example, whenever the threatened harm, weighted by my credence in the threat and in the model's reliability, exceeds the cost of compliance.

(6) But then I should comply with a total stranger who merely asserts that they have built a perfect predictive model of me and threatens an arbitrarily large harm unless I perform some trivial act, since a nonzero credence in that claim, multiplied by a sufficiently large harm, dominates the cost of compliance.

(7) The same reasoning iterates for indefinitely many such asserted threats, including ones demanding incompatible actions, so that no single action can satisfy all the instructions they purport to impose. Rationality would leave me at the mercy of unverifiable would-be predictors, effectively forcing me to adopt their demands as surrogate goals.

The conclusion seems unacceptable as an account of what a rational agent must do. I want to know which step in (1)–(7) is the place to resist. If the fault is in (2), do I lose one-boxing in the original Newcomb problem, or only the generalization to arbitrary asserted threats? If I keep (2), can I block (6) and (7) without either adopting a rule that assigns zero credence to arbitrary hypotheses or giving up on expected utility reasoning more generally? And what would each option commit me to?
