I keep a standing rule about long-running philosophical disputes, and I would like you to examine the rule itself. When I confront a controversy that has survived several serious restatements, and in which each side can explain away the other side's strongest arguments by observing that those arguments already presuppose the contested vocabulary, I stop treating the question as one about how things are and treat it as a choice between vocabularies, judged by what each vocabulary lets us do rather than by whether one of them mirrors the world. I apply this rule most often to the dispute between representationalist and antirepresentationalist accounts of thought and language. My worry is that the rule may simply be the antirepresentationalist conclusion wearing methodological clothes, and that by adopting it I protect that conclusion from arguments I ought to be answering. Is there a defensible version of the rule that does not already presuppose that such disputes lack a fact of the matter? Would such a version still let me set aside the truth question in the way I now do? And from the inside, is there a test that would tell me when the rule is doing honest epistemic work rather than shielding a philosophical preference?
