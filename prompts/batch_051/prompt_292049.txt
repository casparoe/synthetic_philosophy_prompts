I've been going down a rabbit hole on quantum foundations, specifically this psi-ontic vs psi-epistemic thing (whether the wavefunction is a real thing out in the world or just a fancy way of encoding what we know). I'm a software engineer, not a physicist or philospher, so I tried to re-derive the argument in my own terms and wrote it out as numbered steps. It feels airtight to me but the conclusion is kind of strong, so there must be a bug. Can you tell me exactly which step fails, if any? Or is the argument actually valid and I should just accept it?

(1) Suppose, for the sake of argument, that the wavefunction is epistemic: it's only a summary of our information about some deeper underlying physical state, the way a probability distribution summarizes uncertainty about a dice roll.
(2) Then two different wavefunctions can overlap. That is, the very same underlying physical state could be compatible both with being assigned wavefunction A and with being assigned wavefunction B. (If they could never overlap, then different wavefunctions would always signal a real physical difference, which sounds like the opposite of "just information.")
(3) Quantum mechanics contains plenty of pairs of distinct wavefunctions that cannot be told apart with certainty by any single measurement.
(4) Imagine two separate machines. Machine 1 prepares a particle in wavefunction A; machine 2 seperately prepares another particle in wavefunction B.
(5) If both wavefunctions overlap with some region of underlying states, there should be a nonzero probability that both machines happen to produce particles whose underlying states are in that overlap region.
(6) Quantum theory says there is a joint measurement you can do on the pair of particles that has four possible outcomes, and each of the four outcomes has probability exactly zero for exactly one of the four possible preparations (A-A, A-B, B-A, B-B).
(7) Take a pair whose underlying states are both in that overlap region. That pair is compatible with all four quantum assignments. But whatever outcome the measurement returns, that outcome was supposed to be impossible for at least one of the four assignments the pair is compatible with. So the underlying physical state alone would force an outcome that quantum mechanics forbids. Contradiction.
(8) Therefore step 2 must be false: wavefunctions cannot overlap. And if distinct wavefunctions always correspond to distinct physical situations, the wavefunction isn't merely information; it's real.

My hunch is that step 5 is doing something like assuming independent events have independent hidden causes — kind of like assuming random variables are independent just because the processes generating them are separated. But if you're not allowed to assume that, what would that even mean for two "separate" preparations? Is the whole thing valid only under some extra assumption, or does one of the other steps break?
