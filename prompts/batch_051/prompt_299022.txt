I've been reading a state supreme court justice's opinions on void-for-vagueness challenges to street-level ordinances, and I want to predict how she'll rule on a new one. She's never stated a single overarching test; you have to work backwards from her record.

Her record, roughly:

- Struck down an ordinance making it a crime to "loiter in or about a street, sidewalk, or doorway in a way that, under the surrounding circumstances, appears to be for the purpose of begging." Her line was that no ordinary person can tell what conduct "appears" to be begging, and it hands too much to an officer's hunch.
- Struck down an ordinance making it a crime to "behave in a public place in a boisterous, indecent, or morally offensive manner." Her line was that those terms are pure taste.
- Struck down an ordinance making it a crime to "disturb the peace and quiet of a neighborhood or family by unseemly or loud behavior." Her line was that there's no objective line.
- Upheld an ordinance making it a crime to stand or park a vehicle within 20 feet of an automated teller machine except to use it, so long as an officer warns first and gives the person a chance to comply. Her line was that the distance and the warning make it easy to conform.
- Upheld an ordinance making it a crime to use amplified sound in a city park between 10 p.m. and 7 a.m. if it exceeds 65 decibels at the park boundary. Her line was that it's an objective, measurable line.
- Upheld an ordinance making it a crime to sit or lie on a sidewalk in the designated downtown business district between 9 a.m. and 5 p.m., where the district is marked on a map. Her line was that any two officers would enforce it the same way.

Now the city has passed a new one. It makes it a crime to "remain on a marked transit platform or inside a transit station for more than 10 minutes after an officer or transit employee has directed that person to leave," with exceptions for waiting for the next arrival or having just gotten off a train. Take it as given that the marked area, the 10-minute period, and the exceptions are defined clearly enough; the whole vagueness attack is that the law gives the officer or transit employee zero criteria for whom to order out in the first place. Leave aside any free-speech or other non-vagueness issues.

Make a definite, committed call: does she strike it or uphold it? Tell me the two or three candidate rules that fit her record, which one you think she's actually running and why, what each candidate rule says about the transit ordinance, which of her earlier decisions is the closest analog, and, if you think her record still underdetermines things, describe one further hypothetical ordinance that would seperate the leading candidate rules.
