I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and in a few weeks I'm giving a short talk at my company's ML reading group. The position I'm defending is that the meaning of a language model's outputs is not fully settled by the model's weights, its training corpus, and its input-output behavior. What a term refers to is partly fixed by facts about the world and by the causal history through which the model's training signal was connected to that world, so two models that are internally and behaviorally identical could still mean different things by the same word. My main move is an adaptation of the Twin Earth thought experiment to machine learning: consider two copies of the same model that are indistinguishable from the inside but are trained or deployed against underlying environments that differ in a way their inputs never reveal; I claim the internal state alone cannot settle what their terms are about, and that this matters for how we interpret models in deployment settings we know less well than our training data. The audience is mostly applied ML engineers plus a couple of NLP researchers. They are smart and philosophically curious but impatient with distinctions that don't change a system they build; they tend to hold that meaning just is a function of distributional statistics learned from text, that an embedding is already a meaning representation, and that if two systems behave identically, any residual question about meaning is a human labeling choice with no fact of the matter. The two people most likely to push back are a senior ML engineer who thinks entirely in terms of embedding geometry and a Bayesian-leaning NLP researcher who thinks all uncertainty should be a single posterior. Prepare my Q&A prep sheet: the objections this specific audience will actually raise, not the objections a room of philosophers of language would raise, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way these engineers would phrase them. For each, tell me what to concede up front, what to hold, where the reply is softest, and identify the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Include the question I should be most afraid of.
