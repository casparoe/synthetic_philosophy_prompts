I am a retired physicist who came to philosophy of physics only after leaving the laboratory, and I have found myself preoccupied by a debate I once would have dismissed as merely verbal: what effective field theory actually establishes about the relationship between scales. Two friends of mine—one trained in high-energy theory, the other in condensed matter—have argued about this for years without either moving. The high-energy theorist holds that effective field theories are controlled approximations subordinate to a more fundamental ultraviolet completion: their cutoffs encode our ignorance of heavy degrees of freedom, their parameters are fixed by matching whenever a completion is known, and what looks like autonomous low-energy law is just the shadow of renormalization-group flow from above. The condensed-matter theorist holds that effective field theory teaches the opposite lesson: heavy states decouple, low-energy behavior is often universal across radically different microphysics, and a low-energy theory can be well defined and predictively successful even where no ultraviolet completion is known, so physics at each scale enjoys a robust autonomy rather than a borrowed one.

I would like you to draft the joint statement these two could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, in three parts.

First, a numbered list of agreed claims about the formalism: cutoffs, counterterms, renormalization-group flow, relevant and irrelevant operators, decoupling, matching, and universality. These claims should be precise enough that neither side would wish to amend them, and they should distinguish what the mathematics requires from what either side is tempted to conclude from it.

Second, a set of residual disagreements, each formulated so that the holder of the respective view would accept the wording of their own position as faithful rather than caricatured. Separate, wherever possible, disagreements about the formalism from disagreements about interpretation, explanatory priority, or fundamentality.

Third, a list of cruxes: sub-questions on which the dispute genuinely turns, such that a settled answer to any one of them would move at least one side. I suspect, but do not require, that the cruxes will include: whether the matching of low-energy parameters to a known completion is the generic situation or an artifact of the cases we happen to know; whether universality screens off the ultraviolet details or merely summarizes them; whether naturalness problems, in which a low-energy parameter is unexpectedly sensitive to ultraviolet physics, refute the autonomy picture or constitute a special circumstance that leaves its general lesson intact; and whether the existence of multiple distinct ultraviolet completions for one low-energy theory is evidence for autonomy or simply irrelevant to fundamentality. For each crux, specify the argument, physical case, or calculation both sides would accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each side predicts that test will show.

Where the two sides invoke the same example to opposite effect—a known ultraviolet completion, a lattice model near criticality, a low-energy theory for which no completion is known—state exactly what each side claims the case establishes and what would count as revising that claim. Conclude by saying which parts of the disagreement you judge to be merely verbal and which are substantive, with reasons.
