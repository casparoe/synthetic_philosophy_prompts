I'm giving a talk at a workshop on the philosophy of emotion with a cross-cultural strand, and I need to be prepared for a serious question period. The talk is on the Korean Four–Seven debate: in the sixteenth century, Yi Hwang (Toegye) and Ki Dae-seung (Gobong) argued over how the Four Beginnings — the moral emotions Mencius identified, roughly compassion, shame and aversion, deference, and the sense of right and wrong — relate to the Seven Emotions, the ordinary list of joy, anger, sorrow, fear, love, hatred, and desire. Toegye initially held that the Four issue from principle (li) and the Seven from material force (gi). Under pressure from Gobong, who insisted that li and gi never operate apart, Toegye revised the formula: the Four Beginnings are the issuance of li with gi following them, while the Seven Emotions are the issuance of gi with li riding them. Yi I (Yulgok) later pressed the same line harder: strictly speaking, li never issues anything on its own; every emotion is an issuance of gi, with li only riding as its structuring norm. The dispute is usually read as a metaphysical argument about what causes or issues the emotions.

My thesis is that Toegye's revised formula is better read as a normative taxonomy than as a causal mechanism. On my reading, it marks the difference between emotions picked out by their moral content — the Four Beginnings, conceived as good and defined by their proper objects — and emotions picked out as natural kinds — the Seven Emotions, which are normatively mixed. The objections from Gobong and Yulgok land hard against the causal reading but mostly pass over the normative one, and the debate looked interminable only because both sides kept sliding between the two questions.

The audience is mostly empirically informed philosophers of emotion together with a few psychologists working on appraisal theory and basic emotions, all of whom will flinch at talk of 'li' and 'gi' and will want me to say what these notions earn us. Two historians of Korean philosophy who know the correspondence will also be there, and they will be alert to anything that looks like me modernizing Toegye rather than reading him. Prepare my Q&A prep sheet: the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased the way those two groups would actually phrase them, which objections have real force and which are red herrings, what I should concede up front, a two-minute reply to each, where each reply is softest, and the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.
