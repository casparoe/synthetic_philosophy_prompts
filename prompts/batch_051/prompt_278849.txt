I have a set of verdicts about everyday risks, and I want to know what principle, if any, lies behind them. I accept the ordinary definition of a micromort as a one-in-a-million chance of death, and I have deliberately kept the probabilities in cases 1 to 5, 7 and 8 close to one micromort so that the size of the risk is not doing the real work. The numbers are approximate; I am more confident in the verdicts than in the arithmetic.

1. A drive of a couple of hundred miles to visit a friend, carrying roughly one micromort of crash risk: I do this without a second thought, and I would do it weekly without ever recalculating the accumulated risk.
2. A commercial flight carrying the same one micromort of risk: I book it without hesitation. Yet I notice that the thought of a bomb or a hijacking arouses a dread quite out of proportion to its contribution to the overall figure, and I still fly.
3. Living for a year near a factory that imposes roughly one micromort of additional mortality risk on each resident, without their consent and mainly for the firm's profit: I regard this as objectionable and think the operator should be compelled to reduce it, even though I accept exactly the same probability when I choose the drive in 1.
4. An X-ray, with an estimated fatal-cancer risk of about one micromort, ordered to investigate a symptom that is worrying me: I accept it without hesitation.
5. The same X-ray, at the same risk, offered only as routine screening in the absence of any symptom: I hesitate and might decline, though I cannot say precisely why the expected benefit should matter if the risk is the same.
6. A single recreational parachute jump, with an expected fatal risk of about ten micromorts: I decline, and I have declined for years. I suspect my real objection is not to the ten micromorts but to the fact that I would be purchasing a danger I do not need.
7. A one-micromort risk of death taken to pull a stranger from a burning car: I take it without pausing, and I would think worse of someone who refused on the ground that the expected burden was one micromort.
8. Work that is otherwise unremarkable but adds about one micromort per year of risk of a slow and painful death two decades later: I would not take it for a trivial premium, even though I accept the same probability of death by crash when I drive to see a friend for free.

I am aware that the usual psychometric factors — dread, voluntariness, control, unfamiliarity — will be adduced to explain all this. My question is narrower and more demanding: is there a defensible principle of acceptable risk that actually licenses some of these factors and rules out others, and that can say which of my verdicts are reasonable and which are mere bias? I notice two pairs in particular that I may not be able to hold together: 1 against 3, and 4 against 5. Please do not lay out the candidate positions and leave me to choose. Take the strongest committed position you can: state the principle, apply it to all eight cases, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to overturn or modify, and tell me its verdicts on a few cases I have not listed — for example, the same one-micromort risk taken by someone else on my behalf without asking, and a one-micromort risk whose only possible victim is me after a long and healthy life. I would rather have a clear principle that convicts one of my verdicts than a menu of options that lets me keep all of them.
