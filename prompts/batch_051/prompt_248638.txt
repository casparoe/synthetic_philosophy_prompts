I was raised in a devout household and left the faith in my early twenties; my partner and I are now raising our two children, a daughter of seven and a son of four, without religion, while my parents and most of my siblings remain observant. The question I keep returning to is how honest we should be with the children about our unbelief, given that the family's warmth largely depends on our not making an issue of it. When my daughter asks me directly, "Why does Grandpa ask God to bless us if you say there is no God?", I half-answer and change the subject. I feel sure that lying to her would be wrong, but I am not at all sure whether evading, or asking her not to raise the topic at her grandparents' table, is a form of the same wrong or something different. On one side, honesty with one's children seems close to non-negotiable; on the other, candour can read as provocation, and I can see that my children might lose something real — a place in rituals, a sense of being trusted, an ease around the people they love — if I answer every question openly.

For a while I assumed the right policy was "private honesty, public tact": never lie when a child asks directly, but do not volunteer unbelief to relatives, and teach the children to do the same. I have begun to suspect, though, that this drafts the children into a concealment they never chose, and that the line between not volunteering and hiding is less stable than I hoped. I can also see several different things honesty might require here: not uttering falsehoods, answering direct questions truthfully, not letting the children grow up with false beliefs, not using them as props in a pretence, or openly owning what I think even when it wounds. And belonging seems to split in a similar way: being included in ceremonies and meals, being trusted with the family's actual convictions, and feeling at ease among people who hold those convictions.

Please write me an essay that works through this question and defends a considered conclusion about how a non-believing parent should handle honesty and belonging in a devout extended family. Weigh the strongest case on each side, and do not end on a flat "it depends." A qualified verdict is welcome — for example, one that distinguishes younger from older children, or one kind of honesty from another — as long as it is a genuine verdict. The essay should also say, where it matters, whether the parent should be guided primarily by what is best for the child, by what the child is owed as a person, by what the extended family is owed, or by some combination of these. Please write in British English.
