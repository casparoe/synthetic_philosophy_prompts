Take the Volkswagen emissions scandal as a case, and analyze it through Glaucon's challenge in Republic II. By the scandal I mean the 2015 disclosure that Volkswagen had fitted something like eleven million diesel cars with software that cut emissions when the car was being tested and let them run dirty on the road, together with what followed: the EPA's notice, the CEO's resignation, the criminal charges, and the slow revelation of who knew what. The lens is Glaucon's position that no one is just willingly, that what people want is the reputation for justice rather than justice itself, and that a just person given the power of the ring of Gyges would behave just as the unjust person does. I am not after the standard story about corporate greed meeting weak regulation, and I am not asking for the legal history; I want a careful mapping of the one onto the other. The defeat device was, in effect, a working ring of Gyges: it made the wrongdoing invisible to the people who could penalize it. So begin by saying precisely what the device hid, from whom, and for how long, and then ask who in the firm wore the ring — the engineers who wrote the code, the managers who signed off on the regulatory filings, the senior executives, or the corporation as a single agent. Glaucon's challenge is built for an individual shepherd who becomes invisible; does it survive being applied to a collective in which nobody is individually invisible but the organization as a whole is? Then give me the mapping in both directions. What does Glaucon's psychology predict: cheating wherever detection is unlikely, compliance or confession once the risk of detection rises, and a standing preference for the reputation of clean technology over the reality. Compare these predictions with the actual shape of the episode, including the years of undetected success, the internal dissent and whistleblowing that did occur, and the fact that this was never one person's cloak but a shared technical arrangement. Finally, say whether the episode is best read as a confirmation of Glaucon's account, a refutation of it, or evidence that the ring of Gyges is the wrong model for how and why people do wrong inside institutions — and, if it is the wrong model, what a revised version of the challenge would need to claim in order to handle a case like this. Write the whole response as continuous prose in the manner of a long email or letter: no headings, no bullet points, no tables, no bold text.
