In debates over negative utilitarianism, defenders of the classical utilitarian picture keep reaching for a bookkeeping shorthand: that well-being can be represented as a single hedonic ledger, with pleasures as credits, pains as debits, and every debit in principle payable by a sufficiently large volume of credits. The analogy does real work, because it underwrites the conclusion that pleasure and pain are commensurable and that trade-offs between them are always at least conceivable. Defenders of a lexical threshold reject that conclusion, holding that suffering above a certain intensity cannot be outweighed by any amount of lesser pleasure; yet even they often continue to describe their view in accounting terms, as if a threshold-crossing pain were simply an entry with an infinitely large negative value.

I want an audit of this hedonic-ledger analogy, not another direct argument for either side. How far does the analogy actually hold? Begin with the correspondence table: what in the domain of pleasure and pain corresponds to the ledger itself, the individual entries, the unit of account, the running balance, the owner of the account, and the settling of a debt. Then identify the places where the mapping breaks. Press especially on the features the accounting picture quietly imports: that intensities aggregate linearly; that a brief intense pain is fungible with a long dilute pleasure; that a bad in one life can be offset by goods in another, or by many small goods spread across different people; that there is always a determinate answer to how much pleasure would be needed to cover a given pain; and that well-being adds up across moments. For each disanalogy, say whether it matters for the tradability conclusion the analogy is used to support, or whether it is only a harmless convention. Finally, consider whether the lexical-threshold position is best understood as rejecting the ledger outright, as keeping the ledger and assigning an infinitely negative balance to threshold-crossing pains, or as doing something else — and say which reading is most charitable, and what remains of the ledger once that reading is worked through.
