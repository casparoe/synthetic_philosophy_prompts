I chair the quarterly grants panel for the Green Innovation Fund at a mid-sized UK university, and we have about £180,000 a year to allocate. The panel meets for an hour, the finance director signs off on everything we approve, and nobody on the panel has read moral philosophy, so whatever we use has to be quick to run and easy to explain afterwards.

We keep splitting over the same question. Half the panel says our job is to make the university itself frugal—turn the heating down, repair equipment rather than replace it, cut back on flights—because an institution that tells students and staff to consume less has to be seen to do it, and because wasteful spending is the very thing we exist to stop. The other half says that's just wanting to feel virtuous rather than actually change how much waste there is in the world. On their view, what matters is that frugality gets promoted and adopted, so if the best way to create more frugal behaviour across the sector is to spend money in ways that look decidedly unfrugal—flying people to conferences, paying a communications agency, putting on a launch event—then that is what the fund should pay for. The first group calls the second group hypocrites; the second group calls the first group inward-looking.

I want a written decision procedure we can run on every bid, one that does justice to both positions rather than just splitting the pot between them. It should be grounded in whatever moral philosophy actually says about the difference between practising a virtue and promoting it—the usual deontological-versus-consequentialist split, but applied to a single value—yet the theory has to be translated into questions a bursar can work through in five minutes. I want: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it stops being a procedure and hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Be honest about which steps look algorithmic but are actually judgement calls.

Then run the procedure on three bids:

(a) replacing the last three gas boilers on the estate with heat pumps, which would use the whole annual fund but would end our own largest source of energy waste;
(b) flying six staff to a European universities network to persuade other institutions to adopt a shared frugality toolkit, with claimed but hard-to-verify sector-wide savings;
(c) a student-facing app that nudges down energy and water use in halls, together with a polished launch event that the students' union thinks is wasteful PR.

Dont make the procedure rigged so that we always fund the one that makes us feel good about ourselves. If the procedure tells us to reject something we would love to fund, I want to see that. Also say plainly where the procedure is likely to misfire and when we should override it.

And write the answer in British English.
