Consider the following difference in ordinary reactions. A demographer who remarks that the Second World War killed more people than the First World War, or a public health official who chooses a policy because it is expected to prevent more deaths than the available alternative, is generally regarded as doing something sober and responsible. Yet if a museum, a journal, or a public figure set out to determine whether the Holocaust was a worse crime than the Soviet forced labour camps, or whether the Cambodian genocide was worse than the Rwandan genocide, many of the same people would call the exercise obscene or at best gratuitous, even where the underlying figures were not in dispute. Both comparisons concern very large totals of violent and unjust death; only one is treated as morally objectionable.

What, if anything, justifies this asymmetry? Is there a principled difference between comparing harms in order to describe or prevent them, and comparing them in order to rank their evil, or is the prohibition merely a social convention protecting survivors and sparing politicians from awkward questions? I would like the strongest candidate justification you can construct, tested against nearby cases—criminal sentencing, which ranks even grave wrongs retrospectively; a memorial that records how many victims a particular camp or killing field produced; a government deciding which past atrocity to commemorate—and an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, narrowed, or abandoned.
