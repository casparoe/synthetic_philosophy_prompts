I keep returning to a puzzle about two common ways of electing a legislature, because the more I think about them, the more they seem to rest on different pictures of what a vote is. In a first-past-the-post system, each district elects one member; the candidate with the most votes wins even when a majority of the district voted for someone else, and a party can turn forty percent of the vote nationwide into a comfortable majority of seats. In a proportional system, a party that wins ten percent of the vote receives something close to ten percent of the seats. Both arrangements are called representative democracy, and both are defended as fair to voters, but the word fair seems to be doing different work in each. I would like to understand what each system is implicitly presupposing about the point of an election. Does a vote mean something different in the two cases: a choice of a local person, an endorsement of a party, a judgment about a district's shared interest, or a preference about how the country as a whole should be governed? What is being represented: a geographical community whose interests get bundled into one seat, or an electorate whose spread of opinion should be reflected in the assembly's makeup? Do the two systems presuppose different standards of fairness, one tied to giving every district a voice and a clear way to remove bad rulers, the other tied to giving every sizable body of opinion its due share? And would a single coherent theory of representation justify both systems, or does choosing between them already require choosing between two different things that our word representation can mean? I am not asking which system is better; I am asking what each one has to assume about voters, representation, and fairness for its rules to make sense as a way of giving citizens a say.
