I have recently inherited, at short notice, a two-hour undergraduate unit on Frege's Begriffsschrift from a departing colleague, and I have found that my students treat the step from Aristotelian syllogistic to modern logic as a change of notation rather than a change in what can be said at all. They can already manipulate quantifiers, but they cannot articulate, from the inside, what the old subject–predicate machinery could not do; the result is that Frege looks to them like a fussy reformatter. I want to open the unit with a structured activity in which the limitation of syllogistic announces itself to the students in use, before I name it.

You are a philosopher of logic and an experienced designer of active-learning sessions in formal subjects. Please think carefully, begin by sketching two or three candidate mechanisms for the session and why you prefer one, and then give me a fully runnable design for the following setting: sixteen third-year undergraduates who have completed one term of Aristotelian syllogistic (the square of opposition, conversion, distribution, the valid moods) but have not yet been taught quantifier–variable notation. We have ninety minutes, four tables of four, a whiteboard, and ordinary cards and paper.

The centerpiece should be a deliberately limited inference machine that the students operate: a set of physical or procedural rules that implements only subject–predicate reasoning — moving terms between subject and predicate position, checking distribution, applying valid moods — and that is powerful enough to certify several familiar valid arguments, so that the groups come to trust it. Then they should be confronted with material the machine cannot swallow: a relational inference of the sort found in the nineteenth-century example that from all horses are animals one may infer every head of a horse is a head of an animal, and a case of multiple generality such as the two readings of every student read some book. The failure should come from the machine itself: the groups should find themselves forced either to distort the premises or to stall.

Lay out the rules and materials for the machine, the exact prototype sentences you would use, a second Frege-inspired apparatus (function–argument cards with places to be saturated, or some equivalent) introduced only after they have hit the wall, and a minute-by-minute timeline through the ninety minutes. Then give me the debrief: how you would map what happened in the activity onto the real conceptual changes — why relations cannot be squeezed into subject–predicate form, why all and some are second-level, and how nested dependencies between quantifiers appear in the second apparatus. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it, and be honest about which feature of Frege's actual achievement the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by me afterwards.
