I work in formal epistemology, and I keep noticing a structural parallel between two idealizations that are almost never discussed side by side: the logical omniscience problem in epistemic logic and the doctrine of constructive knowledge in law. In standard possible-worlds semantics, an agent is modeled as knowing every logical consequence of what they know and every logical truth, because knowledge is truth throughout the agent's set of accessible worlds and logical truths hold at all worlds. The field mostly treats this as a defect in a descriptive apparatus. The law, by contrast, imputes knowledge deliberately: a party may be found to have constructive knowledge of facts they never actually inferred, where the information available to them plus the diligence a reasonable person would have exercised would have disclosed those facts. Willful blindness is the sharper version, in which an agent who arranges not to know is treated as knowing anyway.

What I cannot stop seeing is that both proceed from an agent's actual evidential state, apply an idealized inference operation, and then treat the output as if it were the agent's own knowledge; and both sit uneasily between a descriptive reading ("this is what the agent knows") and a normative reading ("this is what the agent is answerable for knowing"). My working hypothesis is that these are two instances of a single move, which I have been calling epistemic imputation: the law does it for reasons of culpability and institutional workability, while epistemic logic does it for mathematical tractability. I am not sure how deep the identity is.

I would like a diagnosis of whether this parallel is deep or superficial. Please state the mapping exactly, mark where the two structures coincide and where they diverge, and say what would have to be true of both sides for any result to transfer. If the parallel is deep, can anything actually transfer? For instance, would awareness models, impossible worlds, or algorithmic knowledge from non-omniscient epistemic logic provide a better formal rendering of constructive knowledge than the law's reasonableness standard, and does the legal distinction among actual knowledge, inquiry notice, and willful blindness track the formal distinction between explicit and implicit knowledge? If the parallel is superficial, I want to know the precise joint at which the analogy breaks.

One stress test already makes me hesitate. In possible-worlds semantics, an agent with inconsistent information vacuously knows every proposition, while no court treats a party with conflicting evidence as constructively knowing everything. I am tempted to read this as showing that law operates with a bounded, awareness-restricted closure rather than full logical closure, which would make the parallel deep rather than shallow, since bounded closure is exactly what non-omniscient epistemic logic models. But I can also see the opposite reading: that the law is not modeling an epistemic state at all but assigning responsibility under a norm of inquiry, and the shared word "knowledge" is doing misleading work. Which reading survives the stress test, and what does that tell me about whether the parallel is worth pursuing?
