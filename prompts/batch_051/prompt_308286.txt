I am drafting a short essay for a philosophy of computation seminar, and I would like detailed comments before I revise. The working claim is that the familiar difficulty of reasoning about mutable state is usually misdiagnosed as a difficulty about time and change, when it is really a difficulty about the non-compositionality of identity. My current draft follows. I would especially like comments on whether the central distinction is doing real work or merely relabeling the problem, whether the essay is interesting enough to carry a short piece, whether the analogy to intensionality is helpful or misleading, and where a careful critic would charge overstatement. Suggestions for cutting or developing particular paragraphs are also welcome.

A variable in a pure functional program is a fixed name for a value. In a language with assignment, a variable is better understood as a fixed name for a location: the location abides while the value stored there changes, so identity and state come apart. Assignment makes the point explicit — x := x + 1 is not a claim that x equals x + 1, but a command to change the contents of a place while the place itself continues. Once identity and state come apart, two kinds of sameness diverge, namely the same location with a different value and the same value in a different location. The judgment that two program fragments are interchangeable can then no longer be read off from their current values alone. Two expressions that print the same number may behave differently because one of them denotes a cell that is also reachable under another name. Substitutivity of equals fails not because the value of x is unclear, but because the identity of x is invisible in the value yet relevant to the outcome.

This suggests the core cost of mutation is intensional rather than temporal. We can reason about change over time well enough when time is an explicit parameter; a function from times to values composes like any other function. What breaks is compositional reasoning — reasoning in which the behavior of the whole is fixed by the behavior of the parts — because the meaning of a term now depends on an identity that is global, hidden, and shared. Aliasing generalizes the problem. If a and b happen to refer to the same list, appending through a changes what one sees through b; if they refer to two equal but distinct lists, it does not. Yet whether a and b are aliased is a fact about the whole object graph rather than about either argument, so local reasoning about one part in isolation stops being reliable. Complete information about the graph would restore reliability in principle, but finite reasoners cannot keep the whole graph in view, and language designers keep reaching for rules that make this global fact local: immutability, value semantics, ownership, separation logic, and the like.

One may then read much of programming-language design as a series of attempts to confront the split directly. Immutability refuses one side of the split by denying that an abiding identity can change; there are values, and no persisting thing with shifting contents. Value semantics extends this stance to compound data. Ownership and separation make identity local by allowing a persisting location to change but not to be widely shared. Object identity and pointer comparison take the opposite strategy: make identity explicit so that hidden sharing can at least be checked. That all these designs remain awkward in their own ways suggests the underlying idea — a persisting individual whose observable condition alters — is one we handle fluently in everyday life but have not found a clean formalization for.

I would be grateful for comments. In particular: (i) Is the claim that time is the wrong culprit convincing, or am I merely redefining the problem? (ii) Does the term "intensional" invite confusion among philosophers, who typically use it for meanings or modes of presentation rather than for hidden identity in the sense used here? If so, what should I call the phenomenon instead? (iii) Do I overstate when I say finite reasoners cannot keep the whole graph in view, given that even with the whole graph displayed the reasoning remains error-prone? (iv) What is the strongest objection from someone who thinks mutation is hard simply because it breaks equational reasoning, without any appeal to hidden identity? I would also welcome advice on whether the final sentence collapses into platitude and how to replace it.
