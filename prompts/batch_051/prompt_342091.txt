I'm in a course on contractualism and I keep going in circles on Scanlon's aggregation problem. The way our instructor sets it up, Scanlon has an individualist restriction: each person's reasons count on their own, but you're not allowed to bundle lots of separate complaints together so that they outweigh one person's stronger complaint. In the rescue case — you can save one stranger or save five strangers, everyone in equal danger, no special ties to anyone — he still wants to say you may, maybe should, just save the five. He says this doesn't violate the restriction because the justification isn't that five lives outweigh one; it's supposed to be something about each person's best chance from behind a veil, or a fair selection procedure, whatever that amounts to. Fine. But then someone in my study group wrote this on the board and I can't find the hole:

(1) If Scanlon's individualist restriction is right, a principle can't be defeated by adding together the claims of several people; the number of people on a side gives no single person a stronger reason.
(2) In the one-vs-five rescue case, (1) means the five claims, taken together, don't outweigh the one person's claim.
(3) The only way to justify saving the five instead of running a lottery that gives each of the six an equal shot is to say the five claims, taken together, do outweigh the one claim.
(4) So, given (1)–(3), Scanlon can't justify saving the five rather than running the lottery.
(5) But Scanlon clearly wants to justify saving the five.
(6) So either the individualist restriction goes, or Scanlon is stuck as a Taurek-style coin-flipper, which he explicitly doesn't want.

I suspect (3) is doing the smuggling, but I can't articulate the alternative justification without feeling lik I'm just re-labelling aggregation through words like "chance" or "procedure." So: is the argument valid, and if it is, which premise has to go? If (3) is the failing step, give a committed account of the non-aggregative justification for saving the five that doesn't secretly sum claims — or, if you think every candidate justification secretly does sum claims, say that flat out and explain what that costs Scanlon.
