I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so go easy on me. I'm writing a novel set in a world where karma basically works: its not a metaphor or a vague spiritual mood, it's an observable force, a kind of moral physics. In the book, a character has just lost her flat in a fire, and another character—a retired economist—tries to convince her that karma, even if it works, can't function as a theory of moral desert because the book-keeping doesn't add up. I've written his argument out as numbered steps below. I can't tell wether I've made him too clever or given him a silent hole. Could you tell me if the argument goes through, and if it doesn't, exactly which step fails?

(1) If karma is a theory of moral desert, then for any specific piece of good or bad fortune there must be a determinate answer to which past deeds produced it, for whom, and in what proportion.
(2) Take a single piece of bad fortune: a fire that destroys a block of flats.
(3) In an ordinary karmic world, that fire will be the causal upshot of several different people's past deeds at once: the tenant who once did something cruel, the arsonist who started it, the landlord who neglected the wiring, the firefighter who was slow.
(4) Those people have different desert profiles: for one, the loss may be exactly what she had coming; for another, it may be undeserved, or not yet earned, or earned by some other deed altogether.
(5) But the fire is one event, and it does its damage to all of them at once.
(6) So a karmic theory must apportion the harm of this one event across several people with different desert claims.
(7) There is no non-arbitrary way to apportion the harm of a single event across those different desert claims; any division just reflects a choice about who 'really' deserved the harm.
(8) Since karma demands such an apportionment and cannot supply one, it cannot say what anyone actually deserved from the fire.
(9) A theory that cannot, even in principle, say what anyone deserved in a central case like a shared fatal disaster is not a theory of moral desert.
(10) So karma is at best a story about how deeds tend to shape lives; it is not a theory of moral desert.

Please structure your answer in four sections, in this order. 'Verdict' — two or three sentences saying whether the argument is valid and, if not, which step you would reject first. 'Diagnosis' — about 300 words walking through the argument, giving the corrected version of the failing step and saying whether the conclusion still follows once corrected. 'What the character gets right' — about 150 words on the part of the argument that still has force even if the overall thing fails. 'What would change my mind' — about 100 words on which alternative reading of karma or of desert would flip your verdict, or where your own diagnosis is most likely to go wrong.
