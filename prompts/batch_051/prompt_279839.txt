I'm a high school senior in a philosophy elective on classical Chinese thought. All semester our teacher has been pressing us on one notion: wu-wei, which she glosses as acting without forcing, the way a practiced movement carries itself. She also keeps returning to the self-defeating side of the idea—that the more a person consciously chases effortlessness, the more effortful the whole thing becomes.

Because students keep bringing examples to class, the teacher has accumulated a series of small rulings. I have kept a log of five of them, and for our final project I need to work out what her pattern commits her to in a new case. I am not asking you to do outside reading; everything you need should be in the cases themselves.

Here are the five cases, with her verdicts as I recorded them, paraphrased rather than quoted.

1. A cellist who has played the same suite for twenty years performs it at an informal gathering. Partway through she stops noticing herself, and afterward says her fingers seemed to know the piece without her. The teacher called this a clear case of wu-wei.

2. At a driving range, a beginner is told to stop forcing his swing. Before each attempt he repeats the instruction to himself—relax, do not force it—tightens up, and hits a poor shot. The teacher said this is not wu-wei, because the instruction to be effortless had itself become one more thing he was trying hard to do.

3. A woodworker, usually unhurried, is behind on a commission. He works against the grain of the wood, forces a joint, and keeps checking the clock. The teacher said this is not wu-wei, because he was imposing a timetable on the work instead of following the material where it wanted to go.

4. A swimmer who trained with a club through childhood is caught in a rough current. Instead of fighting it, she lets the water carry her and later reports that there was no point at which she made a decision. The teacher called this wu-wei.

5. A self-taught calligrapher with a decade of daily practice works through an entire scroll without once thinking about how the piece will be judged, and says afterward that the brush moved by itself. The teacher called this wu-wei.

The new case I need to predict is this. A retired professional dancer is being filmed for a short documentary and performs a piece she danced for years. Her body moves fluidly, and the director later says it looked completely effortless. Throughout the performance, however, one thought is running through her mind: let it happen, do not push, you know this piece. It works—she does not push, and the dancing is clean.

Please act as a professional philosopher who works on spontaneous action and Daoist concepts. Think the problem through thoroughly. Begin by brainstorming two or three general principles that could make sense of all five of the teacher's verdicts, testing each principle against each case. Then apply each surviving principle to the dancer, and answer this: if the teacher wants to be consistent with her earlier rulings, should she call the dancer's performance wu-wei? Which of the five earlier cases is the closest analog, and why? If the five verdicts genuinely underdetermine her answer, say what rule she would be committing herself to with each possible answer, take a side on which prediction is better supported, and describe a further test case that would cleanly separate the competing principles.
