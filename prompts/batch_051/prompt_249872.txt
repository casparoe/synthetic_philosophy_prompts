I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar called Commitment, Bargaining, and the Limits of Rational Choice, with about twenty students, most of whom have had one formal course in game theory. In two weeks we reach the unit on commitment races: the bargaining dynamic in which each side can gain by being the first to make a costly, publicly visible precommitment, because a credible commitment shrinks the set of outcomes the other side can rationally hold out for. The students have encountered the textbook examples — a bargainer ostentatiously destroying an option, an army burning the bridge behind it, a driver visibly throwing away the steering wheel — but they receive them as parables, not as structures they could find themselves caught inside. I want one 75-minute session that makes them feel the race endogenously: the advantage of committing first, the cost of committing rashly, and why the race is so hard to stop once it has begun, even when everyone knows a joint agreement to stay uncommitted would be safer.

Design a classroom game for about twenty students that runs with nothing but printed cards, slips of paper, and a whiteboard, with no real money — point payoffs only. The rules should force choices to be made under time pressure through public, irrevocable announcements of commitment, so that some pairs or teams will lock in incompatible positions and crash, and others will win by being first to narrow their own options. Then supply a debrief that maps particular moments of play onto the logic of credible commitment, first-mover advantage, and mutually damaging overcommitment. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of real commitment races the game cannot reproduce and that I will need to supply in lecture.
