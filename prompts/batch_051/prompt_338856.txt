I'm working on a piece about the claim that you can get causal structure out of purely observational data — that if you find the sparsest Bayes net that satisfies the Markov condition for a distribution, and you assume the distribution is faithful to the graph, the arrows you end up with are something like the real causal arrows. My editor keeps asking why anyone should trust these algorithms, and I don't have a clean answer. The move I keep seeing is: people admit faithfulness can fail (deterministic mechanisms, exact cancellation of two paths, latent common causes), then wave their hands and say violations are rare, or measure-zero, or not the kind of case we care about. I'd like to get clear on what a theory in this area is actually suppose to do before I write up any of those defenses, because I suspect several different jobs are being conflated.

So write the desiderata. What must an account of why minimal and faithful Bayes nets track causation get right? Give me the requirements, with the case or consideration behind each. Some of the ones I've scribbled down, but I'm not sure whether they belong on the list or are just my hang-ups: it has to handle latent confounders and selection bias, or at least say honestly when the story needs causal sufficiency and whether that makes the whole thing circular; it has to explain why minimality isn't just picking the graph with the fewest edges in a merely statistical sense (which would be curve-fitting, not discovery); it has to survive the fact that in the linear Gaussian case you can't distinguish a chain from a fork, so the best you get is a Markov equivalence class rather than a single graph; it has to say something about cycles and feedback, since acyclicity is built into the formalism but the world is full of loops; and it has to separate the metaphysical claim that the world really is faithful from the methodological claim that we're entitled to proceed as if it is, because a theory could have one without the other.

Then tell me which of these are genuine desiderata and which are just the prejudices of one camp, which single requirement knocks out the most popular defenses, and whether you think the whole list is jointly satisfiable. That last part is what I most want to know, because if the list isn't jointly satisfiable, then the honest story for my piece is that minimal Bayes nets don't track causation by themselves, and something else has to be doing the work.
