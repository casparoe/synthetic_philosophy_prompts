I keep going back to a disscussion I had with a friend about whether we are allowed to judge historical figures by present-day standards. He was defending Churchill — and later Columbus and Jefferson got dragged in too — and his whole case rested on the line "you can't judge them by today's standards, they were men of their time." I pushed back a bit and he got annoyed, but I genuinely wasn't trying to be difficult; I just couldn't work out what that phrase actually commits him to. It feels like "man of his time" is doing three or four different jobs at once, and the plausibility of the defence depends on which one you pick.

What I'd like is a sort of menu of the precise claims that phrase could be making. For instance, it might be a purely descriptive claim that the person's behaviour was normal in their society; or a stronger claim that they couldn't reasonably have known better; or a claim that we lack the standing to judge people from a different era; or that it's not even the same kind of judgement once you historicise properly; or something else again. For each reading: is it plausible, is it trivially true, and does it actually license the move from "he was typical of his age" to "we shouldn't condemn him"? Then, applying it back to my friend's argument, which readings would genuinely support treating Churchill, Jefferson, or Columbus with moral restraint, and which ones are just sneaking the conclusion in? I'd rather have them laid out separately than have one definitive answer, because my suspicion is that the defence lives by sliding between readings.
