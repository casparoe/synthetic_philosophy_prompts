I keep getting dragged into the free will argument at my department's philosophy club, and one guy's favorite move is to say that appealing to quantum indeterminacy to save free will is "just Epicurus's swerve again, with more physics." He treats the swerve as a reductio: Lucretius has atoms swerving for no cause at no fixed time or place, which is supposed to snap the chain of fate, but a swerve is just a random blip. Randomness is not freedom, its the absence of control. So a quantum event in the brain is like a roulette wheel in the motor cortex: it makes your action random, not yours.

But other people in the club push back that this analogy quietly stacks the deck. They say quantum indeterminism in a decision isn't a dice toss that replaces you at the moment of choice. It's more like an underdetermination: the physics doesn't fix which of your own endorsed options you land on, but your reasons, character and effort are still doing real causal work, and whichever way it tips you've already partly willed it. On that picture, the swerve analogy imports the wrong features — uncaused, arbitrary, reasons-insensitive, subpersonal — before the argument even starts.

I want to write something up for the club that actually audits the swerve analogy rather than one more round of name calling. What does the swerve quietly import into the target, and does the target have those features? After the audit, how much of the anti-libertarian conclusion is actually left? And if the swerve is misleading, what better analogy would do the work?
