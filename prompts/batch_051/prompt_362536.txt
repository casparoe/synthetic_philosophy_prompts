I've been developing a view sometimes grouped under performance legitimacy: roughly, that an authoritarian regime can be legitimate — not merely feared or grudgingly tolerated — when and because it delivers sustained prosperity and public order. I intend the view in two registers that I suspect have to stand or fall together. Descriptively, I claim that in cases like China and Singapore a substantial part of why ordinary people accept the regime is that they credit it with growth, stability, and rising living standards, and that this credit is doing real causal work rather than simply rationalizing fear or indoctrination. Normatively, I claim that a state which reliably outperforms the plausible alternatives on the goods ordinary people have strongest reason to care about can thereby give those people reasons to accept its rule, and in some cases enough reason for the rule to count as justified for them — even though the regime is not democratic, and without any claim that performance is the only thing that matters. I am not defending every high-growth autocracy: I mean it as a genuine but defeasible source of legitimacy, one that can be outweighed.

I want an honest map of what would count against this position, not just what critics already say. What observations about public opinion, protest, regime stability, or specific cases would be evidence against the descriptive claim — for example, what would it look like if acceptance were really tracking coercion or state-controlled information rather than performance, and does anything like that show up? What arguments or conceptual points would defeat or weaken the normative claim — for instance, that legitimacy requires something procedural or contractual that performance cannot supply, or that a claim of right cannot be earned by outcomes in the way I'm suggesting? Which of these are live, which are merely logically possible, and which have already effectively happened? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say for each what a defender of the position would themselves concede as counting against it (as opposed to what only a critic would say), and be willing to tell me if the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability — and if so, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind. The evidence I personally would find most persuasive is either a well-attested case where a regime that plainly delivered growth and order still lost acceptance for reasons unrelated to performance, or a convincing demonstration that the descriptive mechanisms (credit, comparison with alternatives, fear of losing gains) are post hoc rationalizations rather than grounds.

Please give your answer as a JSON object with the following fields:
- overall_assessment: a short paragraph saying where the position ends up after the defeaters are weighed.
- defeaters: an array of objects, sorted from most to least damaging, each with the following fields:
  - id: a short snake_case label.
  - statement: one or two sentences stating the defeater.
  - target: which part of the view it hits — "descriptive", "normative", or "both".
  - decisiveness: one of "fatal", "major", "awkward", or "negligible".
  - status: one of "live", "merely_possible", or "historical".
  - defender_concession: a sentence saying what a defender of the position would and would not concede about this defeater.
  - what_would_activate_it: the observation, case, or argument that would make this defeater bite harder than it currently does.
- most_persuasive_defeater: the id of the defeater that comes closest to my stated sense of what would move me.
- insulation_verdict: a short paragraph on whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability and whether that is a problem for a view of this kind.
