I keep going back and forth with a colleague about AI legal personhood. His entire argument is that corporations are already legal persons, so giving legal personhood to an AI is just more of the same. I'm writing a paper on where that analogy actually breaks, and I want a case built to order. Construct a thought experiment involving an AI system of the sort we could plausibly have in the next decade or two — nothing godlike, no human-equivalent general intelligence required — where the corporate-person analogy gives the intuitively right result for a civil liability question and the intuitively wrong result for a criminal culpability question arising from the very same episode. The civil side should turn on the AI being able, like a corporation, to bear duties and pay damages out of its own assets; the criminal side should turn on the need for a blameworthy mental state (mens rea) that can be imputed to a corporation through its human decision proceedures but cannot straightforwardly be imputed to the AI. The divergence must not come from the AI simply being property or from a sloppy analogy; it should fall out of a genuine structural difference in how corporations and AIs bear responsibility. Once you've described the scenario, walk me through the reasoning the corporate analogy would give in each domain, explain where the two verdicts come apart, and then tell me which feature of the case is doing all the work. Finish by saying how a defender of the corporate analogy would try to resist the example. Please write it all as plain continuous prose, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, and use British English.
