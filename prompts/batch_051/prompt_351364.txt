I teach a philosophy seminar on the epistemology of history, and I want to use the 2016 film Denial, which dramatizes the libel action David Irving brought against Deborah Lipstadt and her publisher, to sharpen our discussion of the line between legitimate historical revisionism and Holocaust denial. The working distinction I have been drawing in the course is that revisionism revises what the evidence says, whereas denialism revises what the evidence is. The film seems to press that distinction in a particular direction: the defense prevails not by refuting Irving's conclusions in the abstract but by tracing, document by document, how he suppresses and distorts the record. I would like a critical reading of the film through this lens. What precisely does the film take the difference between revision and denial to consist in—method, motive, institutional discipline, or something else? Where is its account illuminating, and where does it simplify or stack the deck? I am especially interested in whether the film's criterion generalizes to Armenian genocide or Nanjing denial, where the same epistemic vices may be organized by a state or embedded in long-running disputes over archives, and whether it can distinguish denial from merely tendentious or selective history in those harder cases. Which scenes should I put before students to make the conceptual points, and what does the drama show about the revision/denial boundary that the standard philosophical discussions miss?
