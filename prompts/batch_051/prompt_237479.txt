Spent the whole evening arguing with a friend about whether you can get utilitarianism out of rationality plus a unanimity condition, and he kept waving Harsanyi's aggregation theorem at me like its the end of the conversation. I couldn't quite locate the move, but I also dont want to reject a theorem by vibes. His argument, as best I can reconstruct it:

1. Rational preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms. That goes for individuals, and for 'society' too, as long as we're willing to talk about a social ranking of lotteries at all.
2. So each person i can be represented by a utility function u_i, and society by a utility function W, such that the preferred lottery is always the one with higher expected utility.
3. Society's ranking respects Pareto indifference: if every person is exactly indifferent between two lotteries, then society is indifferent between them too.
4. Harsanyi's theorem says that under these conditions W has to be an affine transformation of a weighted sum of the individual utilities: W = a_1 u_1 + ... + a_n u_n + c.
5. But ranking lotteries by the expected value of a weighted sum of individual utilities is what utilitarians do, broadly speaking; the a_i are just how much each person counts in the social calculus.
6. So anyone who grants that society should have coherent preferences over lotteries and defer to unanimous indifference is rationally forced into utilitarianism.

I think step 4 is a genuine theorem, so the problem has to be somewhere else—probably step 5, or something about treating the u_i as if they measured how well off people are rather than just how they choose under risk. But my friend says that's exactly the math and I'm just refusing the conclusion. Which step, if any, actually fails? If the argument is formally valid, tell me exactly what it establishes and whether step 6's conclusion is as strong as it sounds.
