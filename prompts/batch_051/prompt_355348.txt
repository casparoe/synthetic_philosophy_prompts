The debate about universalisability in moral philosophy has, for at least the last five decades, seemed stuck in a pattern that I find puzzling. On one side stand those who take their bearings from Kant and treat the question what if everyone did that as a test of whether the maxim of one’s action could be willed as a universal law: the wrongness lies in the contradiction that arises when a maxim is universalised. On another side stand those who follow Hare in treating universalisability as a logical requirement on moral language, not a substantive test: moral judgements are universal prescriptions, and the substantive content depends on what one is prepared to prescribe for all similar cases. A third camp, often traced to Singer’s deployment of the question, treats universalisability as a prompt for impartial consequentialist reasoning, in which the decisive issue is not contradiction but whether the aggregate consequences of everyone acting similarly would be unacceptable. The parties largely agree on the cases that initially motivated universalisability, and they largely agree that some version of universalisability matters; nonetheless, the debate over what the question is actually testing has barely moved for decades. I am tempted to diagnose it as a disagreement about the nature of moral judgement itself, rather than about any first-order moral issue: the Kantian sees a judgement as self-legislated rational law, the Hareian sees it as a universal prescription, and the consequentialist sees it as a directive to bring about the best overall state of affairs. If that is right, would the debate dissolve once the philosophy-of-language and moral-psychology questions are settled, or would a residual normative disagreement remain? And what would count as progress: a formal result, an empirical finding, a sharper distinction, or something else?
