Many people hold both of the following judgments with no sense of strain. The citizen who votes in a large election is doing something obligatory or at least admirable, and the citizen who stays home is mildly blameworthy as a free rider, even though everyone knows that a single vote will almost certainly not decide the outcome. By contrast, the person who books a transatlantic flight for a weekend of leisure is not usually thought to be doing anything seriously wrong, even though they know that this one flight will almost certainly not make a detectable difference to the harms of climate change and that the practice in which they are participating does enormous damage in the aggregate. The first act is praised; the second is treated as an unremarkable exercise of freedom. Yet the two cases seem structurally parallel in the relevant respect: each is an individual act of participation in a vast collective scheme whose overall effect is enormous, while the act's own marginal effect is negligible. Is there a principle that justifies treating the dutiful voter and the holiday flyer so differently, or should we revise one of these judgments? Give the strongest candidate justification you can find, test it against nearby cases, and say honestly whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
