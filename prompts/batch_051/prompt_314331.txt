Just got back from a long argument with a friend about liar sentences and self-reference, and I'm not sure my position hangs together. My friend's line is that self-reference is the whole problem: once a sentence talks about itself, truth goes haywire, and the fix is to rule self-referential sentences out as meaningless or defective. I kept resisting, but my own verdicts don't obviously fit any single principle I can state. So I want someone to work out whether there's a principle behind my gut reactions on these cases, and if not, which ones I should give up.

Here are the cases.

(0) 'This sentence contains five words.' Obviously fine and true, even though it's about itself. So self-reference alone can't be what's wrong.

(1) The classic liar: 'This sentence is false.' I want to say this one is genuinely defective — not true, but not simply false either, since if it's false then it's true. There's no stable assignment. But I don't think it's meaningless in the ordinary way; I know exactly what it seems to say.

(2) The truth-teller: 'This sentence is true.' This one seems less explosive than the liar: you can assign it true, or assign it false, and either assignment is consistent. But I still want to call it mildly pathological — underdetermined rather than contradictory.

(3) Curry: 'If this sentence is true, then the moon is made of green cheese.' This one really bothers me. I do not want to accept that it proves the moon is made of green cheese. Yet the reasoning goes: suppose the sentence is true. Then what it says holds, namely that if it is true then the moon is made of green cheese. By modus ponens, the moon is made of green cheese. So from the supposition that the sentence is true I can derive the consequent. And if the sentence is false, then the conditional is true because its antecedent is false, so the sentence is true anyway. My gut says something has to give — either the sentence isn't truth-evaluable, or modus ponens doesn't apply to it, or the 'if...then' here isn't the ordinary material conditional — but I don't know which, and I really don't want to give up modus ponens just to save the moon.

(4) Yablo's list: an infinite sequence of sentences where S1 says 'all the sentences after S1 are false', S2 says 'all the sentences after S2 are false', S3 says 'all the sentences after S3 are false', and so on forever. My friend says this is just self-reference in disguise, secretly circular. But I don't see any sentence in the list that refers to itself, directly or through a chain. Still, no assignment seems to work. If some Sn is true, then all the later ones are false. But then S(n+1) is false, and S(n+1) says that all the sentences after it are false, so if it's false then at least one sentence after it must be true — contradicting the claim that all the later ones are false. So no sentence can be true. But if every sentence is false, then S1 is false, and since S1 says all the later ones are false, for S1 to be false some later sentence must be true. Contradiction again. My gut says this is just as paradoxical as the liar, and banning self-reference won't touch it.

(5) A two-sentence loop: A says 'B is false', and B says 'A is false.' I also want to call this one paradoxical, even though neither sentence refers to itself. My friend bit the bullet and said loops are just disguised self-reference, but I dont know what 'disguised self-reference' is supposed to mean without stretching the idea until it covers everything.

(6) A contingent case: 'The sentance written on the blackboard in room 201 is false,' and I've never seen that blackboard. My feeling here is that whether this is defective depends on what's actually written there. That makes me suspect the pathology isn't in the sentence type or in the word 'this', but in something about how its truth conditions bottom out.

So here's what I want. Is there a coherent principle that gets all of these verdicts, or nearly all of them, and says what distinguishes the bad cases from the harmless ones? If some of my verdicts have to go to save the rest, which ones are the weakest and why? And what does your principle say about a case I can't decide: a finite version of Yablo's list, with ten sentences where S1 through S9 each say 'all the sentences after me are false' and S10 says '2+2=4'. My gut says that finite version is harmless and consistent, but I don't understand why refusing to continue forever should make the difference.
