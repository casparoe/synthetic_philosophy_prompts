I'm chairing my university's new legacy claims office. I took it on because my own family shows up in one of the old cases, so I've recused myself from deciding anything, and now I'm stuck watching other people decide these requests by gut feel. We get a steady stream of them: student groups, descendants, the city, all asking the university to apologize, rename things, or pay compensation for harms from decades or centuries ago. I need a single, reusable procedure my staff (one historian, one lawyer, no philosophers) can run on each request in under a day before it reaches the trustees. Write it as an ordered list of questions, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure can say an outright yes or no, and where it has to punt to the trustees because it's a values call. Ground it in the actual philosophical debate about whether a present institution can be guilty or responsible for what past generations did, and keep the distinction between "the institution acted," "people associated with it acted," and "current members owe something anyway" explicit throughout. Then run it on three cases: (a) the university rented enslaved people from a local slaveholder in 1836 to build our oldest hall; (b) a 1952 building gift came from a donor whose company used forced laborers during the Second World War; (c) a 1960s campus nutrition study used local Black families without consent, and their grandchildren are asking for compensation. I specifically want one case where the procedure says no even if that's not what anyone on our board wants to hear, and an honest note at the end about what the procedure cannot settle.
