I am a graduate student sitting in on an advanced seminar on the border between perception and cognition. Our instructor rules, case by case, on whether an experimental vignette exhibits cognitive penetration of visual experience, but she never states a general criterion, and the students are expected to reconstruct her principle from her track record. I have kept a record of her verdicts, and because next week's manuscript is new to everyone, I want to predict how she will rule before the seminar meets.

Her rulings so far: (1) In a memory-color study, participants who expected a heart-shaped figure to be red set a matching patch redder than controls, but the effect disappeared in a criterion-free task and participants then revealed in debriefing that they had guessed the hypothesis; she called this demand characteristics, not cognitive penetration. (2) In an EEG study, participants who believed a briefly flashed coin was valuable reported its ambiguous surface color as gold more often than those who believed it was worthless; the effect appeared as an early visual evoked potential, survived eye-fixation controls, and was not attributable to decision bias; she called it cognitive penetration. (3) In a spatial-cuing study, participants instructed to attend to one side detected faint targets there faster and rated them as more salient, but a contrast-matching task showed the represented contrast was unchanged; she said it was not cognitive penetration, because the instruction changed how readily the target was seen, not what it looked like. (4) In a reward study, participants paid more for hits at one location adopted a more liberal detection criterion there but showed no change in sensitivity or appearance; she called it a criterion shift, not cognitive penetration.

The new manuscript describes a value-driven attention experiment. Participants first learn that correct responses at one spatial location earn double points. In a later contrast-discrimination task, a Gabor patch at the rewarded location is reliably matched to a physically higher-contrast patch at an unrewarded location, and a signal-detection analysis rules out a pure criterion shift. The proposed mechanism is attentional: the apparent-contrast boost follows the participant's current locus of attention when the task reallocates attention, and it disappears under high attentional load. Thus a motivational state tied to reward changes how the patch looks, but seemingly by changing where and how strongly the observer attends. I would like you to identify which candidate principles are consistent with her track record, state what each principle predicts about this manuscript, explain which prediction is best supported and why, and specify a further experiment or case that would cleanly separate the leading principles.
