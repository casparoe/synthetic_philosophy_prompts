I've come around to the view that the inflection/derivation distinction is real but isn't fundamentally about meanings or dictionary entries — it's about syntax. Inflection is just the morphology that syntax can see and require (plural -s, past tense -ed on verbs, case endings); derivation is the morphology that happens before the stem is handed to syntax (like -er in "singer" or the -ed in "dogged persistence"), and "same word" just means "same lexeme under syntactically forced shapes." So "dogs" is the same word as "dog" but "singer" isn't the same word as "sing," even though in both cases a little bit of glue got attached. I know there are hard cases — English -ing, participles, deverbal nouns like "destruction" — but I treat them as edge effects on a basicaly right view. Tell me what would actually count against this. Which languages or constructions are the strongest counterexamples to the idea that the word boundary is wherever syntax stops seeing inside, and which are just annoying but survivable? I also want to know about the English 's in "the king of England's crown": people say it's a clitic, not inflection, but it's obviously spelling syntactic information, so does it collapse my "syntax-visible" criterion? Is there a way of doing the syntax–morphology interface — say, one where syntax builds words all the way down — that makes this whole way of drawing the line dissappear rather than just relocating it?
