I'm the chair of a city land-use comission, and we're voting next Thursday on a proposal to buy out and demolish the last remaining cluster of an old tight-knit neighborhood—about sixty households, mostly elderly, who run a mutual-aid society, a Saturday school, and an informal credit circle that has kept the corner laundromat from closing twice. The developer wants a transit plaza and 400 housing units on the site. Her feasibility study says it would be irresponsible to refuse: the buildings are in bad shape, the new housing would serve thousands, and the study models each displaced household coming out ahead—larger, safer unit, plus a cash payment that more than offsets their measured loss in 'neighborhood amenities.' Half the commission says approve; refusing is just nostalgia holding the city hostage. The other half says the model is missing something that isn't a preference to be compensated, something more like the community itself being a good rather than just a source of goods for individuals. I need to circulate a decision memo by Friday: recommend approve, reject, or approve with conditions, with a time frame and terms both sides could actually meet. Please engage honestly with the strongest version of the side you recommend against, and work through whether what's lost is a loss to individual residents (which compensation could cover) or a loss of a shared good that compensation can't replace—and what that distinction would mean for how we vote.
