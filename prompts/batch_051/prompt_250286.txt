I am putting together a handout for an AI safety reading group on iterated amplification and distillation, understood as the proposal to build an aligned and competent system by repeatedly decomposing hard tasks into smaller subtasks that a human overseer can evaluate, and then distilling the resulting amplified procedure into a model. The group keeps talking past one another, and I want a diagnostic instrument that sorts people by what they actually believe about the underlying question: does this kind of decomposition preserve alignment and competence? Please write a decision tree, or a taxonomy with an explicit branching key, whose internal nodes are concrete yes/no questions a reader could answer without already knowing the names of the positions. Good candidate questions would include: whether a human overseer can reliably judge individual subtask solutions without being able to judge the whole task; whether a chain of individually acceptable subtask solutions is guaranteed to produce an acceptable whole; whether a model that behaves aligned on each subtask remains aligned when the subtasks are composed; and whether distillation preserves the overseer's implicit standards or only the surface behavior of the amplified process. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first set out the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the actual positions in that space. Where a combination of answers has no defender, say so and briefly diagnose why — because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Please make sure the tree has distinct leaves for at least these positions: the full-decomposition optimist; the overseer-bottleneck skeptic; the view that competence fails to decompose even though alignment does; the view that alignment fails to decompose even though competence does; and the distillation-drift skeptic. Aim for roughly eight to twelve leaves in total. Flag any pair of positions your tree cannot actually separate, rather than papering over the gap. The audience knows the basics of scalable oversight but not the specialist literature, so avoid relying on named papers or authors; the branch questions should be answerable in a sentence or two from the description of the proposal alone, without any background reading.
