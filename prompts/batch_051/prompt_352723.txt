Mathematicians talk as if some choices are better than others even when nothing forces them: they'll call one choice 'canonical' and another arbitrary. I keep hearing the word, but when I push people on it they wave there hands and give examples. I want a working definition of a canonical choice — the property the word is tracking, not a list of paradigm cases. Test it on these:

1. Picking √2 (the positive square root of 2) over −√2.
2. Picking one of the two roots of x² + 1 and calling it i.
3. Picking a basis for R³.
4. Taking the prime factorization of 30 as the unordered multiset {2, 3, 5}, rather than one of the six orderings.
5. Picking right-handed over left-handed orientation for R³.
6. Picking 3 over 4 as 'the' square root of 2 modulo 7.

I'd be surprised if 1 and 4 didn't come out canonical, and if 2 and 3 did; 5 and 6 are where I'm genuinely unsure. For each case, run your definition through it and say what it predicts, and flag any case where it gives no answer or a verdict you'd have to defend. Then say briefly whether 'canonical' is best thought of as invariance under the relevant symmetries, definability without naming something arbitrary, or just a sociological fact about standard practice. Also, while you're at it, tell me how this differs from the technical sense of 'well-defined,' since I blur those two constantly.
