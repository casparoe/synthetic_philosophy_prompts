I've been trying to sort out what I actually think about courage and whether the old martial virtues still matter in a peaceful society, and I'd rather have someone audit my commitments than explain the topic to me from scratch. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) courage is a real virtue, not merely a useful psychological disposition;
(2) the central cases of courage involve facing a genuine risk of serious harm for the sake of something worth protecting;
(3) physical courage (the firefighter, the soldier) and moral courage (the whistleblower, the friend who says the hard thing) are one and the same virtue, not two different things that happen to share a name;
(4) in a comfortable, peaceful society the occasions for physical courage are rare, but a person can still be genuinely courageous without ever facing a real emergency — through controlled danger like climbing or combat sports, and through the way they train themselves to respond to fear whilst the danger is real enough to matter;
(5) recklessness is not courage, even when it faces the same dangers, because courage has to be proportionate: the risk has to be worth the end;
(6) a courageous criminal is still courageous, so courage is not automatically praiseworthy — and yet cowardice is always blameworthy;
(7) we should still cultivate the martial virtues in a peaceful society, even though their original setting has mostly dissapeared.
