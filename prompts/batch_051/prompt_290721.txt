Act as a professional philosopher specializing in the ethics of blame, moral psychology, and the social epistemology of norm enforcement. I have been developing a heterodox account of why hypocrites seem to lose standing to blame, and I would like a candid expert assessment rather than a friendly reconstruction. Please think carefully: before you take a position, steelman the view in your own words, canvas the main things a theory of standing ought to explain, and then tell me plainly where my account is unoriginal, incoherent, or false.

I call the view the 'standing commons' account. The core claim is that standing to blame is not a private feature of the blamer, something they either have or lack, but a shared and depletable resource attached to each norm in a community. Picture every norm as carrying a stock of enforcement credibility: the degree to which invoking that norm in criticism still carries weight among those who might later need to enforce it. Blaming draws on that stock. A blamer with a clean record makes a small, legitimate withdrawal, and their blame can even signal that the norm is alive. A blamer who recently or currently violates the same norm makes a large, corrosive withdrawal: their blame advertises that even the norm's self-appointed enforcers do not really treat it as authoritative, so the community's stock of credibility drops, and later would-be blamers, including blamers with clean hands, find the norm harder to enforce. Thus the friend who habitually cancels and then lectures you about flaking, the parent who smokes while telling a teenager not to, and a government that bombs civilians while condemning another state's war crimes are all objectionable for the same underlying reason: they are spending down a shared stock of credibility in the norms against flaking, smoking, and targeting civilians. The view predicts that hypocrisy is worse when it is public and plausible, that private hypocrisy may leave standing largely intact, that a comically self-unaware hypocrite may damage the norm more than an ordinary one, and that a community can deplete a norm's enforceability through selective enforcement even when every current blamer is individually consistent.

I know the view sounds economic, and I know there are existing commitment-based, speech-act, and epistemic accounts of standing, as well as the standard observation that tu quoque is often a fallacy. What I need to know is whether the shared-pool machinery earns its keep. Is the notion of a commons doing real explanatory work, or is it reducible to a conjunction of facts about individual credibility? Does the view already exist in recognizable form, or under another name, and would the existing version leave out anything mine captures? Where does it break — what cases does it misclassify, and does it collapse into a merely consequentialist claim about the downstream effects of blame? If you were my supervisor, what is the strongest objection you would raise before I invest more time in it?
