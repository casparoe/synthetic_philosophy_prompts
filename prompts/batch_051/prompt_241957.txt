Last night someone showed up 40 minutes late to a dinner party and, as she sat down, shrugged and said "punctuality is the politeness of kings" — her take being that being on time is a ceremonial nicety for royalty, not a real thing ordinary people owe each other. I'm pretty sure that's wrong, but I couldn't say exactly why at the time. I'd like you to pull the proverb apart for me. What are the distinct precise claims "punctuality is the politeness of kings" could be making? I can see a few candidates: (1) punctuality is a courtesy, not a duty, and the courtesy/duty distinction is doing all the work; (2) punctuality is something the powerful owe downward, not something everyone owes everyone; (3) it's really a claim in the ethics of small promises — being where you said you'd be at the time you said is an implicit promise, so lateness is a kind of unreliability even when the stakes are small. There are probably more. For each reading, say whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or just false, and then say which reading would actually license treating a friend's chronic lateness as no big deal. I also want to know where the proverb is doing real work and where it's just a posh way of saying "dont be late". And if you can, connect it to the broader question of when a standing social expectation — like "be on time" — hardens into a promise, because I think that's where the actual argument should happen.
