I have been working with a distinction I call insulated and exposed integrity, and I would like an honest assessment of whether it holds up.

By insulated integrity I mean the condition of a person who remains uncorrupted because the circumstances for corruption never arise. Such a person may refuse to learn information that would create a conflict of interest, avoid relationships that would produce competing loyalties, or arrange to have no access to funds that could be misused. By exposed integrity I mean the condition of a person who is fully within reach of the temptations—money, personal attachment, threats, career interests—and still refuses.

My claim is that only exposed integrity deserves moral admiration. Insulation, I want to say, is not a virtue of the person at all but a property of their situation: it is engineered non-corruption, not integrity. Pushed to its limit, this yields a slogan I keep using: the incorruptible person is not someone who cannot be corrupted; that is simply insensibility, lack of desire, or fanaticism. The incorruptible person is someone who can be corrupted—who would feel the pull—and is not corrupted anyway.

I can make this vivid with two officials. One has arranged a life in which nobody can ask him for anything: he keeps no friends with interests in his decisions, accepts no invitations, and remains deliberately ignorant of anything that could bias him. The other lives inside a dense network of friendships, debts, and party loyalties, constantly touched by requests for favorable treatment, and refuses them at real social cost. Ordinary language might call the first more incorruptible; I suspect he is merely insulated, while the second is the one with integrity.

I know this brushes against older ideas about virtue, continence, and temptation, and perhaps against arguments that character traits need the right situation to count. But I want it checked. Is the distinction coherent, or does it break when I try to draw a clean line between situational protection and self-arranged insulation? Does it already exist in better form? Where am I wrong—especially in seeming to deny the moral value of prudence, avoidance, and institutional design that protects people from temptation? And what, if anything, does the distinction imply about whether incorruptibility is always good?
