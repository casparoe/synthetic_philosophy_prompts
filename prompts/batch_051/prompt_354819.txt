I have no formal training in physics or philosophy, only a hobbyist's reading, and I have been trying out a principle I call the Rule of No Idle Differences. I would like an honest assessment of it. The rule is this:

If two descriptions of a physical situation are such that no measurement, even in principle, could tell them apart, then the respect in which they differ does not describe anything real. What is real is only what the two descriptions share.

This has helped me sort out what I should take seriously in physics. Whether I put the origin of my coordinate grid in one corner of the room or at its center makes no measurable difference, so the origin is an artifact of the description and only distances, angles, and durations are real. A magnetic field can be represented by different vector potentials that all give the same field, so the field is real and the potential is surplus, at least where the potentials differ. And an overall phase of a quantum wavefunction is unreal in the same way, because shifting it changes no probability.

I do not imagine the principle is wholly new, but I want to know whether it has been worked out under a name I would not know, and whether it is actually true as I have stated it. There is one case that troubles me. In the Aharonov–Bohm effect, an electron seems to be affected by the electromagnetic potential even while moving through a region where the magnetic field is zero. That seems to show that the potential, which my rule would call surplus, can make a measurable difference after all. Does that refute the rule, or can the rule be saved by saying that what is real in that case is not the potential at any one point but something more holistic, like the total phase accumulated around a loop? And where else does the rule fail? Please tell me what would change your verdict: which argument, example, or piece of evidence would push you the other way, and where my reasoning is most likely to break. Please answer in American English.
