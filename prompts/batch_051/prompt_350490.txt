I am a retired natural scientist — I spent my career watching people fit models to data and learning how much damage post hoc adjustment can do — and I have recently been reading the decision-theory literature. The idea behind updateless decision theory keeps striking me as an attempt to extend that lesson to an agent's own observations: the agent should select a complete policy, a mapping from every observation it might make to an action, and should evaluate that policy across all the worlds in which the policy is instantiated, without first conditioning on the particular observations the actual world happens to have handed it. The force of the idea shows up in Newcomb-style cases, where conditioning on what one sees makes the policy with the better expected value look locally wrong.

Here is the mechanism I want you to take apart. Suppose several AI labs establish a policy-escrow arrangement. Before certain private information arrives — say, before each lab learns the result of an internal capability audit — each lab must deposit a large sum and file a complete, machine-checkable policy mapping the observations a lab might receive (audit results, rivals' announced progress, regulatory signals) to actions such as pause, share, or race. A neutral supervisor with a stated model of the situation then works out, for each lab, what its filed policy would do in every branch the model treats as reachable. If the policy would choose a dangerous action in some branch, the lab is penalized for that branch even if, in the actual branch, it never received the triggering observation and never did anything dangerous. For example, a lab whose policy says to race only after receiving a high audit score is fined on the strength of the high-score branch even when its actual audit score was low and it paused. The penalty in each branch is scaled to the model's estimate of the expected harm of the dangerous action in that branch. The designers' claim is that this forces each lab to choose the policy that is best before it knows which branch it is in, rather than the policy that looks best after it has updated on the branch it happens to inhabit — an institutional imitation of updatelessness.

Analyze how this arrangement would actually behave, not whether it is a good idea in the abstract. What would it incentivize, what second-order effects would it produce, and how would a sophisticated lab game it? Consider the specification of the policy language, the choice of the reachability model, the audit process, the deposit size, the definition of a dangerous action, and the possibility that the supervisor becomes the real decision-maker. Where is the scheme most likely to fail — including the fairness and legal problems raised by punishing an agent for a harm that never occurred in the actual world — and which failure modes can be fixed without gutting it? I also want to know where the analogy with updateless decision theory breaks down when it is moved from an idealized agent to an actual institution, and where it survives. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on these questions, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
