Somewhere between the rationalist blogosphere and a few arguments I keep having, I keep running into a claim about consent. Put broadly, the claim is this: a competent adult's consent to an act is invalid if it was obtained by deception about something that, had the person known the truth, would predictably have changed their decision — and this is so regardless of whether the act itself caused them any further harm.

I think the word "invalid" is doing at least three jobs in that sentence, and I want to know whether and how each of them could be tested.

1. A psychological reading: the person did not actually agree to the act as it really was; their internal act of will simply did not cover the facts as they stood.
2. A normative reading: whatever the person's mental state, third parties should not treat the consent as having waived a right, transferred a permission, or shifted responsibility.
3. A functional reading: a social practice of treating deception-induced consent as non-binding does better than the alternatives — at protecting autonomy, maintaining trust, keeping people willing to enter future agreements, or whatever the point of consent practices is.

I would like you to convert these readings into precise sub-claims, design the strongest concrete test you can for each, and say exactly what a positive result would and would not show.

Anchor the tests in a running case. A agrees, in exchange for $20, to complete an hour-long mildly unpleasant computer task for B. B obtains this agreement while varying what A is told about the task's purpose and what happens to the data: told it is academic research when it is actually commercial marketing; told nothing; told the truth; actively misled about whether the task includes a brief mood manipulation. The task itself is identical across conditions.

For each reading, tell me:
- what dependent variable you would measure, why it is a defensible operationalization of that concept, and what the operationalization leaves out;
- what pattern of results would count in favor of the reading, and what pattern would count against it;
- whether the test needs vignettes, third-party judgments, or an incentive-compatible task in which participants make real agreements, and which confound worries you most in your chosen method.

Then address the question I care about most. Suppose the effects are large and in the direction the claim predicts: people report that they had not agreed to what happened, observers refuse to treat them as bound, and a non-recognition rule produces better trust and participation. What would that still fail to settle? In particular, I want to know whether any empirical finding could close the gap between "most people judge this consent invalid" or "a non-recognition rule works better" and "they are right to treat it as invalid" — and if not, which part of the original claim is doing the genuinely normative work.

Flag the weakest step in your account: the operationalization, the inference, or the sub-claim you would bet least on. End by saying how confident you are in the overall account and singling out the single point you are least sure of. I do not want a literature review; I want the test design and the limits analysis.
