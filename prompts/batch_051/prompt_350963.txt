I keep seeing Cox's theorem cited as though it proves probability theory is the unique logic of plausible reasoning, but the slogan-y version is too vague for me to know exactly what has been shown. Walk me threw the argument in a way that's honest about which premises are doing the real work—plausibilities being real numbers, consistency with Boolean logic, whatever regularity/smoothness conditions are hidden in there—and which of them a person could reasonably reject if they didn't want to land at probability. In particular, I'd like to know whether the theorem genuinely rules out alternatives like Dempster–Shafer belief functions or imprecise probabilities, or whether those systems escape by violating one of the theorem's assumptions. And please clearly separate the parts that are settled mathematics from the parts that are contested among people who work on this: don't make the theorem sound stronger than it is.
