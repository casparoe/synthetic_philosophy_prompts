Take the standard Liar sentence: a sentence that says of itself that it is false. A familiar response, associated with Kripke's partial-predicate approach, treats the Liar as neither true nor false: it falls into a truth-value gap. Now consider a variation in which the self-referential sentence says of itself that it is neither true nor false — call it the gap-sayer. Work through what a paracomplete theory, modelled by a Strong Kleene fixed-point construction, should say about each sentence. In particular: does the gap-sayer end up true, false, or gappy at the least fixed point, and how does its course through the construction differ from the Liar's? If the gap-sayer turns out to be gappy, explain what the theory can consistently assert about its own verdict on it; if it turns out to be false, explain how that squares with what the sentence says. Then assess whether the gap-sayer raises a revenge problem for the truth-value-gap treatment of the Liar, and say what the comparison reveals about the respective roles of the truth and falsity predicates in generating paradox.
