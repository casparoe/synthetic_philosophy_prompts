I am a novelist researching a book set in a large layered polity, something between a federal republic and a union of states, and for the past several months I have been reading transcripts of legislative debates, regional council minutes, and intergovernmental negotiations. A recurring argumentative move keeps surfacing, and I have not been able to discover whether it has a settled name.

The move has two faces. In one, a speaker resists a central measure by urging that the matter is in its nature local: it concerns a particular place and a particular community; the people closest to it know it best; it should therefore be decided at the smallest level of government competent to handle it. In the other, a speaker resists leaving a matter to local choice by urging that its consequences spill outward: it touches a market, a watershed, or a population that moves, and it therefore belongs at a higher level. Neither argument, taken alone, strikes me as wrong. What strikes me is the asymmetry of their employment. In the records I have gathered, a speaker generally appears to settle on a preferred outcome first and then to select whichever face of the argument points toward it; the talk about local knowledge or spillover is added as a finish, not as a motive. I have found the same delegate maintaining in one month that waste disposal is irreducibly local and in another that the same river basin is irreducibly integrated, apparently because the local authorities favored regulation in the first case and resisted it in the second. No one in the debate seems to treat this as a defect.

I am aware of a familiar principle, much discussed in connection with federations and with the governance of the European Union, which holds roughly that authority should rest at the smallest and most local level that can perform a task adequately. I have deliberately avoided using its customary name, because I want to be sure where that principle ends and whether what I am describing is something distinct from it. My question is consequently narrower than it may sound. Is there an established term for the pattern itself, the conclusion-first, instrumental invocation of that principle, in which a participant's substantive preference selects the argument rather than the argument selecting the level of government? Is the pattern best understood as an abuse of the principle, as a separate phenomenon that merely resembles it, or as something the principle invites through its vagueness about what local and adequately are supposed to mean? If it has no name, I would welcome a coined one, together with a definition and a few boundary cases that separate it from a sincere application of the principle on one side and from ordinary political hypocrisy on the other.
