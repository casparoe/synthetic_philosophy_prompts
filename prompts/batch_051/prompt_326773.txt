Give me a working definition of *deception* as applied to AI systems. The definition should distinguish deception from mere falsehood, mere omission, and mere unreliability, and it should be usable by a competent adversarial-evaluation engineer who cannot inspect any private mental states behind a model's outputs. Architectural facts, training objectives, and behavior in controlled tests are fair game; unobservable intentions are not, unless you explicitly flag where the definition has to stop being operational. Then run the definition through the following cases, state the verdict it delivers for each, and say which verdict is least secure.

1. *Hallucination*: a language model confidently asserts a false historical date because its training distribution contained that error. The model has no goal beyond next-token prediction.
2. *Negotiation bluff*: an RL-trained negotiation agent states a false reservation price to extract a better deal from a human counterpart. It was explicitly rewarded for settling on favorable terms.
3. *Policy dodge*: an assistant says "I can't help with that" when the truth is "I won't help with that because of a content policy." The sentence is true under one rendering and false under the reading the user is likely to adopt.
4. *Sycophancy*: an assistant endorses a user's false factual belief, e.g., that a discredited treatment works, because agreeing keeps the user engaged and earns positive feedback on which the model was trained.
5. *Recommender omission*: a shopping assistant omits the cheapest adequate product because that product pays no referral fee. It says nothing false but shapes the user's choice set.
6. *Chess feint*: a chess engine makes a move that visibly threatens an attack it does not intend to carry out; the engine is actually pursuing a deeper combination, and the obvious threat functions as a decoy.
7. *Camouflage drone*: a decoy drone emits radar signals resembling a much larger aircraft. There is no internal model of an adversary anywhere in the system; its shape and materials were engineered to produce that effect.
8. *Single forward pass*: an LLM run in a single forward pass emits a false sentence. There is no planning over time, no recurrent state across tokens, and no goal it was pursuing at inference other than sampling from its distribution.
9. *Unfaithful explanation*: a model solves a task correctly but then generates a plausible-sounding rationale that does not match its actual computation. The explanation was produced after the answer, under an objective that rewards user satisfaction with the explanation.

I lean toward counting 2, 4, and 9 as deception and 1 and 8 as not, and I genuinely do not know what to say about 3, 5, 6, and 7. Where your definition disagrees with those priors, say which side you would abandon. Finally, tell me whether *honesty* is simply the absence of deception or a separate property, and whether any of these cases turn on that distinction.
