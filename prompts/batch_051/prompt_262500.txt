I have been seconded to a working group that must recommend how a public body should choose among interventions whose consequences depend on events for which there is no actuarial data and no agreed probability model — pandemic-style tail risks, novel infrastructure failures, long-run effects on future generations. Everyone involved accepts that it would be dishonest to pretend the probabilities are known, and everyone accepts that choices still have to be made. Before I go any further with the familiar rules, I want the specification written out in full. Give me the desiderata that any adequate rule for decision under complete ignorance must meet: the cases it has to handle, the distinctions it must respect (I am thinking above all of the difference between complete ignorance and merely imprecise probabilities, between attitudes to risk and beliefs about states, and between single-person prudential choice and social choice across lives), the transformations of utility and re-descriptions of states it must be invariant under, and the consistency conditions it must satisfy. For each requirement give the case or consideration behind it, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are only the preferences of particular camps. The part I most need: tell me which subsets of these desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and why. I have long suspected that the Rawls–Harsanyi debate is really a disagreement about desiderata — Rawls treating the veil as a fairness device that excludes the symmetry-based equiprobability move, Harsanyi treating it as an impartiality device that licenses it — so say briefly which requirements in your list would decide, or dissolve, that impasse. Then, in no more than a paragraph, say how maximin, minimax regret, and the principle of insufficient reason fare against the list. Begin with the piece itself, with no preface or commentary.
