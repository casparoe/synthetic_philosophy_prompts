I've been trying to assemble Leibniz's scattered remarks on moral responsibility into one argument, and the result is so strong it seems to prove nobody is ever blameworthy, which has to be wrong given how much Leibniz cares about sin, repentance, and divine punishment. Here's the chain I get:

(1) The will always follows the strongest inclination, and the strongest inclination is just what appears best to the agent at the moment of choice. Leibniz denies that the will is ever in an equilibrium of indifference; it always goes where it sees the most good.

(2) What appears best is fixed by the agent's perceptions, memories, and prior states, and ultimately — for Leibniz — by the complete individual concept of that substance, which contains everything that will ever happen to that person.

(3) So a wrongdoer, at the moment of acting, could not have seen things otherwise and could not have willed otherwise. To will otherwise they would have needed different perceptions or a different concept, which is just to be a different substance (or a different possible person God didn't actualize).

(4) If someone could not have seen otherwise and could not have willed otherwise, then they are not blameworthy for the act.

(5) So blame has to attach earlier: to a failure of attention, to the habits that kept the good from appearing as it should, to the creature's original limitation.

(6) But by the same reasoning, each of those earlier states was also fixed by prior states and ultimately by the complete concept, so the blame has nowhere to stop.

(7) Therefore, on Leibniz's own principles, no one is ever blameworthy in a way that would justify punishment — yet Leibniz clearly wants sin to be real and God's judgments just.

Where does this go wrong? I want to keep (1) and (2), since they look like core Leibniz, and I'd rather reject some later step than give up his determinism. But I can't tell wich step actually breaks, and I don't know what I pay for rejecting it. If the argument is valid instead, tell me which premise is least costly to give up and what that commits me to. Also say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would flip your diagnosis, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail.
