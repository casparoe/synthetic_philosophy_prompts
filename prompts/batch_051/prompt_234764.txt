Set out, as a working hypothesis, the Epicurean view that death cannot be bad for the person who dies: once a person ceases to exist, there is no subject to bear the harm, so the rational attitude toward one's own death is indifference. Now examine a cluster of familiar practices. Courts enforce wills that control a person's property, body, and posthumous reputation; many legal systems criminalize desecration of corpses, and some recognize offenses against the memory of the dead; medical and financial institutions treat advance directives and deathbed promises as binding after the person has died.

Reconstruct the implicit theory of harm, interests, and death that best explains this bundle. For each practice, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. In particular, say whether the whole bundle can be explained by the interests and expectations of the living—the heirs, the bereaved, the institutions that depend on settled expectations—or whether any adequate rationale must allow that a person's interests can survive her death and be set back after it. If the latter, does that amount to a rejection of the Epicurean view, or can the Epicurean reinterpret the practice? Finally, identify what would have to change in these practices if we took the Epicurean position seriously as a principle of institutional design.
