I run a small blog mostly read by the old LessWrong / rationalist-adjacent crowd, and I keep having the same argument with housemates and friends: does someone owe it to the people they live with, date, or work with to be legible — to actually say what they think, want, or feel, instead of making everyone else infer it? I want to turn this into one of those "which position do you actually hold?" quizzes: eight to ten concrete case questions, two or three answer options each, plus an explicit scoring key that maps answer patterns to positions.

Use cases in the spirit of these (you can add your own): a roommate who slams cupboards instead of saying they're angry; a boss whose feedback is just "be more proactive"; a group member who can't articulate why they oppose a proposal but still wants to block consensus; a new partner who won't say what they want because that would "ruin it"; a friend from a hinting culture who says you should be able to "read the room" when you ask them to be direct; a doctor who gives a diagnosis in pure jargon and never checks whether you understood.

The quiz has to sort people by what they say about cases, not by which label they like, so the options should be concrete choices in the situation rather than little credos. Build the scoring key from the logical space before you assign names. The distinctions I care about: (1) is the burden mainly on the speaker to be legible, or on the audience to get better at reading? (2) is whatever is going on a moral duty, an epistemic norm for group deliberation, or just etiquette/strategy? (3) if there is a duty, does it run uphill (toward people with more power), downhill (from the powerful toward the less powerful), or both? Cross those and tell me which cells are occupied; for the empty ones, say briefly whether the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just underexplored.

The leaves that have to show up: legibility-as-respect, the harm-avoidance view, legibility as an epistemic norm (roughly, making your reasons and confidence levels trackable by others during joint deliberation), a right-to-opacity view (especially against people and institutions with power over you), and the deflationary it's-all-etiquette view. If two of these give the same answers on every case I listed, flag that instead of forcing a fake distinction, and tell me one case that would split them. Audience is group-house, polycule, feedback-culture, neurodiversity-discourse rationalists, so don't write around that; its fine if the cases are a little inside-baseball.
