I have been reading popular explanations of causal inference, and I keep arriving at an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept, even though each step looks sensible on its own.

(1) To say that A causes B is to say more than that A and B occur together: it is to say that if A were changed while everything else relevant stayed fixed, B would change too.

(2) Observational data—surveys, registries, natural variation—only records what did happen. It shows associations among variables that co-occurred, not what would happen under conditions that never occurred.

(3) Any given set of associations can be squared with several different causal pictures. A correlation between A and B could come from A causing B, from B causing A, or from a third factor C causing both, and each picture can be adjusted to fit the same numbers reasonably well.

(4) The only way to distinguish these pictures is to intervene: change one factor deliberately and watch whether the other responds.

(5) For many questions people care about—whether social media use increases depression, whether a higher minimum wage raises unemployment, whether a drug causes a rare side effect years later—intervening is impractical or unethical.

(6) Therefore, for those questions, causal claims are not knowledge; they are guesses dressed as knowledge.

(7) That conclusion is hard to accept, since some causal claims reached without intervention, such as the claim that smoking causes lung cancer, are treated as among the most secure claims we have.

I can feel something slipping in the move from "several pictures fit the data" to "we can never choose among them," but I cannot say exactly where. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? If no single step fails, what background assumptions would have to be added before the observational causal claims in question could be justified, and how would anyone tell that those assumptions hold in a particular case?
