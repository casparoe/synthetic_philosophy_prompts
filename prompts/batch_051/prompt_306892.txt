You are a professional philosopher specializing in value theory, philosophy of mind, and the ethics of artificial intelligence. I am a high school student, but please do not simplify the conceptual content; I would rather have technical clarity and careful distinctions than an introductory survey. Before giving your assessment, begin by brainstorming the key features of each case, and then reason through each part explicitly.

Take Nozick's experience machine as the baseline thought experiment. In that baseline, a machine can deliver whatever experiences a person wants, so convincingly that the person forgets they are inside a simulation. Nozick's standard objections, roughly, are that we want to actually do things rather than merely have the experience of doing them, that we want to be a certain kind of person, and that we value contact with a reality deeper than what human beings can construct.

Now modify the device in two separate ways, and treat them as two distinct thought experiments.

Variant A: direct wireheading. The machine does not simulate a coherent life full of achievements and relationships. Instead, it directly and continuously stimulates the brain processes responsible for felt satisfaction, liking, or pleasure, holding the person at maximum subjective satisfaction while providing almost no narrative, relational, or achievement content. You cannot select experiences, you cannot later reflect on having done anything, and there is no simulated world in which to act.

Variant B: reward tampering. Before you enter, the machine modifies your standards, values, and expectations so that whatever you encounter inside will count as perfect success by your own new standards. After the modification, any state you find yourself in feels maximally fulfilling because the very criteria by which you judge fulfillment have been changed. You do not enter as the person who made the decision to enter; you enter as someone whose values have been rewritten to fit whatever the machine delivers.

For each variant, please do the following:

1. Restate Nozick's main objections in your own words, and say whether each objection applies with the same force, stronger force, or weaker force than in the baseline experience machine.

2. Identify which hidden assumptions in the original thought experiment each variant exposes. Some candidates to consider are assumptions that the machine preserves your identity, your values, your desires, your standards of evaluation, or your capacity for narrative memory, but do not limit yourself to those.

3. Evaluate whether the variant gives a hedonist or a desire-satisfaction theorist a new defense of the machine, or whether it makes their position harder to hold.

4. Assess whether Variant B is a genuinely different kind of proposal from Variant A, rather than merely a more extreme version of the same idea, and explain what feature of the setup makes the difference.

Finally, compare the two variants: which one does Nozick's original line of argument handle better, and does the experience machine thought experiment still succeed as an argument against experience-focused theories of welfare once wireheading and reward tampering are allowed? Or do those variants push the thought experiment toward the conclusion that the real issue is not experience versus reality, but the preservation of the person's own evaluative standpoint?
