I have been reading George Berkeley's The Querist, and I keep returning to one of the early queries. As I understand it, Berkeley asks whether money is not really only a ticket or counter for computing and recording the value of goods, and whether its whole use is to transfer that record from person to person rather than to be itself a store of real wealth. I want to treat that not as an eighteenth-century policy pamphlet line but as a live conceptual claim: money is essentially a system of recording and transferring claims on goods, and the material in which the record is kept is accidental.

How would one test that claim? I want a concrete experimental or observational design — perhaps comparing economies with identical real resources and institutions but different monetary materials, or studying what happens when a credible paper currency displaces coin. State explicitly which result would favor the ticket-counter view and which would favor the rival view that the monetary material or token itself matters in a way that is not merely a recording device. Then tell me what the test would and would not settle. In particular, how much of the disagreement is empirical, and how much is a conceptual dispute about what counts as wealth or about what it means to be a ticket, such that even a decisive result could be reinterpreted by either side? I want the residue laid out precisely.
