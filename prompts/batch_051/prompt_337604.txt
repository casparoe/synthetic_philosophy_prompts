I'm putting together a two-page pre-proposal for an epistemology-of-science funding call, and I want it to be philosophy of science, not sociology or bibliometrics. My half-baked question: why do fields with fast, legible feedback actually accumulate results, while fields with slow or muddy feedback mostly rearrange their vocabularies and fight about methods? Everyone gestures at this, but the standard positions leave the mechanism open. Popperians say falsifiability, but plenty of falsifiable fields have been stuck for decades. The replication people say measurement error, which feels downstream to me. The sociologists say status and turf, which is true but not what's doing the causal work. I suspect the standard categories are too coarse. What matters might not be whether you get feedback, or even how fast, but what kind of information the feedback carries. In some fields the feedback just tells you your last move was wrong — the patient didn't improve, the program didn't compile, the bridge didn't fall (call it "verdict feedback" if you like). In others the feedback tells you something about the shape of the problem space — which direction was roughly better, a small wing change gives this much more lift, a tweak to the model reduces error by that much (call it "gradient feedback"). My hunch is that fields with gradient feedback can improve without anyone understanding why, whereas fields stuck with verdict feedback need deep theory to move at all and mostly don't get it.

So I want the pitch to say that, but sharper. It should: (1) state the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; (2) say why existing positions leave it open; (3)lay out the method — I'm imagining a conceptual taxonomy of feedback structures, plus three paired case studies (aerodynamics vs. macroeconomics, compiler design vs. urban planning, maybe a medical pair), and a toy model of search under different feedback regimes — and say why that method rather than the obvious alternative of regressing 'progress' on 'falsifiability' or citation counts; (4) say what the deliverable would look like for people who actually decide how to fund or organize research; and (5) give an honest assessment of what six months can and can't do, including what would count as failure.
