For a paper I am writing, I would like an analysis of Sweden's switch from left-hand to right-hand driving, completed in a single day in September 1967 (Dagen H), through the lens of David Lewis's theory of convention and broader ideas about how norms change. The facts I am working from are these: Sweden had long driven on the left while its neighbors drove on the right; by the 1960s the great majority of Swedish vehicles already had left-hand steering wheels; a 1955 referendum showed overwhelming public opposition to changing; yet the government planned the switch carefully and executed it on one day, and the new right-hand rule persisted. Please give me a careful mapping of the episode into Lewis's framework: what kind of coordination problem driving on one side or the other poses, who the relevant parties are, and why the old pattern counted as a convention despite being arbitrary. Then use the case to show both what this account explains and where it is strained or silent—especially the role of the state, law, enforcement, and physical infrastructure in moving a population from one equilibrium to another, which a picture of conventions as tacit and gradually evolved may not capture. Conclude by saying what Dagen H suggests about whether a society can change a long-standing convention by a deliberate, centrally planned intervention rather than by unplanned evolution.
