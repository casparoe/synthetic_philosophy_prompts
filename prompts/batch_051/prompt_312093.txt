For my high school philosophy class, I have been tracking the verdicts our teacher gives in his unit on truth and lying. He is an avowed moral particularist: he rejects the idea that morality is governed by a system of general principles, and he often argues that a feature that counts in favor of an action in one context can count against it in another — for instance, the fact that a statement is true. He refuses to state general rules and gets annoyed when students try to systematize his opinions, but his verdicts have been stable enough that I think there is a pattern worth testing.

His cases so far:

1. Lying to someone who is looking for a person they plainly intend to harm, by saying that person is not hiding in the house: he says lying here is clearly right, and that only a machine would tell the truth.

2. Telling a friend that their already-chosen outfit looks fine when it is unflattering and too late to change: he says this white lie is acceptable, because the truth would serve no one.

3. Padding a job application by claiming to have led a project the applicant merely assisted on: he says this is wrong, even though it might help a capable person win a job they can do.

4. A student publicly correcting a classmate's minor factual error in front of the whole class, where the correction is accurate but motivated chiefly by a desire to embarrass: he says speaking the truth here is wrong, because truth is not a weapon.

5. A parent telling their six-year-old that a terminally ill pet has "gone to live on a farm" rather than that the pet was put down: he says the lie is acceptable for now, because the child is not yet in a position to process the reality, though the child should be told a gentler version of the truth as they mature.

This Friday, he will give his verdict on a new case: a parent tells their seventeen-year-old, who has just auditioned for a selective music program, that the audition went beautifully and that they are definitely on the right track. The parent has in fact read the panel's private comments, which say the student is far below the standard for professional training. The parent's aim is to protect the student's confidence and love of music, and the parent also quietly steers the student away from requesting the panel's written feedback. The student keeps practicing, applies to conservatories, and delays considering other paths.

What should I expect him to say? Act as a professional moral philosopher with expertise in moral particularism and the role of general principles in ethics. Think carefully and step by step. Begin by brainstorming the principles and patterns that could account for all five recorded verdicts — both generalist principles he might be following despite himself and particularist readings of his method. Then apply each candidate rule to the new case, identify where they diverge, and make your best prediction about his verdict and his reasons. If two plausible rules genuinely disagree on the new case, state what commitment each answer would reveal, say which earlier case is the closest analog, and specify an additional case that would most clearly separate the candidates.
