I got into an argument with a freind about objective chance, and he summarized a chapter he'd read on Lewis's Principal Principle for me, roughly like this: 'Lewis starts from the subjectivist idea that all probabilities are really just credences — degrees of partial belief — but he also wants there to be objective chances in the world, and the Principal Principle is the bridge between the two. The principle says that if you know the objective chance of some proposition is 0.7, you must set your credence to 0.7 as well, no matter what else you know; that's just what it is to defer to chance. The trouble is that on Lewis's own view chances supervene on the whole pattern of local events, so there are undermining futures: ways things could go that, if they actually went that way, would imply that the chances themselves were different from what they in fact are. In those cases the principle gives contradictory instructions, so the whole project of deferring to objective chance falls apart — especially for Lewis, since he couldn't give up the supervenience picture — and we're left with pure subjectivism.' Something in that last step felt to hasty to me, and the no-matter-what-else-you-know part also made me suspicious, but I don't know enough to say exactly where he went wrong. Can you do an accuracy audit: which parts of that summary are faithful to Lewis, which are subtle distortions, and how would a defender of the Principal Principle respond to the undermining-futures objection?
