I've been trying to get my head around how the EU decides whether something should be legislated in Brussels or left to the member states. There's a subsidiarity check where national parliaments can submit a formal objection saying a draft law should be handled at a lower level, and if enough of them do the European Commission has to at least reconsider the proposal. But the actual test seems to be wheter the member states acting on their own, at central or local level, could achieve the objective well enough — the EU only gets to act when it can do it better. That sounds like a judgement about administrative effectiveness. Yet people also defend subsidiarity in terms of democratic closeness: decisions made by people you can actually vote for, closer to where you live, in a polity you feel some attachment to. What theory of legitimate government does this procedure actually presuppose? Does it quietly treat lower-level government as justified mainly by comparative effectiveness rather than by self-rule or democratic connection? Is the practice coherent, and what would the yellow card procedure have to look like if it were actually enforcing the closeness/self-rule version of subsidiarity?
