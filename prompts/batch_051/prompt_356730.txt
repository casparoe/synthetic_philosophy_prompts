Exam question:

Explain the aggregation problem for Scanlon's contractualism. Your answer should (a) state Scanlon's contractualist criterion of wrongness and its idea of reasonable rejection; (b) explain why Scanlon rejects aggregative reasoning, using one of his own examples; and (c) discuss whether this rejection implies that, in a choice between saving one person and saving five people whose individual claims are otherwise equal, the numbers do not count.

Student's answer:

Scanlon's contractualism holds that an act is wrong just in case it would be ruled out by the principles for the general regulation of behavior that no one could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced agreement. A principle is reasonably rejectable when some person, taking into account only the reasons that apply to them personally—that is, their own interests and aims—could prefer an alternative principle that better protects what they care about. The practical effect is that each person has a veto over any principle that leaves them with an outcome they like less, and this is what makes the view non-consequentialist.

The aggregation problem is that Scanlon's test seems at first to invite exactly the sum-ranking he criticizes. Once we ask whether anyone could reasonably reject a principle, we are tempted to count how many people would reject it, or to weigh the total size of the complaints against it; if that were allowed, contractualism would collapse back into a version of utilitarianism. Scanlon's response is the individualist restriction, which says that a principle must be justifiable to each individual considered one by one, and that the harms or complaints of different individuals may not be summed together to outweigh a single person's serious complaint. He illustrates this with the World Cup broadcast case, in which preventing one person from enduring severe pain would require interrupting a broadcast that a very large number of fans want to watch. Scanlon argues that the fans' aggregated enjoyment gives no valid ground for a principle that would leave that one person in pain for the sake of the broadcast, since such a principle could reasonably be rejected by the one person.

Scanlon accepts, however, that this restriction has a radical implication for rescue cases. Since aggregation is forbidden, the claims of the five may not be added together, and so there is no way for contractualism to say that a choice between saving one person and saving five otherwise similar people should favor the five; the numbers simply do not count. Any attempt to make the numbers count would have to reintroduce the summing of separate complaints that Scanlon has already ruled out. That is why the individualist restriction, for all its attractions, leaves Scanlon unable to explain the basic conviction that one should save the greater number when individual claims are equal.
