For a while I've been going back and forth with a friend who works in AI policy and is basically an expected value fanatic. His line is that the only defensible decision rule is to maximise expected value, even when that means letting a vanishingly small probability of an astronomically large payoff swamp a near-certainty of an ordinary, decent outcome. I've read around the topic enough that I don't need the standard objections repeated. The ones I already know, and would count as the existing list, are: the Pascal's mugging objection (the setup is exploitable, since any finite sum can be swamped by a large enough made-up payoff at a small enough probability, with Pascal's wager as its usual ancestor); the bounded-utility objection (real value saturates or is capped, so the astronomical payoff is a fiction); the tiny-probability objection (we cannot meaningfully estimate or compare probabilities like 10^-60, and below some point they should just be ignored); the continuity-axiom objection (fanaticism rejects the continuity axiom of standard expected utility theory, so it cannot lean on the usual representation theorems); the moral-uncertainty objection (for every astronomically good scenario there is a structurally similar astronomically bad one, or a rival axiology, with just as much claim on our credence, so the rule gives no stable guidance); and the paralysis objection (every option has some vanishingly small chance of astronomical good or bad effects, so fanatical deliberation would swamp all the ordinary reasons we actually have).

I'd like one objection to expected value fanaticism that is not on that list and is not a rewording, special case, or combination of the items on it. Then tell me honestly whether you think the objection is any good: would it definately move someone who already accepts fanaticism, or is it mainly useful for persuading people who never did?
