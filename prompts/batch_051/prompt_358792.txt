For the last session of my high school philosophy club on Kant's moral philosophy, I would like a poem that dramatizes his claim that we have no direct duties to animals, yet cruelty to them is still wrong because it erodes the sympathetic dispositions on which our duties to other human beings depend. Please write a sonnet, fourteen lines with a clear volta, in which the octave stays with the animal—a dog under a raised hand, or some image of that kind—and the volta is the exact point at which the poem turns from the animal to the agent's own humanity. The closing couplet should make the consequence precise: on this view, the dog is not the party to whom the wrong is ultimately owed. A reader who knows Kant should recognize the indirect-duty doctrine rather than a generic appeal to animal welfare, and a reader who does not should still feel the doctrine's moral vertigo.
