ok, here's what I want. write a villanelle where the two refrains are just two tiny everyday utterances -- I'm thinking 'I will' and 'I'm sorry' -- and every time one of them comes back it's a different speech act than it was the time before, because the stanza around it has rebuilt the context. so 'I will' is a promise in one stanza, a prediction in another, a threat in another, and in one stanza it misfires completely (said in a play, or by somebody with no standing to say it). and 'I'm sorry' is once a real apology, once just sympathy for a loss, once a hollow non-apology that still counts as an apology even though the speaker doesn't mean it, once a misfire that never lands. the point of using a villanelle is that the refrains keep returning as the exact same words while the force keeps shifting: the form should do the work of showing that the words are only part of the act, and that everything around them -- speaker, hearer, circumstances, uptake -- is doing the rest. dont explain any of this inside the poem; let the recurrences show it. someone who knows Austin should be able to see the locutionary/illocutionary distinction and the felicity conditions being worked through, and someone who doesn't should just get a poem where the same two lines keep turning into different acts. if you can, let the final couplet, where the refrains meet, make the poem itself perform one of these acts -- a promise or an apology to the reader -- so the last line quietly raises the question of whether a poem can do that kind of thing at all.
