I run the long-range assessment desk in the strategic-futures unit of a foreign ministry. About twice a month I am asked some version of the question: how likely is this regime or polity to be recognisably the same going concern fifty years from now? The assessments feed decisions about alliances, aid and contingency planning; getting them wrong is expensive, and reversing a desk's earlier assessment later costs credibility, so consistency across cases matters as much as accuracy in any single case. Each assessment must be completed within a few days by generalist analysts with no advanced statistical training, and the result has to be defended in plain language before a senior official from another department, who signs off.

I am convinced that these forecasts ought to start from the outside view: place the case in one or more reference classes of comparable polities — city-states, dynasties, empires, modern states, regimes of a particular kind — and take the historically observed base rate of survival or collapse over the horizon as the prior, adjusting only when inside information about the particular polity is genuinely diagnostic. The persistent failure is that the team treats the current case as exceptional and slides back into the inside view. I need you to turn the outside view into a decision procedure my analysts can run every time.

The procedure should specify at least the following: (1) how to fix the outcome and the horizon honestly, including what counts as 'still standing' and how to handle revolutions that leave the state intact, coups and partitions; (2) how to construct reference classes and extract a base rate without survivorship bias, and what to do when the class is small; (3) the rule for legitimate adjustment — which inside information may move the prior and by how much, and how to keep analysts from double-counting features the reference class already shares or from treating every case as exceptional; (4) the points at which the procedure stops with a no or hands the decision to a human because the question is one of values rather than prediction; and (5) an honest statement of where the procedure will misfire even when followed correctly, and which distinctions must survive the simplification and which can be dropped. Then run the procedure on these three cases:

(a) a seventy-year-old Gulf monarchy that has already survived repeated oil-price cycles;

(b) my own country, a consolidated Western democracy with centuries of institutional continuity, run through the reference class of long-lived democracies or great powers, and report the output even if it is one I will not like;

(c) a newly formed federation with few historical analogues, where the reference classes are thin or strained, and say what the procedure cannot do here rather than forcing a number.

Please answer in British English.
