I'm giving a talk next month defending this line: when a biopic or a historical novel dramatizes a real, recently dead person, the main duty isn't accuracy to the historical record. It's the duty not to flatten them into a fate. The distinctive wrong of the genre is turning someone who actually faced live options — they could have done otherwise and it was genuinely hard to see which way to go — into a prop for a pre-chosen plot: the doomed genius, the monster, the saint, the cautionary tale. Factual errors matter only when they erase the live-ness of those choices, not as free-standing violations.

Quick version of the argument, so you know what the objections should actually target: (1) the dead themselves can't be harmed by how we depict them; (2) so any real duty has to land on the living, but (3) it can't just be about upsetting the bereaved — my view has to explain why it's still wrong to turn someone's murdered daughter into a soap villain even when the family consents; so (4) the duty is structural: represent the person as an agent under real, unresolved pressure, because that's what it was to be them. Accuracy is instrumental to (4), not the bottom line.

I'm presenting this to a mixed crowd: half the room are philosophers of art who lean toward creative license, plus moral philosophers (some work on posthumous interests and the ethics of grief), plus two working biopic screenwriters and one public historian. No one there already holds my view.
