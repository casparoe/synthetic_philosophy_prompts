I have been turning over a parallel between George Orwell's account of political language in his essay Politics and the English Language (1946) and a standard worry about large language models, and I cannot settle whether the resemblance is structural or merely verbal.

Orwell argues that political prose degrades when writers reach for dying metaphors and other prefabricated turns of phrase rather than composing sentences whose meaning they have actively chosen. On his view this is not only an aesthetic defect: ready-made language does part of the thinking in the writer's place, so a writer can produce plausible prose without having determined what, precisely, it commits him to. That, he thinks, is what makes such prose hospitable to euphemism and political evasion; the underlying vice is a failure of active thought, and the remedy is a discipline in which one asks what a sentence means before one lets it stand.

A modern worry seems to replay this argument in a different register. A language model generates the statistically most probable continuation of a prompt, and the writer becomes an editor selecting, revising, or discarding machine-produced formulations. The complaint that this is the industrialization of the prefabricated phrase — a way of producing text without having thought one's way through its commitments — looks like Orwell's diagnosis with the act of composition outsourced. But the disanalogy may matter: where Orwell's writer lazily borrows a phrase while retaining authorship, the writer who edits model output may still be exercising exactly the kind of judgment Orwell called for, only later in the process.

I would like a careful verdict on whether this parallel is deep or superficial. Please (1) state the mapping as precisely as possible, indicating which features of Orwell's argument are supposed to correspond to which features of machine-assisted writing; (2) say what would have to be true on both sides for Orwell's causal claim — that prefabricated language degrades thought, and does not merely reflect it — to transfer to the machine case; and (3) identify at least one concrete prediction the parallel would lead one to make that turns out false, or one respect in which the two cases diverge at exactly the point where the analogy is supposed to be doing work.
