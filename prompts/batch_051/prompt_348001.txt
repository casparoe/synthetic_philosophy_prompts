I'm the faculty co-chair of my university's Academic Conduct Board, and we're stuck on a case I said I'd circulate a draft decision for before our Tuesday meeting. Last month a TA in an upper-level philosophy of language course wrote a well-known racial slur on the whiteboard, inside the example sentence "the speaker called the mayor a ___," as part of explaining the difference between using a word and merely mentioning it. He never said it aloud, and the syllabus did warn that slurs would appear as objects of study, but he gave no warning on the day. A student filed a complaint under our conduct code, which bans "discriminatory harassment" but carves out speech with a "clear educational purpose." Half the Board thinks displaying the slur in a classroom, especially without a day-of warning, functions as harassment regardless of intent, because the offense is part of the word's literal meaning and simply seeing it subjects students to it. The other half thinks this is exactly what the educational-purpose exeption is for: the harm of a slur comes from how it is deployed in context, so a signaled example in a philosophy of language course is about as far from harassment as it gets.

Please draft the short decision memo I can circulate: make a definite recommendation—responsible or not responsible, plus any conditions or remedies you think are warranted—and make the Board's reasoning legible. Engage the strongest version of the losing side honestly rather than strawmanning it, and say explicitly where the semantic view of slurs and the pragmatic view actually come apart in this case and where they might not. You are an expert on the semantics and pragmatics of slurs and on university speech policy, so think it through carefully: maybe first set down for yourself the two or three strongest considerations on each side before you commit. I don't need a literature review or citations, just the argument. The memo itself should be continuous prose, written like an email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, and it should lead with your recommendation in the first paragraph. At the very end, tell me how confident you are in the recommendation and single out the point you are least sure about.
