I have agreed to open a small workshop on play and leisure by stating where I stand, and before I read my statement aloud I want it examined for the sort of internal incoherence I may no longer be able to see in my own writing. The position I intend to defend consists of the following commitments.

(1) Play is autotelic: its point is in the activity itself, not in any product, outcome, or external function the activity happens to serve.

(2) I mean this as a claim about value, not merely about definition. The fact that play builds creativity, social bonds, or skill is a real benefit, but it is never what makes an instance of play good as play.

(3) I accept a broadly Pieper-inspired account of leisure: genuine leisure is a condition of receptive stillness and contemplative beholding, distinct both from idleness and from amusement used merely to restore one's capacity for work.

(4) It follows, I think, that leisure should not be identified with self-chosen free time. Some ways of filling free time are restless, stimulus-driven, or escapist, and they lack the stillness and receptivity that define leisure in my sense.

(5) Yet I want to resist the suggestion that this contemplative standard demotes competitive play. Rule-bound, agonistic games — chess, tournament bridge, football played in earnest — strike me as among the most serious and valuable forms of play there are.

(6) I also maintain that play is essentially voluntary: it stands outside the sphere of necessity and obligation, and an activity performed under external compulsion is to that extent not really play.

(7) But I do not conclude that professional athletes fail to play. Being under contract, being paid to win, and following training and competition schedules imposed by others does not, I think, make their play counterfeit.

(8) Nor do I think mercenary motives on their own disqualify anyone. A chess hustler who plays for money is still playing chess, and the game remains good as play.

I would be grateful for a diagnosis rather than a list of doubts. I want to know where these commitments genuinely pull against one another, where an apparent conflict would dissolve under careful restatement, and which single revision would do the most to restore coherence at the lowest cost. Please order the actual tensions by severity, and for each say precisely what would have to give way and what would be lost in giving it up.
