Two friends of mine, Maya and Daniel, got into an argument this weekend about whether it is wrong to use "decimate" to mean "to destroy a large part of" rather than "to reduce by exactly one tenth." Maya said her sister had been corrected for this recently, and the correction was pedantry dressed up as knowledge: a word's Latin root does not fix its present English meaning, words mean what their current speakers use them to mean, and appealing to the earlier sense is the etymological fallacy. Daniel replied that this missed the actual reason for the correction: "decimate" still has an exact use — to reduce by one in ten — and when someone says a city was decimated after most of it burned down, the language loses its only word for that precise proportion. Maya answered that outside of people correcting other people, nobody uses "decimate" in the precise sense, so there is no living word there to lose; insisting on it is like saying "nice" must still mean "ignorant" because that is what it meant centuries ago. Daniel said the cases differ: the semantic change in "nice" is complete, whereas the precise sense of "decimate" survives in military and historical writing, so the word is genuinely ambiguous and the correction earns its keep. Maya then observed that he was being selective, since she had heard him correct someone the previous week for using "begs the question" to mean "raises the question," even though that broader use is common. Daniel answered that "begging the question" is a term of art in logic, and a term of art may keep a fixed meaning, while ordinary words belong to the whole speech community. Maya replied that this just moves the problem back a step: either he can state a principled difference between terms of art and ordinary words that explains why one correction is legitimate and the other is not, or his position is only a preference for the corrections he happens to like.

I could not tell who was right. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move, saying for each argument whether it succeeds against the one it answers. Then take a position on the larger question: is there a principled line that makes one correction acceptable and the other pedantry, or is the asymmetry indefensible? If you propose a principle, test it on at least one nearby case — such as "hopefully" in the sense of "it is to be hoped," or "enormity" in the sense of great size — and say whether it draws the line where a thoughtful speaker should draw it.
