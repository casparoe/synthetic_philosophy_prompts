Here's a thing I've been chewing on. Von Neumann worked on the first actual computer weather forecasts (the ENIAC runs with Charney, 1950), and he was weirdly confident that, once we had big enough computers, we'd understand, predict and eventually engineer weather and climate at global scale. He died in 1957, so he never saw climate science turn into the practice of running dozens of structurally different general circulation models and then treating the spread across them as a kind of uncertainty estimate. If you had to extrapolate from how he actually thought about computation, models, and decisions under uncertainty, where would he land in the modern argument about 'robustness' and decision-relevant uncertainty? Would he be the one averaging every ensemble member with equal weight and calling the ensemble mean the best forecast, the one who wants to throw out models that get some basic physics wrong and trust only the survivors, or the one who says averaging structurally inconsistent models is meaningless and you should pick a single model you understand and push it hard? Or something else entirely? Make the strongest case you can from things he actually said or did, and be explicit about the points where you're just guessing.
