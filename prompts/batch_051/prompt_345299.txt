For a seminar on deontological constraints, prepare a decision tree that sorts a student who accepts the core deontological judgment about killing into one of the main positions on the paradox of deontology — the puzzle that a constraint forbidding the killing of one innocent can require leaving five more innocents to be killed, which looks irrational if the point of the constraint is to reduce such killings.

Every path begins from the student's endorsement of the following case. A surgeon can kill a healthy visitor and harvest his organs to save five patients who will die before morning; the procedure would be undetectable and would not damage trust in hospitals or produce any other bad long-run effects. The student holds that the surgeon may not do this, and that the wrongness is not wholly explained by such long-run effects. A student who says the surgeon may act, or who explains the wrongness entirely by long-run institutional effects, should be routed to one exit leaf — or two, if you keep those two rationales apart — and kept out of the deontological leaves.

The leaves that must appear are: an absolutist agent-centered view, on which the wrongness concerns what the agent does or becomes and no number of other killings could license the act; an absolutist victim-centered view, on which each innocent's inviolability takes precedence and may not be traded away even to protect many; threshold deontology with an agent-relative threshold, on which the constraint fails only at a threshold and delegation or distance does not lower the number needed; threshold deontology with an outcome threshold, on which the constraint gives way once enough violations would otherwise occur, irrespective of who acts; a dirty-hands view, on which the act becomes wrong but required above the threshold and leaves a moral remainder; a Rossian pluralist view, on which there is no fixed threshold and each case calls for an all-things-considered judgment among competing duties; and the minimizing exit view, on which what matters is fewer rights violations overall, so the starting case is rejected once secrecy and long-run effects are controlled.

The nodes of the tree must be concrete cases or specific claims, phrased so that a student can answer without knowing which position each answer leads to, and no node may ask a student to select a position by name. Build the tree out of at least the following cases: the surgical case above, as the direct low-number case; a military commander who can personally shoot one uninvolved villager because an armed group has credibly threatened to massacre N other villagers unless he does exactly that, with the question asked at N = 5, N = 50, and N = 5,000; the same commander at whatever number would move him to act, now offered two alternatives, namely ordering a subordinate to carry out the shooting or authorizing a remote strike in which the villager's death is a foreseen side effect of disabling the group, and asked whether either alternative changes the threshold number; and a triage director who can redeploy a ventilator promised to one patient in order to save five later patients, letting the first die, and asked whether the numbers and judgments differ from the hands-on killing cases. Any additional nodes should come from medicine, law enforcement, military command, or ordinary professional life; apart from the surgical entry case, no standard textbook set pieces.

Before rendering the tree, lay out the logical space from which it is built: the branching questions, the full matrix of possible answer combinations, the coordinates each leaf occupies, and the combinations that are unoccupied. For each empty cell, say whether nobody occupies it because the combination is incoherent, because it is coherent but unattractive, or because it is simply unexplored. Then render the tree compactly with numbered questions, arrows or indentation, and leaf names in small capitals.

Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate by answers to concrete cases alone. I expect the two absolutist leaves to coincide on every first-order question about what the agent may or must do; if they do, say so, and give the one grounding question about why the act is wrong that would separate them, making clear that a verdict-based tree cannot assign a student to one rather than the other. Do not silently merge positions that give the same verdicts for different reasons.

Audience and size: advanced undergraduates who have completed one unit on consequentialism and deontology. Aim for no more than ten nodes and no more than nine leaves.

Lead with your bottom line: one sentence stating which of the candidate distinctions — numbers, agency, delegation, or action versus omission — does the real classificatory work in the tree, and which apparent distinction turns out to be idle. Then give the tree, the logical-space notes, and the flagged pairs, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
