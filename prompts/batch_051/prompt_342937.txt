I'm the economics-and-ideas correspondent for a monthly magazine, and I'm trying to work out what David Hume would have made of the last fifteen years of monetary policy. I've got the broad beats of his position from his economic essays: money is only an instrument of exchange, not wealth itself; in the long run prices track the quantity of money; a sudden injection of coin can briefly stimulate trade because prices and wages adjust with a lag; and trade surpluses are self-correcting, since the inflow of gold and silver that follows them pushes up domestic prices and makes a country's exports less competitive. He was also withering about public credit — as he saw it, government debt compounds until the state either defaults or is crushed by interest, and he thought a clean, deliberate writedown was better than letting it fester. (I'm not after quotes or references; I've got the essays open on my desk.)

The section I'm drafting claims that, transplanted to the present, this framework yields a fairly specific verdict on quantitative easing and on the idea that central banks can safely finance large peacetime deficits by buying government bonds. So I want you to predict Hume's view, and to actually commit rather than give me an on-the-one-hand. Would he have read QE as a benign monetary injection — the short-run stimulus he described — or as public credit by the back door, with an ostensibly independant central bank monetising exactly the sort of debt he thought ended in ruin? In a world of fiat money and floating exchange rates, would he have dropped the specie-flow story or would he have said the exchange rate now does the adjusting that the movement of gold used to do? And what would he have made of the claim that QE was tried at scale without runaway inflation — is that a refutation of his quantity-theory leanings, or just the long and variable lag he might have expected, showing up in asset prices rather than the shops?

Give me an overall verdict plus a confidence score out of ten, and split that into the bits that follow fairly directly from his essays and the bits where you're knowingly extrapolating. And if the whole exercise is anachronistic to the point of breaking — if Hume's categories just don't map onto fiat, floating, central-bank money — say so plainly, because I'd rather kill the section than print something clever that falls appart when you poke it.
