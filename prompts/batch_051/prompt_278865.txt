I was reading W. E. B. Du Bois's 1926 essay "Criteria of Negro Art" with a group of students, and we got stuck on a passage near the end. There Du Bois, as I understand him, insists that his own writing has always been put in the service of winning for black people the right to love and to enjoy, and he refuses to care for any art that is not put to such use. He also seems to claim, more generally, that art can never really be detached from the social struggle in which it is made, because the white public already demands a kind of racial propaganda from black artists and pays for no other.

One of the students built this argument from that passage, and now I cannot find the exit. (1) Du Bois is not merely reporting that art affects politics; he is proposing that art ought to be judged by its service to the liberation of black people. (2) If art ought to be judged by its service to a cause, then the very same formal achievement—the same sonnet, the same gesture in a novel, the same brushstroke—is aesthetically better when it serves a just cause than when it serves an unjust one. (3) But aesthetic merit cannot shift in that way; a masterfully executed image remains masterfully executed even if its moral content is base. (4) Du Bois would have to deny (3), because he would not grant the title of art to beautiful work that nonetheless props up white supremacy. (5) Yet if he denies (3), he seems to collapse the distinction between art and propaganda, or between poetry and a well-crafted political poster. (6) Since Du Bois plainly wanted to preserve something called art—he kept returning to standards of truth, goodness, and beauty—his position is unstable. Which premise should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep Du Bois's overall stance rather than the distinction in (3), but I want to know what I am paying. Please answer in British English.
