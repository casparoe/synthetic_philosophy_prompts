I help run a philosophy of mind workshop at my university, and in a few weeks we are hosting a philosopher who defends a carefully qualified epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness. His view, as he presents it, is not the straw man: he accepts that the felt character of experience—the reddish look of a tomato, the ache of a headache—is produced by physical activity in the brain, but he insists that this felt character never causes anything downstream. It does not cause the words we say, the beliefs we form, or the next brain state. What makes his version interesting is that he concedes all of this and tries to live with it. His usual response to the paradox of phenomenal judgment is that our reports about our own experience are themselves physical events, caused by physical processes, and that their truth and justification do not require the experience being reported to have caused them. He also leans heavily on a lawlike-correlation move: he maintains that the match between an experience and the judgment that one is having that experience is not a fortunate coincidence, because the very neural processes that cause the judgment also cause the experience it describes.

I am moderating the Q&A afterward, and the room will include several people sympathetic to illusionism, who would be very happy to hear him admit that our sense of being conscious reasoners whose experiences matter to our thinking is a causally secured misfire. I would like eight to ten questions that press the paradox of phenomenal judgment specifically, rather than reverting to generic material about the hard problem or inverted spectra. Please include at least one question on the self-stultification charge (the worry that the view cannot be rationally believed or known by the person asserting it), at least one on whether a judgment that is reliably caused by something other than the experience it reports can still count as introspective knowledge, and at least one on why evolution or development would equip creatures with vivid, causally inert experiences plus an apparently irresistible conviction that those experiences guide their reasoning. For each question, include the follow-up keyed to the reply he is most likely to give, especially the anticipated reply that the objection merely assumes a causal theory of knowledge, and the further anticipated reply that shared physical causes secure the correlation.

End with one question that his lawlike-correlation move cannot comfortably absorb: something that asks him to explain why the physical processes in question produce a matched experience-and-judgment pair at all, rather than the judgment alone or the experience alone, and why the content of the judgment should track the character of the inert experience rather than simply track the physical stimulus. I want that final question to leave a real silence, not just provide him with another opportunity to restate the view.
