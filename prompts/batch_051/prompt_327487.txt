I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on free will and moral responsibility — 18 students, one 75-minute session. Design an in-class activity that makes them feel, before it is named, the gap between the metaphysical ability to do otherwise and the varieties of free will worth wanting: acting from one's own reasons, with self-control and authorship over what one does. The activity should put at least one student through a real small-stakes choice (participation points, not money) whose unchosen option is removed by a mechanism the class can see, so that the chooser genuinely could not have done otherwise — and then force students to sit with their own willingness to credit or blame the chooser. No framing lecture up front, and terms such as Frankfurt case, alternative possibilities, and could have done otherwise should appear only in the debrief. Give me rules, roles, materials, timing, and a debrief that maps what happened onto that distinction, says what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and is explicit about which part of the idea the exercise cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself.
