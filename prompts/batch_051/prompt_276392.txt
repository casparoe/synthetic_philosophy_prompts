I keep running into a move in arguments about how much credence to put in long-shot AI-risk scenarios: someone's credences get called "incoherent," and the whole conversation is supposedly settled by the line, if your credences don't satisfy the probability axioms then you're vulnerable to a Dutch book. I've seen it used to dismiss people whose probabilities are just slightly non-additive, which feels way too quick. But when I push on what the line actually claims, nobody gives the same answer. Could you lay out the different precise theses this slogan might be expressing—what kind of Dutch book is at stake (one simultaneous set of bets, a diachronic strategy over time, something involving your updating rule), what exactly gets convicted (your current credence function, your conditionalization habits, your overall dispositions to bet), and what the upshot is supposed to be (that you'll literally lose money in practice, that an idealized bookie could exploit you, or just that you've violated some non-pragmatic norm that the money story is only a dramatic way of illustrating)? For each reading, say whether its trivial, substantive but defensible, or false, and say which ones would actually support the way people use the line to end those arguements.
