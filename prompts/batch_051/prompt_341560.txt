Had yet another argument where someone dropped "Chesterton's fence" like it was a trump card. We were discussing whether the U.S. should abolish the filibuster, and when I said the cost of doing nothing looks higher than the risk of changing the rule, a classmate just said "Chesterton's fence" and leaned back. I get the vague idea — don't tear down a fence until you know why it was put up — but I honestly can't tell what precise claim is supposed to follow from that. Does it mean the status quo gets a rebuttable presumption? That you can't touch an institution until you've reconstructed the original reasons for it well enough to explain them to someone who lived back then? That no one alive can judge an institution they didn't design? Or that the burden of proof is always on the reformer and never shifts? Lay out the different things the slogan could actually be claiming, from the boring/trivial readings to the strong ones, and then tell me which of those readings would actually support using it to shut down my argument about the filibuster, and which are just conservative vibes with no logical force. If the strongest reading that actually does the work has false implications, I want to know that too.
