I'm a moderator on a mid-sized internet forum with a Reddit-style karma system — around 80,000 active users — and we see maybe a dozen contested karma-gaming cases a week. We've always handled them case by case, and now we're getting appeals where users can rightly point out that we've never been able to state the rule. I want a usable decision procedure for one narrow question: when should we strip, freeze, or stop trusting a user's karma score because it no longer reflects a reasonably honest record of how the community judged their actual contributions?

Act as someone who studies reputation systems and has to write policy, not theory. Start by brainstorming the candidate principles someone could be enforcing when they clean up a karma economy, then commit to one and turn it into a checklist my moderators can actually run in ten to fifteen minutes per case. Operate under these constraints: my mods aren't philosphers and don't have data-science tools; every decision must be explainable in two or three sentences in an appeals thread; any reset has to be signed off by two mods; and we'd rather under-police than chill ordinary participation. The principle I'm drawn to is that karma is only useful if it's hard to fake and roughly tracks the community's considered judgment of a person's actual contributions — not their money, not their friendships, and not just their luck with one viral post — but I don't trust myself to turn that into a rule.

Give me the procedure as an ordered list of questions with decision stops: what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, where it hands the decision to a human because the question is a value call, and which steps are really judgment calls dressed up as tests. Then run it on three cases: (a) a genuinely good essayist whose last ten posts were upvoted by an off-site bot ring without her knowledge; (b) a user who deletes every comment that falls below zero and reposts it during busier hours until it scores positive; and (c) a user who earned most of their karma posting early hot takes in breaking-news threads, then sold the account to a marketer, and the account is now pushing a brand's talking points. Make sure at least one of those applications ends in an answer you'd expect me to hate, and say what the procedure can't catch and where it will systematically misfire.
