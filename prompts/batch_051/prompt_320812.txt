After retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have been reading philosophy systematically for the first time, and one methodological difference keeps troubling me. In physics, a thought experiment was usually a constraint: you held the formalism fixed and asked whether a scenario such as Maxwell's demon or Einstein's elevator was coherent, or what the formalism forced you to say about it. In much of the philosophy I now read, vivid hypotheticals seem to do something stronger and more seductive. They do not merely test a view against commitments one already holds; they manufacture the feeling that a conclusion is obvious before an argument has been supplied. Daniel Dennett's label for these devices — intuition pumps — strikes me as uncomfortably accurate, and I have come to think the practice deserves the same skeptical scrutiny as any other instrument of persuasion.

I would like you to write a sustained, deadpan piece of satire aimed at that practice.

Please write it as a glossy promotional brochure, around 650 words, for a fictional consultancy that designs bespoke intuition pumps for philosophers, research institutes, think tanks, and public intellectuals. Mimic the genre of high-end professional-services marketing: open with an About Us paragraph, offer three tiers of service, include a few short testimonials from invented clients, and finish with either a brief FAQ or a one-paragraph case study based on a familiar philosophical thought experiment such as the trolley problem or the experience machine. Do not state the objections outright; let the product details carry them. The satire should land at least the following objections: an intuition pump can smuggle its conclusion in through the choice and framing of the scenario; vividness, emotional salience, and ease of imagining are not reliable indicators of truth; the same scenario can pump opposite intuitions in different people or under different framings; and the resulting intuition is often treated as evidence rather than as a phenomenon that itself needs explaining. The humor should exaggerate features a genuine practitioner would recognize, so that a specialist finds the critique fair rather than a caricature, while an ordinary reader can still tell exactly what is being ironized.

Before you compose the brochure, adopt the role of a professional philosopher who works on metaphilosophy and the epistemology of thought experiments, and who also has a practiced deadpan comic voice. Think carefully: first brainstorm the consultancy's product features, service tiers, testimonials, and the genuine features of intuition pumps each is designed to exaggerate. Only then write the finished brochure, staying in character throughout and never winking at the reader. Aim for approximately 650 words.
