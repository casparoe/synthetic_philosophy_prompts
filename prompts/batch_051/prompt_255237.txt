I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and yesterday two colleagues got into it in the kitchen over Thomson's lamp. You probably know the setup: the lamp starts on at 11:00, someone switches it off at 11:30, on at 11:45, off at 11:52:30, halving the wait each time, so that by noon infinitely many switches have happened. The question: is the lamp on or off at noon?

(1) Dana said it can't be either, so the whole thing is impossible. If it's on at noon, she said, then it got turned on by some switch and then nothing after that turned it off — but there is no last switch; every "on" is followed later by an "off." Same in reverse for off. Since a lamp has to be one or the other, the scenario contains a contradiction, and that's the point: supertasks like this can't actually be performed.

(2) Rafi said that's too quick. The instructions fix the lamp's state for every moment before noon but just don't fix the state at noon. The sequence on, off, on, off... has no limit; that means the description is incomplete, not contradictory. He said a function can be defined on the interval from 11:00 up to but not including noon and simply fail to have a continuous extension. That's a gap in the story, not a proof of impossibility.

(3) Dana came back: a real lamp, whatever the math says, is in some definite state at noon. If both available states conflict with the switching history, then the whole setup can't exist as a physical process. Saying "the math doesn't pick one" doesn't give an actual lamp permission to be in neither state.

(4) Rafi said "conflicts with the history" is doing all the work. A lamp that is on at noon conflicts with no individual switch, because every switch is before noon. All it does is make the state discontinuous at noon. He said Dana is smuggling in a continuity principle — that the end state should be the limit of the states leading up to it — and here the limit doesn't exist. You can't call a scenario impossible just because it violates a regularity you added yourself.

(5) Dana said she isn't assuming full continuity, just that the end state can't be a free lunch. If the version that ends on and the version that ends off are exactly alike at every moment before noon, and nothing at noon makes the difference, then the final state is fixed by nothing at all. In physics, she said, states should be determined by something — a mechanism, a limit, a cause — and this lamp's noon state is determined by none of them.
