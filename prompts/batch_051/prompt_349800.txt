I'm in a class on philosophy of mathematics this term, and we've been doing structuralism and the problem of mathematical objects being "defined up to isomorphism." Last week we read stuff on the automorphism argument — the worry that if a structure has nontrivial automorphisms, then the objects in it can't really have individual identities. I thought I followed it, but a guy in my study group pushed it way further than I expected, and now I cant tell whether the argument is sound or where it breaks.

His argument runs like this:

(1) If an automorphism of a structure maps an object a to an object b, then a and b satisfy exactly the same properties expressible in that structure, because automorphisms preserve all the structure's relations and operations.
(2) So if two objects are related by an automorphism, the structure cannot tell them apart: no statement in its language is true of one but false of the other.
(3) If a structure cannot tell two objects apart, then any distinction between them is not a mathematical fact about the structure; it is only a convention or label imposed from outside.
(4) Take the polynomial x² − 2. Its two roots in Q(√2) are swapped by the automorphism √2 ↦ −√2 fixing Q. By (1)–(3), there is no mathematical fact about which root is √2 and which is −√2.
(5) So the usual definition "√2 is the positive root" is circular or external: positivity depends on an ordering of the real numbers, but x² − 2 by itself comes with no ordering. The "positive root" is just an arbitrary label.
(6) But exactly the same reasoning applies to the additive group of the integers: the map n ↦ −n is an automorphism of (Z, +, 0), swapping 1 and −1. By (1)–(3), there's no fact about which element is 1 and which is −1 within that group.
(7) Yet 1 is not just an arbitrary label! It's the multiplicative identity of Z, the successor of 0, the number of things in a singleton — it's about as canonical as anything in mathematics gets. If this argument makes 1 arbitrary, it proves too much.

I want to keep (5), since it seems right that the polynomial alone doesn't fix a root, but I also don't see how to keep (7) and reject something earlier without giving up the whole structuralist picture. Which premise should I reject, and what do I pay for rejecting it? If the answer is (3), what then makes "√2" refer to one root rather than the other? And if the answer is something else, why doesn't (5)'s point about the ordering collapse into the same problem?
