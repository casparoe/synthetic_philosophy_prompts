You are a professional philosopher of history who is fully at home in the rationalist/LessWrong idiom—priors, model classes, bias-variance tradeoff, explanation as compression. I am a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, which will be obvious from the vocabulary, but the essay I am drafting is aimed at a partly non-rationalist audience; part of what I want from you is a sense of which moves sound insightful to me only because of my home idiom. The essay is for a seminar on big history and its critics, and I am asking for detailed, adversarial-but-constructive written comments of the kind you would give a promising draft in a workshop.

Here is the skeleton of my essay. My central claim is that the familiar debate between grand-narrative authors such as Harari and Diamond and their scholarly critics such as Graeber and Wengrow is not one dispute but two, and participants keep switching between them without noticing. The first dispute is empirical: do the particular examples a narrative uses survive specialist scrutiny? The second is methodological: what is a grand narrative for—is it a set of falsifiable claims about the past, or a compressed, high-level generative model useful for forming priors about how societies work, no single instance of which needs to be exactly right for the model to be useful? My tentative thesis is that the critics often win the first dispute decisively while being largely irrelevant to the second; but, I argue, this is not the exoneration it sounds like, because a historical master-narrative cannot easily buy its bias-variance tradeoff honestly. Unlike a scientific model, its training data were selected under the influence of the very narrative being tested, and there are no new runs of history to validate against. I therefore suggest the right standard is closer to how we judge a prior: not by pointwise fit but by whether the simplifications are predictively useful, which in turn requires being explicit about what would have counted as a different result.

Please comment on: (a) whether the distinction between the two disputes is doing real work or just relabeling "don't judge useful simplifications for inaccuracy"; (b) where I overclaim about the critics, and in particular whether I have made Graeber and Wengrow into strawmen who care only about pointwise fit; (c) the two weakest steps in the argument; and (d) whether the final move of judging grand narratives as priors is coherent given that we cannot replay history. Start by brainstorming the strongest steelman of the critics' methodological position before attacking my argument, and then give specific, revision-oriented comments rather than a list of references. Write the comments in American English.
