I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am currently working on a piece about why the gene's-eye view of evolution still generates so much argument, decades after it was developed by Richard Dawkins and George C. Williams. While doing background reading, I came across a popular science book that summarises the position in the following way:

"Dawkins and Williams established that natural selection operates at the level of the gene, not the organism or the species. A body is best understood as a machine that its genes build in order to get themselves copied; the gene is the real beneficiary of evolution, while the organism is only the vehicle it programs for that purpose. Because genes are selfish in this strict sense, evolution cannot produce genuinely altruistic behaviour — only forms of helping that ultimately serve the helper's genetic interests, such as care for close relatives or the reciprocal exchange of favours. Group selection therefore belongs to the prehistory of the subject: talk of animals acting for the good of the species is sentimental and has no mechanism behind it. An implication is that human beings are ultimately selfish in their deepest motives, since we too were built by selfish replicators."

Something about the final sentence strikes me as too quick, and I suspect the paragraph slides from a claim about genes to a claim about motives, but I am not enough of a specialist to say exactly where it goes wrong. Could you give me a careful, sentence-by-sentence audit of this summary? I am looking for four things. First, what a careful defender of the gene's-eye view would accept in the paragraph, and what they would call an overstatement or a conflation. Second, what Dawkins and Williams actually argued about altruism, kin selection, and the status of group selection, so that I can see which of the summary's claims survive correction. Third, whether the final claim about human motivation follows from the accurate parts of the summary, or is a non sequitur. Fourth, how a critic of the gene's-eye view — in the mould of Stephen Jay Gould, or a contemporary multilevel-selection theorist — would respond, so that my piece does not treat the matter as settled. Since the magazine uses British English conventions, please write your answer in British English.
