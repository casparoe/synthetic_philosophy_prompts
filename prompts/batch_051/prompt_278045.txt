Okay so I've been turning over a distinction about supererogation (doing more than duty requires) and I want an honest assessment of it. I'm going to call it the shape view, because I think the standard picture is obsessed with the pricetag—how much you sacrifice—when it should be obsessed with the shape of the reason—whether the reason is the kind anyone could ever be required to answer to.

Heres the theory. There are two ways an act can look like it goes beyond duty. One is just expensive duty: you're doing something people are already required to do, but in circumstances that make it ruinous or terrifying, like going back into a burning building for a stranger. That's not supererogatory in my book; it's ordinary rescue performed at awful cost. The other kind is supererogation proper: acts that answer to a reason morality recognizes but doesn't require anyone to act on, no matter how little it would cost them. Forgiving a debt you're fully entitled to collect. Donating a kidney to someone you've never met. Telling a painful truth to a friend when you aren't the one who wronged them. Optionality lives in the reason, not in the size of the sacrifice. So a cheap act can be supererogatory and a costly one can be merely dutiful.

I know this brushes against talk about imperfect duties and the 'good but not required' formula, but my specific claim—that cost is strictly irrelevant to classification and only the reason-type matters—might be new, or at least might sharpen things.
