An economics friend sent me an argument that seems to show the standard way of comparing charities quietly rules out all systems-change work, and I can't find the exit. Here it is, tidied up:

(1) Suppose you are choosing between two ways of doing good. For each, what matters is the extra good your donation causes relative to what would have happened anyway — call that its marginal impact.

(2) If you give to a malaria bednet charity, you estimate that impact by looking at the extra nets your money lets the charity buy and the deaths those extra nets avert.

(3) So far, so good.

(4) Now consider an organisation that works on the same problem by paying lawyers, community organisers and civil servants to reform a public-health system.

(5) Its marginal impact is the reform your money caused to happen, not the reform the organisation happened to be wrapped up in.

(6) But a systemic reform comes about through the actions of many actors, and we cannot observe what would have happened without your particular contribution.

(7) Since we cannot estimate that marginal impact, it cannot enter into a rational comparison of the two charities.

(8) And we cannot fall back on giving you a share of the organisation's average impact, because that credits your gift with changes that would have happened without it.

(9) So, on this way of comparing, the systems-change gift comes out worthless.

(10) But if that were right, we would have to say that a donor to the abolitionist movement or women's suffrage achieved nothing on the margin, since no individual donation was necessary or sufficient for the eventual change.

(11) That seems plainly false.

(12) So something has to give: either the marginal-impact criterion in (1), one of the moves from (5) to (9), or the judgement in (11). I would particularly like to keep (1) and (11). Which premise or step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Please answer in British English.
