I am a senior editor at a magazine that covers science and intellectual life, and several times a year our desk faces a version of the same decision. While profiling a scientist or revisiting work that has become canonical, we learn of serious wrongdoing — fabricated data in an influential study, abuse of research subjects, sexual harassment, financial misconduct — that took place decades ago. Sometimes the conduct is legally time-barred; sometimes the person responsible is dead; sometimes the harm is still compounding in ways that are hard to see. The newsroom splits every time. Some editors treat the age of the conduct as nearly dispositive ("that was a different time," "the evidence is too stale"), while others treat it as irrelevant if the underlying facts are well-sourced. We have no shared framework, so the decision tends to be made by whoever argues last.

I would like you to build a decision procedure my editors can actually run: a checklist, or a flow chart in words, with questions in order, a clear statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, a point where the procedure stops with a "no," and a point where it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. Ground it in whatever philosophy and legal theory genuinely know about the moral relevance of time: the difference between a wrong's being harder to prove later and its mattering less later; the possibility that a person is no longer, in a morally relevant sense, the same agent who acted; the community's claimed interest in repose; and the difference between publishing in order to punish and publishing in order to correct a still-living record. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and about the circumstances in which the procedure will misfire. The people using it are not philosophers; it must be usable in under half an hour and its output must be explainable to the editor-in-chief, who signs off.

Then run the procedure on two cases. First: a thirty-five-year-old, now well-documented allegation that a deceased and much-honored scientist fabricated data in a study that still anchors a field; the fabrication has been confirmed by a recent forensic review, but everyone involved is dead. Second: a twenty-year-old pattern of abusive behavior by a scientist who now directs a major institute; the victims did not come forward at the time for fear of retaliation, the legal window has closed, and the behavior appears to have stopped. At least one of these should come out in a way the newsroom will find uncomfortable, and you should end by saying plainly what the procedure cannot settle.
