Adorno and Horkheimer argued that under modern capitalism culture had become an industry: broadcast film, radio, and popular music were manufactured through standardized formulas, and the surface differences among products were what they called pseudo-individualization — mass-produced quirks that allow consumers to feel like distinct individuals while consuming the same underlying product. On their picture the consumer is not sovereign; taste is administered by the industry, and culture's social function is to reconcile people to the existing order by turning entertainment into an extension of work and draining art of whatever is genuinely negative, tense, or resistant.

Now consider how poorly that picture seems to fit the algorithmic present: each person's feed or queue is different, generated in real time from behavioral data; the available catalog is effectively infinite and much of it is produced by users and independent creators rather than by a centralized studio system; platforms claim to give people exactly what their revealed preferences show they want; and the underlying machinery optimizes for engagement rather than for any particular cultural content. On its face, the standardized mass product and the passive audience have both disappeared.

Does Adorno and Horkheimer's culture-industry thesis survive algorithmic personalization, or does it need repair — or is it dead? Identify which premises of the original argument are actually under pressure in this new setting, which premises are strengthened by it, and whether pseudo-individualization still has a role once personalization is real. Then state the strongest version of the thesis that remains, or explain why the framework no longer captures what is worth criticizing.
