I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in critical theory and mass culture: 16 students, one 90-minute session. Design a classroom game that makes the culture-industry thesis associated with Adorno and Horkheimer felt before it is named. I want participants to run into the thought themselves: that a production system can leave creators feeling individual and uncoerced while all their supposedly distinctive products converge, underneath, on the same structural skeleton — surface novelty, structural sameness — and that this happens through rational response to incentives rather than through censorship or orders. From the start of the session until the reveal, the terms culture industry, standardization, and pseudo-individualization must not be spoken, and there should be no framing lecture.

The activity should move participants through three visible stages. First, small teams produce something they genuinely believe is distinctive — a pitch, a concept, a format — under a brief that tells them to be original. Second, the scoring, commissioning, or selection rules they must satisfy quietly price structural novelty out while rewarding cosmetic differentiation, so that teams spend their limited deviation resources on surface features. Third, a reveal forces them to recognize that, once surface identifiers are stripped away, the skeletons of their products are interchangeable. The core effect must be produced by the rules and incentives themselves; the reveal should be a procedure rather than a speech. By the end, participants should come away seeing that the convergence was latent in the rules they rationally answered to, and that a system can generate differences on a preselected surface while leaving the underlying logic untouched.

Please give me everything needed to run the activity: a one-paragraph description of the game; the materials — cards, rubric, tokens, score sheets — and how to prepare them; roles and rules; a timeline with minute marks; what the facilitator says and does at each transition; and a debrief.

In the debrief, map specific moments of the activity onto the idea: the early confidence onto the producer's self-understanding; the rubric onto the objective constraint that does the standardizing; the permitted deviations onto pseudo-individualization; the reveal onto standardization; and the fact that no one was coerced onto the thesis that the system coordinates through producers' own rational choices. Tell me what will predictably go wrong when this is run — a team that cynically games the rubric, a team that produces something genuinely strange, a group that resists the conclusion — and how the facilitator should use each failure as evidence rather than noise. Be explicit about which feature of the culture-industry argument the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply in discussion; do not default to the claim about consumers unless that is the correct answer, and say also where the activity leans on a strong or contested reading of the thesis rather than a minimal one.

Finally, state how confident you are that the convergence effect is a reliable product of the proposed rules rather than an artifact of a particular classroom or facilitator, and single out the one design element you are least sure will work as intended, together with a one-sentence fallback if it fails.
