I spent most of last night arguing with a friend who works in formal epistemology, and we kept circling the same impasse. The topic was higher-order evidence. We started from the standard kind of case: you have worked carefully through a body of first-order evidence and formed a confident belief that P; you then acquire good but higher-order evidence that your reasoning was probably compromised — you are exhausted, or a drug known to degrade exactly this kind of evaluation is in your system, or you have independent reason to think one of your biases is active in this domain — while the first-order case for P still looks compelling on reflection. She defended the conciliationist line: your own reasoning is an instrument like any other, and if you learn that the instrument was probably malfunctioning, you should discount its output and reduce your confidence in P. I found myself unable to inhabit the resulting state — believing P while also judging, in the same breath, that the reasoning that produced the belief was the output of a malfunctioning instrument — without feeling I was trying to occupy two points of view at once. She replied that my resistance was just introspection claiming authority over evidence, and that I was refusing to treat my own mind as part of the world. I replied that her view seems to require a rational agent to hold a combination of attitudes — believing P while judging herself unjustified in believing P — that may not be rationally occupiable at all, which means her policy may never get a grip on any coherent subject.

I don't want a verdict on which of us is right. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, and why the surrounding debate has been so hard to close. My hypothesis after the argument is that the two sides are answering different questions under one name: one side is offering a diachronic updating policy for what to do when you learn you were probably wrong, while the other is offering a synchronic constraint on which combinations of first-order and higher-order attitudes a coherent agent can occupy at a time, and the familiar 'level-splitting' maneuvers and talk of 'calibration' look to me like attempts to keep those two projects apart while appearing to answer the other side. Is that diagnosis right, or is there a single substantive question underneath — something like whether first-order reasoning is transparent in a way that insulates it from higher-order doubt — on which one side is simply mistaken? And given the diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a formal result about update rules and fixed points, a sharper taxonomy of the levels at which evidence can bear, a decision-theoretic argument, or is the disagreement ultimately between starting intuitions that no argument can reach?
