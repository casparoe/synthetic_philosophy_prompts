A private foundation is being established with a one-shot mandate: to choose, from among the candidate futures its program officers and outside proponents assemble, the one it will use its resources to bring about. Every candidate future is represented as a finite, non-empty list of lives, each assigned a numerical lifetime welfare level; positive levels are lives worth living, and negative levels are lives not worth living. To sidestep the familiar clash between total and average views, the board adopts the following rule. A threshold T, any positive number, is fixed in advance and may be changed only by a two-thirds vote of the board. For each candidate future, the board computes total welfare (the sum of the lifetime welfare levels) and average welfare (total welfare divided by population size). A future is admissible exactly when its average welfare is at least T. The board then selects, from the admissible futures, the one with the greatest total welfare; if no future is admissible, it falls back to the future with the greatest average welfare, even though that future lies below T. Ties are broken by lottery.

The board asks you to analyze this rule as a mechanism before it locks in a value of T. In particular: (1) trace how the rule's selections change as T is lowered toward zero and raised above every candidate's average, and identify the discontinuities; (2) explain whether, for a badly chosen T, the rule still licenses a repugnant-conclusion-like verdict—one in which an enormous population of lives barely worth living, clustered just above T, outranks a much smaller flourishing population because its total welfare is greater—and whether any fixed positive T escapes that result robustly rather than by accident of the current menu; (3) analyze how someone who can shape the menu can game the floor by bundling below-threshold lives with high-welfare lives or by splitting a single population into several candidate futures; (4) identify the perversities of the fallback clause, including cases in which the floor removes itself exactly when it begins to bite, and cases in which a tiny upward adjustment in T changes the selection from a high-total future to a very different high-average, low-total future; and (5) say which of these defects could be repaired by modifying the rule (for example, by making the threshold depend on more than a bare average, by using vague welfare ranges rather than precise numbers, or by requiring an external auditor's sign-off to change T) and which defects are just the hybrid rule's way of relocating, rather than solving, the underlying problem. Do not simply praise or condemn total or average utilitarianism. Your response must be at most 900 words.
