The precautionary principle usually gets summed up as something like: don't let a lack of full scientific certainty be a reason to delay protective measures against a serious threat. The cases that make it look obviously right all seem to be sticky ones — a chemical that bioaccumulates, a species that goes extinct, a climate threshold you cross — where the harm, once it happens, is there for good. But a lot of the technologies people actually fight about now are closer to the opposite: a bad software update can be rolled back in an afternoon, a recommender-system change can be reverted, an mRNA vaccine can be reformulated next season, maybe even a geoengineering program could be switched off, with side effects. Suppose the reversibility is real but not perfect. Does the precautionary principle still do its old work for those technologies, or does it quietly turn into ordinary cost-benefit analysis with faster updating? I want you to pin down which premise of the precautionary argument reversibility actually pulls on — the premise that errors are sticky and expensive, or the premise that by the time you learn of the harm it's too late — and then say whether there's a repaired version of the principle that still says something distinctive in a reversible world. So: does it survive, need repair, or is it dead in that setting?
