I am a third-year undergraduate currently taking a metaphysics course on dispositions, laws, and counterfactuals. We have just finished the unit on the simple conditional analysis of dispositions — the view that, for example, a glass is fragile just in case it would break if struck — and on the two standard kinds of counterexample: finks, in which the occurrence of the stimulus would remove the disposition before it could manifest, and masks, in which the disposition remains in place but an interfering factor prevents the manifestation. A classmate has argued that the fink/mask distinction is merely verbal. His claim is that both kinds of case are simply counterfactual failures of the simple analysis, and that the only difference is whether we choose to describe the causal story as the disposition being destroyed or as its being blocked from doing its work. I would like a careful, self-contained explanation of whether he is right. Is there a substantive metaphysical difference between a finkish disposition and a masked disposition, or are they two redescriptions of the same logical situation? If the distinction is substantive, I would like the explanation to make clear exactly what hangs on it — for instance, for the prospects of repairing the conditional analysis, for the thesis that dispositions are intrinsic properties of their bearers, and for how scientists reason about dispositions in unstable environments. If the distinction collapses, I would like an account of what a unified treatment of both phenomena would have to look like, and what it would still need to explain. Please do not provide a bibliography or suggest readings; I need a rigorous treatment of the conceptual issue itself.
