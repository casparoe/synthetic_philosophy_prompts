I'm giving the toast at my father's retirement dinner. He spent three decades in senior roles in the federal government, including serving as a Cabinet secretary, and on four State of the Union nights he was the designated survivor: the Cabinet member kept away from the Capitol at a secure, undisclosed location so that, if an attack on the address killed the President, the Vice President, and nearly everyone else in the constitutional line of succession, there would still be someone to govern. About half the room will be people from that world—continuity-of-government planners, former chiefs of staff, people who know exactly what the role does and does not mean—and the other half will be family and friends whose main reference point is the television drama about the job, which they think of as a thriller. The toast must run about five minutes, land for both halves of the room, and stay warm: a tribute to a living person, not a lecture.

The problem the speech should work through, rather than decorate with, is this. How do you honor someone whose most distinctive service was to be the person left behind to govern if nearly everyone else at the top of the government had been killed? If the speech says thank God it never happened, that risks implying that all the preparation was idle. If it celebrates that he was chosen to survive, it risks implying something unbearable about everyone who was not. If it leans on the glamorous television version, it misses the truth of the work: contingency binders, lonely rehearsals, and a duty whose successful discharge consists in never being discharged. I keep finding that the obvious things to say collapse under that tension. The moral strangeness of the role—the arbitrariness of being made the survivor, the survivor's guilt that seems built into the assignment, the tension between continuity of government as an institutional good and survival as a personal fate—should do real structural work in the speech, shaping what can and cannot be said. Write the toast.
