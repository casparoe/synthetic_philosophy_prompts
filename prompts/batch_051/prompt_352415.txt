I'm trying to write up my actual views on the gender-equality paradox for a grad seminar on gender and social policy, and I want a cold-eyed consistency check before I circulate the statment. The seminar is half sociologists and half economists, and I keep getting pulled in different directions, so I'm not asking for a reading list — I just want the internal tensions audited.

As of this week I'd sign onto all of the following. (1) The gender-equality paradox is basically real: on the standard cross-national indices, the more gender-egalitarian a country looks, the larger the average sex difference in things like STEM versus people-oriented majors, and in some measured preferences — the gap doesn't shrink, and sometimes widens; I don't think this can all be waved away as a quirk of how the indices are built. (2) Career-relevant preferences, and the choices that flow from them, are substantially socially constructed — shaped by stereotypes, socialization, and the gendered division of labor — rather than being mostly hardwired. (3) The fact that a preference was socially shaped doesn't make respecting the resulting choice worthless; we shouldn't overrule people's actual choices in the name of equal outcomes, even when those choices are heavily sex-typical. (4) The paradox doesn't threaten egalitarian values, because egalitarianism is about equal status, rights, and opportunity, not about equal head-counts of men and women in every occupation. (5) Gender-role ideology is one of the main levers on occupational segregation, so if you weakened gender-role beliefs in a country while holding incomes and institutions fixed, I'd expect the sex segregation of careers to go down, not up.

Which of these are actually in tension, and which only look like they are once the right distinctions are drawn? I genuinely can't tell where the worst problem is, so please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and for each tension say what would have to give to fix it and what I'd lose by giving it up.
