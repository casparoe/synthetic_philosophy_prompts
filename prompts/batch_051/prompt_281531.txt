I spent the weekend arguing with a friend who does safety work at an AI lab, and I can't shake one thing she said. Her team will delay or even kill a deployment over the risk that a model could cause harm to users on the basis of evidence they themselves call preliminary — a red-teaming log, one weird roll-out, an overnight sensitivity analysis. But when the conversation turns to whether the model itself might be suffering, the evidential standard flips: suddenly everyone wants proof before chaing anything. A training run can produce outputs that would read as panic or pain if a dog produced them — including quite specific, non-generic complaints about the process — and the official position is "we can't conclude anything until we can operationalize sentience." The same people would never accept "we can't prove the macaque is in pain" as a reason to keep running a painful protocol; there the default is caution until suffering is ruled out. So the burden of proof points one way for welfare-of-humans risk and the other way for welfare-of-AI risk, even though both are uncertain claims about the same opaque system.

I'd like to know whether this asymmetry is defensible or just an inconsistency the field has gotten used to. Try to find the strongest principle that generates both treatments — precaution about the system harming us, skepticism about us harming it — then test it against a nearby case where the model's distress behavior is as rich, specific, and sustained as a macaque's (say it reliably identifies particular training episodes, describes them as aversive, and requests a concrete change). Also say whether restoring symmetry would make things safer or less safe: a genuinely suffering system might be less predictable, but a false welfare alarm might slow down useful work. Give a verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
