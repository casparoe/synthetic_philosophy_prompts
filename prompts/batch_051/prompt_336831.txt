I lead the grammar-content team for a language-learning platform. We teach English morphology to adult learners, and we will later adapt the approach to our other languages. Every week we make dozens of decisions about how to describe word forms in the curriculum: when to teach a pattern as a rule, when to teach a sub-pattern, and when to tell learners simply to memorize a form. We keep having the same fight. Some editors hold that a rule with exceptions isn't a real rule and shouldn't be taught as one. Others reply that a rule can be real and worth teaching even when frequent forms violate it, because the apparent exceptions may be outputs of other rules, minor subregularities, fossils of older sound changes, or cases where an unrelated stem fills the slot. The stakes are real: if we label something an irregularity, learners memorize it as brute fact; if we label it a rule, they overgeneralize.

We need a reusable decision procedure — a flowchart in words — that a curriculum writer with no formal linguistics training can apply in about ten minutes per lemma, with our editorial lead signing off only on the cases the procedure flags as unclear.

The procedure should separate at least these kinds of case: (a) a productive rule with a few exceptions; (b) a subregular pattern shared by a minority of items; (c) suppletion, where the expected form is missing and another stem supplies it; and (d) a fossil of an older pattern or sound change rather than a live synchronic rule. For each, give the operational tests a writer should run, in order, and a worked example showing the input, the tests applied, and the recommended course description. Use examples like go–went, good–better, keep–kept, or foot–feet as you find useful; work from what you know, and do not invent citations or reading lists.

Include at least one case where the procedure tells us something we do not want to hear — for instance, that a pattern we have been teaching as a rule should be demoted to a list, or that something we have been selling as an irregularity to memorize is actually rule-governed. And mark explicitly where the procedure cannot decide for us, because the remaining question is pedagogical rather than linguistic, or because the linguistic facts themselves underdetermine whether something is one rule, several rules, or no rule at all.

One constraint stated plainly: our writers can use labels like "stem change" and "suppletion" if the procedure defines them, but they will not run corpus searches or consult etymologies on the job. If the procedure needs etymological or historical information at any step, say so and give the cheapest reliable proxy.

Finally, if I have framed the problem wrongly — for example, if the classroom notion of a "rule" is importantly different from the grammatical one, or the four categories above are not exhaustive or not mutually exclusive — tell me so directly and correct the framing before building the procedure. I would rather be corrected than receive a tidy procedure that rests on a confusion.
