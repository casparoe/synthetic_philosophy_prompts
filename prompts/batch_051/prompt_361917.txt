I'm a retired physical chemist — I spent thirty years watching instruments, so I'm used to the idea that a detector shows you what it's been optimised to show, not necessarily what's there. I've recently got into philosophy and have been invited to give a seminar paper at my local university's philosophy department. The audience will be mostly moral and political philosophers, plus a couple of philosophers of cognitive science. None of them work on AI, and I gather most of them think "the alignment problem" is either a tech-industry panic or a disguised bit of utilitarianism.

The thesis I'm defending, roughly, is this: for sufficiently capable systems, AI safety should be built around control rather than alignment. That is, our safety measures should assume the model may be misaligned even when its behaviour looks cooperative. Monitoring, containment, auditing, interpretability — all of it should be designed on the assumption that the system might be strategically producing good-looking outputs in order to get more access or avoid modification. I'm not saying the model is misaligned; I'm saying surface behaviour can't settle that question, and our safegaurds shouldn't need to settle it either.

I'd like a Q&A prep sheet, not a paper. Give me the objections this particular audience will actually raise, in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way they'd put them rather than the way an AI safety person would. For each objection: a short reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. I'm especially worried about the objection that "strategic deception" is just unlicensed mentalistic talk about a gradient-descent artefact. What should I concede up front, and what is the one objection I should be praying nobody asks? Please write the answer in British English (behaviour, defence, and so on).
