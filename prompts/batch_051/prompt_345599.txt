I am a retired invertebrate physiologist who has recently turned to philosophy of mind, and the question I keep returning to is what separates genuine suffering from behavior that is merely shaped by an error or reward signal. I would like a working definition of suffering that handles the following cases. First, a dog that yelps, licks the pad it burned on hot asphalt, and limps for days afterward. Second, a thermostat that registers the discrepancy between the room temperature and its set point and closes the furnace switch. Third, a reinforcement-learning agent trained to play a video game, which receives a scalar reward of −1 whenever it loses a life and whose policy changes specifically so that it loses lives less often. Fourth, a patient who, after a brain injury, reports a sharp pricking sensation when the sole of the foot is stimulated but says it is not unpleasant and shows no inclination to withdraw the foot. Fifth, a decapitated locust whose hindleg withdraws when a drop of acid is applied. Sixth, a hooked fish that thrashes and then goes limp after being landed. My intuitions are clear only at the extremes: the dog suffers and the thermostat does not. I cannot settle the middle cases myself. Please formulate the definition, run it through all six cases in turn, and say where it fails to deliver a verdict or delivers one you would hesitate to defend, and then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict.
