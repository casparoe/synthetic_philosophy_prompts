A recurring move in debates about abandoned software is the analogy with derelict physical property. Its users argue that a boarded-up warehouse still has an owner, a vacant lot still has a titleholder, and mere neglect does not transfer title to the person who starts using the space. From this they conclude that old software remains fully owned no matter how long it has gone unmaintained, and that running, copying, archiving, or patching it without permission is best understood as a tidy form of trespass. The analogy is carrying the argument: it is what turns the owner's silence into continuing consent.

I want a detailed audit of how far this analogy actually holds. Give a correspondence table first: what is supposed to correspond to the building, the deed, the fence or lock, the physical decay, the squatter, the upkeep, and the abandonment itself. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the ownership conclusion from those that are merely cosmetic. In particular, address whether any of these breaks the comparison in a way that undermines the conclusion: the non-rivalry of copying, since nothing is taken from the owner when a copy is made; the difference between neglect and abandonment as signals of intent; the difference between a known, locatable owner and an unknown or unlocatable one; and the absence for most old software of anything like a public title registry. Also say whether adverse possession, which belongs to the very domain the analogy leans on, cuts for or against the conclusion, and whether the analogy's users are entitled to quietly drop that feature.

Finally, give a verdict: does the trespass conclusion survive the disanalogies, does the analogy have to be revised to state the opposing case, or should abandoned software be compared to something else entirely, such as orphaned works, and what would that replacement comparison license instead?
