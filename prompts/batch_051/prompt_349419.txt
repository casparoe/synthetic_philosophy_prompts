I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been reading Hume's essay Of Tragedy because I want to write something about why people willingly watch sad films. He has this move that keeps bothering me. He claims that in a well-written tragedy the sorrow and anxiety the audience feels are not just a cost you pay for the pleasure — they actually feed the pleasure. As I understand him, the painful passions stirred up by the drama, when they come with eloquence and the pleasure we take in imitation, are converted into the dominant feeling of delight, so that the more powerfully the playwright brings us to feel the character's distress, the more we enjoy the whole thing. The displeasure becomes a kind of fuel, not a deduction.

I keep thinking this sounds like a testable psychological claim dressed up as an aesthetic one. So here's what I want: design the strongest empirical test you can of Hume's idea that negative affect in an aesthetic context can amplify enjoyment rather than merely accompany it. Give me the concrete setup — what stimuli you'd use, what you'd manipulate, what you'd measure, and what comparison conditions would matter. Then tell me what pattern of results would genuinely favor Hume and what would count against him, including whether self-report ratings of enjoyment would be enough or whether you'd need something like physiological arousal, choice behavior, or time spent re-engaging with the material. And then be honest about the residue: even in the best possible outcome, what would the experiment still leave untouched, like whether the pain is literally transformed into pleasure or whether we're just relabeling the whole mixed experience after the fact because it feels flattering to say so.
