I've been thinking about updateless decision theory for a while and keep hitting a wall. The idea, roughly, is that you shouldn't decide act by act; you should act according to the policy you'd have wanted to commit to before you learned which of these situations you're in. That sounds right to me in the abstract, but whenever I test it against specific cases my gut verdicts seem to point in slightly different directions, and I can't state the principle cleanly. My only candidate is basically that: choose the action, ask what policy it's part of, pick the policy you'd have endorsed in advance. But that feels circular, and I can't tell whether it means I have to pay up after the fact.

So here are my actual verdicts, as honestly as I can report them:

- Ordinary Newcomb: an opaque box gets a million dollars iff a very good predictor (99%) predicts you'll take only that box, and there is a clear box with a thousand; you can take one or both. I'd take only the opaque box.
- The hitchhiker: I'm stranded in a desert, the driver will give me a ride only if I sincerely intend to pay him a thousand dollars when I get home; he can detect lies perfectly but can't collect later. I'd form the intention, and I actually would pay once home, even though at that point the ride is sunk and the payment is a pure loss.
- Transparent Newcomb: same predictor, but before I choose I can see that the opaque box already has the million in it and the clear box has the thousand. I still think I'd take only the opaque box, although a loud part of me says two-boxing is an extra thousand for free and the prediction is already made.
- Counterfactual mugging: a very powerful machine tells me it flipped a fair coin. On heads it asks me for a hundred dollars. On tails it would have paid me ten thousand dollars, but only if I would have paid on heads. The coin came up heads, and it asks for the hundred. I'd pay.
- A one-shot prisoner's dilemma against an exact copy of me, in two identical rooms, both knowing the other chooses exactly what I'll choose: I'd cooperate. Against a random stranger in a normal one-shot prisoner's dilemma I'd defect.

The pair I go back and forth on is transparent Newcomb and the hitchhiker-after-I'm-home: both say 'keep acting as the precommitted agent,' but the Newcomb one feels fanatical in a way the hitchhiker one doesn't, and I don't know wether that's a real distinction or just my nerves. Can you tell me what principle—or small set of candidate principles—captures all five verdicts? If no clean principle captures all of them, say which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, and why it's that one rather than a slightly more complicated principle that saves everything. Then apply whatever account you end up with to a case I genuinely haven't settled: before playing one ordinary game of Newcomb, I'm offered a pill that would just irrreversibly make me unable to two-box; am I supposed to take it, does it depend on the pill's price, and is the 'take the pill' answer an application of the same principle or a cheat?
