In the United States, a company can put a binding-arbitration clause in a consumer contract, an employment agreement, or a nursing-home admission form, and when the company later breaks the law — wage theft, employment discrimination, consumer fraud, serious neglect — the worker or customer is generally pushed out of court into a private, confidential tribunal. There may be no jury, no class action, no public written decision, and only the narrowest review on appeal. Most people, and the law, treat this as ordinary contract enforcement: a regrettable feature of fine print, but not a scandal. Yet imagine the same logic extended one step further. Suppose a legislature allowed a criminal defendant to be tried and sentenced in a private, confidential tribunal, with looser evidentiary rules and minimal appeal, on the ground that the defendant had signed a form agreeing to resolve the matter that way. Almost nobody would call that a neutral dispute-resolution clause; they would call it the abolition of public justice.

What principle, if any, draws the line between these two cases? Is it that criminal law punishes and stigmatizes while civil law only moves money around; that the state is itself a party in a prosecution; that liberty matters more than property; that public condemnation must be delivered in public; or something else? I want the strongest candidate principle you can construct, tested against the cases where the distinction starts to bleed: an eviction, a deportation, a civil-commitment hearing, a government fine. If your principle holds the line, show that it does not merely track wealth or the accidents of legal classification. If it cannot hold the line, say so plainly, and tell me whether the right response is to shrink private adjudication of statutory rights or to accept its extension further than we currently do.
