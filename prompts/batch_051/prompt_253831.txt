In Leviathan, Hobbes defends a version of sovereign immunity through an authorization story: subjects covenant with one another to authorize the sovereign's actions, so whatever the sovereign does to a subject is done by the subject's own authority; it cannot be an injury in the strict sense, since no one can commit an injury against himself. The sovereign may sin, but cannot be unjust to subjects. Modern legal systems, however, have developed a different picture: the state is treated as a distinct legal person that can enter contracts, own property, commit torts, and violate constitutional rights in its own name. Does Hobbes's argument survive this development? Identify the exact premise that this legal-personality doctrine puts under pressure, and say whether the argument can be repaired in a weaker form that still supports any part of sovereign immunity.

Structure your response as follows:

1. The Hobbesian argument (about 150 words): reconstruct the authorization argument and state which premise does the main work.

2. The legal-personality development (about 150 words): explain how the doctrine of state legal personality differs from Hobbes's commonwealth of authors.

3. Assessment (about 250 words): say which premise the development falsifies or bypasses, and whether the argument is dead or merely needs repair.

4. What can be salvaged (about 200 words): if repair is possible, state the weaker principle that remains; if not, say what a defender of sovereign immunity should argue instead.
