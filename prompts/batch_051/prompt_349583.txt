I direct the scenario practice at a geopolitical risk analysis firm. Each quarter we are asked to produce a probability, for one major power we cover, that it will undergo a severe political break within the next twenty-five years: collapse of central authority, territorial fragmentation, military occupation by a rival, or a revolution that replaces its basic constitutional order. These judgments go to institutional investors and to two governments' planning units. A missed call on a major power is expensive for clients and damaging for the ministries that rely on us, while a false alarm erodes our credibility with both. The analysts are resourceful generalists with no statistical training and about two days per case. Our review committee currently expects three things in each report: a plausible narrative, several historical parallels, and a gesture at uncertainty. I believe the narratives are doing too much of the work. They are quick, coherent, and wrong in predictable ways: they dwell on the current leader, the intelligence of the regime's plans, the size of its military budget, and other inside-view details that I have learned, from years reading rationalist discussions of reference-class forecasting and the outside view, to distrust.

Please write a reusable decision procedure my analysts can follow, in order, to produce a twenty-five-year political-break probability from historical base rates and explicit, pre-registered adjustments. For each step, specify the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, and what to do when the answer is weak or unavailable. The procedure should cover at least the following: how to define the reference class, including inclusion criteria, period, and minimum case count, without the definition being chosen to flatter the case at hand; how to extract a base rate and represent uncertainty about it when the underlying data are coarse and contestable; which features of a present-day power may adjust the base rate, and under what sort of evidence; how to audit an analyst's reasoning for inside-view contamination; how to combine disagreements among analysts; and how to report the final number so the review committee can see which parts are mechanical and which are judgment. It must run within two days by generalists without statistical training. I would rather have a coarse but honest procedure than a polished model with hidden judgments. Mark every step where the procedure cannot decide for itself, including places where a values call must be handed to a human. Design it so that when a forecast is wrong we can later identify which step needed to be different, rather than simply concluding that the narrative was bad.

Then apply the procedure to two cases and show the workings. First, the Four Basins Compact: a large, fast-growing, fiscally solvent but politically closed power with a restive coastal minority and no major war since its unification 140 years ago. Second, the Thalander League: an old, wealthy, internally liberal federation whose regional military advantage is fading while its confederal institutions gridlock. Assume both cases satisfy whatever basic eligibility criteria the procedure imposes. For each, show which reference class the procedure selects, what base rate it yields, which adjustments are actually licensed by the supplied facts, what final probability range emerges, and what the dominant remaining uncertainty is. Make sure at least one of the two worked cases is one where the procedure outputs a conclusion the analyst would likely not have reached from the inside view alone. Finally, state explicitly what this procedure cannot do: which questions it cannot answer at all, which questions it answers only by smuggling in a value judgment, and which kinds of future it is structurally blind to. Do not cite sources or rely on being able to look anything up; the procedure should require only widely known historical patterns and the facts supplied in the case.
