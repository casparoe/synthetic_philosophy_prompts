I'm a philosophy professor and I supervise about six PhD students and a postdoc, which means I have some version of this conversation every couple of weeks: a student tells me, with what looks like complete sincerity, that finishing the dissertation is the most important thing in their life, and then week after week they produce nothing and can't really explain why. I want a reusable procedure I can run in the fifteen minutes before a supervision meeting, to sort out whether I'm looking at (a) garden-variety akrasia, where they hold the judgment 'I should be writing' and keep acting against it even at the moment of avoidance, (b) the kind of time-inconsistency people talk about with hyperbolic discounting, where their preferences genuinely flip as the delay between effort and payoff changes, so the Tuesday-morning self doesn't want what the January self wanted, (c) their values actually drifting, so that the dissertation has stopped being the thing they want, or (d) depression or burnout that just shows up as writer's block. And then I need the corresponding move for each: when do I help them build commitment devices and external deadlines, when do I argue with the present self, when do I take 'I've realized I don't actually want this' at face value and help them leave without shame, and when do I push them toward the counseling center?

The procedure has to be honest that I can't observe preferences, only words, behavior, and my own memory of what they said three months ago. I want it to flag every step where I'm making a judgment call and just pretending its a decision rule. It should also stay clear about the difference between the student who, at the moment of avoidance, still says 'I should be writing right now' and feels bad, and the student who at that moment has stopped caring about the project and only regrets it later—my sense is the first is closer to akrasia and the second is closer to what the discounting picture predicts, but I want the procedure itself to test that rather then rely on my hunch. Every spring I have to sign a progress report that can cut their funding if I say they're not making satisfactory progress, so the stakes are real, and I'm not a clinician, so I need to know where the procedure should hand off to the counseling center.

Work it through on two or three invented but realistic cases, including at least one where the procedure tells me something I don't want to hear—either that I've been coercively propping up a project the student has genuinely abandoned, or that I've been respecting a 'present self' whose preferences are just the usual discounting and I should have locked in deadlines much earlier. End by saying what the procedure cannot do.
