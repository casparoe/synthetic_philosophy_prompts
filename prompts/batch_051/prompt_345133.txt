I've been turning over a version of the nonidentity problem that came up in a working group on obligations to future people, and I keep going in circles. Imagine a society choosing between two long-run energy policies, abstracting away from effects on anyone already alive. Under Policy A, a program of sustained investment, future people live moderately well. Under Policy B, a path of extraction and depletion followed by adaptation, the same number of future people come to exist, but their lives are far worse — still worth living, but marked by chronic scarcity, avoidable illness, and precarity. The familiar identity complication applies: over the relevant timescales, the choice between A and B reshuffles who meets whom and when particular people are conceived, so no individual who exists under B would have existed under A. Against that background, someone in the group ran an argument that seems to prove such choices can never be wrong. (1) An act wrongs a person only if it makes that person worse off than they would otherwise have been. (2) A person is made worse off than they would otherwise have been only if there is an alternative course of action under which that same person would have existed and fared better. (3) There is no such alternative for any person who exists under Policy B: had A been chosen, those people would never have existed. (4) So no person under B is worse off than they would otherwise have been. (5) So, by (1), no person is wronged. (6) If no individual is wronged by a policy, the policy is not wrong. (7) Therefore Policy B is not wrong. That conclusion seems to prove too much: by the same reasoning, a policy that knowingly leaves the entire future population with lives only marginally worth living would wrong no one, provided only that those lives are worth living.

My question is not a request for a survey of the nonidentity literature; I want a diagnosis of where this particular argument goes wrong, if it does. Which premise or step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep the judgment that Policy B is genuinely wrong, and I would also like to keep the idea that being worse off is a comparative matter — worse off than some alternative for the same life. Those two commitments keep pulling in opposite directions inside the argument, and I cannot yet tell whether the real difficulty is a slide from the absence of an individual victim to the absence of any wrong, or something in what it means to wrong people whose existence is itself one of the things we chose. Please lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence — the specific step you reject and what you would replace it with — and then give the reasoning for that diagnosis, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
