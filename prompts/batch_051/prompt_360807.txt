I'm a novelist at work on a book whose protagonist, a disciplined and outwardly successful professional, receives a terminal diagnosis and must decide whether the work that structured his life ever gave it meaning. I have been rereading Tolstoy's The Death of Ivan Ilyich as I draft, and I would like a careful philosophical reading of the novella through Moritz Schlick's essay on the meaning of life. My working understanding of Schlick is roughly this: he treats the question of life's meaning not as a question about whether a whole life has some external purpose, but as a question about the difference between activity done as a means and activity done for its own sake; he calls the latter "play" and holds that meaning is found there rather than in achievements, duties, or long-term goals. Read the novella through that distinction. I want a criticism that is faithful in both directions: it should not reduce the story to an illustration of Schlick, and it should not distort Schlick to make the story fit. Where does the play/means distinction genuinely illuminate Ivan Ilyich's life, his illness, and especially his relationship with Gerasim? Does Tolstoy give a different diagnosis of the emptiness of Ivan's life than Schlick's account would give, and if so, are they in tension or complementary? What does the novella dramatize about the fear and isolation of a meaningless life that Schlick's argument may leave out?
