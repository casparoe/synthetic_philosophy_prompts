Few philosophical observations are as easy to state as the paradox of hedonism: the more deliberately and directly one pursues one's own pleasure or happiness, the less reliably one attains it, while the same states often arise as a by-product of wholehearted involvement in other things. Proposed explanations of this phenomenon, however, tend to be assessed by tacit and conflicting standards. I want those standards made explicit.

Set out the desiderata for an adequate theory of the paradox of hedonism: what it must explain, which cases it must get right, which distinctions it must respect, and which neighbouring results it must be consistent with. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it. Then say which desiderata are non-negotiable and which are optional or partisan, and flag any pairs you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. Close with a brief score of three positions against the list — a naive act-by-act hedonism (pursue pleasure on each occasion), a qualitative hedonism in Mill's tradition (ranking pleasures by kind rather than by intensity or duration), and a self-effacing two-level hedonism (pleasure is the only intrinsic value, but the theory advises agents to live by irreducibly non-hedonic commitments) — keeping the scoring subordinate to the criteria.

The list should not settle for easy generality. Among the cases and distinctions I want to see handled are these. The paradox seems to afflict the pursuit of diffuse states such as a good mood or a rewarding holiday far more than simple pleasures such as quenching a thirst, and a theory should either ground that contrast or explain it away. It should also separate making pleasure the goal of a single action from making happiness the organising aim of a life, since the paradox may run at only one level. It should say whether the paradox is an empirical regularity of human psychology, a conceptual truth about pleasure as a by-product, or a hybrid, and what would confirm or disconfirm each reading. It should deliver a mechanism — self-monitoring, the corruption of once-intrinsic activities, hedonic adaptation, or something else — rather than merely restating the datum.

Two further constraints matter to me. The theory must not overgeneralise: aiming directly at truth, achievement, or a friend's wellbeing does not seem to backfire in the same way, so an account that predicts self-defeat for every valued end has lost a distinction it needed. And if the theory presents itself as a rescue of hedonism rather than a refutation of it, it must explain how a value best realised by not being aimed at can still serve as one's ultimate practical standard; otherwise it has not answered hedonism's problem, only moved it.
