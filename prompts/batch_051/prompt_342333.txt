Suppose a national data-protection authority introduces the following property-style regime for personal data. Any commercial organisation that processes personal data must hold a separate licence, issued by the authority, for each person whose data it uses. Each resident may set the annual fee for the use of their own data at any amount between zero and a statutory ceiling; the fee may be changed at any time and applies uniformly to all organisations. An organisation that declines to pay the fee may not use that person's data, whether by direct collection or by inference from other people's records, such as social-network connections, location traces, or aggregated purchasing profiles. The authority pools all licence fees and pays them out quarterly to residents in proportion to the number of licences held for their data, after retaining one per cent for its running costs. Assume that much commercially valuable data is relational — records often concern several people at once, and a profile can be inferred about someone who has never directly supplied data — and that no organisation may condition access to a service on a person's accepting a lower fee.

Treat this as a mechanism to be analysed rather than a policy to be judged in the abstract. Walk through what it incentivises among residents as price-setters, among firms when choosing whose data to license and how to design products, and among data brokers and the licensing authority itself. Identify where the scheme would first be gamed or break down, the second-order effects on what data is collected at all and on what consent comes to mean, and say which failure modes could be fixed by amendment without gutting the scheme and which could not. Please write your answer in British English.
