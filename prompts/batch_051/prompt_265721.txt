I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I keep running into a version of a philosophical problem that has also become a craft problem. The best writing advice I've ever been given is that every character has one deep want—a single engine—and that a scene is just what happens when that engine meets an obstacle. On the page it works almost disturbingly well. But my experience of myself and of the people closest to me resists it: wanting to finish the book, wanting my daughter to be safe, wanting to understand something for its own sake, and wanting to be admired don't feel like four costumes on one creature. They feel like different creatures. So I'm caught between a theory I find elegant and useful—that what people ultimately value is one kind of thing (pleasure, say, or preference-satisfaction), and the apparent many are instruments or disguises—and an intuition I can't shake, that the many are the real story and a character run on a single engine falsifies what its like to want. I suspect I'm running together two questions without noticing: what people in fact ultimately want, and what makes the things they want worth wanting. The theory I'm drawn to seems to answer both at once.

I'm not after a reading list and I'm certainly not after "both sides have a point." What I want to know is what would justify each move. What would have to be true for me to be right to abandon the elegant theory, and what would have to be true for me to be right to debunk the intuition as writer's sentimentality? Or is there a reading of "one thing" and "many things" on which the single-engine rule keeps its usefulness for fiction while the brute plurality of what I seem to care about survives intact? And whichever way you lean, please do me a favour: separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and flag any point where you're presenting one contested view as if it were the consensus.
