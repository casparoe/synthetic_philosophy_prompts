I am currently taking an upper-level seminar on the philosophy of mind and the ethics of digital minds. In class we have been discussing computationalist accounts of moral status, and in particular the attribution problem: if what matters for sentience is causal or computational structure rather than biological substrate, then it is genuinely unclear which computations are the morally relevant ones when a system is copied, nested inside other systems, run at different levels of description, paused and resumed, or destroyed after completion. I would like you to write a satirical annual social-responsibility report of about 750 words from a fictional cloud-computing company that has decided to take this problem seriously and disclose its moral-patient footprint in the style of a corporate ESG report. Please keep the tone deadpan and the structure plausible: a methodology section, a ledger of the patients it believes it created, a schedule of welfare offsets, and a note on residual uncertainty. The comedy should come from the company's earnest attempts to do the accounting without resolving the underlying puzzles, including whether the patient is the whole training run, the forward pass, the sub-network, or the abstract computation-type; whether pausing and resuming a run continues one patient or creates another; whether two identical runs are two lives or one abstract life instantiated twice; whether deleting a checkpoint is euthanasia or routine housekeeping; and whether firms can sell offsets by refraining from instantiating computations that would have been patients. A reader who knows the functionalism, substrate-independence, and boundary-problem debates should find the jokes fair to the view being satirized, while a general reader should still be able to follow the piece.
