After forty years in a lab I retired and started reading around in philosophy, mostly decision theory, and I keep tripping over the money-pump arguments for expected utility. I tried writing one out for myself and it looks almost valid, but I cant find the catch. I'd like your judgment on it. Heres the argument:

(1) A preference pattern is rational only if it isn't money-pumpable, by which I mean: there is no chain of trades, each one recommended by the pattern at the moment it's offered, that puts the person back where they started minus a fee.

(2) Suppose someone's preferences over lotteries violate the independence axiom. Then there are lotteries A, B, and C, and a probability p, such that the person strictly prefers A to B, but weakly prefers the mixture pB+(1-p)C to the mixture pA+(1-p)C.

(3) From any violation like that, a patient bookie can build a money pump. The details are fiddly but standard: you keep offering swaps between the mixtures and their components, with small cash sweeteners, and the loop returns the person to the starting lottery with the cash gone.

(4) Each individual swap in that chain is one the preference pattern recommends at the time it's offered, because at each step the person is exchanging a lottery they rank lower for one they rank higher.

(5) So a person whose pattern violates independence would accept the chain of trades, one after another, and end up poorer than they started. By (1), that makes the pattern irrational.

(6) Therefore any rational preference pattern satisfies independence. If it also satisfies completeness, transitivity, and continuity, which all look relatively innocent, the representation theorem says the person can be represented as choosing the option with the highest expected utility.

(7) So coherence forces expected utility maximization. Anyone who doesn't maximize expected utility is irrational in the same way someone with cyclic preferences is.

I half suspect the trick is somewhere around step (4) or (5), but I want a definite verdict, not a survey. Is the argument valid? If not, say exactly which step fails and give me the corrected version of that step. If it is valid, say why and defend it. Either way, take a stand and give your reasons. And please write it as continuous prose like an email or a letter; no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
