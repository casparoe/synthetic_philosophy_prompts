I've been trying to get straight how you're supposed to revise beliefs when you learn a conditional — someone tells you "if A then B" — rather than a plain fact like "A" or "B". I have a working rule and a bunch of verdicts about particular cases, but one of my verdicts grates against the rule, and I want the principle behind my verdicts, or a principled way to tell me which verdict to drop.

My working rule: when I learn "if A then B" and I treat the conditional itself as the only new information, (i) set P(B|A) to 1; (ii) leave P(A) where it was; (iii) inside not-A, leave all relative probabilities untouched. It's felt like a decent rule of thumb.

Cases:

1. Paratrooper. Dropped into a region with four equally likely quadrants: Red-Hills, Red-Plains, Blue-Hills, Blue-Plains, each 1/4. The radio says: "If you're in Red territory, you're in the Hills." My verdict: Red stays 1/2; Red-Plains goes to 0; Red-Hills becomes 1/2; Blue-Hills and Blue-Plains stay 1/4 apiece. (Fits the rule.)

2. Same layout, swapped. Now the radio says: "If you're in the Hills, you're in Red territory." My verdict: Hills stays 1/2; Blue-Hills goes to 0; Red-Hills becomes 1/2; Red-Plains and Blue-Plains stay 1/4 apiece, so Red climbs to 3/4. That feels right.

3. Biopsy. A doctor says: "If the biopsy comes back positive, the cancer has spread." Before, P(positive)=0.6, P(spread)=0.3, P(spread|positive)=0.4, P(spread|negative)=0.15. My verdict: P(positive) stays 0.6; P(spread|positive) becomes 1; P(spread) becomes 0.66. Fine.

4. The letter. I'm waiting on whether a letter arrived (A) and whether it was signed (B). Prior: P(A)=0.4, P(B|A)=0.6, P(B|not-A)=0.2, so P(B)=0.36. A friend who has seen the mail says — and I'm treating this as carrying exactly that conditional information and nothing more — "If the letter arrived, it was signed." Under my rule: P(A) stays 0.4, P(B|A) becomes 1, P(B|not-A) stays 0.2, so P(B) rises to 0.52. But suppose she'd instead said: "If it wasn't signed, the letter didn't arrive." I take that to be the same information, just the contrapositive. Apply the rule to that phrasing and the antecedent is "it wasn't signed", so P(not-B) stays 0.64 — i.e. P(B) stays 0.36. So the rule sends me to two different places depending on which words she happened to use. My verdict: whichever way she says it, I should end up in the same place. I'd rather fix the rule than accept that.

5. The locked gate. I'm certain the back gate didn't open last night, P(B)=0. I'd had a 20% hunch the dog barked anyway. A neighbor says: "If the dog barked, the back gate was open." My verdict: given that the gate never opened, I should now be certain the dog didn't bark. The rule chokes: clause (i) wants P(B|A)=1 while P(B)=0, which forces P(A)=0, but clause (ii) said leave P(A)=0.2. One clause has to win.

So: is there a precise principle that gets all five cases right, or do I have to give up one of the verdicts — in particular, my feeling that the contrapositive carries the same information? State the candidate principle, say which case motivates each part, run it on a further case or two I haven't considered, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts it can't keep.
