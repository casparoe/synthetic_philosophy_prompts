For a feature I'm writing about why museums, courts, and critics still cannot agree on what makes something a work of art, I've persuaded two philosophers with rival theories to attempt an adversarial collaboration. One holds a broadly aesthetic theory: an object is an artwork if and because it was made with the intention that it be appreciated for its perceptual and formal character — its shape, color, sound, expressiveness, composition, and the way those features are organized. The other holds a broadly institutional theory: an object is an artwork if and because it has had the status of a candidate for appreciation conferred on it by someone acting within the artworld — an artist, curator, critic, collector, or dealer — against the background of an ongoing art-historical practice. They have argued past each other for a year and have now agreed to co-sign one document. Write the document they should produce. It should open with a numbered list of claims both can sign — about paradigm cases, about the difference between being art and being good art, and about the role that history and social context play even on the aesthetic view. It should then give a table of their remaining disagreements in neutral wording, each entry phrased so both would accept that it formulates their own view correctly; the table should distinguish the genuinely contested questions (for instance, whether the maker's intention is necessary, whether artworld conferral is necessary or sufficient, and whether those who confer status must be answerable to some independent standard) from disputes that turn out to be merely verbal. After that, identify the cruxes — the sub-questions that, if settled, would move at least one side — and for each crux specify a concrete case both would accept as evidence. Use cases such as: a person who produces a formally striking carved object in total isolation, never having encountered any artworld; a gallery object and a materially indistinguishable object sitting in a hardware store; and a work whose entire content is an instruction or idea with nothing sensible to contemplate. For each case, record in advance what each philosopher predicts and what they would take the other's prediction to show. The finished document should be something two rivals could both sign without embarrassment and should make clear exactly where the disagreement now stands.
