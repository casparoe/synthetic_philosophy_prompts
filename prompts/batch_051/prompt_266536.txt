I teach a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and a dispute has been simmering all term about how far Good's theorem actually reaches. The theorem, usually credited to I. J. Good, says roughly this (I am paraphrasing rather than quoting): an expected-utility maximizer faces a fixed set of states, a fixed menu of acts, and a fixed utility function over act-state outcomes, and considers acquiring a signal whose only causal upshot is to revise the agent's probabilities over those states; the ex ante expected utility of the policy of acquiring the signal and then choosing the act that maximizes expected utility conditional on it is always at least as great as the expected utility of choosing without the signal. The proof is, in essence, the convexity of the maximum operator. But my colleague insists this licenses a much stronger slogan — that a rational agent can never be harmed, even in expectation, by cost-free information — which I resist, though I am not certain exactly where my resistance should live.

Here is the claim I want your credence about, stated as precisely as I can manage. Claim C: there is no defensible account of rational decision-making on which an agent with stable preferences, a fixed menu of acts, and full awareness of the decision structure can be made worse off, in that account's own ex ante terms, by acquiring a cost-free signal that merely reduces uncertainty over the payoff-relevant states; every apparent case of rational information avoidance — declining to learn a prognosis, refusing a friend's honest opinion of one's manuscript, avoiding genetic test results — is either a case where the information carries direct hedonic or resource costs, a case of unstable or information-dependent preferences, a case of act-state causal dependence, or a case of non-Bayesian processing rather than a stable ideal of rationality. My own credence in C is roughly 0.75 and my colleague's is roughly 0.2; I want to know where the weight of that disagreement actually sits.

Please respond as a professional decision theorist responding to a referee report would: think carefully, begin by brainstorming the space of candidate readings of C and the strongest apparent counterexamples — including ambiguity-averse or regret-based decision rules, cases where the act of learning changes the agent, and cases where the phrase cost-free is doing more work than it appears — and settle on the reading you will assign a number to before giving that number. Then decompose your credence. At minimum, assign rough probabilities to these sub-questions: (i) the formal theorem is correct for the idealized Bayesian case it is standardly taken to cover; (ii) rational in C is best read as that idealized Bayesian notion rather than as some more demanding or more permissive standard; (iii) the familiar examples of information avoidance all reduce to the exceptions listed in C, with none left over; (iv) some non-expected-utility framework genuinely licenses rationally refusing free information without thereby abandoning a defensible ideal of rationality. Show how these combine into your final credence, and state which single intermediate number, if changed, would move your final credence by the most. Finally, and this matters to me more than the number itself, tell me what would change your mind: what kind of argument or fully specified case — a provable failure of the value-of-information result under weaker conditions, or a concrete example of an agent we should regard as rational who is robustly better off not knowing — would most plausibly shift your credence in C, in which direction, and where you judge your own reasoning most likely to fail.
