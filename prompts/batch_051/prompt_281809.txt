In an epistemology seminar, my professor has repeatedly dismissed a family of moves by saying that you can't pull yourself up by your own bootstraps. The first target was a reliabilist line I floated: if my color vision really is a reliable process, then I can know that the wall is red by looking; I can also know by introspection that my color vision reported that the wall is red; from these two I can infer that my color vision got it right on this occasion; and by compiling enough such occasions I can come to know that my color vision is generally reliable. She called this bootstrapping and treated its circularity as obvious. The second target was a piece of "easy knowledge": from knowing that the wall is red, plus the fact that a red wall cannot also be a white wall merely lit so as to look red, I could come to know that the lighting is not fooling me without any further investigation. She said that was bootstrapping too.

I want the slogan pinned down. What are the distinct precise claims someone could be making when they say that a piece of reasoning pulls itself up by its own bootstraps? For each reading, indicate whether it is close to trivial, substantive but defensible, or so strong that it is probably false, and explain which of the two inferences it condemns and which it leaves standing. I especially want to know whether there is a reading that condemns the track-record inference to the reliability of a faculty without also forbidding all track-record reasoning about instruments, experts, and one's own senses, and whether any reading condemns the easy-knowledge deduction. Present the readings as a menu of alternatives with explicit verdicts, rather than defending a single one.
