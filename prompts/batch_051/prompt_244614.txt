I've found myself defending a position in my school's philosophy club and I want to find out how exposed it really is. The position is that punctuality and the keeping of small promises — arriving when I said I would, sending the message I promised to send that night, returning a borrowed book by the date I named — are matters of courtesy and self-presentation rather than moral obligations, unless the other person genuinely relies on me and suffers a concrete, foreseeable loss when I fail. Generalizing from this feels natural: the wrongness of breaking a promise tracks the foreseeable harm it causes, so where the harm is negligible or can be repaired with a quick apology and a rescheduled plan, there is nothing left over morally, however irritating the lapse may be. On this view, punctuality and small-scale reliability are social lubricants, useful habits we should mostly cultivate, but not the sort of thing one is answerable for in itself.

I want you to tell me what would count against this view — not a general critique, but a ranked map of its exposure. Which cases, arguments, or observations should genuinely move me, and which should I be able to absorb without giving up the core idea? Order the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether a committed defender of the position would concede it as a real cost or dismiss it. Be concrete: a promise whose topic matters enormously to the other person while looking trivial to me; a promise repeated so often that the small breaches form a pattern; a promise made under social pressure; a friend who reorganized her entire day because I said I would be there at six. I would also like you to consider whether the view can protect itself by broadening what counts as harm — hidden reliance, emotional costs, the gradual erosion of trust — and whether doing that eventually insulates it from every counterexample; and if it does, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind or just a sign that it is a flexible principle. I would personally be most moved by a case where someone breaks a small promise and something has clearly gone wrong even though I cannot point to any harmed interest — but I suspect that is exactly the intuition my view is built to explain away, so tell me honestly if I am gerrymandering. End with a verdict on whether the best version of the view survives, and what it has to give up in order to survive.
