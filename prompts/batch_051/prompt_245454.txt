For an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of mathematics (16 students, 90 minutes), design a structured game that makes participants feel the epistemic predicament behind computer-assisted, unsurveyable proofs. The game should simulate a certification committee for a small mathematical journal. Provide an invented combinatorial theorem whose purported proof splits into thirty-two tedious cases. Checking one case honestly should take several minutes and should be openly verifiable, but no student should be able to check all thirty-two cases in the time available. The pressure point is a "verifier" — played by a computer program, a teaching assistant following a sealed rule, or an envelope of precomputed results — that can check any case in seconds, but whose internal work is opaque to the committee. Participants must decide, under a protocol they partly choose (delegation of cases, spot-checks, cross-checks, a voting rule), whether to certify the theorem or reject it.

They should not hear the philosophical issue named up front. The dilemma should emerge from the rules themselves: each person is quite confident about the cases they personally checked, but the committee's certification will inevitably rest on second-hand reports, partial checking, and an opaque but fast verifier. Include roles (author, case-checkers, verifier, editorial board), an exact checking protocol, a timeline, materials, and a scoring scheme that does not involve real money. The debrief should draw out what actually happened — moments of trust, suspicion, free-riding, or false confidence — and map those moments onto the structure of the four-colour theorem's computer-assisted proof and, if useful, the Kepler conjecture: the mathematical community accepts a theorem on the strength of an exhaustive case analysis that no single mathematician has surveyed in full, together with trust in a mechanical checker. Say what predictably goes wrong the first time the activity is run (for example, a group that certifies because everyone else seems to, or a group that stalls and rejects everything because no individual possesses the whole proof) and how to handle it without lecturing prematurely. End with an honest note on what the game cannot reproduce — above all, that real proof-assistant code is itself inspectable in a way the in-game verifier is not, and that the actual case analyses are far too large for any classroom simulacrum.
