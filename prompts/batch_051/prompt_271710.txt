Here's a weird asymmetry in how people talk about moral goodness. Ask someone whether a person can be too morally good and they'll almost always say no — morality is the one thing you can't have too much of, and anyone who complains about it just doesn't want to feel judged. But then describe an actual moral saint: someone who spends nearly every spare hour helping others, always acts from duty, won't laugh at mean jokes or gossip, and never buys themselves anything nice because that money could save a life. The very same person will usually say they admire that person, maybe even revere them, but they wouldn't want to be married to them or have them as a close friend; they'd find them exhausting, humorless, maybe a little creepy. So ordinary judgment seems to hold both that maximal moral goodness is the highest ideal and that the maximally morally good person is not someone you actually want around.

What I want is a verdict on wether that pair of judgments is defensible or just an inconsistency we live with. Here are some candidate reconciliations: the saint is fine and our aversion is just self-interest; we think the saint is making a mistake, like treating being moral as being maximally useful; or there's nothing contradictory in admiring a person while not wanting to share a life with them. But don't just list these — pick the strongest candidate you can find, test it against nearby cases (someone exactly the same but a little less extreme; someone maximally good who somehow keeps a sharp sense of humor; someone maximally good toward causes but not toward people), and then say whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or dropped. Be honest about what your answer does to the claim that you can't be too good. Don't dodge by saying such a person is psychologically impossible; take the description at face value.
