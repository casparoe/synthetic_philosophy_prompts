I've been trying to figure out what grown children actually owe their parents, and my gut keeps giving me verdicts that don't obviously fit the usual "repay what they gave you" idea. Here are a bunch of cases and my honest reactions; I want to know if there's one principle behind them, or which verdicts I have to drop.

1. My parents paid my full college tuition and we've stayed close — they call, they visit, they never made me feel like I owed them anything. Now they're in their late 60s and got hit with a big medical bill. I think I should help substantially. But I don't feel like I'm paying back tuition; it feels more like helping people who've always been in my corner.

2. Same tuition history, but the relationship went cold: my parents and I talk twice a year, and the calls are stiff. Now they ask me to cover the same kind of medical bill. I don't think I owe the same thing. Maybe I'd cover an urgent basic need, but not the big bill.

3. My parents were neglectful and quietly mean through my childhood. They never hit me or starved me, but there was no warmth. Now they need home care. I don't think I owe them much of anything; making sure they're not literally on the street feels like more than they're entitled to.

4. An old friend once let me live with him rent-free for a year when I was broke and talked me through a bad depression. Now he needs rent help and a lot of emotional support. I think I owe him a lot — not a counted year, but real, ongoing help.

5. My mom gave up a career to raise us and now says I owe her a comfortable retirement, and also regular gratitude and deference. I think: no specific retirement dollar amount, and definitely no deference. The sacrifice earns warmth and maybe extra attentiveness, but it's not a bill she can present.

6. My dad is declining and needs daily care. But moving him in would wreck my marraige and force me to quit my job. I don't think I'm obligated to blow up my life. I'd help find good care and visit often, but I draw the line there.

7. My sister and I grew up in the same house with the same money and the same parents. She can't stand them; I'm genuinely close to them. I think it's fine that I do a lot more for them. The "debt" doesn't split 50/50.

8. My other friend's parents were genuinely abusive. Now they're old and lonely and want her back in their lives. I think she owes them nothing. Anything she does is charity, not duty.

My working theory is that once you're an adult, what you owe your parents is basically what you'd owe a close friend, given the actual relationship you have — not repayment for the costs of being raised. The past counts only through the present relationship: trust, closeness, a habit of mutual care. So parents can't cash in old sacrifices like a savings bond.

What I want: does this friendship principle survive contact with my verdicts, or does it force me to drop some? Where does it break? If it's mostly right but needs patching, what's the smallest repair that saves the good verdicts? And what does your best principle say about cases I didn't list — for instance, a warm but financially chaotic parent who keeps creating emergencies, or a parent who was loving but also guilt-tripped and invasive? Tell me straight which of my verdicts don't fit and should go.
