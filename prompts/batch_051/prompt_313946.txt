You are an expert in AI governance and in the philosophy of agency, with the skills of someone who regularly interprets policy language: plain meaning, underlying purpose, what drafters would have said, and what a reasonable reader would understand. Think carefully rather than reaching for a familiar answer.

I am a compliance officer at a mid-sized logistics company. I review one or two proposed AI deployments a month, and the ruling I make on this one will be cited as precedent by both our engineering teams and our auditors, so it has to be defensible rather than merely prudent.

Our internal AI use policy, Section 4.2, reads:

"Employees may use AI systems as tools to prepare analyses, drafts, summaries, and recommendations. The responsible employee must review and approve each final work product before relying on it, and remains accountable for that product. An AI system may not be used as an agent; that is, an employee may not delegate to the system the decision and execution of a course of action on the employee's behalf."

We wrote this clause in 2023, before our data-science team had built the system described below, and none of us had this case in mind.

The proposed deployment, "Conveyor," takes a natural-language business objective — for example, "identify why overnight shipments to the Gulf states are frequently delayed" — and works through a long sequence of analytical steps over several hours. It drafts and rewrites its own database queries, runs them against internal warehouses, builds tables, tests hypotheses, generates charts, and criticizes its own intermediate results. At each stage it decides for itself what to do next; no human sees or approves the intermediate steps. It runs in a sandbox and cannot send emails, move money, or modify production systems. A human analyst reviews the final report before anyone relies on it.

The engineering team says Conveyor is a tool: it only prepares analysis, a human approves the final work product, and it cannot take any external action. My compliance staff says Conveyor is an agent under Section 4.2: within its sandbox it plainly decides and executes a course of action, and the human approval arrives only after the fact. I need your reasoned view on whether deploying Conveyor would violate the clause.

Work through each of the following and then give a recommendation:

- The ordinary meaning of the clause, and in particular what "decide and execute a course of action on the employee's behalf" would convey to a reasonable employee.
- The purpose of the clause, and which interests — continuous human judgment at genuine decision points, clear answerability, preventing unsupervised systems from carrying out multi-step plans — it was probably meant to protect.
- What the 2023 drafters would most likely have said if asked about Conveyor, given the kinds of systems they were probably imagining.
- What a reasonable user notified of this policy would understand the policy to promise about a system like Conveyor.

Where these approaches diverge, say so and explain why; where they seem to agree, check whether the agreement comes from the same reasoning or from coincidence. Then state which reading the company should adopt and why, and be explicit about which parts of that recommendation rest on contested policy values rather than on the text.

Finally, address the drafting question: could Section 4.2 be rewritten so that it clearly catches Conveyor without also banning harmless analytical tools, such as a dashboard that refreshes on a schedule or a pipeline that mechanically reformats and archives data? If no rewrite can do that, say what this shows about what the rule was ever able to protect.

Before you commit to an answer, examine the tool/agent distinction itself rather than merely applying it: a system is not an agent simply because it is complex or does many steps, and it is not a tool simply because it is sandboxed or because a human signs off at the end.
