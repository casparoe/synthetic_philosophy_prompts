I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I'm drafting a scene where an art theory professor hands out one of those "which account of art do you actually hold?" quizzes at an MFA seminar. I want the quiz in the scene to actually work, not be the kind of thing a lazy screenwriter would invent. Write me an eight-to-ten question quiz about what makes something art. Every question should be a concrete case -- a bed in a museum, a forged Vermeer, whale song, Duchamp's Fountain, a child's drawing, a neural net's painting -- with two or three answer options, and the questions should sort people by their verdicts, so no question should just ask "do you think art is whatever institutions say it is." Include a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto at least these positions: aestheticism, formalism, institutional theory, historical narrativism, and anti-essentialism or the open concept view, plus a cluster account if you think it's distinct enough to earn a leaf. Build it from distinctions rather than from the labels: artifacthood, intention to be art, aesthetic quality, artworld uptake, descent from earlier art. Then flag any pair of positions your questions can't cleanly separate -- I have a feeling institutionalism and historical narrativism blur on a lot of readymades -- and if some combination of answers lands on an empty cell, say whether it's empty because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Two or three sentences up front about which questions do the most discriminatory work would help me make the quiz feel real in the scene.
