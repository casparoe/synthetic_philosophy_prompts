I am currently taking an advanced seminar on formal epistemology and modal logic, and we have been comparing possible-worlds semantics with probabilistic models of belief and chance. I want to organize the main candidate accounts of how the qualitative notion of possibility relates to the quantitative notion of probability before I write my term paper. Could you sort the following six positions into tiers (A through D, with A the highest) by how illuminating they are as general accounts of the relation, and give a one-sentence reason for each placement? Please judge them by how well they handle two familiar mismatches between the notions: a far-fetched possibility can carry negligible or even zero probability, while a probability-zero outcome, such as a dart landing on an exact point, can nevertheless be possible.

1. Reductive probability-first view: a proposition is possible just in case its probability is greater than zero, necessary just in case its probability equals one, and impossible just in case its probability equals zero, where probability is a standard real-valued measure.

2. Regular-probability repair: the same reduction, executed with regular or non-Archimedean probability functions, which assign a positive infinitesimal measure to every relevant nonempty possibility and thereby avoid the measure-zero counterexamples.

3. Modality-first, measure-second view: one first fixes a qualitative modal structure, a space of possibilities together with an accessibility or outcome relation, and then introduces probability as an additional measure over that structure; the qualitative modal facts constrain admissible measures but are not reducible to any measure.

4. Comparative-possibility view: probability is a quantitative representation of a more fundamental qualitative ordering of propositions according to how possible they are relative to one another, so probabilistic talk is really graded modal talk rather than an independent idiom.

5. Threshold contextualism: modal vocabulary has no separate subject matter; to say that a proposition is possible, or that it is probable, is just a context-sensitive way of reporting that its probability clears a low or high threshold on a single probability scale.

6. Mutual-irreducibility view: possibility and probability are distinct tools and neither reduces to the other; probability grades possibilities without ever constituting them, and the two mismatches are exactly what one should expect once that division of labour is accepted.

I would like the tier placement to reflect not only internal coherence but also how much each position improves our understanding of both notions, rather than merely stipulating or renaming one side of the distinction.
