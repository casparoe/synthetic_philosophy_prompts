I am a novelist rather than a professional philosopher, and I have spent much of the past year writing a book about a permanently inhabited Mars. In the course of plotting it, I found myself defending a position about space colonization that the novel keeps circling around, and I would like a candid assessment of it before I let the book commit to it.

I call it the Trust-and-Reversibility Principle. Its central claim is that a permanent off-world settlement should regard itself not as the owner of the world it occupies but as a trustee of it. The trust corpus, as I conceive it, is threefold: the world's unmodified trajectory, the open-ended interests of the generations the settlement will bring into being, and Earth's residual stake in a solar system whose largest choices have not yet been made. From this I derive two tests. The Ratification Test holds that a colonial policy is presumptively permissible only when the settlers can state, in some detail, how a reasonable future beneficiary, fully informed and not taking the settlement's continuation for granted, would ratify it. The Irreversibility Test holds that the harder an intervention is to undo, the heavier its burden of justification must be. A sealed greenhouse passes easily; thickening the Martian atmosphere to warm the planet does not, because it forecloses a Mars that later people might have preferred to retain, and nobody alive now is in a position to ratify that foreclosure on their behalf. My instinct is that this captures what disturbs people about terraforming while still permitting a modest, revisable settlement.

I know this may simply be the precautionary principle under another name, or something already worked out in intergenerational ethics, or it may collapse under the non-identity problem, since the future people in question would not exist but for the settlement. What I would like is an honest reading of the idea: is it coherent as stated, what existing positions is it closest to and where does it depart from them, what does it get right, and where does it break or stop being distinctive? I would rather hear where it fails than be told it is original.
