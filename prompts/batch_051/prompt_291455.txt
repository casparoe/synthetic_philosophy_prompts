I cover science and ideas for a living, so I've spent a lot of time around both the neuroaesthetics people and the philosophy people, and I keep running into the same stuck debate over whether beauty is objective. Over the years I've ended up with a way of thinking about it that I haven't been able to find a name for, so I've been calling it "instrumental objectivism about taste." Here's the whole thing.

Hume's true judges, in his essay on the standard of taste, are basically good instruments. His move is usually read as saying the standard of taste is whatever the true judges converge on, once they've got delicacy, practice, comparison, and freedom from prejudice. I want to read that as a claim about instrumentation: the standard is the readout of a properly callibrated instrument, not the instrument's say-so. A thermometer doesn't create heat; it's just a device that, when it's working right, tracks a feature that's already out there. Aesthetic judgments don't create beauty, well-calibrated judges track it. The standard of taste is a standard of good instrumentation, not of consensus or majority vote. If the calibrated judges converge, that's evidence there's a real property being tracked; if they diverge, you look for a calibration error rather than declaring beauty relative.

Now I know the obvious comeback: beauty seems response-dependent in a way heat isn't, and Hume himself says something like beauty isn't a quality in the object, it lives in the mind that contemplates it. I don't think that kills my view—I'd say beauty is more like color, which is partly out there and partly in the visual system, or like "room temperature," which is real but partly defined by human comfort. What I'm less sure about is whether I've actually come up with anything new. Is this just the old ideal observer theory with a lab-coat metaphor? Is there a name for it in the literature? And if you're feeling generous, give me the strongest version of my view, then tell me three places it cracks—especially the case where two equally well-calibrated judges flat-out disagree about a piece of art and the view has to just stare at the floor.
