I'm not a philosopher, just someone who keeps finding himself in long arguments with friends, but a recent conversation has been nagging at me and I'd like it worked through rigorously. Last spring a friend of mine—sober, careful, not given to inventing stories—told me she had seen a bobcat in the city park near her apartment, well outside where bobcats are normally found. I believed her without much hesitation; I didn't demand a photograph, and I quietly updated my picture of what can show up in that park. A few months later the same friend told me that, during the weeks after her mother died, she was sitting alone one evening when she was suddenly flooded with the sense of a presence—something she described as being held and quietly spoken to—and she came away convinced that God, or at least some reality beyond the physical world, had made contact with her. What struck me was that I, and the others she told, treated this second report entirely differently. Nobody accused her of lying, and nobody denied she'd had the experience. But we reached for psychology—grief, exhaustion, the mind's hunger for comfort—and none of us treated the experience as even weak evidence that such a presence exists. Yet on the surface both cases are first-person reports from a reliable informant about a surprising experience; both could easily be mistaken; and both would be important if true, though in rather different ways. One surprising report updated our picture of the world, while the other only updated our picture of her.

What I want to know is whether this asymmetry in how we treat the two reports is defensible, or whether it is just a comfortable double standard. I don't want the obvious answers—that the second claim is far less likely, or that there's a familiar natural explanation for it—unless they've been tested. A bobcat in a city park is also unlikely, and ordinary perception has its own known failure modes. By the same token, we often accept entirely unverifiable first-person reports of pain, love, or dreams without treating them as evidence about the world, so privacy alone can't be the whole story. A good answer should tell me which feature is actually doing the epistemic work: that the claim is about something supernatural, that the experience is uncheckable by others, or that a ready psychological story can be told about it. If the asymmetry should be kept, I want the exact principle that keeps it; if it should be revised, I want to know what a more consistent practice would look like.
