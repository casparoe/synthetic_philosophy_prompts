I've been arguing with a friend about population policy and I keep circling back to an asymmetry I can't get rid of. Governments routinely describe a policy that prevents deaths as producing so many "lives saved" — a road-safety program expected to prevent 200 traffic deaths a year is credited with saving 200 lives, even though nobody knows which 200 people will be saved. Aid agencies call a famine that kills ten thousand a catastrophe. But the same governments treat pronatalist spending as just that, spending. If a country scraps a child allowance or stops funding IVF, and its own projections say that means two thousand fewer children will be born next year, no cost-benefit analysis lists "two thousand people who will now never exist" as a cost. Propose that and people look at you like you've said the unused eggs are starving. So a death that hasn't happened yet is a countable harm, but a life that hasn't started isn't a harm at all, even when both are statistical.

I want to know whether there's a principle that actually generates that asymmetry, or whether it's just a psychological habit about what we're willing to grieve for. The strongest argument I can see is that the would-be traffic victims already exist and would be deprived of the rest of there lives, whereas the never-born have nobody to be deprived — there's no particular child to point at. But I'm not sure that survives contact with a hard case. Suppose a couple is deciding whether to have a second child, and they'd be loving, well-off parents, and the child would have a very good life. If they decide not to, has anything bad happened? Has the world lost something? If your answer is no, can you still say a larger population is better in any sense, or is every pronatalist argument just about the preferences of people who already exist? And if you say yes, something was lost, can you still explain why the scrapped child allowance isn't a loss of two thousand lives? Pick the strongest defense you can find, test it against those cases, and give me an honest verdict on whether the ordinary asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
