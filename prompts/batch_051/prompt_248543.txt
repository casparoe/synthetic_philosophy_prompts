Yesterday a friend and I spent the whole evening arguing about whether expected-value reasoning survives contact with gambles in which the time average and the ensemble average come apart, and by the end he had built an argument that seems to prove too much: he claimed it shows expected utility theory is false and that the only defensible rule for choice under risk is to maximize expected logarithmic growth. I could not find the step to reject, but I am unwilling to accept the conclusion. I will set the argument down as he gave it.

(1) An agent who cares only about wealth, and prefers more wealth to less, should accept a gamble exactly when accepting it raises the expected value of the wealth held. A rule that systematically rejects gambles with higher expected wealth makes its followers poorer on average.

(2) Consider a coin flip that multiplies your wealth by 1.5 on heads and by 0.6 on tails. Its expected multiplier is (1.5 + 0.6) / 2 = 1.05, so a single play raises expected wealth by five percent.

(3) Therefore a rational wealth-maximizer should accept this gamble whenever it is offered, and should welcome an indefinitely long sequence of independent repetitions.

(4) But over a long sequence, no one experiences five-percent growth. Since wealth is multiplied each round, the growth rate a single player actually realizes is the geometric mean of the multiplier, the square root of 1.5 times 0.6, which is approximately 0.949, a contraction of about 5.1 percent per round. With probability one, the player's wealth tends to zero as the number of rounds increases.

(5) So the policy of accepting at every round maximizes expected wealth at each step and yet drives the agent to ruin almost surely. A standard of practical rationality cannot recommend a policy that makes ruin almost certain while also calling the refusal of that bet irrational; that, he said, is why expected-value reasoning is broken for this gamble.

(6) The reason expected value still calls the bet favorable is that the arithmetic mean spreads the agent over an ensemble of parallel copies. In the ensemble, a few astronomically lucky histories carry the mean upward, but the real agent occupies one path only. For any agent who lives through one history and faces a stream of multiplicative risks, the relevant quantity is the time average over that path, not the ensemble average.

(7) A one-off gamble is not a different kind of problem; it is simply the case in which the sequence has length one. A correct decision rule cannot flip between the one-shot and repeated settings on the basis of a fact external to the act itself. If the rule uses the time average for the sequence, it must use the time average for the single play as well; otherwise it treats two identical choices differently only because of how many other choices happen to be scheduled afterward.

(8) Expected-value reasoning is therefore wrong not just for repeated multiplicative gambles but for decisions under risk generally. The correct rule is to maximize the expected logarithm of wealth, since the logarithm is what compounds along the single path an agent actually lives; maximizing expected log wealth is equivalent to maximizing the time-average growth rate.

(9) That is the conclusion I cannot accept. It would mean risk attitudes are not a matter of preference at all but are dictated by the mathematics of compounding: everyone should choose as if they had logarithmic utility, and expected utility theory, which treats the curvature of the utility function as something an agent's preferences can determine, is simply false.

I would most like to keep expected utility theory, but I want to know the bill. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, does rejecting the argument force me to say the repeated gamble above should actually be accepted, or can I reject that gamble while keeping something like expected utility theory? And which step fooled us by looking innocuous?
