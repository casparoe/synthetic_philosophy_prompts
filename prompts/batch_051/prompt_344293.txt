I work as a clerk for a small district council, and one of my jobs is to work out, often on the spot, whether somebody has actually done the thing their words seem to do. Most weeks I'm minuting council meetings, I run the bit where new members take their declaration of office, and occassionally I deputise as registrar for weddings and civil partnerships. The same question keeps turning up in different clothes: did this person actually do the thing by saying the words? Real examples from the last year: a member said "I so move" while another member was still speaking and the chair hadn't called her; a couple said their vows in the ceremony room, but it was a rehearsal, with no witnesses except a photographer, and the paperwork was never started; and a senior officer said "I resign" in a row and emailed ten minutes later to say she didn't mean it and wanted it treated as never said. We currently handle these cases by feel, and diffrent clerks have given diffrent rulings on nearly identical facts, which is starting to look bad. I have no legal training beyond the obvious; the chair signs my ruling off after the fact.

I'd like you to turn what philosophy of language knows about speech acts and performative utterances into a procedure I can actually run. I'm not asking for the law's answer in any particular jurisdiction, by the way: I want the philosophical skeleton, with the places where law or standing orders would have to override it flagged clearly. I gather there's an old list of conditions for a performative to come off—something like there has to be a recognised procedure, the right people and circumstances, the procedure carried out correctly and completely, and then further stuff about intentions, sincerity and uptake—but I can't turn that list into a decision I'm allowed to make at 7:45 on a Thursday.

Please give me a checklist or decision tree I can use quickly, often in the room with people waiting. For each question: the order, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with "not done" (or "not yet done"), and where it has to hand off to a human because the theory runs out or a value judgement is doing the work. Then run the procedure on the three cases above—the motion, the rehearsal vows, the resignation—and make sure at least one of them is a case where the procedure gives me an answer I won't like, and say so plainly. Finally, flag the sorts of case the procedure is likely to misfire on, and what needs to be settled by law, custom or the chair before the philosophy can even get going. Answer in British English.
