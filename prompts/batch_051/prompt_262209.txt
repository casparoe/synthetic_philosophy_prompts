I cover the boundary between philosophy and biology, so I'm contractually obliged to notice when a metaphysics distinction starts doing cell biology's job. In the dispositions literature there's a pair of concepts I can't stop thinking about. A "fink" is a disposition that would be destroyed by the very trigger that's supposed to activate it, so the manifestation never happens. A "mask" is different: the disposition is still fully there, but some separate condition interferes so it can't manifest. The stock examples are a live wire that trips a safety fuse the moment you touch it (fink) versus a live wire wrapped in rubber (mask).

Here's my problem. G-protein-coupled receptor desensitization seems to be a fink in literal molecule form: the receptor is disposed to keep signaling while the agonist (the activating ligand) is bound, but the binding itself recruits arrestins that uncouple or internalize the receptor, so its own stimulus kills the disposition it was exercising. An antagonist at the same binding site is then a mask: the receptor's disposition to fire if an agonist bound is still there, but the blocker keeps the binding from happening, so the disposition never gets to manifest.

I cant tell if this is a real structural identity or just me pattern-matching on the phrase "something prevented something." If it's real, does the fink/mask split track a distinction pharmacologists already draw under other names — for instance, between kinds of tolerance, one where the target itself gets desensitized and one where the body counteracts the effect downstream? And if it's superficial, where exactly does the mapping start to break?
