I have been arguing with a colleague about the familiar Bayesian idea that rational inquirers who start with different priors will eventually wash out their disagreement as they condition on a shared body of evidence. My colleague defends a strong version of that idea: once two reasoners have updated on the same long sequence of observations and have thereby been brought into near-agreement about some hypothesis H, no single additional observation could reopen a large gap between them. His reasoning is that, after the shared evidence has already come to dominate their prior differences, one further observation affects both reasoners by the same likelihood ratio, so it can move their posteriors only slightly and in tandem. I suspect this trades on a confusion between a true asymptotic result and a false finite-sample monotonicity claim, but I would like a clean case with which to make the point in a seminar.

Construct the simplest thought experiment you can, with two Bayesian reasoners, a binary observation sequence, and arithmetic simple enough to check by hand, that meets the following specifications. (1) Take a two-hypothesis partition, H and not-H, where H assigns success probability p and not-H assigns success probability q to each binary observation; the reasoners share these likelihoods and differ only in their priors, and each assigns positive prior probability to both hypotheses. (2) Specify the two prior distributions, with substantially different credences in H. (3) Exhibit a finite data sequence after which the two reasoners, updating by strict conditionalization, have posterior probabilities in H that are within 0.001 of one another. (4) Extend that sequence by exactly one further binary observation after which their posterior probabilities in H are more than 0.25 apart. (5) Identify the feature of the case that does the work: explain why a single new observation can have sharply different effects on two reasoners who were nearly agreed a moment before.

Finally, reconcile the case with the standard Bayesian merging-of-opinions theorems. State the relevant asymptotic claim precisely enough to show why this finite non-monotonicity does not refute it, and say what a defender of my colleague's strong claim should and should not concede in response. The scenario is for a philosophy-of-science discussion, so do not cheat by giving the two reasoners different likelihoods, different observations, or zero priors; the only permitted difference is their prior odds.
