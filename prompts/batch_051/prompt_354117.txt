I'm an undergraduate in a course on theories of the atonement, and I'd like to analyze some practices from my home congregation through the lens of the three views we've studied—penal substitution, satisfaction, and moral influence—rather than write another summary of the positions. The same congregation seems to presuppose a different theory depending on what it's doing, and I want to know exactly what each practice commits it to.

Consider these three practices:

(1) Sunday worship. The opening songs describe Christ as having taken upon himself the punishment our sins deserved and as having satisfied a demand of divine justice. The communion prayer thanks God that Christ bore what we owed, and the sermon regularly explains the cross as God's way of paying a debt without loosening the demands of justice.

(2) Lent and Good Friday. The Lenten meditations present the passion as the supreme display of self-giving love, meant to transform the participant's motivations. The Good Friday service focuses on Jesus' refusal to retaliate and on the Gospel account of his forgiving his executioners, and the closing invitation urges people to let the example of the cross change their hearts. Debt, punishment, and payment language do not appear.

(3) The church's restorative justice ministry. The program teaches that justice is primarily about repairing the harm done to victims and the community, that the offender should confess directly to those harmed and make concrete amends, and that victims are free to forgive without anyone else's being punished for the wrong. Its training materials explicitly resist the idea that punishing an innocent substitute could satisfy justice.

For each practice, tell me which theory of the atonement it strictly presupposes and which theories it merely fits with. In particular, I'm not sure whether the Sunday worship practice is really committed to penal substitution or could just as well be read as Anselmian satisfaction, since both speak of debt and payment; say which specific elements of the practice, if any, force one reading over the other. Then explain where the three practices presuppose inconsistent views of how the atonement works—the gap between the restorative justice ministry and the worship practice seems especially sharp—and judge whether that is a genuine inconsistency or whether the theories could be limited to different contexts without contradiction. Finally, walk through what would have to change, concretely, in each practice if the congregation tried to bring all three under one theory: once under penal substitution, once under satisfaction, and once under moral influence. Where a practice is underdetermined by the theory—that is, where more than one theory would license the same practice—say so explicitly and identify what further detail about the practice would settle the matter.

I'm not asking for a bibliography or sources; I want an analysis of the practices themselves, using the distinctions from the course.
