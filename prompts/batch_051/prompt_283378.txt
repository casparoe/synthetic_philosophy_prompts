I chair the academic integrity committee at a university where the faculty senate is about to vote on a proposed rewrite of the undergraduate honor code, and I need to submit our decision memo to the Provost before the senate meets. The proposal landed just after I had spent a long evening arguing with a friend about Han Feizi. My friend held that Han Feizi was right: a well-run institution should be designed for ordinary, self-interested people, with rewards and punishments reliable enough never to depend on virtue. I resisted, arguing that a system that treats students as calculators of advantage tends to produce exactly that, crowding out the moral motivations it pretends to cultivate. The faculty proposal now looks to me like that argument turned into policy.

The current code opens with an aspirational preamble about the kind of people students are trying to become, and it gives the Honor Council broad discretion to weigh intent, context, remorse, and remediation. The proposal deletes both. Under it, every student and instructor who becomes aware of a suspected violation must report it; any finding of responsibility triggers a fixed penalty (automatic failure in the course and a transcript notation for a first offense, suspension for a second, dismissal for a third), and the Honor Council may not adjust this for circumstances. A report that leads to a finding earns the reporter a positive conduct-record notation described as a trustworthiness credential plus priority course registration the following term; knowingly failing to report a violation would itself be an offense carrying half the scheduled penalty for the underlying misconduct. The proposal's authors describe this as the two handles, punishment and reward, and say the code should work even if no one is virtuous.

Write the decision memo we can send to the Provost: recommend adopting the proposal, rejecting it, or adopting it with specified conditions, and make the recommendation something the senate could act on. The memo should also give the committee durable criteria for deciding when honor-code enforcement should lean on incentives, when it should lean on virtue, and when it should mix them. Include an honest statement of the strongest consideration on the losing side, including the best version of my friend's Han Feizi point, and say where that point actually has force and where it overreaches. Act as a political philosopher who knows Han Feizi's Legalism well and has thought carefully about intrinsic motivation and institutional design; start by brainstorming the strongest case for each side before you commit. After the formal memo, append a short note to me saying how confident you are in the recommendation and singling out the single point you are least sure of.
