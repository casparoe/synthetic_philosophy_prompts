I retired a few years back after a career in population ecology, and I've been working through moral and political philosophy in my spare time. The idea I keep circling is that compromise between value systems works like trade: each side gives up something it cares about less to get something it cares about more, and the result can be genuinely better for both even though they don't share a standard of "better." I like the idea, but I keep finding versions of it that are way too quick, and I want a way to seperate the serious versions from the hand-waving.

So here's what I'd like. Before I read any more defenses of it, write me the desiderata: what would an adequate theory of moral compromise between value systems actually have to get right? I mean the full list of requirements, with the case or consideration behind each one, and which are non-negotiable versus which are just the preferences of a particular camp. In particular, make it handle at least these. (a) The two sides often don't share any common scale for weighing concessions, so the phrase "gains from trade" is itself doing work it hasn't earned. (b) The difference between compromising on actions or policies versus compromising on the values themselves — people often say they can do the first but not the second without self-betrayal; don't let the theory collapse that. (c) Cases where splitting the difference is exactly what both sides find unacceptable, and the only live option is a decision-procedure like a lottery or taking turns — is that still a compromise, or is it just a way of alternating whose values get enacted? (d) Cases where a compromise looks mutual only because it pushes the cost onto a third party — think of a hospital committee that "compromises" on conscientious refusal versus patient access in a way that leaves the patient worse off than either side's own proposal. (e) The fact that one side's "minor" concession can be the other side's sacred violation, so any theory that just prices concessions is dead on arrival. Also tell me if any pair of those requirments can't be jointly satisfied — that's the part I most want to see. Then score the usual broad options (a preference-based trade model, a deontological side-constraints model, a virtue-based civic-friendship model, or however you'd carve the space) against the list in a sentence or two each. Keep the weight on the requirements, not the scorecard.
