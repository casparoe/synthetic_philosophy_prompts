I cover mathematics and the philosophy of science for a general-interest magazine, and I am finishing a piece about how people should revise their opinions when evidence falls short of proof. In particular, I have been trying to say whether Jeffrey conditionalization—the rule for updating when the evidence merely makes a proposition more probable, say moving your confidence that the cloth is green from 0.5 to 0.8, without any proposition becoming certain—is a genuinely distinct kind of learning or just a notational shortcut. A decision theorist I interviewed gave me the following argument that it is only a shortcut:

(1) In every case of uncertain evidence—the blurry figure at dusk, the dial read through a scratched lens—the thing you come to know with certainty is a fact about your own experience: the figure had a certain look, the dial appeared to read 0.7. (2) So every such case can be represented as the certain acquisition of a proposition describing how things appeared to you. (3) The rational response to acquiring a proposition with certainty is ordinary Bayesian conditionalization: your new degree of belief in any hypothesis H should be your old degree of belief in H conditional on that proposition. (4) Since such an appearance-proposition is always available, conditioning on it yields exactly the final degrees of belief the situation calls for, and we never need a rule for the case where no proposition becomes certain. (5) Therefore Jeffrey conditionalization, whatever its notation, is not a distinct variety of rational learning.

I have a suspicion that something goes wrong in the move that turns "the evidence" into "my noticing the evidence," but I cannot put my finger on it. Tell me, as precisely as you can, whether this argument is sound. If not, which step is the first to fail, or what suppressed premise is false, and what is the correct statement of that step? I am especially interested in whether conditionalizing on a proposition about how things looked can be made equivalent to shifting one's probability in a proposition about how things were, and what would have to hold for that equivalence to obtain.
