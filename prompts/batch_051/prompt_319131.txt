The exam question was:

Explain what it means to say that an omission caused an outcome, and critically discuss one serious objection to the claim that omissions can be genuine causes. Illustrate your answer with a concrete example.

The student's answer was:

To say that an omission caused an outcome is to say that the outcome would not have occurred if the person had performed the action they failed to perform, where we identify the relevant action by asking what the person should have done in that situation. In the standard example, a lifeguard who sits in his chair while a swimmer drowns causes the swimmer's death by omission, because the death would not have occurred if the lifeguard had done what his duty required — namely, jumped in and rescued the swimmer. The main objection is that absences cannot be genuine causes: an absence is not an event, and it does not transmit energy or exert force. But this objection can be answered by noticing that an omission is never a bare nothing. The lifeguard was not simply doing nothing; he was sitting in his chair, and that positive state was part of the causal history of the death. Since every omission can similarly be redescribed as some positive event or state that the agent was in, the contrast between negative and positive causation disappears: omissions are ordinary positive causes after all, just under a different description.
