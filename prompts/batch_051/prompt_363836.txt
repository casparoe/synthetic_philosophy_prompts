I run a research organization that commissions adversarial collaborations between defenders of opposed positions in philosophy and theology. The current project pairs a cessationist and a continuationist on the question of the miraculous spiritual gifts. The cessationist holds that the distinctive gifts of the apostolic period — prophecy, tongues and their interpretation, words of knowledge, and apostolic-type healing — were bound to the founding era of the church and have ceased as a standing feature of its common life. The continuationist holds that these gifts did not cease with the apostolic generation and may rightly be expected, sought, and exercised in the church today. Both sides affirm that God is free to act miraculously, and both agree that the dispute is not about divine power but about the normative standing and expected distribution of the gifts in the post-apostolic church.

Write the document this collaboration should have produced after a year of good-faith argument. It must be a statement both could sign, in three parts.

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of substantive claims both can affirm without reservation. These should not be truisms; they should fix the object of the dispute. Include what each side counts as falling under the category of spiritual gifts, what both affirm about God's power and faithfulness, what both take the New Testament evidence to show at least prima facie, and what practical difference the disagreement makes for the church. Where a would-be agreed claim turns out not to be acceptable to one side, do not force it.

2. The precise disagreement. State where the two positions part ways, in wording each side would accept for its own view and the other recognizes as fair. Be explicit about which part of the disagreement is substantive and which part is verbal — for example, distinguish a one-off extraordinary act of healing from a recognized standing ministry of healing, and distinguish the claim that the gifts should be expected from the claim that they are merely possible. If part of the dispute dissolves once terms are sharpened, say which part and which part survives.

3. A disagreements table. For each crux — a sub-question such that a settled answer to it would move at least one side — state the crux; the kind of consideration both sides agree bears on it (a textual argument, a pattern in church history, a conceptual argument about the function of the gifts, or an ecclesial consideration); and each side's prediction, recorded in advance, about how that consideration will come out when fully examined. Both participants agree in advance that at least two cruxes must appear: first, what the primary function of the sign-gifts is — to attest and accompany the founding revelation given through the apostles in a way that ties them to that era, or to build up the church as a standing feature of its common life; second, how to read the pattern, and the apparent gaps, in post-apostolic reports of prophecy and apostolic-type healing — as the expected consequence of the gifts having served their foundational purpose, as the result of spiritual decline and lost expectation, or as an artifact of later classification and suppression. You may add further cruxes.

Do not render a verdict. Represent each side as the strongest version of itself, not as the other side's caricature. No footnotes, bibliography, or citations; refer to positions and arguments by name or description, and where the historical record is genuinely ambiguous, register that ambiguity as part of the crux rather than resolving it by assertion.
