I am a PhD student in moral philosophy, working on aggregation and the ethics of rescue. I want to take Taurek's one-versus-five case, alter a single parameter, and ask how the standard positions shift once the change is made.

Take the familiar setup. You have one unit of a scarce drug. Six strangers will die without treatment. One of them, A, needs the entire unit to survive; each of the other five, B1 through B5, needs one fifth of the unit to survive. In the standard case, anyone who receives a needed dose survives for certain. The main positions can be stated starkly. Aggregative views hold that you should save the five, because five survivors are better than one. Anti-aggregationist views hold that the number of possible beneficiaries gives no moral reason on its own: the five do not jointly have a stronger claim than A's single claim, so the count does not settle what you may do (some add that a fair coin between the two groups best expresses equal respect). Lottery views hold that numbers should influence but not wholly dictate the decision, for example by giving each side a chance proportioned to the lives at stake.

Now change one thing. Suppose the drug is not certain to save members of the larger group. Each of B1 through B5, if given one fifth of the unit, has only a fixed probability p of surviving, where p = 0.6, and the outcomes are independent. Doses are capped: no member of the larger group can take more than one fifth, and extra drug does not raise anyone's probability. A remains certain to survive with the full unit and certain to die without it. The choice is thus between one certain survivor and a lottery over the five that yields, in expectation, three survivors.

What should each of the three families of view say about this variant, and does the case still separate them? Specifically: (i) Should the aggregative view simply compare expected numbers, and would it therefore switch to saving A whenever 5p is less than 1, that is, whenever p is below 0.2? Or is there something within the aggregative outlook that forbids treating a chance of a life as a fraction of a life? (ii) Does the anti-aggregationist now have to say that A has the strongest individual claim, since a certain claim to life beats each B's merely probabilistic claim, making the decision determinate for every p below 1? Or can a consistent anti-aggregationist refuse to compare certain and probabilistic claims, and thereby retain the original permissiveness? (iii) What would a weighted-lottery view require here, and does its proposed weighting remain stable as p changes? Finally, say which position, if any, you think the variant favors, how confident you are in that judgment, and what the point is on which you are least sure.
