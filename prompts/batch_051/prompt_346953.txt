At a philosophy of biology seminar, the group got stuck on whether an ant colony should count as one organism rather than many. The person defending the superorganism view kept relying on the analogy that the colony is like a single animal body: the workers are the somatic cells, the queen is the germ line, the exchange of food and pheromones is circulation and signaling, the soldier castes are an immune system, and the winged reproductives that leave to found new colonies are gametes. The skeptic answered that the mapping is loose at nearly every point, pointing out that workers in many species can lay male eggs, that some colonies have multiple queens, and that no ant is as specialized as a heart or a liver. Treat the body analogy as an argument and audit it. Lay out the full mapping it needs, separate the disanalogies that matter for the superorganism conclusion from the ones that do not, identify what the body as source domain quietly imports that a colony lacks, and say whether the conclusion that the colony is an organism survives once those disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing more work than it can carry. If the audit points to a better analogy or to a criterion the debate should pivot to, say so. Please write the answer in continuous prose, as in a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; where correspondences would be set out in a table, spell them out in sentences.
