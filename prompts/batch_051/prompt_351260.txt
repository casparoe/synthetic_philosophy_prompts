I'm a PhD student in developmental psycholinguistics and my advisor asked me to write up a one-page "position statement" before our next meeting, mostly so we can figure out wether what I believe is even coherent. I wrote the following and now I suspect some of it clashes, but I honestly can't tell which parts, and i dont want to walk in there with a document that falls apart on the first poke. Here's what I wrote:

(a) I accept the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument in what I'd call its careful form: the primary linguistic data a child hears underdetermines the grammar she actually converges on, so something in the learner has to be doing nontrivial work, and at least some of that something is language-specific.

(b) But I don't think LLMs are an interesting counterexample to that. They train on trillions of tokens with an explicit prediction objective and massive capacity, whereas a child hears vastly less, so the fact that a model can extract grammar from text doesn't tell us a human could do it from her actual input.

(c) I'm also inclined to think a good chunk of what "Universal Grammar" was traditionally supposed to do is really done by domain-general inductive biases: simplicity, a preference for conservative generalization, something like rational model selection. I don't have a problem with that.

(d) I hold the standard line that children converge without negative evidence — without being corrected — and that this matters for any account of learnability.

(e) But I also think indirect negative evidence is real and important: when a construction a child expects never shows up in the input, that observed absence helps prune overgeneralizations. I've taught this and I'd rather not abandon it.

(f) I'm sympathetic to the Subset Principle-style move: a learner that defaults to the smallest language consistent with its data will never overgeneralize irretrievably, and that looks like a fairly general bias, not a language-specific one.

(g) I think the cross-linguistic diversity problem is serious for strong nativism: whatever is innate has to be abstract enough to be compatible with the full range of attested human languages, which risks making it so weak that it explains almost nothing.

(h) And yet I also want to say there has to be some built-in bias that rules out "pathological" grammars — grammars no human language uses. If a child could in principle converge on something radically unlike any attested human language, we haven't actually explained the universal patterns, weak as they are, and I think something does rule those out.

So: which of these are in genuine tension, and which pairs only look like they clash once you restate them charitably? I'd like the genuine tensions ranked by severity rather than a list where everything clashes with everything, and for each one, tell me what the cheapest revision would be — which commitment has to give, and how much of it.
