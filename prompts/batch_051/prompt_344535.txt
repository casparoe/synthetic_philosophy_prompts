I spent most of my career in a natural science where a result was treated as secure only when several putatively independent methods pointed in the same direction. Since retiring, I have been reading Bayesian confirmation theory and now see how much load that practice places on a formal assumption. If two lines of evidence are conditionally independent given a hypothesis, their likelihood ratios multiply, so two individually weak findings can jointly support the hypothesis far more strongly than either does alone. The operative premise is not unconditional independence; it is that, once the hypothesis and the relevant alternatives are conditioned on, the ways the methods might err do not share a common cause. My question is what happens when that premise is not established but merely presumed. In much contemporary empirical work, the independent methods are not separate instruments or laboratories; they are analysis pipelines run on the same data, with different model specifications and software choices but shared measurements, shared background conventions, and mostly shared auxiliary assumptions. Does the standard argument survive this shift from independence as a physical fact to independence as a modeling assumption? Which exact premise does the shift put pressure on, and is there a repaired version that says something weaker but still useful, perhaps about robustness across components of an analysis rather than independent confirmation, or does the multiplying of likelihood ratios here overstate the evidential value? I am interested in what happens to the argument itself, not in policy recommendations for science.
