I teach an upper-level undergraduate course in formal epistemology, and we have just finished the unit on Bayesian convergence and the washing out of priors. I want a 30-minute closed-book quiz that separates students who can repeat the slogan that with enough data the prior no longer matters from students who can state precisely what the convergence results do and do not say. Please write five multiple-choice questions, four options per question, all built around a shared stem: two Bayesian agents with different proper priors over a Bernoulli parameter θ in [0,1], who observe the same sequence of flips and update by Bayes's rule. You may vary the priors across items — Beta priors, uniform priors, two-point priors, and so on — but no item should require calculus to answer, and no item should mention or depend on a named theorem or on outside reading.

The quiz should be diagnostic rather than definitional. Ensure that each of the following five misunderstandings is encoded in at least one distractor, and phrase the correct answers with the appropriate qualifiers, such as only in the limit, with probability one, or not necessarily:

(1) treating convergence in the limit as if it supplied a uniform finite sample size after which the priors can be ignored;

(2) treating the almost-sure guarantee as if it held for every possible data sequence with no exceptions;

(3) treating washing out as automatic for any non-dogmatic prior, while ignoring whether the prior's support rules out the true parameter;

(4) treating merging of posterior beliefs as if it guaranteed merging of decisions or actions, ignoring utilities, thresholds, and the fact that finite-time agreement may still not be close enough;

(5) treating the speed of washing out as prior-independent, as though the theorem gave a uniform bound on how much data suffices.

After the questions, provide an answer key that functions as the model answer: for each item, give the correct option and explain in one or two sentences why it is correct, then state for every distractor the specific misunderstanding it is designed to catch. Conclude with a marking scheme of one mark per correct response, five marks total, no partial credit, and for each item a one-line note on the most predictable error from a student who knows only the slogan.
