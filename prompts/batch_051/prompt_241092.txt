Me and a few friends run a tiny film-and-philosophy club, and this week we're doing Get Out (2017). I've been asked to open with a philosophical angle, and I keep circling the question of what actually makes a statement racist. One view says it's about intent: you meant to demean someone on the basis of race, or you didn't. Another says it's about effect: the statement lands as racialized and does harm no matter what you meant. And then there's the dog-whistle picture, where something sounds colorblind or even complimentary at the surface but sends a different message to a subset of hearers. That third option complicates both the intent view and the effect view, because the speaker has some deniability and the effect is uneven across the audience.

I'd like a reading of the film through those categories. The Armitages and their guests are constantly peforming post-racial progressiveness: Chris gets told how much they admire Obama, how strong and cool black people are, all while something else is going on underneath. The garden party is full of outwardly nice remarks that play differently the second time, then there's the silent auction and the Coagula procedure, where it turns out Chris was never a guest but the prize. Some characters, like the brother, are openly hostile, while others seem to think they're just being friendly or even admiring. So I'm trying to work out: does Get Out land on one side of the intent/effect/dog-whistle debate? Where does it resist all three, maybe by suggesting that obsessing over statements is itself a way of not seeing the actual horror? And does the film add anything to the philosophical debate that the usual examples—a slur, a joke, a politician's code word—miss? I want the discussion to be faithful to both the movie and the philosophy, so I'd rather have a reading that says where the lens stops fitting than one that just slots scenes into jargon.
