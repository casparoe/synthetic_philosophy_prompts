I have come to defend a position in political philosophy that I would like pressure-tested rather than polemically attacked. The position is this: a constitutional monarchy is defensible within a democracy, when it is defensible at all, solely on narrowly functional grounds. The hereditary sovereign earns her place by being a head of state with no independent political mandate, which fits her for two roles that a partisan president performs badly: acting as a neutral referee during moments of constitutional discretion—dissolutions, invitations to form a government after an inconclusive election, and the exercise of reserve powers—and standing for the continuity of the constitutional order across changes of government. I do not defend the institution on grounds of tradition, national identity, or aesthetic attachment, which I regard as carrying no justificatory weight; and I grant that heredity is a standing moral anomaly in a democracy, so the entire burden falls on the functional benefits.

I want an honest map of what would count against this view. Which institutional findings, historical episodes, or comparative results should most shake my confidence? I am thinking, for instance, of parliamentary republics with deliberately weak presidencies that seem to secure the same neutrality without heredity; of episodes in which monarchs or their representatives were accused of partisanship or exercised reserve powers controversially; and of the standing worry that the view can be qualified until it is unfalsifiable by treating every failure as a failing of a particular occupant rather than of the institution. I would also like to know what the view commits me to predicting—for example, whether I should expect monarchies to handle hung parliaments and minority governments more smoothly than otherwise similar republics, and whether the failure of such predictions would be decisive or only awkward for the view. Please distinguish decisive defeaters from merely awkward ones, say which of them a committed defender of this functionalist position should concede as counting against it rather than dismiss, and give a frank assessment of whether the position is insulated from refutation and whether that insulation should worry me.
