I have a deliberate policy about the artists whose work I most value: I try not to learn about their private conduct, and when something does reach me, I work to keep it from reorganizing how I experience the work. This began after a writer I had taught for years was credibly accused of serious abuse, and I found that the biography no longer stayed outside the books; reading felt mediated by what I knew. Since then I have skipped biographies, declined conversations that begin with the claim that I really should know what he did, and generally treated my ignorance about artists' lives as something worth protecting. I tell myself the point is to judge the work on its own terms rather than by proxy for the artist's character.

But I do not do this in other domains. I want to know whether a clothing brand uses forced labor, and I think someone who avoided that knowledge in order to keep enjoying cheap clothes would be doing something wrong. The asymmetry is not obvious to me. Last week a friend offered to tell me what I needed to know about a musician whose records I love; I declined, and she said I was choosing my own enjoyment over the victims. Is this policy defensible, or is it motivated ignorance dressed up as an aesthetic principle? If it is defensible, under what conditions: does it matter whether the artist is living or dead, whether I buy the work, perform it, or teach it, or whether the wrong was grave, recent, or formally acknowledged? If it is not defensible, is there a narrower version that is—a way of keeping biography from dominating the work without blinding myself to morally significant facts?
