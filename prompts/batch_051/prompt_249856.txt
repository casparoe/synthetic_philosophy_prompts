You are a philosopher of criminal law and moral responsibility, and you have been asked to adjudicate an asymmetry that runs through ordinary punitive judgment. Consider two offenders whose crimes are identical in harm, planning, and awareness, and whose punishment would deliver the same deterrence and incapacitation. Offender One has a stable personality, no relevant neurological impairment, and planned the crime over months. Offender Two planned and committed the identical crime in the same state of awareness, but his behavior had changed sharply only after a slow-growing tumor in his ventromedial prefrontal cortex began compromising impulse control—discovered only after the crime. Ordinary practice treats One as fully deserving of severe punishment and Two as warranting mitigation, treatment, or something short of full desert: One provokes indignation, Two provokes something closer to tragic sympathy. Is this asymmetry defensible, or is it an inconsistency we are stuck with once a skeptic points out that both lives and both brains are equally the product of causes outside the agent's control? Frame your answer as a confrontation between a Dennett-style compatibilist, who holds that just deserts can survive determinism because desert tracks the exercise of ordinary capacities for practical reasoning and self-control, and a Caruso-style free-will skeptic, who holds that basic-desert responsibility is never available and that punishment can be justified only in a quarantine-like, non-retributive way. Do not merely report the two positions; use each to test the asymmetry against nearby cases—addiction, childhood abuse, antisocial personality disorder—and then say what you would keep, abandon, or revise. Begin with a brief brainstorm of the candidate principles that might do the justificatory work, and end with a firm verdict.
