I co-direct a weekend crisis-negotiation workshop for undergraduates in international relations, usually 18 to 24 students, and for this session I can split them into two alliances with several small teams on each side and give them two 75-minute blocks with a short break. Nuclear deterrence has recently become personal for me: my younger brother is finishing his training as a naval officer, and in a recent letter he wrote that nuclear weapons would be irrelevant if no sane leader would ever use them, so he cannot see why planners treat them as more than expensive scenery. I recognize the same intuition in many of my students, and I doubt a lecture on mutual vulnerability would shake it. I want a structured game that makes participants run into the stability–instability paradox themselves, before I name it: the more secure the strategic nuclear stalemate looks, the more rational limited conventional probes become, because each side expects the other not to escalate over stakes worth far less than annihilation; yet that very boldness tempts each side to make threats that deliberately leave something to chance, which reintroduces the possibility of precisely the war the stalemate was supposed to prevent. Design a runnable two-alliance game in which participants begin a round believing the catastrophic option is effectively off the table, discover from the scoring structure that this belief makes them bolder in small probes, and then find themselves tempted to manipulate accident risk as a way to restore credibility. Include roles or rules, materials, a payoff scheme that requires no real money, a timeline for the two 75-minute blocks, and a debrief that maps specific moves and rounds onto the paradox, the credibility problem, and the logic of leaving something to chance. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run the game and how to handle it, and be explicit about which feature of the paradox the game cannot reproduce and that I will need to supply in closing. The insight should emerge from the rules, the scoring, and the chance mechanism, not from anything I say before the debrief.
