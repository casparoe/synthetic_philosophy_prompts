In my work building event-sourced systems, semantics always arrives from outside a record: a log entry is about a payment only because a projection, a schema, or a human reads it that way, and a source of truth is a governance convention rather than a natural property of the data. I am fluent in that vocabulary — event logs, projections and read models, producer-consumer contracts, replaying history under a new interpretation, and the bootstrap problem of who writes and interprets the first schema — but I have no formal philosophy background.

I recently learned that philosophers have a sustained debate over whether the mind could be like that all the way down. One camp, associated with John Searle, holds that words, sentences, diagrams, and the states of a running program have only derived intentionality: they are about things only because some mind assigns them a meaning, much as the bytes in a log are about a payment only under an interpretation. This camp insists that the chain of assignment must bottom out. At least some mental states — beliefs, desires, perceptions — have original or intrinsic intentionality: they are about their objects in their own right, not in virtue of being interpreted by some further mind. Without such a bottom, the argument runs, there is an infinite regress of interpreters and no one to give the whole chain its semantics. This camp adds that computation is syntax all the way down and observer-relative, so no amount of computation can generate that original aboutness.

Another camp, associated with Daniel Dennett, treats the demand for original intentionality as a mistake. On this view, the aboutness we ascribe to a human mind is itself the product of a mindless evolutionary design process together with the intentional stance we adopt when we attribute beliefs and desires to make behavior predictable. There is no point in the causal or interpretive chain at which aboutness becomes intrinsic rather than assigned relative to some interpreter or purpose. A nervous system is simply an unusually complicated device whose semantics is as derived, in the relevant sense, as the semantics of an event log.

Restate this debate claim by claim in the vocabulary I actually use. Show me what maps onto a log as against a projection, onto a producer-consumer contract, onto replaying history under a new schema, and onto the practice of declaring one log canonical. Then give me an honest residue analysis: which claims translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no expression in my vocabulary at all.

Bear down on two questions in particular. First, does the Searle side map onto the objection that no chain of projections and interpretations can terminate in a record that interprets itself? Second, does the Dennett side map onto the observation that a source of truth is always a convention, never an intrinsic property of the data? Finally, tell me whether what fails to translate is a gap in my vocabulary or evidence that original intentionality was a placeholder for something my vocabulary already teaches me to do without.
