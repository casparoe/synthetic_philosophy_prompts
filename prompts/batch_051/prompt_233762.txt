At a reading group on Mill's On Liberty last week, two members fell into a long argument about a mutual friend of ours, Lena, who has left a stable government job to spend two years at a rural art collective trying to paint, farm, and write a novel. We had been discussing the sections where Mill defends individuality and what he calls experiments in living, and the argument turned on whether his view licenses Lena's choice.

Evan said Lena is exactly the kind of person Mill was writing to protect. Her old life may have been comfortable, he argued, but its shape was largely set by other people's expectations, whereas her new life is a genuine trial of a different way of living. Even if she fails, he said, the failure is not socially worthless: it adds a data point to the standing question of whether that kind of life can work, and Mill's central point is that such experiments need protection from the tyranny of opinion and custom.

Maya replied that this turns every whim into a protected experiment. On her reading, Mill's argument is not that nonconformity is valuable for its own sake, but that it is valuable when it probes a way of living that might turn out to be an improvement. If there is no real reason to think anyone will learn anything from Lena's project, she said, calling it an experiment is just dressing up a preference in philosophical language.

Evan answered that you cannot tell in advance which unconventional lives will prove illuminating—that is part of what makes them experiments—and if the majority gets to screen departures for plausibility before they are lived, it will predictably screen out the very departures it most needs, because by its own lights those departures look unpromising. The functional value of experiments in living, he said, depends on our not pre-filtering them with conventional standards of seriousness.

Maya pushed back that this erases a line Mill himself draws. Mill does not, she said, protect experiments that cause harm or breach distinct obligations to other people; and a decision like Lena's often does impose real costs on family, colleagues, and friends who rely on her. So the epistemic argument for letting all experiments run is not the whole of Mill's view, and the hard question is where the burden of proof sits: on the person departing from an established way of living to show that her departure is a real experiment, or on the rest of us to show that it is not.

They asked me to referee, and I could not.
