Since Katy moved back in with us—she's 29 and works full-time at the clinic, she just can't afford a one-bedroom here—I've been going to the neighborhood assocation meetings, and a lot of what I used to believe has gotten scrambled. The current fight is about a proposal to allow four units on any corner lot within half a mile of the light rail, with no required parking. The usual sides formed in about four minutes: the neighbors say it would kill the neighborhood, the pro-housing people say the neighbors are just gatekeepers with cutesy lawn signs. Everyone keeps talking about who has a right to the city, but nobody in the room can say what that would actually mean.

After too many nights arguing with myself, I wrote down a principle I've been calling "presence tenure," and I honestly can't tell if it's a real idea or just my NIMBYism with a nicer hat. It goes like this: your voice in what gets built on a block should be proportional to how much of your actual day-to-day life is anchored there, not to whether you own anything. The retired couple in the bungalow who've walked to the corner store for forty years get a big say. So does the guy renting the basement apartment, even though he owns nothing. The landlord who owns three houses on the street but lives two towns over gets almost no say about the block, whatever property rights he has over the buildings. Would-be residents get a provisional say too—not a veto over current residents, but enough that current residents can't simply outvote everyone else's need to live somewhere. If your life is already anchored here (you work at the clinic, your kid goes to the school, you coach soccer), you've got a foot in the door, and the city has to make room for you.

At the last meeting I found myself using this to argue both ways about the same building. I used it to say the four-plex should be allowed, because the people who need it already have their lives here and just need somewhere to sleep. Then I used it to defend my own objection: I didn't buy a house on a quiet street so that a year of construction, extra cars, and losing the old oaks would become part of my daily life. Both felt like applications of the same principle, but they can't both be right.

So tell me straight: is "presence tenure" coherent, or is it just a metaphor doing the work of an argument? If it already exists under a real name, tell me the name and where mine differs. What does it get right, and where does it break? Give me the three hardest cases I haven't considered (a halfway house, a teardown mansion, a long-vacant lot, whatever) and tell me which of my two verdicts about the four-plex I'd have to give up if I kept it.
