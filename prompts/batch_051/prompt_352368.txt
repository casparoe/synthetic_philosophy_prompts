I’m a novelist, not someone with training in philosophy, but I’m trying to get inside a character who keeps deciding at four in the afternoon that he won’t drink that night, then by ten he’s ordered a bottle. I started reading Aristotle’s Nicomachean Ethics online (the Perseus translation) because I wanted something older and less self-helpy than the habit books. Two passages in Book VII look to me like they pull in opposite directions, and I can’t tell if I’m misreading them.

In VII.3 Aristotle writes: “When therefore there is present in the mind on the one hand a universal judgement forbidding you to taste and on the other hand a universal judgement saying ‘All sweet things are pleasant,’ and a minor premise ‘Yonder thing is sweet’ (and it is this minor premise that is active), and when desire is present at the same time, then, though the former universal judgement says ‘Avoid that thing,’ the desire leads you to it (since desire can put the various parts of the body in motion).”

But earlier in Book VII he says: “The profligate yields to his appetites from choice, considering it right always to pursue the pleasure that offers, whereas the man of defective self-restraint does not think so, but pursues it all the same.”

The second passage makes the unrestrained man sound like he holds the judgment that the act is wrong and does it anyway. The first passage seems to say the relevant knowledge isn’t actually active—desire sort of bypasses it. For my character, I need to know whether these two can be reconciled, and if so which reconciliation is faithful to the text and which is just patching over a hole. Is Aristotle distinguishing standing from occurrent knowledge? Is it about which premise of the practical syllogism is active? And does the stuff I’ve seen people say about hyperbolic discounting—preferences flipping as the future gets close—capture anything Aristotle is saying, or is that just me smuggling modern economics into him? Please give me a reading a reasonably careful novelist could use without turning the character into a philosophical zombie.
