I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about deontological constraints—the thought that some actions are wrong no matter how much good they would produce—and my verdicts on particular cases have become more specific and less easy to defend than I expected. I would like you to help me determine whether these verdicts can be captured by a single principle, and if not, which of them I should abandon.

Here are my cases and verdicts.

The floodgate. A river has burst its banks and is about to flood a house in which five people are sleeping. I can close a floodgate that will save the house, but the diverted water will flood a watchman's shack and drown the watchman. I think closing the gate is permissible, even though I foresee his death from the start.

The lifeboat. Six people are in a swamped boat that cannot support them all and will sink before help arrives; none of them can swim. The only way to save five is for one passenger, who cannot swim, to be pushed overboard to his death. I think pushing him is not permissible, even though the arithmetic is the same as in the floodgate: one death, five saved.

The hostage-taker. A hostage-taker is about to shoot five people one by one. He tells me that he will release all five if I shoot an innocent stranger whom he has brought to the scene; if I refuse, he will carry out the five shootings himself. I think I may not shoot the stranger, and I do not think raising the number to fifty, or five hundred, would change that verdict. But I cannot explain why this is not simply a refusal to dirty my own hands at the cost of five innocent lives.

The cave-in. Five miners are trapped by rising water in a gallery. The only way to reach them is to set off a charge; the blast will also collapse a narrow ledge where a sixth miner is known to be resting, killing him, though his death plays no part in opening the way to the five. I think setting the charge is permissible.

The broken promise. I have promised a friend that I will meet him at a certain time, but on the way I come upon a pedestrian who has been hit by a car and is bleeding heavily. I stay to help and miss the meeting. I think breaking the promise is permissible.

The floodgate and the lifeboat trouble me most. In both cases I know that someone who would otherwise have lived will die as a result of what I do, and in both cases one death prevents five. The only difference I can point to is that in the lifeboat the passenger's being pushed overboard is what keeps the boat afloat long enough for the others to survive, while in the floodgate the watchman's drowning plays no part in saving the five. I keep reaching for that distinction, then wanting to discard it as a rationalization.

The principle I keep reaching for is something like this: it is never permissible to intend the death of an innocent person, even in order to prevent a much larger number of innocent deaths, but it is permissible to bring about innocent deaths as a foreseen side effect of preventing harm, provided the harm prevented is serious enough to outweigh them. That principle seems to yield my first four cases, and something similar—a constraint that gives way in genuine emergencies—would handle the promise. But it leaves me with two doubts. First, the distinction between what I intend and what I merely foresee is doing almost all of the moral work, and I am not sure why the contents of my mind should determine whether a foreseeable death is a permissible side effect or a forbidden killing. Second, although the principle tells me not to shoot the stranger in the hostage-taker case, it does not tell me why I should obey a prohibition on killing when obeying it leaves five people dead at someone else's hands. If the point of the prohibition is that innocent persons may not be treated as expendable, refusing to shoot seems to treat the five as expendable in order to avoid becoming the agent of the stranger's death; if the point is simply that as few innocent people should die as possible, then shooting seems to be what the principle should demand. The first option looks like moral self-indulgence; the second looks like giving the prohibition up altogether. I cannot accept either as it stands.

I would like you to propose the best principle you can—possibly one that adds something about agent-relative reasons, the separateness of persons, or the conditions under which constraints may be permissibly violated—that yields all or most of my verdicts. State the principle precisely, and say which clause is motivated by which case. Then apply it to further cases I have not settled: (a) the hostage-taker makes the five one hundred; should the constraint ever give way to sheer numbers, and if not, how is that to be justified? (b) An outsider tells me that if I break a serious promise I have made to a colleague, five other people will be moved to keep their own comparable promises, whereas if I keep mine, those five will break theirs; does the same agent-relative structure apply to promises, or is the prohibition on killing special? (c) A case in which the one person I would have to kill is not innocent but is himself part of the threat, such as one of the hostage-takers; does the principle distinguish between the innocent and the threatening, and should it?

Finally, I want the most defensible single principle, not a menu of options. Which of my verdicts, if any, does it force me to give up? Please also say how confident you are in the principle you defend, and single out the point you are least sure of.
