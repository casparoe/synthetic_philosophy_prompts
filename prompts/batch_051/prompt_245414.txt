A longstanding issue at the intersection of metaontology and the theory of verbal disputes is how to tell when a dispute that sounds substantive is really merely verbal. Consider two rival tests.

(1) The translation test. A dispute over a claim is merely verbal if there is a neutral paraphrase of each side's position such that both parties accept the paraphrase, and the appearance of disagreement is fully explained by the different intensions or extensions they assign to some term in the claim.

(2) The joint-carving test. A dispute is merely verbal only if the translation test is satisfied and neither use of the disputed term is better than the other in naturalness, explanatory power, or inductive projectibility. If one side's use tracks a more joint-carving property—one that supports broader, more stable generalisations—then the dispute is substantive even though both sides accept every neutral paraphrase.

Construct a scenario that separates the two tests. It should be a realistic exchange between two people who agree on all the descriptive facts—the measurements, causal relations, outcomes, and the like—but still disagree about a classification. One party asserts a sentence of the form 'x is F' and the other denies it, with F an ordinary kind or status term (alive, species, disease, addiction, or similar), not a technical term from metaphysics and not the standard debate over tables and composition. Engineer the scenario so that the translation test classifies the dispute as merely verbal while the joint-carving test classifies it as substantive; a case that merely stipulates the verdicts fails the exercise.

Then run the checks. State each side's utterance and the neutral paraphrase both accept, and explain why the translation test gives its verdict. Next identify the precise asymmetry in what each usage lets its user infer, explain, or predict, and show why that asymmetry makes one side's use more joint-carving on the above criterion. Finish by saying which feature of the case does the work and whether removing it would make both tests classify the dispute as verbal; and explain how a defender of the translation test would resist the suggestion that the asymmetry should matter.
