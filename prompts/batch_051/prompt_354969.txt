I am a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am working on a long feature about how people should act when they are unsure between competing moral theories. In an interview, a decision theorist gave me a compressed version of the following argument against the idea that moral uncertainty can be tamed by a single decision rule. At the time I found it too quick, but now that I have set it out as numbered steps, I still cannot locate the failure, if there is one.

Here is my reconstruction:
(1) When you are uncertain between rival first-order moral theories, the rational way to act is to maximize expected choiceworthiness: weight each action's choiceworthiness under each theory by your credence in that theory, then choose the action with the greatest weighted sum.
(2) But "maximize expected choiceworthiness" is itself a normative theory — a theory about how one ought to respond to moral uncertainty.
(3) One can be uncertain between that theory and rival second-order rules, such as "do what the single most credible moral theory says" or "avoid the worst-case moral outcome."
(4) By the same reasoning as in (1), when you are uncertain at that higher level, you should apply whichever second-order rule has the greatest expected choiceworthiness.
(5) But that proposal is itself another theory, and the same question can be raised about it; the problem therefore reappears at every level.
(6) Thus there is no principled stopping point, and the instruction to act on your best normative view never bottoms out in a determinate verdict about what to do.

Is this argument valid? If not, which step fails, and why exactly? If it is valid, must I accept the conclusion — and what does the conclusion actually show? I half-suspect the move from (5) to (6) is too fast, but I cannot articulate an alternative account of how a determinate verdict is generated if the regress never stops.
