I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on early modern metaphysics, and I am drafting the final examination. I should like one essay question built around a specific passage from Berkeley that we did not discuss in lectures, so that students cannot simply reproduce my commentary. I have in mind the following passage from George Berkeley, *A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge*, Part I, Section 5:

"But my conceiving or imagining power does not extend beyond the possibility of real existence or perception. Hence, as it is impossible for me to see or feel anything without an actual sensation of that thing, so is it impossible for me to conceive in my thoughts any sensible thing or object distinct from the sensation or perception of it."

The question should ask students to do three things: first, to reconstruct the argument Berkeley is making in this passage and explain how it supports his rejection of material substance; second, to assess the argument's force by identifying the assumption on which it trades about the relationship between conceiving and possibility; and third, to consider one serious objection to Berkeley's move and discuss whether he has the resources to answer it. The question should be framed so that a student who has memorised only the slogan *esse est percipi* cannot do well on it.

Please provide: (a) the essay question, worded as it would appear on the examination paper; (b) a model answer of approximately 700 words; and (c) a marking scheme that separates an excellent answer from a merely competent one by naming the specific interpretative and critical moves each must contain. The marking scheme should identify, for each of the three parts of the question, at least one characteristic error that students commonly make.
