I'm giving a seminar next week to a joint audience of behavioral economists and organizational psychologists, titled "The Dollar Auction Is a Misleading Model of Escalation of Commitment." My thesis is that the dollar auction captures only one narrow escalation mechanism—competitive bidding to avoid losing the bid—and that treating it as the canonical illustration of escalation leads both researchers and practitioners to overestimate the role of sunk-cost-style loss reasoning and to underweight three mechanisms that do more work in real organizations: identity threat and face-saving, accountability for the original decision, and escalating project-level uncertainty (projects that grow more uncertain rather than more informative as they go). I will argue that these mechanisms call for different interventions—such as separating the person from the original decision, redesigning governance so continuation decisions are not made by the original sponsor, and forcing explicit re-evaluation of exit criteria at each stage—rather than the usual "set a reservation price in advance and teach people to honor it" advice that the dollar-auction frame naturally suggests.

The audience is roughly thirty people: eight or so behavioral economists who view the dollar auction and escalation of commitment as core experimental paradigms and are skeptical of claims that laboratory games mislead more than they illuminate; a dozen organizational psychologists who do applied work on project de-escalation and care mostly about what works in real firms; and the rest are practitioners from a consultancy that trains executives to "quit early," several of whom already use the dollar auction in workshops as a vivid opening exercise.

Include, clearly marked, the objection I am hoping nobody asks. You are a senior behavioral scientist who has presented this kind of work to both constituencies many times. Before drafting, think carefully about what each group most wants to protect in its own framework, and about which empirical facts I would need on my side for each reply to land. Keep the whole prep sheet under 900 words, and make it maximally useful in the room: flag the one or two points where I should concede up front rather than fight.
