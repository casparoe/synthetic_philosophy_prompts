Last night I spent a long time arguing with a friend about Kelly betting and geometric rationality, specifically about whether someone offered a 60/40 biased coin at even odds should bet their whole bankroll. The argument got tangled, and I came away unsure which of us was mixing together different questions. Because I was a participant, I don't trust my own read of that argument. Today, though, I heard a cleaner version of the same dispute between two other friends, and I wrote it down while they were talking. I want you to adjudicate that exchange carefully.

Here is the setup, which both of them accept. You have a bankroll. You are offered a gamble on a coin that lands heads with probability 0.6. Each round you may stake any fixed fraction f of your current bankroll on heads at even odds: if heads comes up, you keep the stake and win an equal amount on top; if tails comes up, you lose the stake. Rounds are independent and can be repeated indefinitely. A dollar staked has expected profit 0.6(+1) + 0.4(−1) = 0.20, no matter how much is staked. For this coin, the Kelly stake is 20% of the current bankroll.

Sam argued first. "Every dollar staked has the same positive expected profit, so the expected value of the gamble is linear in the amount you stake. If you take maximizing expected final wealth seriously, you stake the whole bankroll every round. Anything less is leaving expected value unclaimed. A smaller stake might be psychologically comfortable if you are risk-averse, but that is a preference about money, not a requirement of rationality."

Rina replied. "Stake everything and a single tails leaves you with nothing. After N rounds the probability you are solvent is 0.6^N, which tends to zero, while the expected wealth is 1.2^N, which tends to infinity. Nearly every actual path ends in ruin, and the expectation is carried by a vanishingly improbable blowup path. The right target is the growth rate, which means maximizing expected log-wealth. For a fixed fraction f, the expected log-growth per round is 0.6 ln(1+f) + 0.4 ln(1−f), and it is maximized at f = 0.2—the Kelly stake. Even a fixed 50% stake has positive expected value every round but negative expected log-growth—0.6 ln 1.5 + 0.4 ln 0.5 is less than zero—so it tends to zero with probability one. Positive EV per round and long-run ruin are perfectly compatible, and that is exactly why arithmetic EV is the wrong lens."

Sam continued. "You have just changed the goalposts and declared victory. Maximizing expected log-wealth is expected-utility maximization with logarithmic utility for wealth—one particular risk-averse preference among many. For an agent with genuinely linear utility, the theorem really does say: bet everything. Kelly is not a rival to expected utility; it is expected utility wearing a log-utility costume, and its authority depends entirely on whether that utility function describes the agent."

Rina answered. "The logarithm is not merely a taste. It matters because log-wealth tracks the typical path under multiplicative compounding: the bankroll multiplies round by round, and 0.6 ln(1+f) + 0.4 ln(1−f) is exactly the log of the geometric mean growth factor per round. Kelly therefore maximizes the geometric mean—the growth of the typical path—and, asymptotically, the median of final wealth. In the long run a Kelly bettor outperforms any other fixed-fraction bettor with probability one. That is a different criterion—geometric rationality—not raw risk aversion. Arithmetic expectation across repeated multiplicative trials is a bad aggregate, because it lets a zero-probability tail of jackpot paths dominate what typical paths actually do."

Sam replied. "Choosing the typical path—the median—as the thing to optimize is itself a value judgment, not a neutral fact. And your long-run argument depends on indefinite repetition. In a single 60/40 flip there is no compounding and no growth rate, and the median argument loses most of its force. At best you have shown that all-in is a bad way to chase arithmetic expectation when the game repeats many times. You have not shown that expected final wealth is the wrong target for a one-shot choice by someone with linear utility. The real lesson is just that virtually nobody has linear utility over total wealth."

Rina responded. "No one-shot choice lives in a vacuum. Anyone's bankroll is embedded in an ongoing stream of future opportunities, so the long-run structure is the relevant one. And you still have not dealt with the ruin feature: a rule that says 'your expected wealth is enormous' while 'the probability you are not broke is 10^-6' is not merely risk-neutral—it is a defective way of aggregating possible outcomes. The burden is on you to explain why the arithmetic expectation across parallel counterfactual paths should govern an agent who lives one path, when almost all of those paths end in ruin."

That is where they left it.
