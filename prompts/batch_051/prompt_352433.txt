You are a professional philosopher specializing in normative ethics and in the formal structure of moral theories. I am a retired natural scientist who now runs a reading group for other retired researchers; we have been working through the difference between being a certain way and promoting a corresponding state of affairs, and we have chosen frugality as our test case. I would like you to produce a sorting instrument—specifically, a decision tree—whose questions a reader can answer without knowing any philosophical labels, and which then places the reader in one of the main positions on whether frugality is something one should practice oneself, something one should promote in the world, or something else again.

Begin by thinking carefully, and briefly lay out the dimensions that you believe genuinely separate positions here: for example, whether frugality has intrinsic or merely instrumental value, whether the relevant norm is agent-relative or agent-neutral, whether frugality is fundamentally about spending less money or about consuming fewer resources and producing less waste, and whether its primary bearer is the individual or the institution. Then choose the three or four dimensions that do the most work, and build a branching decision tree from the logical space of answers, not from a list of labels. Label each branch with the concrete question or claim that splits it, expressed in ordinary language.

At least one branching question must use the following case: a consultant who is flown first-class and put up in expensive hotels while advising companies on how to reduce waste, and whose ability to secure those reductions depends on such displays of success. The relevant question is whether that spending is permissible, required if it produces more frugality overall, or a regrettable side effect that still leaves the act wrong or vicious.

The tree should include, as leaves, at least these positions: (1) a deontological view on which each person has a duty to be frugal in their own spending and consumption regardless of the aggregate consequences; (2) a consequentialist view on which what matters is promoting frugality, so that conspicuous consumption that reliably causes others to be more frugal can be the right act; (3) a virtue-ethical view on which frugality is a trait of character worth having for its own sake; and (4) a political view on which frugality is chiefly a duty of officeholders and institutions with respect to public or shared resources. Each leaf should carry one sentence stating that position's most awkward commitment. You may add further leaves only if the logical space clearly contains positions not covered by these four.

Flag any combinations of answers that the tree permits but that no known position occupies, and say for each whether it is unoccupied because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. If two of the named positions are not separated by your chosen questions, say so explicitly rather than manufacturing a distinction. Keep the final instrument to at most seven leaves and four splitting questions.
