I have been trying to determine what I actually think about humility and modesty by recording my verdicts on particular cases. I would like you to work out what principle, if any, captures these verdicts; what that principle commits me to about some further cases; and which of my verdicts, if any, I must abandon in order to get a principle worth keeping. Please be explicit about tensions in my pattern rather than smoothing them over.

Here are the cases.

1. A colleague who is unquestionably the leading expert in a small field deflects a student's question about how the field began by laughing and saying it is mostly his fault, before moving on. I find this genuinely modest and likable, not false modesty.

2. A senior scientist, asked whether she is among the people competent to judge a contested result, answers plainly that she is one of the few people in the world competent to judge it. Her judgement is accurate. I find this completely appropriate, though many would call it immodest.

3. A junior doctor tells a patient that she is fairly sure the illness is viral, but that he should return if it does not improve because she has been wrong before. She means exactly what she says. I find this an exemplary kind of intellectual humility.

4. Another doctor gives the same hedging answer, but the patient later learns from the notes that the doctor had no actual doubt and added the hedge only to make the patient feel cared for. I find this worse, in an epistemic respect, than a confidently delivered wrong answer.

5. A minister is asked in a live debate about a specific detail of policy that the role plainly requires them to know, and answers that they do not know. Part of me respects the honesty, but I cannot call it a clear virtue overall: I also think they have failed at something their role requires.

6. A retired soldier who was decorated for bravery never mentions it. His family learns of it only after his death, from old comrades. I admire the silence deeply, and I do not think it was required of him, but I cannot say exactly why it impresses me as much as it does.

7. A scientist applying for a grant writes that the proposed project has about a one-in-five chance of working and explains the main reason it might fail. I think this is honest and probably good for science, but I also half-blame the funders for declining to take the gamble, and I am not sure I would write the same in their place.

8. Someone I know is forever running themselves down in conversation: their work is no good, their ideas are confused, their efforts amount to nothing. After a while I feel this is not humility at all; it is a form of self-absorption, and I notice I avoid them.

9. An undergraduate with a passing interest tells me that an entire established field of climate science has been taken in by groupthink, though they have read a handful of blog posts and cannot state, even roughly, the basic physics. I find this arrogance, not independence of mind.

10. A dissenting scholar has spent years working through the arguments for the position she rejects. She can state that position as well as its defenders, and she can answer the standard objections to her own view. I think she is entitled to refuse to defer, and that to call her refusal arrogance would be a mistake.

11. At dinner, someone who has just published a well-reviewed book answers an innocent question about what they have been writing by recounting, at length and with obvious pleasure, the book's arguments and the praise it has received. Nothing they say is false. I find it unattractively immodest.

12. In a job interview, a woman sets out her qualifications, achievements, and salary expectations plainly, without apology or self-deprecation. I think this is exactly what the situation calls for and no immodesty at all, though I know people who would call her immodest on grounds I reject.

I can see some of the tensions on my own. Cases 3 and 4 are near-identical in what is said, but I judge them in opposite ways. Cases 9 and 10 both involve declining to defer, yet I want to distinguish them sharply. Cases 1, 6, 11, and 12 are all ways of truthfully presenting oneself, and I split several ways, apparently according to manner and setting. I want to know whether one principle, or a small set of principles, can make all of this hang together.

Here are some further cases on which I have not settled:

- A writer has finished a manuscript but keeps revising it because they genuinely cannot judge whether it is good enough to publish.
- A public figure loudly confesses minor faults in order to be praised for what one might call performative humility.
- A person asked what they are good at cannot, in all seriousness, give a single answer, because they have never been allowed to see any of their abilities as strengths.
- An employee tells their line manager, in a performance review, that they are not good at a task the whole team relies on them to do, which is true.

Please state the principle or the small set of candidate principles precisely, tie each clause to the cases that motivate it, and give its verdicts on the further cases above. Say honestly which of my original verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate, and whether it is better to give those verdicts up than to complicate the principle in order to keep them. My own suspicion is that I have been running together two virtues: one about assessing oneself accurately, and another about not making oneself the centre of other people's attention. I would like you to tell me whether that distinction does the necessary work, or whether it merely replaces one muddle with two.

Please write your answer in British English.
