I am currently taking an epistemology course, and we have just spent several sessions on the bank cases, attributor contextualism, and pragmatic encroachment. In seminar, these views are consistently presented as rival explanations of the same linguistic and intuitive data: contextualism is introduced as the semantic thesis that the truth-conditions of "S knows that p" shift with the attributor's conversational context, while pragmatic encroachment is introduced as the thesis that whether S knows that p depends constitutively on the practical stakes for S. I have come to suspect that this way of drawing the map is quietly misleading, and I would like help working out the suspicion.

Please argue for the following position: the view usually called subject-sensitive invariantism—according to which "knows" has a single invariant meaning, yet two subjects with identical evidence can differ in whether they know the same ordinary proposition solely because of their differing practical stakes—should be classified as a genuine form of contextualism, not as an invariantist alternative to it. In particular, argue that once subject-sensitive invariantism grants that the extension of "knows" can vary with the subject's practical situation in this way, the invariant meaning it insists on preserving is doing no real explanatory work, and the view has effectively conceded the central semantic claim of contextualism. Build the argument as forcefully as you can, but without pressing it so hard that a careful invariantist could dismiss it as a merely terminological reclassification.

Since I will use this to sharpen a term paper, please end by saying what would most likely change your mind: identify the strongest reply or consideration that could lead you to a different classification, or the precise point in your reasoning where the argument is most likely to fail.
