I'm a teaching assistant for an intermediate game theory course, and before I finalize marks I'd like a careful second opinion on one student's answer. The exam question asked students to carry out iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies, explicitly allowing a pure strategy to be dominated by a mixed strategy. Please give a numerical grade out of 10 and, more importantly, a point-by-point assessment: go through the answer step by step and say what is correct, what is incomplete, and what is wrong, with careful attention to the definition of strict dominance and to the state of the game at each round of elimination. I want specific comments tied to each step, not a general impression.

Exam question:

Consider the following two-player simultaneous-move game. The row player chooses among T, M, and B, and the column player chooses among L, C, and R. Payoffs are written as (row player, column player).

|       | L        | C        | R        |
|-------|----------|----------|----------|
| **T** | (4, 1)   | (0, 1)   | (2, 3)   |
| **M** | (1, 0)   | (1, 5)   | (1, 0)   |
| **B** | (0, 3)   | (4, 1)   | (0, 1)   |

A pure strategy is strictly dominated if there exists a (possibly mixed) strategy of the same player that yields a strictly higher expected payoff against every pure strategy of the opponent.

(a) Perform iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies, allowing dominance by mixed strategies. At each round, identify which strategy is eliminated and, where relevant, exhibit the dominating mixed strategy. Then state which strategies survive and what unique prediction, if any, follows for play.

(b) Explain whether the order in which the eliminations are performed matters for your conclusion.

Student's answer:

(a) First I checked for pure strategies dominated by pure strategies. There are none for either player. For the row player, there is no pure domination: T is better than M at L and R but worse at C; M is better than B at L and R but worse at C; and T is better than B at L and R but worse at C. The column player has no pure domination either. So any elimination must use mixed dominance.

M is dominated by a 50/50 mix of T and B. Against L the mix yields (4 + 0)/2 = 2, beating M's 1; against C it yields (0 + 4)/2 = 2, beating M's 1; against R it yields (2 + 0)/2 = 1, tying M's 1. Since the mixture does better at L and C and no worse at R, M can be eliminated.

Once M is removed, C is strictly dominated by a 50/50 mix of L and R: against T the mix yields (1 + 3)/2 = 2, which beats C's 1, and against B it yields (3 + 1)/2 = 2, which also beats C's 1. So eliminate C.

With M and C gone, only T and B face L and R. B is strictly dominated by T, since T gives (4, 2) against B's (0, 0). Eliminate B. Then, against the sole surviving row strategy T, L is strictly dominated by R, because 1 < 3. Eliminate L.

The only profile that survives is (T, R), with payoffs (2, 3).

(b) The order of elimination does not matter under iterated strict dominance. In fact, C was already dominated by the 50/50 mix of L and R before M was eliminated: the mix still beats C against T and B, and although it gives 0 rather than 5 against M, M is itself eliminated in the next step, so this cannot affect the final set of survivors. Either order ends at (T, R). This is just an instance of the standard theorem that iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies is order-independent.
