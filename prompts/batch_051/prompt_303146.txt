I have been trying to pull apart two things that both get called speciesism. The first is a special concern for one's own species simply because it is one's own. The second is a bias toward animals whose suffering is legible to us — animals that cry out, bleed, or make faces in ways we can read — and away from animals whose pain is expressed through signals we do not recognize and often do not notice. In practice the two usually point in the same direction, because our own species is also the easiest to read. But I suspect the second is doing most of the work.

How would one go about testing that suspicion? Design the strongest test you can of whether ordinary moral judgments track species membership as such, rather than cues that merely correlate with it, such as facial expressiveness, vocalization, body size, warm-bloodedness, and overall similarity to humans. A good test would probably need to pry those apart — for instance, by using unfamiliar or invented organisms, or by varying the cues while holding species fixed. Set out the design, say what pattern of results would favor each reading, and then be precise about what even a clean result would leave unsettled, including whether any remaining preference for one's own species should be read as a prejudice or as a defensible partiality.
