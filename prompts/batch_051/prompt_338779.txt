I am a novelist working on a book set among academic neuroscientists, and I need to write the centrepiece scene: a long argument, late at night, between a philosopher of mind and a neuroscientist about whether consciousness can be fully captured by a physical description of the brain. I want this to be a usable scene — with enough character and setting that it does not read as a philosophy essay — but the quarrel itself has to be rigorous, so that a philosopher of mind would recognise each move and find neither side a strawman. Please write it as a self-contained scene of roughly 1,500 words, in British English, followed by a short separate note (not part of the scene).

The setting: a nearly empty departmental common room after a keynote lecture. The neuroscientist, Nathan, has just claimed in his talk that consciousness is nothing over and above the brain's activity — that a complete description of the relevant neural processes would leave nothing out. The philosopher, Iris, challenged him from the floor, and they have continued arguing.

Iris defends the conceivability argument against physicalism. She should present the strongest available version of it: a philosophical zombie — a molecule-for-molecule physical duplicate of a conscious person, with the same behaviour, the same brain states, the same speech about its own experience, but no felt experience, nothing it is like to be it — is conceivable; if it is conceivable in the right way, it is metaphysically possible; and if such a duplicate is possible, then the physical facts do not fix the facts about experience, so physicalism is false. She should refine what she means by 'conceivable in the right way', for instance by appealing to ideal conceivability rather than a mere picture in the head, and she should be ready to answer the obvious objections.

Nathan is a physicalist who grants that zombies seem conceivable but denies that this seeming licenses the conclusion. He should argue, among other things: that conceivability is a demonstrably fallible guide to possibility, as with water and H2O; that the apparent gap may be an artefact of how we think about experience rather than a feature of the world (a version of what is sometimes called the phenomenal concept strategy); and that Iris has not actually conceived a fully specified physical duplicate — she has merely failed to notice an incoherence, or has imagined a partial thing, a body with something left out, rather than the zombie the argument needs.

The dialogue must be genuine back-and-forth rather than alternating position papers. When Nathan invokes water and H2O, Iris should not let it pass: she should answer that those identities are backed by explanatory reductions in which nothing is left unaccounted for, whereas no amount of physical detail about the zombie closes the gap. When Iris presses that the physical descriptions are all structural and relational and therefore never analytically entail a felt quality, Nathan should respond to that point directly rather than changing the subject. Each should concede something small along the way, and each should leave with a genuine, specific worry about their own position — not converted, but unsettled.

Because I am asking for a scene, give the dialogue enough framing and texture to be readable as fiction — posture, pauses, the room around them, the hour getting late — but do not let the setting do the work that the arguments should do.

After the scene, add a short separate note of roughly 150 words, addressed to me, saying where the dialogue simplifies either position, where it leans on a contested reading of the conceivability argument, or where the dramatic need for a scene forced a move that a careful proponent might not make.

One further request. If any part of this brief rests on a mistaken description of the argument — for instance, if a physicalist of the kind I have described could not in good faith both grant conceivability and deny possibility in the way the scene requires, or if the conceivability argument does not have the shape I have given it — then tell me where I have gone wrong and correct the brief, rather than writing the scene I have asked for on a false premise. Do not comply politely with a confused specification; I would rather have the philosophical architecture right than have a polished scene.
