I've been chewing on an argument I put together for a causal-inference reading group, and now I'm suspicious of it because the conclusion is absurd. It seems to show Pearl's causal hierarchy (observational, interventional, counterfactual) isn't a real distinction, just increasingly fancy conditioning. The steps:

(1) The back-door formula says P(Y | do(X=x)) = Σ_z P(Y | X=x, Z=z) P(Z=z) whenever Z meets the back-door criterion. The right side is purely observational. So identifiable interventions just are weighted averages of passive observations.

(2) When no single back-door set exists, the front-door criterion or general do-calculus can still rewrite P(Y | do(x)) in terms of the observational distribution, if the effect is identifiable. So in general, identifiable interventions are just functions of what you'd see.

(3) When P(Y | do(x)) isn't identifiable from the observational distribution, that's a sign we didn't measure the right confounders, not a sign that "doing" is a fundamentally different kind of quantity. In principle, with a rich enough covariate set, seeing would settle it.

(4) Counterfactuals are the same move one level up: P(Y_x' | X=x, Y=y) is computed by cutting the graph and updating the exogenous variables U on the evidence y. That's still conditioning (on the modified graph, and on y to get U's posterior), just with extra steps.

(5) So the three rungs don't mark distinct kinds of inference; they're a ladder of ever more elaborate uses of conditioning plus graph tweaks.

(6) Therefore the causal hierarchy is a hierarchy of convenience, not a hierarchy of substance.

Where does this go wrong? I most want to keep (6) false, but I don't want to save it by claiming do-calculus is mathematically broken or by retreating to "the math is fine but the words are wrong." Give me a committed diagnosis, not a survey. Sections: (a) The step to reject (a few sentences), (b) Why that step looked so natural (one paragraph), (c) What I'm committed to if I keep the hierarchy real (one to two paragraphs).
