Here are the criteria I rely on for saying that a physical object is complex in the assembly-theoretic sense: (1) it takes many steps to produce; (2) later steps build on earlier ones as reusable subassemblies rather than merely repeating a single operation; (3) the object is not a low-probability accident of a single bulk process; and (4) its specific arrangement, not just its ingredients, does the causal or functional work the object does. Now consider a mature multispecies biofilm, such as dental plaque: it contains hundreds of species organized into channels, layers, and gradients; it is produced by billions of cell-division events rather than by any single template or gradient; and its spatial organization matters to its pH control, waste export, and resistance to antibiotics. Yet every step in its construction is the same local rule—a cell divides and the daughters adhere—and no stored blueprint determines where any given cell ends up. Go through my four criteria and say whether the biofilm comes out complex. Where the verdict is unclear, say whether the ambiguity lies in the biofilm or in the criteria, and whether many repeated steps of one type should count differently from fewer steps of many distinct types.
