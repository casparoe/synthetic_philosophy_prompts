Consider the following situation. In a species of social insect or a biofilm-forming bacterium there is a heritable variant—call it the prudent type—that lowers its own bearer's individual reproductive success but raises the survival or productivity of the whole group. It is tempting to conclude that prudent behavior is a group-level adaptation and that group selection is the explanation. But the same observable pattern could be produced by individual-level or gene-level processes: kin-directed effects, pleiotropy, linkage to a gene selected for another reason, or an ecological correlation that makes the trait's group benefit an accidental byproduct.

Design the strongest test you can of the claim that group selection, as a distinct causal process over and above individual-level and gene-level selection, contributed to the spread of the prudent type. Specify what you would measure or manipulate—for instance, group composition, migration, relatedness, or the trait's fitness effects within and between groups. Say what pattern of results would favor the group-selection reading and what pattern would favor the lower-level reading. Then state clearly what even a clean positive result would not establish: in particular, whether it shows that groups are units of selection in the same sense that organisms are, and whether the trait is an adaptation for the group or merely a trait that a multilevel statistical partition happens to describe. Finally, address whether a negative result could ever be decisive, given that past selection pressures may leave no detectable trace in present populations.
