I'm a new member of my city's Rent Board — we're the ones who vote each year on the allowable increase for rent-stabilized apartments — and I've landed on this comission right as we're deciding whether to keep vacancy decontrol, where a landlord can reset a unit to whatever the market will bear the moment a tenant leaves, or to put caps on the unit that follow it from tenant to tenant. The board is split roughly in three. One camp says vacancy decontrol is the only thing keeping small landlords from letting their buildings rot, and that any cap is a price ceiling that would do to housing what the old gas-price ceilings did to supply. Another camp says that's the kind of thing people say when they've never had to keep a kid in the same school, and anyway the actual driver of displacement here is churn; if stability costs a little supply, that's a price worth paying. I keep noticing that some of the same people who treat our city's minimum wage hike as an obvious justice win are the first to call a rent cap a distortion, and I'm not sure wether that's incoherent or whether a price floor on labor really is different in kind from a price ceiling on housing.

I don't just want a vote recommendation. I want the memo to name the question both camps keep dodging: are we defending a price control because it fixes some market failure — monopsony, monopoly, bad information — or because some things, like keeping a roof over your head or a wage you can survive on, shouldn't be settled by whatever clears the market? Those two justifications set very different standards for what counts as a fatal objection. Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before Thursday's vote: recommend a concrete course of action (keep vacancy decontrol, abolish it, or something in between with exemptions or a phase-in), and take the strongest argument on the losing side seriously instead of just restating our side's talking points. And since I'll need to defend this to people with strong priors, tell me what would actually change your mind — which finding or argument would flip the recommendation — and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
