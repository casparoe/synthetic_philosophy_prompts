I hold a view I have been calling the tractability constraint: no account of what we ought to do, believe, or value can be adequate for agents like us if conforming to it would, in general, require solving computationally intractable problems. By intractable I do not mean undecidable; I mean the more familiar situation in which the space of options, hypotheses, or possible distributions is finite but grows so quickly that no physically realizable process could search it within an agent's lifetime. Expected-utility maximization over a realistic action space, or full Bayesian updating over a sufficiently rich hypothesis space, looks to me like a standard that cannot be complied with, and I have come to think this is not merely a practical inconvenience but a reason to reject such standards as statements of what we ought to do. I have started treating this as a fixed point in how I assess consequentialism, Bayesian epistemology, and theories of justice that require comparing enormous numbers of arrangements.

I know the standard replies, and they leave me unmoved: that a criterion of rightness is not a decision procedure; that the principle that ought implies can concerns physical ability rather than computational tractability; that worst-case hardness is compatible with easy typical cases; that approximation and satisficing can preserve whatever is right-making without the full computation; and that a norm can be true even if no one can use it. My remaining unease is that I cannot see what a norm is for, as a norm for creatures like us, if it cannot even in principle guide action.

What would actually count against the tractability constraint as I have stated it? I want the strongest examples of requirements that are plausibly correct and yet demand computations that are intractable for any bounded agent in a way that is not just an artifact of a crude formalization. And I would like an honest assessment of how much the view risks: does it make contact with cases that could refute it, or has it been stated so abstractly that it is insulated from defeat? Where, exactly, should I expect it to fail first, and if its defenders patch it, does it collapse into something trivial like the claim that usable theories are preferable, other things equal, or something false like the claim that hard problems are optional?
