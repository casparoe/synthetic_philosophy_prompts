I'm a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, and my dissertation concerns realism about interpretability: whether the features that interpretability researchers extract from neural networks are things the network genuinely has, or artifacts of the methods used to find them. I've been working with three conditions for a network's genuinely representing a feature. First, an information condition: some internal state must carry recoverable information about the feature. Second, a use condition: the state must be causally implicated in the network's downstream behavior, so that intervening on it makes a difference to what the network does. Third, a robustness condition: the state must be a stable structure of the network rather than something a sufficiently flexible probe can manufacture. Now consider a case I want to use as a test in a paper. A transformer is trained to predict legal moves in a board game, and a linear probe on its residual stream decodes the full board state with high accuracy. But when I ablate the direction the probe identifies, the model's move predictions barely change, and a control probe trained on the same activations to decode a randomly assigned game label that the model was never rewarded for tracking also performs well above chance. Is the board state represented in the residual stream? Work through my three conditions one at a time, say where they underdetermine the answer, and then tell me whether the case shows that one of the conditions is doing all the work or that the conditions need revising. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating the question.
