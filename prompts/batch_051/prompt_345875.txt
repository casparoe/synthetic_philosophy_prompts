I've been chewing on this since a school board meeting I went to with my sister, where the debate over a new health curriculum kept splitting along religious lines. One speaker would say that as a person of faith they couldn't accept it, and a board member would tell them that wasn't a legitimate public reason, and they'd just talk past each other. I get the attraction of the Rawls-ish idea of public reason: when the state coerces people, its justifications should be ones citizens of any religion (or none) could accept, not appeals to a particular revealed truth. But I've never seen anyone think through what happens if you actually enforce that. So here's the counterfactual I'd like you to reason through.

Starting next year, the United States adopts a new constitutional rule: no official may cite a religious or otherwise contested comprehensive doctrine in any official justification for a law, regulation, or court decision. Private motives aren't policed; the rule only governs what can appear in the official record of justification — floor speeches, committee reports, judicial opinions, signing statements, that kind of thing. Nothing else changes. Walk me through the next forty years. Which arguments and coalitions become unsayable in public, and what replaces them? Do religious citizens develop fluent secular translations of the same commitments, do they learn to gesture at the real reason without saying it, or do they defect from the process and fight through other channels? Does the culture get more civil or more cynical, or both in different places? And as you go, keep an eye on whether the secular-translation requirement is a real gain in mutual respect or just a tax on people whose reasons don't fit the official vocabulary. I'm less interested in a single prediction than in the chain of mechanisms, and I'd like you to flag which steps your confident in and which would collapse under different assumptions about how sincere most people really are.
