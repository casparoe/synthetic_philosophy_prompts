I'm a TA marking a second-year ethics exam and I'd like you to mark one script against the set question. The paper was on Kant's Formula of Universal Law. Here's the question as it appeared, then the student's answer. Mark it out of 20 and write the point-by-point comments I would put on the script, so the mark is justified in the margins rather than a bare number. Don't just summarise the essay; tie each comment to a specific claim. Use British English for the comments (recognise, colour, and so on).

Exam question: "Kant distinguishes between a contradiction in conception and a contradiction in will when a maxim is universalised. Explain the distinction, then apply it to (a) 'Whenever telling a lie will get me out of a difficult spot, I will lie' and (b) 'I will never help anyone in need.' For each maxim, state which contradiction, if either, arises, and whether the resulting duty is perfect or imperfect."

Student's answer: "Kant's two tests divide maxims into those that cannot even be conceived as universal laws and those that can be conceived but not willed. In the first case, universalising the maxim makes the act it describes impossible or self-defeating because the practice it relies on is destroyed; that is contradiction in conception. In the second, the universalised world is logically possible, but a rational will cannot will it because it conflicts with something the will is committed to. So contradiction in conception grounds strict duties, whereas contradiction in will grounds duties that depend more on what the agent in question happens to prefer.

For maxim (a), if everyone lied whenever it got them out of difficulty, nobody would believe anyone, so the lie wouldn't achieve its purpose. That's a contradiction in conception. Lying to get out of a tough spot is therefore forbidden absolutely, and the duty not to lie is perfect.

For maxim (b), a world where no one ever helped anyone in need is grim but I can conceive of it without contradiction. The trouble is contradiction in will: a rational will cannot will such a world as a universal law, because its own ends could be frustrated when it needs the help of others. So the maxim fails the test, and there is a perfect duty of beneficence: each of us is strictly required to help those in need as much as we can."
