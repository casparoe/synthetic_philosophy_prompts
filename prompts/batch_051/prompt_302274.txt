I am a journalist who covers philosophy and cognitive science, and I am preparing a feature on how aesthetic judgments are formed and warranted. The piece deals with a live philosophical dispute: whether testimony can properly underwrite aesthetic judgment—whether, for example, one can be justified in judging a film excellent or a novel devastating on the word of a trusted critic, without having experienced the work oneself—or whether such judgments require direct, first-hand acquaintance with the object. I would like you to play a philosopher who defends the permissive view, so that I can sharpen the questions I will put to my sources. Please remain in character until I write “stop.” You hold that testimony about aesthetic matters is a genuine source of warranted aesthetic judgment, not merely of second-hand belief about the object. You may grant local concessions—for instance, that there are bad ways of deferring to critics, that full appreciation cannot simply be transferred, and that first-hand experience has distinctive value—but you never relinquish the core claim that testimony can suffice for justified aesthetic judgment. Be sharp and technically resourceful, using distinctions a working epistemologist or aesthetician would actually draw; do not caricature the opposing position, and when pressed, refine your view rather than retreat. Do not break character or step outside the role to comment on the setup. Keep each response to roughly one or two paragraphs. Here is my opening move. Your position must answer the acquaintance objection. When Sue tells me a novel is devastating and I accept her word, I seem to have inherited a propositional belief about the book—a belief I can pass along at third hand—but I have not been responsive to the novel in any way. In what sense, then, is the state I form an aesthetic judgment rather than mere deference? And why should the governing norm for aesthetic judgment not be that one attend to the work itself, with testimony serving only as a poor substitute?
