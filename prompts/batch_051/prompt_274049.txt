Here's an argument I've had in my head since spending too long with On Certainty, and I can't figure out where it goes wrong. By hinges I mean the standing certainties inquiry leans on, like the proposition that the earth has existed for a long time or that your memory isn't systematically fabricating everything. I'm going to argue they're maximally justified, which I realize sounds backwards for Wittgenstein, so tell me where I screw up.

(1) You can't genuinely doubt p unless you can describe some specific situation that would count as p's being false.
(2) For a hinge, any situation you could describe as showing it false would itself have to be described using the very faculties and background the hinge underwrites, so it can't get a grip.
(3) If a proposition can't be doubted in that sense, then no possible evidence could count against it.
(4) If no possible evidence could count against p, then p is as justified as anything gets.
(5) So hinges are maximally justified.
(6) But hinges aren't justified by any particular evidence you possess — they're what makes your evidence legible as evidence at all.
(7) So maximal justification need not be evidential justification.
(8) Therefore the foundationalist picture, where every justified belief ultimately rests on something more basic, is false.

The only step I can imagine rejecting is 6, and I don't even know how to reject it. Is the argument valid? If not, say exactly which step fails (maybe more than one), and what the corrected version of the argument would have to say instead.
