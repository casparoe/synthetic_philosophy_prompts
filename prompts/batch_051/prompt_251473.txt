I keep trying to figure out whether the lying/misleading distinction is doing real moral work or whether it’s just a thing people say because lying feels bolder. Heres my argument that there isn’t really an intrinsic difference. Can you go step by step and tell me if it’s valid, and if not, which exact step is the first one to fail?

(1) Lying is asserting a proposition you believe to be false.

(2) Misleading is intentionally causing someone to form or retain a false belief through a communication, whether by what you say or what you leave pragmatically implied.

(3) The moral status of a communicative act depends on two things: what the speaker intends the hearer to believe, and the reasonably foreseeable consequences for the hearer’s practical reasoning.

(4) For any lie, we can construct a misleading case with the same intention and the same foreseeable consequences: the speaker says something strictly true but chosen to produce the same false belief.

(5) The only thing that distinguishes such a lie from such an act of misleading is which sentence is used—i.e., whether the false proposition is asserted or merely implicated—and that difference by itself has no moral weight.

(6) So lying and misleading are morally equivalent when intention and expected consequences are held fixed; the distinction is at most a stylistic or etiquette-norm thing.

I half-worry there’s a deontological move I’m missing where assertion carries a special warranty or promise, but that move feels like it’s exactly what (5) is supposed to debunk. Does the argument go through, or is one of the steps bad?
