You are a philosopher of mind and cognitive science. I want you to work through a puzzle about an actually existing practice, and to do so explicitly rather than jumping to a conclusion. Begin by brainstorming two or three plausible ways of interpreting the practice before settling on the one you think is most defensible.

Here is the practice. In commercial aviation, pilots are required to use printed or electronic checklists for nearly every procedure, from routine engine starts to abnormal and emergency drills. The same regulatory and training regime also designates a small number of procedures as "memory items": steps that must be executed immediately from memory, without consulting any checklist, even though a checklist may be physically present in the cockpit. A pilot who performs an ordinary procedure from memory instead of reading the checklist is treated as having committed an error, while a pilot who reaches for a checklist during a memory-item procedure is treated as failing a test or evaluation. So the same institution, in the same cockpit, sometimes treats external written guidance as the required site of the procedure and sometimes treats consulting it as disqualifying.

What theory of the mind, and of where cognitive competence resides, does this dual practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view as precisely as you can. In particular: does the practice treat the checklist as literally part of the pilot's cognitive process, or only as a scaffold, substitute, or memory aid for a process whose real location is inside the pilot's head? Does it treat this the same way in the memory-item case and the ordinary procedure case, or does it quietly switch between two views?

Then evaluate whether the practice is coherent. Some features of the practice may follow from operational constraints—time pressure, error rates, the unreliability of memory under stress—rather than from any real commitment about where cognition is located. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with, and identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, such that several different theories of mind could license it. Bring the extended mind thesis to bear: does the thesis predict or explain the memory-item/checklist split, does it sit uneasily with it, or does it show that the split rests on a false boundary between internal and external cognition?

Finally, tell me what would concretely have to change in pilot training, certification, and error analysis if we replaced the presupposed view with the position that cognitive processes do not stop at the skull, and that an agent's competence can be partly constituted by reliably coupled external resources. Do not produce a reading list or cite sources; reason from the practice as described.
