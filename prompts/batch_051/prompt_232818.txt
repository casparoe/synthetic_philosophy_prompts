I'm taking a philosophy of AI course this term, and one of our units is on whether an AI system can interpret another AI system — not merely predict it, but say what its internal representations mean in something like the way a cognitive scientist attributes representational states to a subject. The papers we read describe a now-familiar setup: researchers train a separate explainer model, or use the target model itself, to produce natural-language descriptions of a component's function, and they score an interpretation by how well it lets them predict or retrodict the component's activations on held-out inputs. The strongest versions of this program make a striking leap: a sufficiently good explanation is said to recover the features the network actually uses, not just a compressed redescription of its input–output mapping. My worry is that this is a conceptual claim being treated as though it were just another engineering benchmark.

I would like a way to bring evidence to bear on that leap. Suppose we have two interpretation systems for the same target model: one is highly predictive of the component's behavior but arguably only a finely tuned behavioral summary; the other is less predictively clean yet claims to track the component's real representational role. Design a concrete test that could distinguish these two systems, stating what result would favor each reading. Then be explicit about what a positive result would and would not settle: if the would-be "representational" interpreter passes the test, does that establish that the AI is genuinely interpreting the other system, or only that it has cleared one more behavioral hurdle? And is the leftover gap simply a limitation of current methods, or does it indicate that the phrase "really represents" does not pick out a property any test could confirm?
