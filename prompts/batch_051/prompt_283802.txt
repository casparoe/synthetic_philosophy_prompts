I am a PhD student working on the governance of emerging technologies, and I would like help thinking through a decision that has come up in an informal working group I belong to. The group spans several labs and has no formal decision procedure. We have all been studying the same finding: a straightforward method for re-identifying people in supposedly anonymized public datasets. Any one of us who has the technical details could release them on their own initiative, and a single release would be enough to make the method widely available. Some members argue that release would let journalists and civil-society groups demonstrate how weak current anonymization practices are; others of us, myself included, think our best estimate is that release would do more harm than good.

What unsettles me is the reasoning I catch myself and others using. Each of us has formed a private judgment. My own is that release is probably harmful in expectation, but I keep telling myself that if the method were truly as dangerous as I sometimes fear, one of the more experienced or cautious members would simply refuse to release it, and their refusal would protect all of us. I am treating the group's caution as an implicit veto on my action. But that cannot be right: no member can veto another, and one publication is all it would take. Several members, talking informally, describe the same pattern: each of us is hesitant in our own case but reassured by the thought that the group will somehow stop a genuinely bad release. The practical result is that several people, myself perhaps included, are now leaning toward releasing, not because we have become convinced it is wise, but because we trust the rest of the group to prevent a mistake.

Something about this feels structurally wrong, and I would like to know exactly what the error is. How should I reason about whether I, individually, should release the method? Is the deference-to-the-group line a mistake, and if so, what kind of mistake is it? What decision rule should a responsible person in this position adopt, and does the fact that anyone can act unilaterally change the threshold at which one ought to act? I am more interested in an analysis of the structure of the situation than in generic cautionary advice.

Please also say how confident you are in your analysis, and single out the point you are least sure of.
