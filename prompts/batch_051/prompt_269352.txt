I am a novelist, not a decision theorist, and I am drafting a scene in which a character must first choose between a certain legacy and a gamble, and later choose between two further gambles. The scene is built out of the Allais paradox, but I have altered one feature, and I want to know whether the standard analysis survives the alteration.

In the canonical design I have read about, the first choice is between A: one million dollars for certain, and B: an eighty-nine percent chance of one million, a ten percent chance of five million, and a one percent chance of nothing. The second choice is between C: an eleven percent chance of one million and an eighty-nine percent chance of nothing, and D: a ten percent chance of five million and a ninety percent chance of nothing. People often prefer A to B and yet prefer D to C. Expected-utility theory rules this pair of preferences inconsistent, because under the independence axiom the two choices reduce to the same core comparison and should be ordered the same way.

For my character, the nothing outcome is replaced by ruin: a fixed loss of size X, written as a payoff of minus X dollars. My first choice is between A: one million for certain, and B: an eighty-nine percent chance of one million, a ten percent chance of five million, and a one percent chance of minus X. My second choice is between C: an eleven percent chance of one million and an eighty-nine percent chance of minus X, and D: a ten percent chance of five million and a ninety percent chance of minus X.

I would like your analysis of two things. First, the formal question: under expected-utility theory, does a strict preference for A over B still force a strict preference for C over D after this replacement, for every size of X, or is there some threshold in X at which the argument begins to strain? Please show the expected-utility calculation so I can see whether the independence axiom survives intact. Second, the descriptive and normative question: would the usual Allais pattern of preferring A to B and then D to C become more likely, less likely, or roughly as likely once the common bad branch is a large loss rather than nothing? And is the pattern easier or harder to justify as rational once ruin is in play?

Finally, I would also like you to be self-critical: say which argument or consideration would most likely change your verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
