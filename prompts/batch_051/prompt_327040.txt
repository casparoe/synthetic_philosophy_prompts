In a reading group a friend of mine keeps reaching for the same analogy whenever we argue about whether the UN should have real enforcement powers. The analogy is that states in the international system are like individual people in a state of nature. Just as individuals — for Hobbes, anyway — are in a war of all against all until they hand over some of their freedom to a sovereign, states are stuck in anarchy where each judges its own cause, so the obvious fix is the same one: states should give up some sovereignty to a common authority, maybe a world government or at least something a lot stronger than the Security Council. His conclusion is that anyone who wants perpetual peace but rejects world government is being inconsistent.

I have a vague sense that Kant does something strange with exactly this analogy in his little book on perpetual peace — that he runs it and then stops short, recommending only a voluntary federation or league of states rather than a coercive world state. But I cant reconstruct the argument from memory, and I don't want a reading list or citations, I want the actual audit.

So: how far does the analogy hold? Lay out the correspondence table — what plays the role of the individual, the state of nature, the sovereign, the transfer of rights, the contract — and then the places where it breaks or quietly imports something from the domestic case that doesn't carry over. I especially want to know whether Kant's stopping point is principled or whether it's just him wanting peace while refusing the means that would actually deliver it. If the analogy really is doing work, does it ultimately support my friend's conclusion (world government) or Kant's (a federation without coercive enforcement), and which disanalogies matter for that difference and which are just noise? If the analogy collapses for the intended use, tell me whether there's a better one and what it is.
