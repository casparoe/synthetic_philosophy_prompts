I've been mulling over a blog post a friend sent me about Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals. It felt watertight while I read it, but since then I've got a nagging sense that one or two of the moves overreach, and I don't trust my own ear for the formal bits enough to say exactly where. Here's a condensed version of what the post claimed:

"Lewis starts from the Equation: the probability of an indicative conditional like 'If it rains, the match will be cancelled' is just the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent. Using that, plus the standard idea that a class of probability functions is closed under conditionalising, he showed that the probability of the conditional collapses to the probability of the consequent on its own. So 'If it rains, the match will be cancelled' and 'If there is a plague of locusts, the match will be cancelled' have to get the same probability, which is absurd. Lewis's own conclusion was therefore that indicative conditionals don't express propositions with truth conditions at all, which is why the ordinary probability axioms simply don't apply to them. A common objection is that this just shows the Equation was too strong, but that misses the point: Lewis's proof only needs the Equation to hold for a family of probability functions closed under conditionalising, so the triviality is robust. If you insist on keeping truth conditions for conditionals, the only option left is to treat 'if' as the material conditional, because its probability automatically coincides with the conditional probability."

I find the line about the material conditional especially suspicious, but I can't articulate why.
