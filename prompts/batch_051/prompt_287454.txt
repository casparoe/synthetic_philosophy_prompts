I'm applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship and need a two-page proposal. My half-formed idea concerns the expected value of information and the cases where information seems to harm the person who receives it. Good's theorem says that for a single expected-utility maximizer, costless information whose receipt doesn't change the utility function can never have negative expected value: before you see the evidence, the expected advantage of deciding after seeing it is at least zero. But there remain uncomfortable cases — ex post regret, strategic settings in which the fact that one player knows changes what others do and can leave the better-informed player worse off, and situations where learning changes the learner's aims rather than merely her beliefs. I suspect these cases aren't all failures of the same assumption, and that the literature runs together two senses of the value of information: a choice-theoretic sense (does the selected action rank higher in expected utility?) and a welfare sense (does the informed person's situation go better by her own lights?). Turn this into a proposal with a precise, determinate question; a diagnosis of why existing positions don't settle it; the method you would actually carry out month by month, including where the project is philosophical and where it needs to establish one or two formal results rather than survey them; and a specification of what would count as success and what would count as failure. Don't include a bibliography or ask me to supply one; I'll add citations myself. Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this topic, and don't present a contested view — for example, that information can rationally have negative ex ante value in strategic settings — as the consensus.
