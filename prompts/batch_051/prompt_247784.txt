I've been turning over an asymmetry in the law and in ordinary judgment, and I can't tell whether it's a principled line or a collective pretence.

Take three things, each of which seems fine on its own: (1) telling people an embarassing but true fact about someone — publishing an affair, say; (2) asking someone for money; (3) threatening to do something you're legally allowed to do. Now combine them: 'give me ten grand or I'll publish the affair.' Suddenly it's blackmail, a serious criminal offence. The only new element is the combination itself, the conditional structure. That's odd, because we don't normally think conjoining two permissible acts makes an impermissible one: if it's fine to eat lunch and fine to walk to the park, it's fine to eat lunch while walking to the park.

The asymmetry gets stranger the closer you look. If I simply publish the affair, I might be a journalist or a gossip, but I'm not a criminal. If I ask for money (no threat attached), get refused, and then publish anyway, I'm still not a blackmailer — just unpleasant. But if I link the two, and offer to withhold the disclosure for a price, I'm a criminal. So the very same publication is lawful or criminal depending on whether I offered to sell my silence beforehand.

And we allow what looks like the same transaction in other clothes. In a settlement, one side can pay the other for a confidentiality clause — effectively buying their silence — and nobody calls that blackmail. A solicitor can write 'my client will sue unless we settle', a conditional threat to do something lawful unless paid. So why is 'pay me or I'll sue' a settlement letter but 'pay me or I'll publish' a crime? Is the difference that suing vindicates a right and publishing an affair doesn't? But I have a right to publish, and a right to ask for money. Or is it that the blackmailer would rather not publish anyway, while the claimant would genuinely rather sue? That seems to rest a lot on reading the threatener's private preferences.

I want the strongest principle you can find that justifies drawing the line where the law draws it, and then an honest verdict on whether it survives the confidentiality-clause case and the strike case ('pay us more or we walk out'). If it doesn't survive them, tell me which way to move: drop the blackmail offence, or extend it to settlements and strikes?
