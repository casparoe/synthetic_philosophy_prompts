I keep getting pulled into the same argument at every formal epistemology event I go to, and this time it actually made me want to pin a number on it. The question is how much Aumann's "no agreeing to disagree" result really explains persistent disagreement in the wild — politics, philosophy, even stalled scientific controversys. A colleague of mine takes the standard line: if two truth-seeking agents are Bayesian, share a common prior, and have common knowledge of each other's posterior probabilities for some event, their posteriors cannot differ; so if two sincere, well-informed people keep disagreeing after full disclosure, the interesting difference between them must be their priors. My suspicion is that the common-prior assumption is doing nearly all the philosophical work, and that the other modeling choices — common knowledge of rationality, the event-and-partition setup, treating people as Bayesian in the first place — are either just as load-bearing or are the places where real people fail first.

So here's what I want from you. Give me your actual credence in the following claim: in realistic cases of persistent disagreement between two sincere, roughly rational, well-informed people, the best explanation, once you idealize them along Aumann's lines, is a difference in priors rather than a difference in information or a failure of the theorem's other assumptions. And I want the number decomposed, not just asserted. In particular, I'd like to know how much of the credence rides on wether the common-prior assumption is the fragile one, how much on whether "prior" in the theorem means anything like "prior" in the ordinary story we tell about why people differ, how much on whether what actually fails in real disagreements is the common-knowledge or partition modeling rather than the priors, and which single sub-question would move your credence the most if you got a sharp answer to it. I'm also aware there are results in the formal literature where simply having agents announce their posteriors back and forth can induce agreement without common priors, which might mean the standard gloss overstates what common priors are doing — say how much weight that puts on the scale, if any. And do flag where you're giving me the settled core of the result versus where formal epistemologists still genuinely disagree, because I don't want a contested reading presented as the consensus.
