Act as a professional philosopher working in practical ethics, and think carefully before committing to an answer. Last night a friend and I argued for hours and kept circling an asymmetry we could not resolve. Most people would say that a hiker who walks past an injured rabbit without helping is callous, and that a homeowner who leaves a wounded stray cat to die on his porch has done something wrong. Yet almost no one thinks there is any comparable obligation to prevent the far larger amount of suffering that occurs among wild animals without human involvement: the deer dying slowly of infection, the bird starving through a late frost, the zebra pulled down and partly eaten while still alive. We seem to treat the small, present, chance rescue as obligatory and the large, ongoing, systematic suffering as outside the sphere of duty. My friend and I tried to ground this in naturalness, the doing/allowing distinction, proximity, and the risk of ecological damage from intervention, but each justification buckled when we imagined that the wild suffering is something we could prevent cheaply, locally, and with little risk — a vaccinated colony, a fed herd in an unusually harsh winter. Brainstorm the strongest principle that would actually generate both of the ordinary judgments, test it hard against the cheap, local, low-risk cases, and say whether the asymmetry should be kept, revised, or abandoned.
