For a civics project I have been looking at the international rankings that compare countries by self-reported happiness, and I am trying to pin down when a country's high score reflects real happiness rather than just a style of answering. My working criteria for the survey to count as measuring happiness are: (1) the question means roughly the same thing across languages and cultures; (2) the answer reveals a stable condition of a person's life, not a fleeting mood or a wish to look good; (3) the person is in a position to judge their life against genuine alternatives; and (4) the score can be improved only by changes that make life actually better, not by changes that make people answer the question differently. Now take this borderline case: a country consistently ranks first on a survey that asks people to place their lives on a ladder from 0 to 10. Its culture, reinforced by schools and public media, prizes contentment above striving. People are taught from childhood that wanting things brings misery, they rarely complain because complaining is seen as ungrateful, and they regard their country as a happy one and would be ashamed to give a low rating. Their answers are sincere and stable, and no one is threatened into answering. Does the survey, as answered in this country, measure happiness by my four criteria? Go through the criteria one by one and say where they leave the matter unsettled; then say whether the case shows that one of the criteria needs revision, and whether it weakens the case for a government trying to raise its country's ranking. Keep your answer under 400 words.
