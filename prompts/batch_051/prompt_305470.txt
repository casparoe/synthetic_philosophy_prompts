Two people in my social choice seminar got into it about Arrow's impossibility theorem. I'll call them Dana and Max.

(1) Dana said she had a counterexample. Her rule is: for any pair of alternatives x and y, if x Pareto-dominates y (everyone weakly prefers x to y and at least one person strictly does), rank x above y. If neither Pareto-dominates the other, rank them by voter 1's strict preference. If voter 1 is indifferent between them, declare x and y socially indifferent. Dana says this rule shows Arrow's conditions are consistent: it accepts any profile of individual preference orderings, it satisfies independence of irrelevant alternatives because it only looks at pairwise x-versus-y rankings, it's Paretian by construction, and it isn't dictatorial, since Pareto could override voter 1 whenever everyone else agrees against him.

(2) Max said that can't be right, because Arrow's theorem rules out any such rule. He thought the catch is that the social ranking won't always be transitive—specifically in some profile where voter 1 is indifferent and the others form a kind of cycle.

(3) Dana came back: when voter 1 has strict preferences over everything, the rule just copies voter 1's ranking, so it's transitive there. So transitivity isn't obviously the issue. She also said the tie-break only does anything when voter 1 is strict, so the real question is whether non-dictatorship fails—and she didn't see how it could, since the Pareto clause can rank one alternative over another even when voter 1 is indifferent.

(4) Max said the non-dictatorship claim is where it breaks. If voter 1 strictly prefers x to y while everyone else strictly prefers y to x, then neither alternative Pareto-dominates the other, so the rule hands it to voter 1. That makes voter 1 a dictator. Dana replied that being a dictator requires that voter 1's strict preference always decide the social ranking, and she thought there could be a profile where voter 1 prefers x to y but the Pareto clause puts y above x. Max said: impossible, because the only way Pareto could put y above x is if voter 1 himself weakly prefers y to x.

Please adjudicate move by move. For each numbered move, say which claims are right and which are wrong, and why. In particular: does the rule actually satisfy unrestricted domain, Pareto, and independence of irrelevant alternatives? Is voter 1 a dictator? Does the rule always produce a transitive social ordering? If transitivity fails, give the smallest profile you can that exposes the failure. Write the answer in American English.
