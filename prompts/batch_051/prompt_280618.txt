I'm a reporter covering science and ideas, and I've been poking at a research institute that just adopted a governance rule I cant stop thinking about. The rule: every collective decision—who gets a fellowship, who gets promoted, which projects get another year—is settled by anonymous vote. But before the decision is announced, the institute's full-time administrator, who didn't vote and isn't a researcher, writes a public "rationale for the decision." The rationale has to present the outcome as following cleanly from the institute's published principles; it can't mention the margin, and it can't say the committee was split or confused. Nobody has to sign it or gets to edit it, and members are expected not to publicly dispute a rationale after it goes up, since doing so would partly reveal their own vote. The rationales go into a permanent file, and in later meetings people cite earlier rationales as if they were precedent. The director says the point is to give the institute a single coherent mind, because the actual committee is a mess. I want to write a piece about what this rule actually does. How would it change what people bother to argue in meetings, how the administrator writes as the file grows, and how future votes go? Does the file eventually become the real decision procedure even though it started as window dressing? What are the gaming strategies and failure modes—what happens the first time a rationale conflicts with what everyone privately remembers—and which ones can be fixed without gutting the whole scheme?
