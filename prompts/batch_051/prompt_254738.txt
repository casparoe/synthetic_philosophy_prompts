The way Aristotle reports it in the Physics, roughly: before you can cover the whole distance you have to cover half of it, and before that half of it, and so on without end, so you can never even get started; the usual everyday answer is "but I just walked across the room," which feels like it misses the point. I want to get clearer on what would actually count as meeting the argument rather than dodging it.

So my question is: what are the desiderata for a genuine resolution of the dichotomy? That is, what any adequate response has to do—what the argument's moving parts actually force you to address, what constraints from the original set-up can't be quietly dropped, what distinctions a solution must respect (traversing a segment vs. completing a supertask, potential vs. actual divisibility, mathematical modeling vs. physical motion, the sequence of tasks vs. the limit of the sequence). For each desideratum, give the case or consideration behind it, and say which ones are negotiable and which would make the "solution" just a restatement of the conclusion that motion happens. Then, using that list, rule on a borderline candidate: the standard calculus-style response that an infinite series with terms 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, ... has a finite sum, so the infinite divisions don't add up to an infinite distance or time. Is that a solution to Zeno's dichotomy, or a precise way of describing one half of the puzzle while leaving the other untouched? End with a one-sentence summary line in the format \answer{...} giving your overall verdict on the calculus-style response (e.g., "genuine resolution," "partial resolution," "not a resolution," or a short label of your own).
