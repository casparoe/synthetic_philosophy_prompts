Consider the following argument, offered as a reply to Max Black's two-sphere thought experiment. Black described a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres of exactly the same size, composition, temperature, and color, at a fixed distance from each other in otherwise empty, symmetric space; he took it to show that the identity of indiscernibles can fail, because we have two things with all the same qualitative features.

(1) In Black's world, each sphere has every purely qualitative property the other has: each is iron, each is spherical, each is one mile from an iron sphere, and so on.

(2) Therefore each sphere has every property the other has.

(3) If two things are numerically distinct, then at least one of them has a property the other lacks. (This is the identity of indiscernibles.)

(4) So the two spheres are not numerically distinct; they are one and the same sphere.

(5) But the scenario says there are two spheres, and one sphere cannot be one mile from itself or occupy two places at once.

(6) Hence Black's world is impossible: the phrase 'two qualitatively identical spheres' is self-contradictory.

I suspect the argument trades on an ambiguity in 'property' — in one breath meaning purely qualitative features expressible in general terms, and in another breath meaning any feature whatsoever, including identity-involving ones such as 'being this very sphere.' But I want a precise verdict, not just a gesture at that ambiguity. Is the argument valid under any single reading of 'property'? If so, is it sound? If not, state exactly which step fails under which reading, and say what the right conclusion about Black's thought experiment is. Commit to one diagnosis and defend it.
