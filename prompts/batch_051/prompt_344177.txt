I'm teaching Locke's Second Treatise to undergrads next week and want a handout that sorts them (and me) into exegetical camps on the labour theory without just asking "which reading do you like?" So: build a decision tree/taxonomy of the main interpretive positions on what Locke thinks makes an original acquisition legitimate, anchored on this passage from ch. V, sec. 28 — the part people usually skip because they're still thinking about the acorns:

"Thus the grass my horse has bit; the turfs my servant has cut; and the ore I have digged in any place, where I have a right to them in common with others, become my property, without the assignation or consent of any body. The labour that was mine, removing them out of that common state they were in, hath fixed my property in them."

The tree should be built from the logical space first, not from a list of famous names. Each branch should be a question a student could answer from the text or from small cases: does the labour need to be your own (so what do we do with the servant cutting the turfs?), is the "enough and as good left in common" clause a necessary condition on each acquisition or just a standing description of the pre-money world, does mixing labour ground the right or merely mark what's been taken from the common stock, does the spoilage limit bind after money exists, and would consent of the commoners be needed if the labour argument failed? Leaves should be the positions themselves — strict-proviso Lockean, labour-desert reading, consent-based reading, whatever else the space gives you — with maybe six or seven total. Flag any pairs of leaves the tree can't seperate, and mark the empty combinations with a one-line guess at why nobody sits there. Keep it to about a page.
