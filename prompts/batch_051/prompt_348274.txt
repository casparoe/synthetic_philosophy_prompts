I've been teaching the Church-Turing thesis and have worked myself into a corner over a single case; I'd like you to help me think it through in an organized way.

Let me fix a working list of the standard marks of an effective procedure, the kind of procedure the Church-Turing thesis takes as its target. An effective procedure is: (1) specified by a finite set of exact instructions; (2) executable without insight, intuition, or ingenuity—nothing but mechanical rule-following; (3) deterministic, so each step is fully determined by the current state of the execution together with the instruction, and the same input always leads to the same output; (4) defined over discrete, finitely representable symbols; and (5) guaranteed to reach its output after finitely many steps for any defined input. In consequence, (6) a patient clerk with enough paper, time, and care could carry it out and reproduce the very same result.

Now consider this case. An engineer builds a device whose elementary operations include drawing a fresh bit from a genuinely indeterministic physical process—say, a photon striking a half-silvered mirror—and writing 0 or 1 according to the outcome. The device runs a fixed finite program, requires no judgment from its operator, and is configured to decide, for any numeral given as input, whether the denoted number is prime. For each input it halts in bounded time and prints "prime" or "composite"; it may occasionally print the wrong answer, but it is guaranteed to print the right one with probability at least 2/3 on every input. Because the fresh bits are physically random, two runs on the same input can take different computational paths and can even end in different printed answers. A deterministic clerk, or an ordinary Turing machine, cannot reproduce a particular run unless it is supplied with a transcript of the exact random outcomes in advance.

Does this device execute an effective procedure? Go through the criteria one at a time and say where the case fits, where it plainly fails, and where a criterion itself is doing more contested work than it first appears (I suspect criterion (3) is the flashpoint, but I want each criterion checked on the merits). Then reach a verdict and say whether the case shows the standard criteria are a stipulative definition, a corrigible idealization, or an empirical thesis in disguise. In particular: ought the criteria be amended so that a procedure with this kind of randomness counts as effective, or should we say instead that the device computes no single-argument function—only a deterministic function once the random bit-string is treated as a second argument—and which of those descriptions leaves the Church-Turing thesis untouched, and which one puts pressure on it?
