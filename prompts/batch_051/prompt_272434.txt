I'm trying to figure out where I actually draw the analytic–synthetic line, because Quine's arguments make me suspect I don't have a consistent one. Here are my gut verdicts on a pile of sentences. 'All bachelors are unmarried' and 'all vixens are female foxes': analytic, true just in virtue of the words. 'Copper conducts electricity' and 'water freezes at zero Celsius under normal conditions': synthetic, learned from the world. 'Hesperus is Phosphorus': synthetic — the identity had to be discovered — though I also want to say it couldn't have been false, which already bothers me. 'Water is H2O': synthetic, but it feels different from 'copper conducts electricity': H2O feels like what water is, not just something true of it. 'Gold is the element with atomic number 79': I want to call this analytic now — I'd refuse to call a non-79 substance gold — even though the same sort of discovery got it from the world. 'Nothing is red all over and green all over at the same time': I lean analytic, but that might just be a failure of imagination. 'Cats are animals': analytic, though I can imagine zoology reclassifying things in a way that makes it shakier than 'vixens are female foxes'. And in a twin world with a liquid that looks, tastes, and behaves like water but has a different microstructure, I want to say its not water, which nudges the water case toward the gold case and away from the copper case. Is there one account of analyticity that captures most of this? Which of my verdicts would it force me to drop, and does what survives hold up as a real distinction or go the way Quine predicted? Please also run whatever account you settle on on a case I'm still stuck on: 'a straight line is the shortest path between two points.'
