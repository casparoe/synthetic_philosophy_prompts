Someone in my reading group keeps bringing up that sphex wasp thing to settle the free will debate, and I can't tell if it's actually doing any work or whether it's just a good story. You know the one: the wasp stings a cricket, drags it to the mouth of the burrow, leaves it at the threshold, goes inside to check the tunnel, comes back out, drags the cricket in. If you move the cricket a couple inches away while shes inside checking, she comes out, drags it back to the threshold, and then goes inside to check all over again — and she'll do this forever if you keep moving it. She never seems to notice that the tunnel's already checked and the cricket just needs to be brought in.

His argument is: the wasp is a mechanism because it can't reorder or skip a step when the task changes. We can. When we see a step in a plan has become pointless we skip it. So the real difference between us and the wasp is flexibility — revising a routine on the fly — and that flexibility, not some spooky contra-causal power, is what free will actually is. So he says compatibilism is basically right and the libertarian demand for an uncaused cause is a mistake.

I want to audit the analogy properly: what maps onto what in the wasp–human comparison, where it breaks, what the wasp story quietly imports about what we're doing when we act flexibly, and whether his conclusion — free will is just flexibility — survives the disanalogies. Does the wasp even show what he says it shows? And if the analogy is bad, is there a better one for the same point, or does the point die with the wasp?
