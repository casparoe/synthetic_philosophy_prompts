I teach political philosophy, and over the last few years Ive drifted into a firm rule when I read work on frontier AI policy. If an author is currently employed by one of the labs actually building the largest models, I heavily discount whatever they argue about compute governance, and especially about the need to pause or license training runs. A colleague says this is ad hominem with nicer clothes, since insiders are the people who know what a ten-to-the-twenty-six FLOP run can do and often have the most detailed safety case. But my experience is that the 'please regulate us' move coming from inside a lab is entangled with the fact that a pause or a licensing regime is also a competitive move: it raises everyone's costs, but it raises your rivals' costs more, and it can lock in your lead while you look public-spirited. The technical arguments also shift every quarter, so I can't evaluate most of them directly. Last week I caught myself dismissing out of hand a report by two lab researchers who argued that a voluntary moratorium above a specific compute threshold would be in every lab's interest, and I honestly couldn't tell whether I was being sensible or just lazy and suspicious.

So I'm asking whether the shortcut is defensible. Under what assumptions about motives, coordination, and access to evidence would one careful insider's 'we should pause' claim outweigh the worry that they are building a regulatory moat out of their safety credentials? And is there a better version of the rule that keeps whatever is good in it without the bias, or should someone who can't audit the technical case just decline to have a view on compute governance at all?
