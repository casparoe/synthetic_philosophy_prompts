A year ago I'd have rolled my eyes at this, but after reading a bit of Wang Yangming for a book group I now can't stop thinking about the unity of knowledge and action (zhixing heyi). I got into a long-running argument with my brother about it: he says knowledge and action are obviously two seperate things — you can know perfectly well that you should apologise to someone, or quit a job, or get up early to visit your nan, and still not do it. I'm now the one defending the unity view. We're not scholars, but we've gone enough rounds that we're finally trying to write down what we'd both sign.

Write the document we should produce: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the unity-of-knowledge-and-action camp and the knowledge-and-action-are-distinct camp. It should have:

(1) a numbered list of agreed claims both camps can sign, phrased so neither side is quietly smuggling in its conclusion — include some claims about self-deception, about a person's actions being evidence of what they actually hold, and about the difference between merely saying a thing and genuinely knowing it;

(2) the residual disagreement, first in neutral wording, then as a table with each side's prefered formulation in its own column, so each side would accept the wording of its own view — the unity side will say something like: only full, action-involving knowledge counts as genuine knowledge, so where the action is absent, the knowledge was never fully there; the distinct side will say: a complete and sincere judgement that one should act can be present while the corresponding action is still absent;

(3) the cruxes — the sub-questions that would actually move one of you if settled. I suspect one crux is whether clear-eyed akrasia (knowing the better, doing the worse, with full awareness at the time) is possible, and another is whether 'he didn't really know' is a substantive explanation or just a re-labelling of the fact that he didn't act;

(4) for each crux, at least one concrete case both camps agree bears on it — I want the smoker who can spell out everything about why he should quit and relapses anyway, and the manager who reports misconduct in every situation except the one where it costs her — and for each case, what each camp predicts about how the other should describe it, written down in advance.

And please don't let the whole thing get settled by 'it depends what you mean by knowledge' — if part of the argument does turn out to be merely verbal, say so explicitly and then keep going with the part that isn't.

Keep it under about 1,200 words, and could you write the whole thing in British English please.
