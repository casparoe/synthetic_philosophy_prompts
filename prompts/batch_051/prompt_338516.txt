You are grading a short-answer exam in an upper-level American philosophy course. The question posed to students was:

In C. S. Peirce's essay The Fixation of Belief, what distinguishes the method of science from the method of authority, and why does Peirce think the method of science is preferable even though it does not guarantee the removal of doubt? Your answer should address the role of the realist hypothesis and Peirce's fallibilism.

A student wrote:

Peirce thinks the method of authority is an improvement over tenacity because it overcomes individual caprice, but it still aims to keep belief fixed by suppressing disturbing novelties; an institution may teach one doctrine, enforce it, and shield people from dangerous questions. The method of science, by contrast, does not derive its conclusions from human will or from what merely seems agreeable to reason. It supposes that there are real things whose characters are independent of how we or anyone thinks about them, and this is the decisive difference. Science treats inquiry as a public, self-correcting activity: my observations and yours can conflict, and testing lets us settle the conflict by making belief answer to something external, so beliefs are gradually brought into agreement. Since science is fallible, it does not provide certainty, but it is the only method that gives us a guarantee that in the long run opinion will converge on the truth. That is why Peirce regards it as the most satisfactory way of fixing belief.
