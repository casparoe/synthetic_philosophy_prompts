For a seminar on revelation and sacred texts, I'm trying to get clear on what it would even mean for a translation to be divinely inspired. I'd like you to work as a philosopher of religion with expertise in translation theory, and to think carefully: begin by brainstorming different senses in which a text could be called inspired, then commit to a definition. I want a working definition of "divinely inspired text" precise enough to answer this question: can a translation inherit inspiration from an inspired original, and under what conditions? Test your definition against the following cases, and say which come out clear and which come out borderline. For the purpose of the prompt, assume that an original biblical text in its source language is a clear case of inspiration, and that a loose paraphrase by a poet who knows no biblical languages and works only from an existing translation is a clear non-case. The contested cases are: the Septuagint, the ancient Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures, in passages where the Greek departs from the surviving Hebrew; the King James Version, produced by a committee from the best manuscripts then available to them; a modern committee translation made from a critical text; a revision that corrects what are now recognized as mistranslations in an older translation; and a translation of a translation. Propose the definition, run it through the cases, note where it gives a surprising or counterintuitive verdict, and either revise the definition or defend that verdict. If it helps, distinguish whether inspiration attaches to the wording, to the semantic content, or to the act and circumstances of the translator. Also indicate whether your definition leaves room for the KJV-onlyist claim that one particular translation is specially inspired. Please write your answer in American English.
