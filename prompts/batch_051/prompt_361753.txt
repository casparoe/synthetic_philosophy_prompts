A dispute that has been running through our political theory seminar for over a year now seems to have exhausted its arguments without producing any real movement. The topic is whether wealthy democratic states have a right to exclude would-be immigrants, or whether borders are presumptively unjust and free movement should be the default. What strikes me is how much the two camps already concede to each other. Both sides agree that border enforcement coerces people who never consented to the enforcing state's authority. Both agree that many would-be migrants would be dramatically better off if admitted, and that part of the wealth of the states doing the excluding is a downstream product of conquest and extraction. Both even flinch at the fact that a person's birthplace fixes so much of what they can expect from life. Yet the exclusion side keeps returning to the thought that a political community that cannot control its own membership cannot govern itself or sustain the institutions its members have built together, while the open borders side keeps returning to the thought that the self-government of insiders cannot by itself justify coercing outsiders, and that citizenship by birth looks like inherited privilege rather than a reason.

After watching this loop for a year, I have a hypothesis: the two sides are not disagreeing about a single proposition at all. It may be that one side is asking what institutions a self-governing association requires, while the other is asking what could justify coercion over non-members. Or the real fault line may be whether associative and patriotic attachments are genuine moral reasons that can bear this much weight, as opposed to partiality that feels weighty from the inside but cannot survive outside scrutiny. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a verdict on immigration.

What kind of disagreement is this, exactly—a hidden factual or institutional question, a difference about what counts as a moral reason, different questions traveling under one heading, or bedrock intuitions about political association that neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction that separates two debates, a sharper formulation of the question, a relevant thought experiment or institutional design, or an argument that the two positions are not actually inconsistent and the dispute has been miscast from the start?
