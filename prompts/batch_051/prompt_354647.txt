I have been puzzling over how courts handle legal texts that exist in several languages, and I would like help working out exactly what the practice commits them to. International treaties are often concluded in several languages at once, with each language version declared equally authentic rather than one being a translation of another. When the versions appear to conflict, courts ordinarily do not single out one language as the original and treat the rest as attempts at it; they proceed on the assumption that the several texts express one shared legal meaning, and they try to reconcile the differences using the wording alone. That seems to presuppose a striking view of meaning: meaning would be something detachable from any particular formulation and standing behind all of them at once. Yet the same legal systems are, in other contexts, extremely suspicious of paraphrase within a single language. Legislative drafters negotiate individual words, and a party can argue that altering one phrase in a statute changes its legal effect.

I would like you to reconstruct the implicit view of meaning, especially cross-linguistic synonymy, that the equally authentic versions practice presupposes. Distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely coheres with, identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined among rival theories, and say what in the interpretive practice would have to give if the presupposed view were replaced. In particular, does the practice commit the law to there being a determinate fact of the matter about which of several rival readings is correct? And how much pressure does Quine's indeterminacy of translation thesis put on that commitment, if that thesis is understood roughly as the claim that the totality of observable linguistic behavior is compatible with incompatible translation manuals, with no fact of the matter to choose among them?
