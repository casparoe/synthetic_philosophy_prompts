I've just started as a pricing-risk officer at a small spread betting firm, and the main thing on my desk is pre-trade clearance of the odds our traders quote. The firm's line is that any set of quoted odds has to be free of Dutch books — meaning no punter can buy a combination of them and be guaranteed a profit whatever happens. My background is compliance, not philosophy, so I need a procedure I can hand to a junior analyst who also doesn't know the formal stuff. It should be a run-in-order checklist, about 20 minutes per set of odds, covering what to check first, what counts as a fail, what gets escalated to me, and where the analyst has to make a judgement call that the Dutch book theorem itself doesn't settle.

Base it on whatever Dutch book arguments actually show, not on the folk version. Run the checklist on three cases. First, a trader quotes decimal odds of 1.5, 3.2, and 4.0 on the three possible winners of a match. Second, the same trader adds a fourth outcome at 19.0. Third, a trader's odds all pass the obvious implied-probability test, but he got them from a model that combines a base rate with new information using a method I can't reconstruct from the sheet. I think that last one is fine by the theory but my boss wants a yes/no coherence call on every set, and I need you to say whether the procedure can deliver one.

Also please keep the whole thing in British English and mark which parts of the answer are mathematically settled, which parts are the standard philosophical reading of the Dutch book argument, and which parts are genuinely contested — e.g. whether avoiding Dutch books is the right explanation of why the probability axioms govern belief, rather than just a way to check the bookkeeping.

One thing I don't want: a procedure that ends with 'sum the probabilities to one'. I already know that bit.
