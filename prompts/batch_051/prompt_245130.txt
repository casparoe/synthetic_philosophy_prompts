Clinical genomic medicine now routinely includes a consent practice that lets patients decide how much they want to know about themselves. Before a genome is sequenced, patients can often choose which categories of secondary findings they wish to receive — for example, findings that reveal a sharply elevated risk of a late-onset disease for which there is no effective early intervention — and they may opt out of some or all of them. Some bioethical and legal frameworks call this the right not to know. The same institutions that treat informed disclosure as central to medical autonomy thus permit patients to refuse information about their own genomes. Yet decision theory seems to point the other way: Good's theorem says that for a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer with fixed utilities who updates by conditionalization, the expected value of costless information is never negative. What view of information, welfare, or autonomy does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether it is coherent, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and explain what would have to change in the practice if it were instead run on the principle that more information can never make a rational agent worse off.
