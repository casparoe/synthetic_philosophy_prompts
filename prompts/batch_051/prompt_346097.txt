The debate over whether large language models genuinely have beliefs and understand language, or merely behave as if they do, seems to have stalled. One side, following something like Dennett’s intentional stance, holds that belief ascription is vindicated by the predictive success of treating a system as a rational agent with beliefs and desires; the other side holds that belief talk is answerable to facts about internal representations, world models, or underlying mechanisms, and that in their absence the ascriptions are only metaphorical. My working hypothesis is that this is not one disagreement but two questions under one word — one about the epistemology of prediction, the other about cognitive architecture — and that the impasse persists because the sides differ about what would settle the matter. Diagnose the structure of the disagreement: is it substantive, verbal, or two research programs talking past each other, and what is doing the work — a disagreement about the criteria for belief, about which evidence counts, about the role of idealization in explanation, or a hidden empirical claim? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, an experiment, an interpretability result, a formal result, a change of question, or an argument that no movement should be expected?
