Just got back from a long argument with a friend about discounting the future, and I cant tell whether the five verdicts I ended up defending hang together. (1) If we can prevent 100 deaths next year or 100 deaths in 2300, and I stipulate that humanity definitely survives, the victims are equally well off, and the cost is the same, I want to say the two options are morally equal — the date alone shouldn't matter. (2) But when the rollout of a life-saving drug is delayed ten years, I do think the delay makes the policy worse, and not just because of uncertainty or opportunity cost. (3) I balk when a cost-benefit guide discounts benefits at a constant 5%: a trillion dollars of prevented flood damage in 2400 comes out worth about a used car today, and that can't be right. (4) Still, if the choice is a hospital now versus a seawall whose benefits only show up in 2300, I'm fine using a 4% rate, because the alternative is parking the money in an index fund earning 4% — that's opportunity cost of capital, not a judgment that future people count less. (5) And about my own life: I'd rather have a month of travel this year than an identical month in 30 years, but I think that's only because I might be dead or sick by then; if I knew I'd live and be healthy, I'm not sure any impatience would be left, though my friend says I'm kidding myself.

Is there a principle, or a small set of principles, that captures all five, and which of the five does it force me to give up? Give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion: put the best candidate principle in the first item, and use the rest to show how it handles the cases and where it says I'm wrong.
