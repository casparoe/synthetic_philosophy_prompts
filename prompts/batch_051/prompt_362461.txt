In the foundations-of-induction seminar I help convene, one regular participant—call her Dana—has become our de facto arbiter whenever a disagreement arises about which of two competing hypotheses deserves the higher prior. She describes herself as a Solomonoff-style inductive reasoner: prior weight, she says, should be a decreasing function of program length in a universal programming language, with two to the minus program length as the canonical factor. I have come to suspect that this self-description hides a genuine ambiguity. She could mean that the prior for a generator is fixed by the single shortest program that produces it, so that the relevant weight is two to the minus its Kolmogorov complexity. Or she could mean the full Solomonoff mixture, in which the weight is the sum of two to the minus program length over every program that generates the same sequence, so that a generator with hundreds of near-shortest implementations can accrue considerably more weight than one with a unique program only slightly shorter.

Her track record so far is consistent with both readings. Between a cyclic generator that emits 010101... and a lookup table that stores the observed bits outright, she gave the cyclic generator a vastly higher prior, pointing out only that its shortest program is much shorter; the number of equivalent implementations was never the deciding factor. Between two generators whose shortest programs came out the same length in every reference language we tried, and whose families of near-shortest implementations were of comparable size, she called the priors a tie. And when someone proposed scoring the same pair with two different off-the-shelf compression libraries, she rejected that on the ground that a prior should not depend on a parochial coding scheme; she wanted a universal scheme, even if the freedom to choose the reference machine made her uneasy.

This week's case is the first that cleanly separates the readings. Generator A has a unique shortest program of length 100, but that program would require longer than the age of the universe to run before it emitted its first prediction. Generator B has a shortest program of length 102, but hundreds of distinct programs of length 102 or 103 generate it, and all of them run quickly. Predict how Dana will rank the two priors, and defend the prediction. In doing so: specify the candidate rules that fit her track record, including whether a speed prior that discounts absurdly slow programs is doing independent work; state what each rule predicts about this case; give a best guess about her verdict, with the reasons for preferring that reading; and identify a further case that would cleanly settle the single-shortest-program reading against the all-programs-mixture reading.
