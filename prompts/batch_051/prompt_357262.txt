A recurring claim in courses on decision-making and negotiation is that the dollar auction exposes the underlying structure of escalation of commitment. In the auction, an auctioneer offers a single dollar to the highest bidder under a predatory rule: both the highest and the second-highest bidders must pay their final bids, but only the highest bidder receives the dollar. Once two parties begin bidding, each faces the choice of conceding a loss already incurred or raising by a small increment in the hope of escaping it, and bidding routinely runs past the value of the prize. The moral usually extracted is that real episodes of prolonged commitment — a failing project that keeps being refinanced, a lawsuit in which both sides spend more than the amount in dispute, an intervention whose costs have passed any plausible benefit — are the same trap with more dignified trappings, and that the rational response is therefore to disregard sunk costs and exit as soon as the marginal cost of continuing exceeds its marginal benefit.

I am not yet convinced that the analogy does as much work as it is made to do. Real escalation cases differ in ways that may be structural rather than incidental: parties often cannot observe each other's budgets or bottom lines, reputations and threats matter beyond the immediate payoff, the prize itself may change value during the contest, both sides can impose costs on each other as well as on themselves, and there is frequently an audience or a principal for whom withdrawing looks locally rational but carries a price in some larger game.

I would like an audit of this analogy. Please set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the dollar, the two bidders, the bids, the auctioneer, and the point at which the auction turns into a trap. Then identify the main disanalogies between the auction and real-world escalation of commitment, separating those that matter for the intended conclusion (that continued commitment is a sunk-cost mistake from which rational agents should exit) from those that are merely incidental. Finally, assess whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and, where the analogy fails, say what a better analogy or model would need to capture that the auction leaves out.
