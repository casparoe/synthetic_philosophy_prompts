Please write a decision tree, for an applied ethics handout, that sorts someone into their actual position on whether an individual has a moral reason to avoid participating in large-scale collective harms. Anchor the tree in three running cases: buying one conventionally raised chicken, taking one long-haul leisure flight, and casting one vote in a congressional race in a non-competitive district. The purpose is to separate positions that sound alike when given labels but come apart on two fault lines: whether a very small probability of making a decisive difference can ground an individual reason, and whether an act can be wrong even when its expected causal effect on the harm is zero. The branch questions should be answerable by someone with no philosophical training and no prior knowledge of the labels; ask only in plain terms about expected difference, the relevance of what would happen if everyone did the same, participating versus causing, and whether the reason is held by the individual or by the group. Build the tree from the logical space: derive the questions first, then locate positions in the combinations of answers, and mark any combination nobody defends. Present it as an indented outline, each branch labeled with the claim that splits it. Include as leaves at least: the pure expected-harm view; the no-individual-obligation, or inefficacy, view; the participation or complicity view; an exemplarity or virtue view; a rule-generalization view; and a collective-duty view. For every leaf, give one sentence stating the most awkward commitment the person routed there must accept; if a defensible leaf has no standard name, label it descriptively. For empty combinations, say in one sentence whether the position is internally inconsistent, merely unappealing, or simply not yet defended. Close by flagging any positions your tree cannot separate and why. Keep the response under 700 words.
