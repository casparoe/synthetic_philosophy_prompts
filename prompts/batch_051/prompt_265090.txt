Nozick's utility monster was offered as a thought experiment against aggregative utilitarianism: if one being could derive vastly more welfare from each unit of a resource than any number of other beings could, then the theory would by its own lights require diverting resources to the monster while everyone else is left worse off. At the time, the monster was a stipulated bare possibility, not a feasible object of policy. Now suppose the development of digital minds turns it into a live design problem: it may become possible to engineer minds whose welfare capacity per unit of resource is far above any biological ceiling, and to choose how many such minds to create and with what capacities. Does Nozick's objection survive this shift from a philosopher's fiction to a decision about which kinds of minds to bring into existence? State precisely which premise of the argument the development bears on, whether the argument still goes through in its original form, needs repair, or collapses, and if it needs repair, give the repaired version and specify what it can still show.
