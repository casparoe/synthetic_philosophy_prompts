I'm a science journalist working on a long piece about the old problem of animal pain in evolutionary history, framed around the pre-Fall creation debate. The hook keeps returning to two passages I was assigned to read side-by-side. In On the Origin of Species, near the end of the chapter on Instinct, Darwin says — and I'm paraphrasing from memory here — that he cannot persuade himself that a good and omnipotent God would have deliberately created ichneumon wasps with the express intent that their larvae feed inside the living bodies of caterpillars. And Genesis 1:31 says, in the translation I grew up with, that after God finished making everything, he looked at all he had made and saw that it was very good.

That combination is the whole problem in miniature: a natural world full of parasites, death, and slow consumption, but also a scriptural declaration of finished creation as very good. I want to know whether these can be honestly reconciled, and at what cost. Candidate moves I've heard include: that very good is about order and fittingness, not moral innocence; that animal death and predation existed before any human fall and are not actually evil; that the fall had some sort of cosmic or even retroactive effect on creation; that Darwin is importing sentimental moral categories into biology; or that Genesis should not be read as natural history at all. Which of these, or which combination, does the least special pleading, and which are just apologetic sleight of hand? If you had to rank the reconciliation attempts by intellectual honesty, where would each land, and is the whole exercise even one a science journalist should treat as a live question rather than a historical curiosity?
