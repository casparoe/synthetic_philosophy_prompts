"The sorites paradox runs like this: one grain of sand is clearly not a heap, ten thousand grains clearly are, and it seems that removing a single grain can never turn a heap into a non-heap. By repeated application of that premise we reach the absurd conclusion that one grain is a heap. So at least one premise must go, and most solutions try to explain why the tolerance premise is false without saying anything too strange about ordinary language.

Supervaluationism, associated with Kit Fine, says that words like 'heap' are semantically incomplete: there are many admissible ways of precisifying 'heap', and each precisification draws a sharp line somewhere. A sentence is true outright, or supertrue, if it comes out true on every admissible precisification; it is false outright, or superfalse, if it comes out false on every precisification; otherwise it is neither true nor false. The existential claim 'there is a boundary between the heaps and the non-heaps' is therefore true, because on each precisification some particular grain is the boundary, even though no particular grain is the boundary. Classical logic is preserved: for instance, excluded middle is valid because on any precisification either p or not-p holds, so 'p or not-p' is true on every precisification. Since every sentence is true or false on each precisification, supervaluationism also preserves bivalence; it just relocates the assignment of truth values to the precisifications rather than to the unsharpened language. So the paradox is solved by denying that the tolerance premise is ever true: on each admissible precisification there is a last heap, so the inductive step fails.

Epistemicism, defended by Timothy Williamson, also preserves classical logic and bivalence, but for a different reason. On this view there really is a sharp boundary, but we cannot know where it is. The reason is that the meaning of 'heap' is semantically indeterminate: our use of the word is compatible with all the different precisifications, and although one of them is, as a brute matter, the correct one, nothing in our linguistic practice picks it out. This is why competent speakers refuse to draw the line, even though the line exists. Williamson adds that any true belief about the cutoff would fail to be reliable, since nearby cases are too similar for us to discriminate, so ignorance is inevitable. Epistemicism thus solves the paradox by rejecting the tolerance premise outright: one grain does make a difference, and we are simply not in a position to say which one.

Of the two, I find supervaluationism more plausible. It better explains why borderline cases feel genuinely unsettled, since our hedging reflects the lack of a determinate truth value until the word is sharpened. Epistemicism, by contrast, has to say that there is a fact of the matter that no amount of investigation could reveal, which makes the boundary look almost magical. The supervaluationist picture is also more charitable to ordinary speakers, because it explains why they refuse to draw the line without saying they hold a false belief. So on balance supervaluationism gives the better account of the sorites."
