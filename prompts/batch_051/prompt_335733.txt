I am a doctoral student in media studies, and I am preparing the Q&A segment of a departmental speaker series in media philosophy. The guest is a media theorist who defends a Kierkegaardian diagnosis of online discourse. Her position, in summary, is that Kierkegaard's critique of the nineteenth-century daily press transfers with surprising fidelity to present-day platforms: the press summons an abstract public rather than a concrete assembly; it levels substantive distinctions among persons into an envious equality of opinion; and it rewards curiosity and chatter at the expense of committed, passionate action. She also insists that the corrective Kierkegaard gestures toward, a recovery of inwardness and of the category of the single individual, is not political quietism but a condition for any defensible media ecology. Compose exactly five questions that expose the weakest joints in this position, and for each question add the follow-up that becomes necessary once she gives the reply she is most likely to give. The five questions should address, in turn: whether leveling smuggles in an aristocratic suspicion of equality that cannot be imported into democratic media criticism; whether an algorithmically sorted, tribally segmented platform public is really the same phenomenon as the anonymous, leveling public of the 1840s press; whether a remedy that begins from inwardness can avoid counseling withdrawal from the material and institutional conditions of platform power; whether the distinction between press and public can survive a media environment in which every reader is also a publisher; and, finally, what a platform would have to be like for her to concede that it escapes the Kierkegaardian critique. Present your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences and containing a question plus its follow-up, with no introduction or conclusion.
