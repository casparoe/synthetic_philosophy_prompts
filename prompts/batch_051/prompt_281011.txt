The original gene–meme analogy treated cultural variants—catchphrases, tunes, rituals, fashions—as replicators: units copied with enough fidelity that variations could accumulate and be selected among, roughly as genes are. A generation of later work attacked the copying half of that picture, arguing that human cultural transmission is mostly reconstructive: people infer the gist or function of what they observe and rebuild it from their own knowledge, so variants blend, drift toward shared "attractor" forms rather than staying as discrete copied units, and rarely form neat branching lineages. The selection half has meanwhile been transformed by algorithmic platforms, where a slogan or image macro can be duplicated exactly millions of times while recommender systems decide what spreads by optimizing engagement rather than anything like the variant's own reproductive success. Assess whether the replication-based view of memes survives these two developments together. Which premise of the original analogy is actually under pressure—copying fidelity, the individuation of variants into lineages, or the idea that selection tracks something analogous to fitness—and is there a repaired version of "meme" that says more than "a thing that spreads"? Weigh the strongest arguments on each side, but do not end non-committally: defend a definite verdict about whether "meme" remains a useful theoretical term, survives only as a metaphor, or neither.
