For the last year I have been using a large language model as a first-pass reviewer of code changes and as a debugging assistant. Without having made any deliberate decision about it, I have drifted into a certain idiom. In my notes and in conversations with colleagues I say things like: "the model believes the defect is in the rate limiter and keeps anchoring on that," or "it expects the configuration keys to be lowercased, which is why it normalizes them." By this I mean something sober: across many prompts and contexts the system behaves as though a small set of claims about the codebase is steering its outputs, and I can often predict and repair its failures by writing down what it "thinks" and then correcting it explicitly in the prompt.

A colleague with whom I share a good deal of intellectual background — we read the same forums, and he is more occupied with AI risk than I am — has started pushing back whenever I talk this way. His position, as I understand it: the system is a next-token predictor trained to imitate human text; it does not believe or expect anything; my idiom is a category error, and a consequential one, because it silently imports the rest of the package — a unified agent, stable attitudes, an internal picture the model is trying to make true. The fact that the idiom predicts behavior, he says, does not make it true, and the habit will cause me to over-trust the system and misread its failures.

I feel the force of this. But I also notice that the belief idiom is doing real work. When I tabulate the model's apparent "beliefs" and then correct one of them, the downstream behavior usually changes in the way a junior colleague's behavior changes when you correct a mistaken assumption. My forecasts about how the system will handle novel inputs are better when I model it as having a small set of mostly stable "beliefs" about the codebase than when I model it as a purely statistical artifact. And when I try to restate my predictions without any belief-like vocabulary, the descriptions become long, gerrymandered, and post hoc; they list regularities without capturing what unites them.

So I have a practical question and a theoretical one. Practically: is my way of talking and reasoning about the system a useful stance to keep, a confusion to train myself out of, or a useful tool with specific failure modes that need quarantining? Theoretically: under what conditions, if any, is it legitimate to say that a system of this kind literally has beliefs, and does the sort of system I am using meet them?
