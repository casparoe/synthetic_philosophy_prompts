I have been turning over the following argument and would like you to assess it. It concerns the way markets are supposed to aggregate dispersed information, and I cannot settle whether it is sound or whether one of its steps is doing more work than it can bear.

(1) A rational trader who voluntarily buys at a price must expect the purchase to improve her position; a rational voluntary seller must expect the sale to improve his.

(2) Consider a case in which the only asymmetry between buyer and seller is that each holds different private information about the value of the asset: their prior beliefs, attitudes to risk, and endowments are otherwise identical.

(3) The buyer should then reason that, since the seller is rational and shares her prior beliefs, the seller's willingness to sell at this price is evidence that the seller's private information makes the price look high; the buyer should accordingly revise her valuation downward relative to what her own signal alone suggested. The seller can reason symmetrically about the buyer's willingness to buy.

(4) It follows that each party, before agreeing, will treat the other's willingness as adverse news—evidence that the price is less favourable than their own private information suggested. Once both condition on the fact of the other's willingness, at least one of them should no longer expect to gain; so no trade resting purely on private information should occur between rational agents of this kind.

(5) But real securities markets do exhibit trade, and the standard view is that this trading is precisely what brings private information into prices. Therefore, if market participants are rational in the sense of (1)–(4), the standard view cannot be right: prices cannot be aggregating private information through voluntary trade.

Is this argument valid? If it is not, identify the first step that fails, specify the reading of its key terms under which each earlier step goes through, and say how much of the conclusion in (5) survives once the argument is repaired. If you think it is valid, state the conditions under which it holds and explain what it does and does not establish about actual markets.
