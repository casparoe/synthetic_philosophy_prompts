I hold a specific version of eliminative materialism. The claim is not that there are no inner causes of behavior and not that cognitive science should dispense with representations; it is that beliefs and desires, understood as discrete propositional attitudes with determinate semantic contents, are posits of a stagnant folk theory, and that a mature neuroscience will replace this framework rather than reduce or vindicate it, much as chemistry replaced rather than found phlogiston. I want you to treat this as a risky empirical thesis, not a terminological preference. What would have to be true for it to be wrong? Which actual or foreseeable developments — in Bayesian cognitive modeling, comparative and developmental psychology, decision neuroscience, or the study of how animals and artifacts represent their environments — would count decisively against eliminating the attitudes, and which would only show that belief-talk is a convenient compression for states an eliminativist already accepts under another description? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say what a committed eliminativist would themselves concede as a failed prediction or anomaly, and give an honest verdict on whether the view as stated has been insulated into unfalsifiability — and whether that is a vice or the appropriate caution of a framework-level claim.
