I've been working through the Schopenhauer–Nietzsche dispute over pity and it's left me bothered by something in ordinary moral talk. Schopenhauer makes compassion—immediate, non-self-interested participation in another's suffering, aimed at their good—the entire foundation of moral worth: an act is moral, he thinks, just in so far as it flows from that. Nietzsche fires back that this is exactly the emotion we should distrust most. Pity, on his account, contains a hidden dose of looking down: it turns the other person into a spectacle of weakness so the pitier can feel strong and generous, and by coddling the sufferer it keeps the suffering around. Same emotion, two completely opposite appraisal.

And here's the asymmetry I can't resolve. We use compassion as our standard term of moral praise: the person who wades into a canal to save a drowning dog, or sits up with a terrified patient, counts as a moral hero precisely because they are moved by another's pain. But we are far less admiring when we are on the receiving end. Being pitied feels diminishing; it reclassifies us as someone to be handled rather than met, and we'd often rather be helped by someone acting out of duty, respect, or even cold competence than by someone whose kindness casts us as a sufferer. And when pity operates at close range over time—a woman wiring money to her gambling brother so he never hits bottom, while also needing him to stay the helpless one—we stop calling it virtue and start calling it codependency or pathological altruism. So the same felt concern is moral gold when it aims at a stranger and something closer to an insult or a sickness when it is aimed at us, or when it lingers. Is there a principled difference between compassion and pathological pity—something about the helper's motive, the recipient's autonomy, the relationship, or the consequences—or do we simply praise pity when we identify with the helper and resent it when we occupy the receiving end? I want the strongest distinction you can defend, tested against the gambling-brother case and the case of unwanted help from someone who genuinely means well, and an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
