For our philosophy of law seminar we've been playing Nomic, the game where the rules include rules for changing the rules (including the rule-changing rules themselves). I've come up with a distinction I want stress-tested, because I can't tell whether it's trivial, false, or actually useful. I want to seperate two things:

(i) Procedural self-application: a rule sets out a procedure for changing rules, and that procedure can be applied to that very rule. E.g., 'any rule can be amended by a two-thirds vote' can itself be amended by a two-thirds vote.

(ii) Status self-reference: a rule whose content is about its own legal force. E.g., 'this rule is unrepealable,' or 'this rule remains valid no matter what the amendment rule says.'

The paradox I keep running into is only ever the second kind: a rule gets adopted under an amendment rule, and then its content stops that amendment rule from touching it. My claim is that (i) is harmless and (ii) is where all the real trouble is, and that when people think (i) is paradoxical they're silently reading it as (ii). I call this the 'status/procedure gap.' When I try to prove it I end up going in circles. Is the distinction coherent, is the claim right, and is this already a named thing in legal theory that I've reinvented? Give me the three strongest objections and tell me plainly whether any of them kills the distinction rather than just making it awkward.
