Consider two philosophical positions on the moral status of individual contributions to large-scale collective harms and goods, with climate change, industrial animal agriculture, and electoral politics as the running examples. The causal-efficacy position holds that a token contribution to a collective outcome is required (or its omission wrong) only insofar as that token act itself can be expected to make a causal difference to the outcome, holding fixed what the other agents will in fact do. The participation position holds that an individual act can be wrong or required in virtue of being a constituent of a collectively produced harm or good even when the token act is causally inefficacious, because agents may be required not to take part in collective wrongdoing and may have duties to do their part in a needed collective effort. Suppose proponents of these two positions have spent a year in structured, good-faith exchange. Draft the document their neutral facilitator would produce: a joint statement both parties can sign, in which what they now agree on is made explicit, each remaining disagreement is restated in wording that both sides accept, and the cruxes—and the tests they jointly accept as bearing on those cruxes—are recorded.

The document must be at most 800 words (with any table counting toward the limit) and must not cite authors or external works. It should develop the two positions solely from the characterization above and the stipulated cases below.

Use the following structure.

1. Agreed claims (numbered). Give at least six non-vacuous claims. They must cover at least: the stipulated but-for facts in the three cases; the claim that collectives can cause harms that no token act causes; a shared rejection of the argument that what would happen if everyone acted that way settles what the individual should do; and a shared distinction between the deontic status of an act and the evaluation of the agent.

2. Residual disagreements (table). Use three columns: the issue stated neutrally; the causal-efficacy side's wording of its own view; the participation side's wording of its own view. Include at least four rows. At least one row must concern the unit of act-description (for example, whether "buying this package of meat" may permissibly be redescribed as "sustaining industrial animal agriculture" when the moral assessment is done); at least one row must concern the asymmetry between refraining from a collective harm and positively contributing to a collective good when the personal costs, default behavior, and background incentives differ; and at least one row must concern the deontic frame—whether the individual's question is primarily "what should I do, given that others will act as they will" or "what should we do, and what follows for my part."

3. Cruxes and tests. Identify at least three sub-questions such that settling each would move at least one side. For each crux, state (a) a concrete case or stipulated fact that both sides accept bears on it, and (b) each side's prediction or verdict for that case, recorded in advance. The cases must include: a fixed-lot production case in which one foregone purchase is guaranteed not to change the number of animals slaughtered because the producer sets the slaughter run in advance and demand fluctuations are absorbed as waste or price adjustments; a threshold case in which a boycott or referendum needs exactly 5,000 contributions to succeed and 4,999 others are already committed; and an overdetermined-threshold case in which 7,000 people contribute when 5,000 would have sufficed. Additional cases may be added if the word limit allows.

Throughout, treat both positions as live, defensible options; do not reduce either side to a strawman. The document should end with a final sentence identifying one apparent disagreement that, on closer analysis during the year, turned out to be merely verbal and can be set aside.
