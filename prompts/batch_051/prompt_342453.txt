A close friend of mine was mugged and seriously injured last year, and I have been surprised by how much the aftermath has unsettled my views. When the offender was sentenced, I expected to feel something like satisfaction that he would get what he deserved; instead I mostly noticed the machinery of it — the offender’s childhood, his psychiatric history, the chain of bad options, and a prosecutor using words like choice and wickedness so confidently that the causal story felt irrelevant. Since then I have been reading around in the philosophy of punishment, and two positions keep pulling me in opposite directions. One says punishment is justified because mature human beings are reason-responsive agents who genuinely deserve blame and hard treatment for what they choose to do. The other says that once you take the causes of behavior seriously, desert is an illusion, and the state should replace punishment with something like a public-health quarantine model: humane restraint and rehabilitation aimed only at preventing harm, with no moral condemnation attached. I do not want an essay taking sides; I want a story that makes the tension emotionally concrete.

Write a short story set in a near-future city that has actually adopted the second position. Punishment has been abolished. People who are found dangerous after an offense are placed in comfortable but involuntary therapeutic custody, and the whole system is carefully designed not to blame or stigmatize them. The protagonist should be someone who has been wronged — perhaps a parent whose child was killed by a repeat offender, though use a different premise if that feels overfamiliar — and who finds that they cannot live inside a system that refuses to say that some things are deserved. The story should be fair to the new system: show its genuine humanity, its effectiveness, or the relief it gives the people subjected to it. But also show what is lost, in the texture of ordinary relationships and in the way people see each other, when a community stops treating wrongdoers as answerable agents. Do not resolve this with a tidy moral; aim for an unsettling final moment that lets the reader feel the pull of both positions.
