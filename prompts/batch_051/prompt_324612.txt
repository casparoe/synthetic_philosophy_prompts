I keep returning to the problem of divine hiddenness, not through any philosophical training but because I cannot make sense of a God who is supposed to love me while remaining impossible to find. The explanation I am most often offered is that God's silence is like a teacher who stays quiet during an exam. The teacher knows the answers and could announce them, but doing so would defeat the whole exercise: the students would not learn the material, and their answers would not show any real understanding. By the same logic, the argument runs, God stays hidden so that our faith and our seeking can be genuinely ours, and so that no one is coerced into belief by an overwhelming show of force.

I have begun to suspect that this analogy does more work than the people who use it notice, because it quietly borrows the entire setting of a school — a teacher whose existence the students already take for granted, a syllabus, an announced test, a set period of quiet, and a reliable moment when the papers are handed back. I want you to take the analogy apart for me.

Build the correspondence point by point: what is meant to map onto the teacher, the students, the exam conditions, the silence, the syllabus, the marks, the penalties for wrong answers, and anything else the analogy needs before it can carry a conclusion. Then show me where the mapping breaks. Separate the breaks into two groups: surface differences that do no real damage, and structural disanalogies that matter for the conclusion the analogy is used to defend — namely, that divine hiddenness is compatible with a loving God and does not count as evidence against one.

In particular, I want you to test three doubts of mine. First, in an ordinary exam the students know they are sitting one, know why the teacher is silent, and know that a teacher exists to be answered to; in the God case none of this seems to be supplied. Second, a teacher tests because she does not already know how the students will perform, whereas an all-knowing God would learn nothing from the test, which changes what the silence could even be for. Third, a teacher's silence is short and has a fixed end, after which the teacher becomes visible and the results are given; God's silence appears to have no announced endpoint. For each of these, tell me whether it is a structural break or only a surface difference.

End with an honest overall judgment: once the disanalogies are on the table, how much of the original conclusion can the exam analogy actually support? If it cannot do the work, describe a fairer analogy for divine hiddenness, and say whether that replacement analogy ends up supporting the same conclusion or the opposite one.
