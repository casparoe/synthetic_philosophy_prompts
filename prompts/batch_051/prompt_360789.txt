You are a moral philosopher who works on universalizability in its Kantian and post-Kantian forms: the formula of universal law, Hare's universal prescriptivism, and the generalization argument associated with M. G. Singer. Think through several candidate principles before settling on one, and do not jump to the first answer that sounds plausible.

Ordinary moral discourse treats "what if everyone did that?" as a serious, sometimes decisive objection, but it applies the test very selectively. It is confidently applied to fare evasion, tax fraud, and free-riding on shared resources: in these cases the individual act seems to depend on others' not doing it, and universal performance would destroy the practice. Yet the same test is rarely applied — and may be mocked when it is — to choices whose universal adoption would be at least as consequential: choosing not to have children, refusing dangerous but socially necessary work, or spending one's life in pure mathematics while others grow the food. If everyone acted on those maxims, the results would be drastic; nevertheless "what if nobody had children?" is not treated as a serious refutation of remaining childless, and many people who accept the fare-evasion inference would deny that it settles the childless case. At the same time, the test is treated as irrelevant even where it would yield an answer: we cannot all be philosophy professors, but no one treats that as a reason against becoming one.

The pattern is suspicious: the test gets wheeled in for acts we already condemn or fear, and quietly set aside for acts we respect, tolerate, or recognize in ourselves. I want to know whether anything principled justifies this asymmetry. Please do three things. First, state the strongest principle you can find that picks out the class of maxims to which the test is properly applied — or that captures the defensible core of the ordinary practice. Second, test that principle against the childless case, the dangerous-work case, and at least one further case of your own choosing that is hardest for the principle. Third, give a verdict: should the asymmetry be kept, revised, or abandoned? And is "what if everyone did that?" best understood as a genuine universalizability test, as a fairness rule about free-riding dressed in universalist language, or as a rhetorical device used to lend force to conclusions reached on other grounds?

If you think the question as posed is built on a mistake — for instance, that the childless case and the fare-evasion case do not actually share a test, or that ordinary usage is not best described as an asymmetry — say so directly and reformulate the question rather than answering it as asked.
