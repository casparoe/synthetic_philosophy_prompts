In most developed legal systems, the criminal law is officially organized around the idea that there is no crime without a guilty mind: the prosecution must prove some level of mens rea — intention, knowledge, recklessness, or at least negligence — for each element of the offense. Yet the same systems maintain a sizable class of offenses that dispense with mens rea in whole or in part. Statutory rape, for instance, usually admits no defense of reasonable or even documented mistake about the victim's age. Selling alcohol to a minor is often punished even when the seller demanded identification and was shown a convincing forgery. A broad family of public-welfare and regulatory offenses punishes acts the defendant did not know were prohibited, and some systems add vicarious criminal liability, under which an employer may be convicted for an employee's act that the employer neither authorized nor could reasonably have prevented. The result is that a system which requires a culpable mental state for a thief or a killer will nonetheless convict and fine, and sometimes imprison, people who are not alleged to have chosen the wrong, foreseen the harm, or fallen below any standard of care.

What does this bifurcated practice presuppose about the nature of criminal punishment and moral responsibility? Does it presuppose that punishment is not essentially about blame, but is at least sometimes a regulatory instrument for allocating the costs of dangerous activities? Or does it presuppose a different theory of responsibility, on which one can be answerable for occupying a role or creating a risk without any culpable mental state? Reconstruct the implicit theory or theories as precisely as you can, and distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Then assess whether the two tracks are coherent with one another: can the fault-based track and the strict-liability track be justified by a single underlying theory of punishment, or do they express incompatible views of what a conviction says about the defendant? Finally, say what would have to change if the system adopted a consistently fault-based view: which offenses would need to be redefined, which regulatory or protective goals would become harder to achieve, and what would be gained or lost. Where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where several rival theories would license the same rules — say so explicitly.
