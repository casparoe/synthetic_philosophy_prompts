Two people in my office got into an argument that never got resolved. Anika had spent a week modelling whether a contract dispute would end up in litigation and landed on 80%. Then somebody showed her a calibration tool built from her own past forecasts, which said that in high-stakes cases like this she overestimates by about 15 points and "should" sit at 65%.

Anika refused to move. "I already worked through the case-specific evidence. If I drop to 65 just because a model says people like me overestimate, I'd be in a state my own first-order read says is too low. That's epistemic akrasia. The calibration data says nothing about this actual dispute; it only tells me I might be wrong, and 'I might be wrong' isn't evidence about the contract."

Jamal pushed back: "You're treating the 80% as if it was read off the world. It wasn't. It came out of a reasoning process with known base rates, and learning your process overshoots in exactly this kind of case is evidence. Calling it 'meta' doesn't license ignoring it."

Anika: "But if that's allowed, then every time I notice I'm fallible I should shave my credence toward 50/50, and there's no principled stopping point. The 15-point correction is just base-rate noise; it doesn't carry any fact about this dispute."

Jamal: "Not toward 50/50—toward the calibrated estimate. And you can't write it off as noise just because its about you. If the tool is well-built, that correction is most of what you actually know about your reliability."

Can you adjudicate the exchange move by move? Say who is right about what in each turn, and what, if anything, both are missing.
