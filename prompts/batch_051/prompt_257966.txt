I chair a three-officer board of review that decides whether disciplinary charges should go to trial, and I need a decision memorandum to circulate before we meet on charges against a judge advocate who refused a commander's order to sign a legal review. The case keeps returning to an old question in a new form: when is deference to authority a virtue, and when does it become an abdication of the very judgment a hierarchy needs from its experts?

Captain Voss, a judge advocate with eight years' service, was the legal advisor to a brigade commander preparing a deployment. The commander ordered her to produce the operational-law annex for the operation and to countersign it as the brigade's formal legal review. She produced the annex but refused to countersign. Her objection was to the annex's treatment of proportionality: it aggregated the expected military advantage of the operation across the whole campaign while directing that civilian harm at individual strike points would not be separately weighed unless it was likely to be extreme. She judged this a misstatement of the law of armed conflict, though she conceded the interpretation was arguable. After a contentious meeting, the commander ordered her to sign a slightly narrowed version; she again declined and instead sent a dissent memorandum through the legal branch rather than remaining silent. The dissent delayed the deployment, and two reviewing colonels later split on whether her legal analysis was correct.

The charges are disobeying a lawful order and conduct prejudicial to good order and discipline. Her defense is that her office carries a professional duty of independent legal judgment, and that deference stops where it would force her to certify as lawful a document she believes is not. Signing with a dissent noted, she argues, would still have laundered the decision; an institution that trains people to obey orders should especially protect those who obey slowly enough to check the legal merits. The prosecution answers that the order was not manifestly unlawful, that the commander heard her advice and was acting within a zone of arguable legality, and that once a commander has decided, a subordinate legal advisor has no veto. Allowing a captain to refuse a countersignature on her own reading of a contested standard would turn legal advice into a veto and invite every staff officer to substitute private conviction for the chain of command.

The board must recommend one of three dispositions: refer the charges to trial, dismiss them, or dispose of the matter administratively. Write the memorandum I will circulate. It should state a clear recommended disposition, justify it, and engage seriously with the strongest consideration in favor of the disposition you reject, explaining why it does not carry the day. The board has also been asked to articulate a standard for future cases: the conditions, if any, under which a subordinate may refuse to sign a document that would record his or her professional endorsement of a superior's decision. Keep it workable enough that a commander could apply it before the next deployment; this is a decision document, not a law review article.
