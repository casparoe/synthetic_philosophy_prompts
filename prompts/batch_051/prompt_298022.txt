I keep noticing this pattern alot and I've never found the right word for it, and honestly it's started to feel a bit personal because it happened in my team at work last month. One of my colleagues criticised a project lead for how she'd handled a handover – he was right, honestly, the documentation was a mess and two deadlines slipped because of it. Then someone in the meeting said he'd done exactly the same thing two years ago, and the whole discussion just collapsed. Nobody disputed the substance. If the new starter had said the same sentence, it would have landed fine. So the criticism wasn't false or irrelevant; it just seemed to lose its force because of who said it. And it's oddly selective. If he'd never run a handover in his life, nobody would have dismissed him; if he'd once failed a driving test, I doubt it would have come up at all. It's not the same as the old playground 'you did it too' reply, because that's aimed at whether the claim is true, and this was aimed at something more like his right to be the one saying it.

Then last week I saw a version of it at the school gates – a mum was told to 'sit this one out' when she criticised another parent for exactly the kind of hovering she does herself, and again the criticism was probably right. I'm not after the blanket term for the playground move; I know that's just an informal fallacy. I'm after the more specific thing, if it has an established name in philosophy or argumentation theory: when a speaker's own past conduct gets treated as cancelling an otherwise sound criticism, not as evidence against the criticism. If it fits several concepts but none exactly, I'd rather you say which is closest and how the described thing differs from each. If there's no name, what would you call it, and where are its boundaries? I can feel that it's not always just noise – sometimes it really does seem indecent to keep pointing a finger – but I can't articulate when it's legitimate.
