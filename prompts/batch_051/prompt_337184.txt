Most legal systems give a competent adult's present treatment refusals decisive weight: a patient may decline life-saving treatment today, for reasons others find poor, and clinicians must comply. The same systems also enforce instruments by which an earlier self binds a later one. A living will, for instance, can refuse artificial nutrition and hydration years in advance, and it will typically be honoured once the maker has lost capacity, even if the patient later asks for treatment or, having developed advanced dementia, expresses no consistent wish at all. Some jurisdictions go further and enforce Ulysses contracts, in which a person with a history of mental illness authorises detention or involuntary treatment during a future episode, including against their stated wishes in that episode. A directive of this kind usually remains revocable while the maker retains capacity, but once capacity is lost or altered in the anticipated way, the earlier self governs and the later self's expressed preferences may be overridden. So the law allows a person at t1 to exercise an authority over the person at t2 that it would not grant to a spouse, a doctor, or a court, and that it would not allow the t2 person to cancel simply by changing their mind. What theory of personal identity, and of the authority of the earlier self, does this bundle of practices presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as sharply as you can, distinguishing what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates. Assess whether the practice is coherent: does it presuppose the same thing across living wills, dementia cases, and Ulysses contracts, and is it consistent with the reasons the law gives for respecting a competent patient's present refusal? Finally, say what would have to change in the handling of these instruments if one instead held either (a) that the later self is not the same person as the signatory, or (b) that only a person's present experiential interests matter.
