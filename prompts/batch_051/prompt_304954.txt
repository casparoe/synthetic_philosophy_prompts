I want to understand what I actually think makes something a case of idolatry, where I mean that in the broad sense of valuing something in the wrong way, not just the narrow sense of bowing to a carved figure. I will record my reactions to a series of cases and then ask whether any single principle can account for them.

Here are my verdicts.

1. A farmer during a drought carves a small wooden figure, sets food before it, and prays to it for rain and for his children's safety, believing that it hears and answers. I take this to be a clear case of idolatry.

2. A father of a daughter who needs constant care because of profound disabilities has given up his career, his friendships, and most of his sleep to care for her. He says, without bitterness, that she has become the whole purpose of his life. I do not think he is valuing her the wrong way; I admire this.

3. A painter keeps a quasi-monastic routine, calls her studio a sanctuary, refuses any commission that would compromise the work, and says art is the one thing she would never betray because it is the only thing that makes existence bearable to her. I do not think this is idolatry.

4. A man tells his wife that she is his whole world, resents her having close friendships, holds her responsible for whether his life feels worthwhile, and says he could not go on if he lost her. I think he values her in the wrong way, idolatrously.

5. A football supporter has attended every home match for thirty years, spends much of his income on tickets, weeps when the club is relegated, and says the club is the one thing that has never let him down. But he missed a match without much agony when his daughter was ill, and he does not think the club's success would excuse lying or cruelty. I do not think this is idolatry; it is excessive but not wrong in kind.

6. A nationalist calls the nation holy, resists public discussion of its historical crimes, and says he would lie and break promises for the nation because the nation's survival outranks ordinary honesty. I think this is idolatry, and more dangerous than the farmer's.

7. A company founder has missed her children growing up, checks revenue before she checks on her family, calls the company's mission sacred, and treats any threat to it as a moral outrage. I think this is idolatry.

The pairs that trouble me are the father and the husband (one person becomes the entire meaning of another's life in both, yet I call the first love and the second idolatry) and the painter and the founder (both treat something they made or do as sacred, yet I call the first admirable and the second misplaced). I also cannot say offhand what separates the football supporter from the nationalist.

Is there a principle about what it is to value something in the wrong way that delivers all or nearly all of these verdicts? State the best version of the principle, explain which cases motivate each clause, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Then apply the principle to a case I cannot settle. A woman says that the only thing she is ultimately loyal to is the truth, refuses to tell even kind lies, describes her inquiry as a form of devotion, and says that if a truth would make her whole life feel meaningless she would still want to know it. Does she count as valuing the truth idolatrously? Why or why not?

End your response with a single line beginning "Summary:" that states your final principle in one sentence.
