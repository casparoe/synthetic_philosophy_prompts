Consider the practice, familiar from post-war German legal proceedings, of treating certain statutes enacted under the Nazi regime as so morally repugnant that they afforded citizens no legal justification for acts that would otherwise have been crimes. A court taking this view is not merely saying that the statute was bad law or that it ought to have been otherwise; it is saying, in effect, that the enactment did not count as law for the purposes of the case before it. Reconstruct the theory of law this practice presupposes. Does it strictly presuppose a natural-law position of the kind associated with Fuller in his debate with Hart, or can a legal positivist accommodate it by distinguishing a norm's validity from its judicial application, or by describing the court as applying retrospective legislation? Say which features of the practice would be decisive between these interpretations. Then consider the same conduct as a standing doctrine rather than as a one-off response to a discredited predecessor regime: would a legal system that routinely withheld the title of law from grossly unjust statutes thereby commit itself to a particular view of the relationship between law and morality, or could it remain agnostic on that question while simply adopting a rule of adjudication? Finally, state what would have to change in the practice if the presupposed view were replaced by its rival.
