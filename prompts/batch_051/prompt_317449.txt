I am a PhD student in political theory working on the ideal of self-sufficiency, and I have noticed that my philosophical commitments are affecting my own intellectual habits. I follow a rule of epistemic autarky: I will not endorse a claim—whether about politics, economics, or history—unless I can reconstruct for myself the main argument or evidence for it from primary sources or from things I already understand well. If I cannot, I withhold judgment, even when the specialists are nearly unanimous; I would rather have no view than a borrowed one. Recently this led me to remain agnostic about the causes of a major historical collapse because I had not worked through the relevant archival or quantitative material myself. A colleague says I am repeating, at the level of the individual inquirer, the mistake that critics attribute to autarkic states: I am giving up the gains from the division of epistemic labor and will end up with narrower, more amateurish, and more idiosyncratic beliefs than I would have if I sometimes deferred to others. I see the force of this, but I also think there is a difference between endorsing a borrowed conclusion and actually understanding what one believes. Is my rule defensible as it stands? Is there a reformulated version that keeps the demand for first-hand understanding without cutting myself off from expert testimony, and under what conditions about my own reliability relative to that of the relevant expert community would epistemic self-sufficiency be the better policy?
