I work in software, and I keep noticing an asymmetry between how the law treats abandoned code and how it treats abandoned physical property. I want to understand what view of ownership makes that asymmetry coherent, if any view does.

Consider a proprietary codebase whose owner is gone: the company dissolved, the author died, and no one is maintaining, selling, or licensing it. As I understand U.S. copyright law, that code remains under ordinary proprietary restrictions and will not enter the public domain for decades, so I cannot legally fork, patch, or redistribute it even though there is no identifiable person who would be harmed if I did. But with physical property the law allows ownership to lapse in roughly similar situations: a car left in a field long enough can be towed, auctioned, or claimed through abandonment or adverse possession. So the same legal system seems to say that ownership of an abandoned object can end, while ownership of abandoned code cannot.

What theory of ownership or property is the law presupposing here, and is it consistent? Is it treating ownership of the code as a relation to an owner at all, or as a standing legal claim that persists even when no one can exercise it or benefit from it? I suspect the lost-versus-abandoned distinction in physical property turns on intent to relinquish, but a dissolved corporation can form no intent about anything, so that distinction seems to cut the wrong way. Is the inconsistency real or only apparent? And which commitments about property, harm, or value would have to change for orphaned code to be treated more like abandoned goods?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Also tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
