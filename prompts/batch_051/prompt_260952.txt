I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, but I also moonlight as a grader for a friend's night course on the history of economic thought. I wanted a seccond set of eyes on how to score a short-answer question on Hume, since I keep going back and forth and dont want to lean on my own instinct.

The exam question was:

"In his essays on money and trade, Hume argues that a nation should not be anxious about the balance of trade or the amount of gold it holds. Explain his argument for this claim, including the mechanism he uses, and state what role the money supply plays in his account."

The student wrote:

"Hume is responding to the mercantilists, who thought a nation's wealth was its stock of gold and that a trade deficit meant the country was bleeding away its treasure. Hume says that worry is misplaced. His main argument is the specie-flow mechanism: if a country starts losing money, its domestic prices fall. Lower prices make its exports cheaper for foreigners and its imports dearer at home, so the trade balance swings back and the gold flows in again. So the balance of trade is self-correcting; a country can't simply drain itself of money forever. Since prices adjust automatically, Hume thinks money is completely neutral: the supply of money has no effect on output or employment even in the short run, because any change is instantly absorbed by a proportional change in prices. That's why he says the magistrate's real job is just to promote industry and let the quantity of money do whatever it does. You could even read him as saying a trade deficit is actually good for a country, because the fall in prices makes its goods more competitive and spurs exports. So for Hume the stock of money doesn't determine national wealth; what matters is real factors like the industry and labour of the people."

Grade it out of 10 and give me the point-by-point comments you'd write: which claims you'd mark right, which you'd flag, and why. I want the specific justification for the score, not a general impression.
