I keep coming back to Sen's liberal paradox, and I'm trying to figure out how much it actually shows rather than how much it just looks like it shows. Here's the setup, for anyone who hasn't stared at it recently: a prude and a lewd person, one copy of some scandalous book, and three possible social states — the lewd reads it, the prude reads it, or nobody reads it. The lewd ranks them: prude reads it > lewd reads it > nobody reads it (he'd find the prude being scandalized either hilarious or good for him). The prude ranks them: nobody reads it > prude reads it > lewd reads it (he'd rather take the hit himself than let the lewd be corrupted). Now impose two things that each sound unobjectionable. Pareto: if both people prefer X to Y, society prefers X to Y. Minimal liberalism: each person is decisive over at least one pair that is properly their own business — the lewd decides whether the lewd reads the book, and the prude decides whether the prude reads it. You get a cycle. Pareto makes prude-reads beat lewd-reads, since both prefer that. The lewd's right makes lewd-reads beat nobody-reads. The prude's right makes nobody-reads beat prude-reads. There's no best option; the social ranking goes in a circle. The standard takeaway: you can't have unrestricted domain, Pareto, and even a sliver of individual liberty in one social choice rule.

I'm inclined to think that's a genuine tension between liberty and unanimous preference, not just a quirk of how economists write down 'rights.' So my question is: what would count against that view? I want a map of its exposure. What would have to be true for the paradox to be an artifact of modeling rights as preferences over final outcomes, rather than as constraints on what choices people actually get to make? Are there concrete setups where a society respects both Pareto and individual rights and nothing cycles, and would they refute the theorem or just show the conditions don't all bite at once? Which escape route is actually live — dropping transitivity, restricting admissible preferences, weakening Pareto, or reformulating rights as constraints or game forms — and which ones are merely logically possible but toothless? Be honest about which evidence would be decisive versus merely awkward, and tell me wether the standard conclusion is well insulated, and if it is, whether that's a problem for treating it as a substantive claim about liberty and welfare instead of a definitional artifact.
