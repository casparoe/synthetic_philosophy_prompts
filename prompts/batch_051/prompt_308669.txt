A couple of my friends got into a whole argument the other night about another friend of ours, Marcus. Marcus has hated his job for years but keeps saying he has no choice, because leaving would mean losing the health insurance his kid needs. We were talking about why he talks that way, and it escalated.

(1) Dana said it was a textbook case of Sartrean bad faith. The insurance issue is real, but it doesn't *determine* what Marcus does. He treats his situation like it's a force that shoves him into the job each morning, when really he's the one who keeps choosing to stay—free even inside a situation he didn't choose.

(2) Rob pushed back: so now a parent who stays in a job he hates for his kid is lying to himself? That's a wild standard. By that logic any hard choice can be redescribed as bad faith, because we could always technically do something else—technically he could abandon his family too, but nobody sane calls that a live option.

(3) Dana said that's not the point. Bad faith isn't that the constraint is fake; it's that Marcus is confusing his facticity with what follows from it. The kid's medical needs are facticity, the labor market is facticity, but facticity doesn't come with instructions. Saying "I can't leave" passes off his own refusal of the alternatives as a fact about the world.

(4) Rob said if that's the standard, half of ordinary talk is bad faith. When Marcus says "I can't," everyone understands he means "the other options are unacceptable to me," not that he's literally nailed to his desk. Turning a common idiom into a philosophical indictment feels like finding pathology wherever people speak normally.

(5) Dana answered that ordinary speech can still be bad faith when it hides the choice from the speaker himself. What tipped her off about Marcus is that he doesn't seem to mean it as shorthand. He means he actually has no freedom here, and that belief conveniently rescues him from ever having to ask whether he should reevaluate.

(6) Rob replied that maybe Marcus is just honest about his own motivations. He values the kid's stability above his own happiness, so relative to what he's willing to consider, leaving isn't an option. If that's a "choice," it's a choice with one live alternative, and it's strange to demand that everyone describe their life like an existentialist.

Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right about what? Also, are either of them getting Sartre wrong anywhere, and what are both of them missing?
