I have been rereading Origen’s On First Principles, Book IV, and trying to pin down exactly where his approach to Scripture diverges from modern literalism. Early in Book IV, Origen argues that the divine origin of the Jewish and Christian scriptures is shown by the fact that, unlike any merely human legislator or philosopher, their teaching won voluntary allegiance across nations. In Frederick Crombie’s translation he writes:

“No one, then, has been able to introduce and make known what seemed to himself the truth, among, I do not say many foreign nations, but even among the individuals of one single nation, in such a manner that a knowledge and belief of the same should extend to all.”

What interests me is the transition he then makes. Having established, to his own satisfaction, that the text is divinely inspired, Origen does not conclude that every sentence must be factually true in its plain sense. Instead, he proposes a criterion roughly of this sort: where the literal sense of an inspired text is impossible, internally contradictory, or unworthy of God, that defect was placed there deliberately as a signal to search for a spiritual sense, and the reader who refuses to do so falls into error.

Now suppose a contemporary theologian accepts Origen’s argument about the text’s divine authority, and even accepts something close to his rule about textual strain, but applies it very differently to Genesis 1. This theologian reads the six creation days as ordinary 24-hour periods, while acknowledging that the sun is not created until the fourth day. They resolve the difficulty by postulating a temporary non-solar light for the first three days, and they claim this preserves the literal truth of Genesis without abandoning divine authority. Is this position a genuinely Origenian application of the nonliteral criterion, or a fundamentalist refusal to apply it? Work through the relevant criteria one by one: where Origen sees an impossibility, whether a hard harmonization counts as finding the spiritual sense, whether unworthiness of God does any independent work here, and whether the theologian is seeking a hidden meaning or only repairing the plain sense. Then tell me whether this borderline case shows that Origen’s criteria underdetermine how much strain a reader must feel before moving from literalism to allegory.
