I'm taking a course this term on the governance of emerging technologies, and we have spent the past few weeks on the thesis that competition among AI developers imposes a systematic cost on safety. On the account we read, a developer that invests in caution — audits, slower deployment, redundant safeguards — pays an alignment tax in time, money, or capability, while a rival that skips those measures captures the market; the least cautious actor sets the pace, and safety becomes a luxury that competition punishes. I have been trying to test this framework against a non-AI case whose facts are public and well documented, because I suspect both the framework and the case look different under that transfer.

Consider Boeing's 737 MAX. In the 2010s, Boeing faced direct competitive pressure from Airbus and the A320neo, and the MAX was designed to match that aircraft's economics without forcing airlines into expensive new pilot training or a new type rating. The larger, repositioned engines changed the aircraft's handling under some flight conditions, so Boeing added the Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System (MCAS) to compensate. The system relied on a single angle-of-attack sensor input, was not explained in pilot manuals or initial training, and the differences from the previous generation of 737 were minimized through the certification process. After the Lion Air and Ethiopian Airlines crashes killed 346 people, regulators grounded the fleet, and the subsequent investigations attributed the outcome not only to the software itself but to delegated certification, schedule and cost pressure, and assumptions about how quickly pilots would respond.

Analyze the 737 MAX episode through the lens of the competitiveness constraint and the alignment tax. I want a disciplined mapping rather than a loose analogy: who are the players, what options and payoffs does the framework attribute to them, what does it say Boeing and the regulators faced, and what would it have predicted they would do? Then assess which features of the case the framework explains well and where it goes silent or misdescribes — regulatory capture, the accumulated monoculture of the 737 airframe, and internal corporate culture may not all reduce to competition, and the alignment tax was originally formulated for misaligned AI, not for a known engineering risk. Finally, run the case in the other direction: does the 737 MAX support the claim that competitive pressure enforces an alignment tax, or does it suggest that the framework needs to be stated more narrowly — perhaps because reputation, liability, and the eventual grounding did discipline the race after all?
