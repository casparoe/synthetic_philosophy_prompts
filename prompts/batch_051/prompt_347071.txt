Suppose you are a moral philosopher who specializes in reproductive ethics, including the debates over liberal eugenics and procreative beneficence, and I have brought you a pattern in ordinary judgment that I want you to adjudicate. Consider this asymmetry. A couple who uses prenatal testing and terminates a pregnancy because the fetus has a serious genetic condition such as Tay–Sachs is widely regarded as making a responsible, even compassionate choice, and many health systems subsidize such testing. But a couple who uses preimplantation genetic diagnosis to select an embryo because it is expected to be taller, to have higher cognitive ability, or to be male is likely to be seen as engaging in objectionable eugenics, even when the technology and the act of selection are otherwise quite similar. Some jurisdictions ban the second while funding the first. I want to know whether anything actually justifies this asymmetry, or whether it is a leftover intuition we should give up. Please take the strongest candidate justifications seriously—for instance, the distinction between treating disease and enhancing a child, between preventing harm and conferring benefit, or between environmental shaping such as schooling and genetic selection—and test each against nearby cases, including selecting against a disability versus selecting for a valued trait, using an elite sperm or egg donor versus selecting an embryo with the same expected trait, and spending heavily on tutors and music lessons versus selecting for similar traits. Then give an honest verdict: should we keep the asymmetry, revise it, or abandon it? Work through the strongest arguments on both sides before you conclude. I want your answer in continuous prose, like an email or a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
