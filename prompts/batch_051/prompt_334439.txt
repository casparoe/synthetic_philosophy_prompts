As a novelist, I keep returning to a structural echo between Simpson's paradox and the way a story's emotional through-line is read. By Simpson's paradox I mean the reversal or disappearance of a trend when groups that each display it are pooled, because the groups contribute unequal numbers and differ on some background factor. Take a novel in which, within every scene, two characters grow warmer — at the close of any scene they are closer than they were at its opening — yet the book as a whole leaves an experienced reader with the settled impression that the pair is drifting apart, because the scenes that begin warm are early and brief while the scenes that begin cold are late and long. The mapping I have in mind is this: scenes are the subgroups, the within-scene warming is the within-group trend, and the reader's impression of the entire arc is the pooled estimate, with scene length playing the role of group size and a scene's position in the book playing the role of the confounding factor. Is this a real structural identity or a superficial resemblance? I would like to know what would have to be true of scenes, narrative time, and readerly attention for the structure of Simpson's paradox genuinely to carry over; where the mapping gives out; and one thing the parallel predicts that should be false in actual reading.
