Here's something I keep going back and forth on. Jain philosophers hold that reality is anekāntavāda — many-sided — and that any statement is only true from some limited standpoint, a naya, so no single description of a thing captures the whole truth about it. But the same tradition also teaches syādvāda, the doctrine of conditional predication, and lays out a sevenfold scheme, saptabhaṅgī, that sure looks like a complete enumeration of the ways any predicate can apply: in some respect a thing is, in some respect it is not, in some respect it both is and is not, in some respect it is inexpressible, and then the remaining combinations. These two commitments seem to conflict. If every view is partial, what makes the sevenfold scheme the one exhaustive account of predication? And if the scheme is itself just one more partial standpoint, whats left of its authority? Give me the best reconciliations you can think of — different senses of partial, a first-order versus second-order distinction, the idea that the scheme is only a therapeutic or pedagogical device, whatever else — and then tell me which reading is actually honest and which is special pleading.
