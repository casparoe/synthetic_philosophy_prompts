So I cover brain–computer interfaces and the mental-privacy beat for a magazine, and I've ended up with a homemade term in my notebook that I keep using in every piece I draft, which is usually a sign I should have someone kick the tires on it before I fillet it in print. I call it "draft cognition." The idea is that most of what a BCI or even a consumer headset records is not finished thought: it's the neural equivalent of a sentence you typed and deleted, the half-formed intention to say the thing you'd never say, the association that flashes and gets suppressed before it becomes an impulse. Privacy law and the mental-privacy debate, as far as I can tell, mostly treat all of this as the most sensitive content imaginable — reading the diary. But I think that's the wrong frame, for an almost opposite reason: because its draft, it deserves a different kind of protection, not just more of the same. My analogy is a journalist's notebook: unpublished material gets protection precisely because it isn't finalized, and treating it as the person's settled view would be a kind of defamation of the self. So the loose principle goes: the less a thought is endorsed, the weaker its evidential force about who you are, but the stronger the protection against having it mistaken for an endorsed thought.

Suppose a headset picks up the motor-cortex flicker of a word you swallowed before saying it to your partner: not your decision, but not just noise either. I think ordinary users feel that this is more theirs than their published opinions, while the engineers and lawyers keep treating it as less theirs, and that gap is why people feel violated even after they've clicked consent. I've been calling the two horns the "draft ownership" problem: either drafts are less yours, so companies can mine them freely; or they're more yours, the way unfinished work is more private. What I want checked: is "draft vs. final thought" actually doing any work, or is it a metaphor with a law-review sheen? What's the strongest friendly version of the principle, and where does it break — e.g., could a prosecutor argue a plan was just a draft fantasy and never admissible, or, worse, use draft-level data to prove someone's real but never-expressed bias? Give me the three best objections and whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable.
