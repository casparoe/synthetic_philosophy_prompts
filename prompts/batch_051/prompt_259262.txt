You are a professional philosopher working in metaphysics and the philosophy of music. Please think through the following problem carefully. Begin by brainstorming the strongest versions of both the theoretical position and the competing intuition before you diagnose the conflict; the task is a negotiated diagnosis, not a verdict for one side.

I find a broadly type-theoretic account of musical works compelling. On this view, a musical work is an abstract structural type, and the many performances we attend—and, on some versions, the recordings we listen to—are its tokens or instances. The view explains several things that any account should explain: performances with different sounds, instruments, and interpretive choices can all be of one and the same work; the work is not identical to any particular score or performance; and the work is not destroyed if a score burns or every recording is lost. It is unified and economical, and it avoids treating a work as a scattered object spread across times and places.

Yet I also have a firm, unshaken intuition that composers create their works. It seems to me that before a composer finishes a symphony, that symphony does not exist, and that the act of composition brings something new into being. If every score, part, and recording of a work is lost, I want to say that the work itself can be lost, not merely that it becomes undiscoverable. But on the straightforward version of the type theory, the relevant type exists independently of the act of composition, so the composer would have discovered or selected a pre-existing structure rather than created anything. That strikes me as getting something central about music wrong—or at least as paying too high a price for theoretical tidiness.

I do not want a verdict on which side wins. I want to know what it would take for each side to give, and whether there is a reading on which both survive. If the type theory is basically right, what would it take for the creation intuition to be the thing that yields—would I need to be shown that the intuition is really tracking something else, such as the act of first tokening, specifying, or making a structure publicly available? If the intuition should hold, what would it take for the theory to be amended—would we need a notion of abstract objects that can be brought into existence by an act of indication or determination, so that a work is a type as indicated by a particular historical act of composition? Or is there a reading of 'creation' and 'existence' on which both sides survive, and how would I tell whether that reading is a genuine reconciliation rather than a merely verbal settlement? Be explicit about which facts of musical practice each option preserves and which it gives up, and about what further considerations—conceptual, aesthetic, or empirical—could decide among them.
