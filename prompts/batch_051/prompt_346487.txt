I keep replaying an argument my roommate made during a campus fight about whether the pro-choice and pro-life student groups should strike a deal—say, the pro-choice group supports expanded adoption funding if the pro-life group supports funded contraception. He claims the logic generalizes to nearly every moral conflict, and that if you deny that, you're just dressing up a preference as a principle. I think he's proving to much, but I can't find the bad step. Here's the argument as he gives it:

(1) If two sides in a moral dispute each judge, by their own current values, that a package of concessions leaves them better off than the status quo, then each has decisive reason to accept it.

(2) For nearly any real moral dispute, such a package exists. A moral view is a bundle of concerns with different weights; wherever two parties weight things differently, there's a swap: each concedes a bit on a thing it weights less to gain on a thing it weights more. By their own lights, both come out ahead.

(3) So, for nearly any real moral dispute, each side has decisive reason to accept some compromise.

(4) Therefore someone who rejects every compromise in such a dispute is either acting against her own values or assigning an overriding weight to not making deals with the other side. Either way, it's ordinary trade-off behavior—so there's no principled limit on compromise, only a (possibly very high) price.

(5) But then take a climate group offered a permanent rainforest reserve in exchange for backing a new pipeline, or a union offered a raise in exchange for giving up the right to strike. By this reasoning, if they refuse, they're just weighting 'not dealing with this opponent' above the concrete good. That feels wrong. Those refusals look less like trade-offs and more like treating something as not for sale.

(6) When I say that, my roommate replies that 'not for sale' is just another item in the bundle of concerns, and premise (1) already includes it. That's exactly the move I can't escape.

So which step goes wrong, if any does? I'd most like to keep the thought that the campus adoption/contraception swap is a genuine mutual improvement, but I can't accept that everyone is rationally required to bargain over everything. What do I give up if I reject the step you'd reject?
