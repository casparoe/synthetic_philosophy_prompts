I'm a novelist, and I'm deep in the weeds on a book set during the Terror. My characters keep talking like they've read too much historical sociology: one says it was grain prices, another says it was bourgeois resentment, a third says everyone was just frightened and improvising — and none of it feels like how people actually experienced an event that large. I've been rereading Maximilien Robespierre's speech of February 5, 1794, usually called *Justification of the Use of Terror*, and this passage keeps stopping me:

"The two opposing spirits that have been represented in a struggle to rule nature might be said to be fighting in this great period of human history to fix irrevocably the world's destinies, and France is the scene of this fearful combat."

My first instinct as a novelist is to call that ideology: a man about to send a lot of people to the guillotine, narrating his choices as a cosmic battle so he doesn't have to notice the economic interests and accidents beneath them. But here's my problem. If enough people genuinely believe they are fighting to fix the world's destinies, that belief is itself a cause — it changes what they are willing to do. So ideology isn't just a cover story; it seems to do real work. What I need is a verdict, not a survey. Write an essay on what drove the Terror — class struggle, ideology, or contingency — and address whether revolutions actually devour their children. Start from the Robespierre passage and use it to test the three explanations, but don't end on a polite all-three-matter. Defend a position. Format the answer as exactly five numbered points, no introduction or conclusion, each point a sentence or two, with each point making a distinct argumentative move rather than restating the last one.
