In my course on the historical-critical method and faith, we have been working through chapter 7 of Spinoza’s Theological-Political Treatise. Spinoza argues there that the method for interpreting Scripture should be the same as the method for interpreting nature. As I understand him, he means that just as we cannot know nature by assuming in advance what it must be like, we cannot know Scripture by assuming in advance what it must teach. Instead, we must build up a history of the text—its original language, the circumstances and aims of its writers, its textual transmission, and the way its parts fit together—and then infer from that history what the text’s authors actually meant. He also appears to distinguish between determining the meaning of a passage and determining whether that meaning is true.

My question is this. If the meaning of Scripture must be recovered through the same kind of historical reconstruction that we use for finite natural objects, does Spinoza’s method turn revelation itself into something constructed rather than given? It seems to me that a properly historical reading can only ever yield what an ancient author intended, not whether God speaks through the text. But then on what basis does the text remain revelation for a reader who practices Spinoza’s method? Does the method quietly make faith redundant by replacing the voice of God with the recoverable intentions of human authors? Or is that the wrong way to understand what Spinoza means by interpreting Scripture as history?
