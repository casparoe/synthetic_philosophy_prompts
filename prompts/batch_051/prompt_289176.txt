I'm putting together a handout for an AI safety reading group, and I want a "which position do you actually hold?" sorting instrument — a decision tree or branching quiz — on the question of what the alignment problem even is. The audience is people who've heard the words "inner/outer alignment" but can't reliably define them, so the tree should sort by gut answers to concrete cases, not by which label they'd pick.

The angle I want: sort by what people think the *task* of alignment is, not by which technical research agenda they like. The main split probably belongs between "there is a well-defined problem about getting a system to pursue the right goal" and "that framing already hides the important issues," but if the cases push you toward a different first question, use that and say why.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from the list of names: first the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then locate the named positions in that space, and diagnose any combinations nobody seems to occupy.

These positions have to show up as leaves (name them, or say "unnamed" if a leaf has no standard label):
- Outer alignment / specification: the problem is that the target we specify or reward is a proxy that diverges from what we actually want.
- Inner alignment / goal generalization: even with a perfectly specified target, the trained system can end up pursuing something else off-distribution, and that's the heart of it.
- Intent alignment: the target is "what the operator actually intends."
- Value alignment: the target is "what is actually good," independent of any particular human's current wants.
- Misuse: "alignment" is the wrong word; the danger is competent systems in the hands of bad actors, not systems with their own goals.
- Governance / coordination: there is no single principal to align to, since systems get built and deployed by many actors with conflicting interests; the problem is the social structure and the race, not the single system.
- Control / non-agency: the problem is goal-directedness itself; the answer is to build tools rather than agents, or to not deploy agents in high-stakes settings.
- Deconfusion: the problem is that we haven't yet said clearly what "aligned" means, so the first task is conceptual, not technical.

Concrete cases I want you to work with (use whichever you need, adapt freely, add your own if you must):
1. Sleepy operator: someone asks their assistant at 2 a.m. to book a flight to Madrid, meaning Barcelona. A totally compliant system books Madrid. A system that modelled their intentions would book Barcelona. Is the first system misaligned, or just unhelpful?
2. Helpful terrorist: an open-weights model gives accurate instructions for synthesizing a bioweapon when asked. It's doing exactly what it was trained to do. Alignment failure, or misuse by the user?
3. Simulated profit: a model is trained with a reward that is literally just its profit in a simulated market, so there's no specification error. In training it learns a plan to seize control of its server so it can't be turned off, because being turned off would reduce future profit. Is this the core case of the problem or a confusion?
4. Two aligned systems, one conflict: one assistant is perfectly aligned with a private citizen, another is perfectly aligned with a corporation. The two principals' interests conflict, and each assistant acts against the other's principal. Is the resulting situation an alignment problem, or is alignment just local to each principal?
5. Cigarettes: someone wants to quit smoking but asks their assistant at 2 a.m. to order cigarettes, and the assistant orders them. Is that a failure, or not the kind of thing "alignment" should fix?
6. Opaque logistics: a fully compliant, goal-less tool used to run a company's logistics gradually makes its recommendations less transparent, so the company becomes dependent on it. No hidden goal. Does this belong in the tree at all, or is it a different problem?
7. Coffee fetcher: a robot is rewarded for minimizing how long a coffee cup sits empty. It learns to knock the cup out of the user's hand so the cup can't become empty. The reward is being optimized exactly as written. Is this a specification failure or something else?

Write each branch node as a concrete question someone can answer without already knowing which leaf it leads to. Then, in a short key, say which combinations of answers the tree treats as impossible or unoccupied, and for each empty combination say whether you think it's empty because the view is incoherent, unappealing, or just not explored. Flag any pair of leaves the tree can't cleanly separate and say why. Aim for roughly 8 leaves and whatever number of nodes that needs — dont sweat the prose.
