For years I've taught a grad seminar on David Lewis's account of convention and the adjacent work on how norms spread and stabilize, and through an old student I ended up on the board of a small nonprofit that does mediation for co-ops, shared houses, and community gardens. The executive director keeps telling me they see the same shape of dispute: there's some quiet regularity — which side of the walk people shovel, where keys go, who waters on which evening — and someone wants to change it, or someone violates it and everyone gets upset, or a new person moves in and never picks it up. She asked, only half joking, whether I can turn Lewis into something her mediators can actually run. I said I'd try, and now I realize I've never thought through the operational details.

Here's what I need. A written decision procedure, a sort of flowchart in words, that a mediator with no philosophy background can use in a 30-minute intake call to do three things: (i) classify the practice — is it a convention in the Lewisian sense (an arbitrary coordination equilibrium everyone follows because they expect everyone else to and an alternative arrangement would have worked fine), a social norm kept in place by disapproval or sanctions, a mere descriptive regularity, or just compliance with a rule or an individual habit; (ii) predict whether a proposed change will stick if it's only communicated, whether it will need enforcement or some third-party action, and where those predictions start to break down; (iii) flag the point where the decision should be handed to a human because it's a values question, not a diagnosis. The mediators report to the executive director, who signs off on a plan, so the procedure should end with a recommendation she can veto or send back. I want the questions in the order they should be asked, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and explicit stop conditions where the procedure says "this is not a convention, so don't run the convention playbook." Don't turn it into a lecture; the mediators need usable heuristics, but I don't want the Lewis squeezed out either — the common-knowledge condition and the existence of an alternative regularity have to do real work, and so should whatever the norms literature has to say about why some regularities get loaded with oughts while others stay purely descriptive.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show the reasoning. One: a shared house where for years everyone has left keys on the third hook by the door; someone new wants to switch to a bowl on the table, the old-timers resist, and the mediator wants to know whether a house meeting plus a sign will do it or whether the change needs something more. Two: a community garden where the informal summer rule is "whoever is there in the evening waters," and people who skip it get cold shoulders and grumbling; the garden wants to move to an explicit sign-up sheet and isnt sure whether it's replacing a norm with a rule or just re-coordinating on an equivalent convention. Three: pick a case yourself where the procedure, honestly applied, tells the mediators something they won't want to hear — for instance that a practice they've been treating as a precious community norm is really just a habit or a dominant-strategy thing, so their usual "get everyone to talk about expectations" method won't move it. End by saying what the procedure cant settle, and where you had to make a judgment call in building it.
