I've been reading a popular-science book about symmetry in physics, and it lays out Noether's theorem in one tidy paragraph:

"Noether's theorem says that for every symmetry of a system's equations there is a corresponding conserved quantity. If you shift an experiment to a different place and the physics doesn't change, momentum is conserved. If you rotate the apparatus, angular momentum is conserved. If the laws don't depend on when the experiment is performed, energy is conserved. And the gauge symmetry of electromagnetism gives you conservation of electric charge. That is what physicists mean when they say symmetry explains conservation laws: the symmetry of the underlying equations is the reason the corresponding quantity is conserved."

I recognize the broad picture, but a few things make me uneasy. I vaguely remember that not everything physicists call a "symmetry" — like the parity operation — comes with a conserved charge in this way, and I'm unsure whether the step from "the symmetry is mathematically tied to a conservation law" to "the symmetry explains the conservation law" is something a careful foundations-of-physics person would accept. Could you tell me which parts of this paragraph are accurate, which are quietly conflated or overstated, and whether the closing claim about explanation survives once the details are corrected?
