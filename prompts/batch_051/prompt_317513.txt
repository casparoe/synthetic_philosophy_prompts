You are a professional philosopher who works on the philosophy of mind and language, with particular expertise in Gilbert Ryle's account of category mistakes. Please think slowly and carefully: before you give your final analysis, brainstorm at least three distinct ways of interpreting the practice described below, weigh them against one another, and then say which interpretation seems most defensible and why.

I have been puzzling over a practice in experimental psychology and its popular reporting. Neuroimaging studies and decision experiments are routinely described with psychological verbs whose grammatical subject is an organ rather than a person: a study is summarized as showing that the brain decides, that the brain inhibits an impulse, that the brain anticipates a reward, that the brain lies, or that the brain is surprised. In a famous line of experiments, subjects are asked to flex a wrist or press a button at a time of their own choosing, and the results are standardly reported as a neural signal building up before the subject becomes aware of having decided, so that the brain decides before the person does. Yet the very same institutions that fund and circulate these descriptions — courts, schools, psychiatric clinics, ordinary moral practice — go on attributing deciding, knowing, lying, and being surprised to whole people, and they hold people answerable for what they decide and do. A friend in neuroscience tells me I am overthinking this: person talk and brain talk, he says, are just two levels of description of the same events. I am not convinced that response is philosophically innocent.

What I want to know is what this reporting practice presupposes about the mind. Does it presuppose a particular theory of mental terms — for instance, that they ultimately denote processes inside the skull, so that person-level attributions are merely a loose convenience? Or does the practice commit something like Ryle's category mistake, by taking predicates whose primary home is the whole person and applying them to one part of the person, thereby smuggling a hidden agent back into the picture in the very vocabulary that was supposed to exorcise it? Reconstruct the implicit philosophy of mind that the practice presupposes, but be careful to distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and to identify where it is philosophically underdetermined — that is, where several different theories of mind would license exactly the same headlines. Then explain what would have to change in scientific reporting, and in our picture of the agent, if we replaced that implicit theory with a Rylean account on which mental predicates are true primarily of whole persons in virtue of their dispositions rather than true of their parts. In particular, would a consistent Rylean have to object to every sentence of the form the brain does such-and-such, or only to a certain way of understanding such sentences?
