I've developed a policy for arguments about the fate of countries and empires, and I'm not sure it's anything more than a bias with a spreadsheet. Whenever someone confidently predicts that a country is in terminal decline, or has peaked, or will break apart within the next generation, I ask them to go backwards before going forwards: pick the reference class of past states they think this one belongs to, and tell me how many of those actually had the trajectory they're predicting. If they can't produce a base rate, I more or less discount the forecast to zero, no matter how good their specific story is. Last month a housemate gave a long, careful argument that the United States is entering a period of accelerating institutional decay that ends in some kind of crackup, and I dismissed it because he couldnt tell me the historical frequency of rich democracies decaying into crackups. My other housemate says I'm the one being irrational: he says the outside view looks objective, but the whole action is in choosing who counts as a relevant analogue — late-republic Rome? 1970s Britain? the USSR? the Habsburgs? — and since I get to choose, my base rate is just my conclusion rounded to a percentage.

So: is this rule defensible as an epistemic policy? When does the outside view genuinely beat the inside story, and when does it just move the storytelling one level up? Is it right to discount a specific forecast to near zero just because nobody has computed a base rate, or should the absence of a base rate only be one factor among others? And is there a version of the rule that preserves the anti-narrative discipline without letting me rig the comparison class — and how would I tell, from the inside, whether I'm using the honest version or the rigged one?
