I'm a novelist, working on a courtroom thing, and I keep noticing a pattern in group decisions that I don't have a word for. It goes like this. Take a dozen people who have to get a yes/no question right—whether the river water is safe, whether the accused did it. If each person, answering seperately, is just a little better than a coin flip, then a majority vote makes the group almost always right once the group is big enough. But if each person is just a little worse than a coin flip—say they all share a small bias in the same direction—then the exact same majority vote makes the group almost always wrong, and more wrong the bigger it gets. Same counting rule, opposite outcomes, and nothing visible in the room tells you which case your in.

The part that really snags me is the overlap between people's mistakes. In real life, people's errors correlate. If everyone in the room has the same blind spot, adding more of them doesn't just fail to help; it makes them more unanimous and more confidently wrong. But I've gathered that correlation isn't automatically bad—when people are correlated because they're all picking up on the same real signal, it helps. So whether a bigger group drives toward truth or away from it seems to depend on something the group can't observe from inside. I'm after a name for the whole two-sided pattern: a decision rule that pushes a majority toward certainty regardless of whether the initial tilt points at truth or error, with correlation either rescuing it or poisoning it. Wisdom of crowds only covers the sunny half. If there's no settled name for the whole pattern, I'd rather have a sharp name and a definition with actual edges than a list of near-misses.
