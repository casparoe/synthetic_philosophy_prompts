I am drawn to the philosopher David Lewis's account of penal substitution, and I want a negotiated verdict on the conflict it produces for me.

As I understand Lewis's proposal: the doctrine that Christ suffered the punishment for our sins should not be read literally, as the claim that an innocent person was really punished in the place of the guilty. That would be juridically repellent and arguably not substitution at all. Lewis's alternative is that penal substitution is a divinely authorized game of make-believe. Christ's suffering counts as our punishment within a fiction God in some sense accepts or institutes; the claims that our guilt was transferred to Christ, that Christ paid our debt, and that justice was satisfied are not literally true but true-in-that-fiction, and God treats them as if they were true for the purposes of reconciliation. This tempts me because it avoids the moral scandal of punishing the innocent while still explaining how substitution language can function; it dissolves the mysterious idea of literally transferred guilt; and it assimilates atonement to the familiar, well-understood phenomenon of counting-as in games of make-believe, where we already know how such truths work. Systematic economy, moral cleanliness, and explanatory continuity all pull me toward it.

But the intuition on the other side will not yield. I am convinced that atonement, if it occurs at all, must be robustly real. If the Cross merely takes place inside a story God agrees to pretend is true, then it seems my guilt has not been dealt with but only narrated around; a divinely authorized pretense is not the sort of thing that can satisfy justice or remove guilt. The intuition is not that I want the doctrine to be dramatic, but that the fiction account changes the subject: I asked how atonement actually occurs, and I have been given an account of how we may speak as if it occurred. It is like being told a debt is canceled because the bank has agreed to pretend that a payment was made.
