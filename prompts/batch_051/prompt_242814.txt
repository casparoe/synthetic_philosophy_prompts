I am a retired research physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of science seriously, and I would be grateful for help assessing something I read. A popular article I came across summarizes Paul Feyerabend's position roughly as follows.

Feyerabend's Against Method is said to argue that there is no such thing as the scientific method. The historical record, according to the article, shows that successful scientists repeatedly violated the standard rules of method — induction, falsification, the prohibition on ad hoc adjustments — and often made progress precisely by violating them. The central example is Galileo, who is supposed to have prevailed through rhetoric, selective presentation of evidence, and a good deal of propaganda rather than through any shared procedure. Feyerabend is then said to conclude that, methodologically, anything goes: there are no rules of scientific method at all, and one research strategy is no better or worse than another. Finally, the article draws the further conclusion that, since science has no special method, it has no special epistemic authority either; astrology or traditional medicine is therefore no worse off than physics, and the state has no business giving science a privileged place in education.

My own instinct, formed by a career in the laboratory, is that the historical observations are probably sound but that the slide from no single method to no standards at all is suspicious, and that the step about astrology may be a caricature. But I do not know Feyerabend's texts well enough to say exactly where the unfairness lies.

Could you go through this summary claim by claim and tell me what is fair to Feyerabend, what is a common overstatement or misreading, and what a careful Feyerabend scholar would object to? In particular, I would like to know whether the article's conclusions about standards and about astrology actually follow from what Feyerabend established, or whether they rest on a conflation of distinct claims.
