I'm a handling editor at a math journal, and a couple times a year I get a submission where the main theorem rests on a computation that no single human being could check by hand — a SAT-solver exhaustion, a massive interval-arithmetic certification, a huge symbolic computation, or lately a neural network that found a candidate counterexample or a key inequality. I want a reusable procedure, call it a decision checklist, for these submissions. I have maybe two or three days per paper, I'm not a software engineer and not trained in formal verification, and my recommendation goes to the chief editor who mostly trusts me but wants a defensible reason either way. The stakes are the usual for a journal: a false accept is a public embarrassment and possibly a retraction, a false reject sends the authors somewhere else and delays the field.

I've read the standard hand-wringing about the four-color theorem and the Kepler conjecture, and I take the real worry to be not just that code has bugs but that an unsurveyable proof isn't the kind of thing a mathematical community verifies the way it verifies a normal proof — the theorem ends up certified rather than explained, and nobody quite knows what epistemic state the community is in. I'd like that philosophical worry translated into actual steps, not a position paper. What should I demand from authors before I even send the paper out? What should I ask referees to do, and what referee claims should I actually trust? When does an independent reimplementation settle the matter, and when is it just the same mistake made twice? How much does formal verification of the code help, and what does it leave open? How should the procedure change when the computation is one small load-bearing lemma versus when the entire theorem is a case split with ten million cases and no conceptual idea visible? And where in the checklist am I just making a judgment call dressed up as scrutiny — which steps can no amount of process eliminate?

Then apply the procedure to two or three realistic invented submissions, one of which should push toward a decision I'm going to hate, and end with an honest paragraph on what the procedure cannot tell me, for instance whether the theorem is true as opposed to merely checked by some particular machine. No headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables please — just write it as continuous prose, like an email, first step to last, because I'll be pasting it into a note for the other editors.
