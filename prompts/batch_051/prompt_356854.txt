I've been turning over the cluelessness objection to consequentialism — the worry that, because our acts set off chains of consequences we cannot trace, we never really know what we are doing when we judge an act by its results. I'm not interested in the familiar response that we can know the likely effects of ordinary, everyday actions. I want to know whether the objection can be run as a tight argument rather than left as a vague unease. I've tried to compress it into numbered steps, and I keep going back and forth about whether it is sound.

Here is the argument as I would put it:

(1) The moral status of an act, according to consequentialism, is fixed by the total value of all its consequences — not merely the consequences salient to the agent at the time of choosing.
(2) Consequences ramify without any natural stopping point: every consequence has further consequences, and those have further ones, and so on.
(3) The aggregate of an act's consequences beyond any tractable horizon is overwhelmingly larger than, and potentially opposite in sign to, the near-term consequences we can actually assess.
(4) Regarding those beyond-the-horizon consequences, we have no more reason to expect net good than net harm, and no principled way to bound their magnitude or probability.
(5) Therefore, for any pair of available acts, we are never in a position to judge which act has the greater total value once all consequences are included.
(6) So consequentialism fails as a decision procedure because it gives no usable ranking of acts, and it fails as a criterion of rightness because it makes decisive a property we are in principle unable to identify.

I suspect (4) is doing something in the neighborhood of applying a principle of indifference to an unbounded and unestimated space of possibilities, but I cannot tell whether that is the only problem, or whether the other steps carry their own load of equivocation — for example, between consequentialism as a criterion of rightness and consequentialism as a decision procedure, or between being unable to know the actual total value and having no rational best estimate given one's evidence. So: is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what is the strongest reading of that step on which it fails? And is there a repaired version of consequentialism that still yields guidance once the bad step is fixed?

One further request, so the answer does not settle too comfortably into a verdict: tell me which consideration would most easily change your diagnosis — for instance, an argument that the step you blame can be rebuilt rather than discarded, or a reason to think the criterion/decision-procedure distinction cannot bear the weight the repair places on it. I would like the point where your reasoning is most exposed to be flagged explicitly.
