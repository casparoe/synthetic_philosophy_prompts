I have just come away from a long argument with a friend about whether acknowledging that large historical events are overdetermined should undermine confidence in singular causal explanations of them — the sort that says the war happened because of the alliance system, because of the assassination, or because of the decisions taken in the weeks before it. His line was that once two or more distinct causes would each have been sufficient on their own, 'because of X' becomes arbitrary: honest history would either have to list everything or give up causal talk in favour of narrative description. I pushed back, but the argument revealed that neither of us could actually state what a causal explanation of a complex historical outcome is answerable to once causes are multiple, overlapping, and redundant.

So before either of us changes position, I want the specification first. Write the desiderata that an adequate account of multi-causal explanation in history must meet: the cases it has to handle, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the constraints it must respect — with the case or consideration behind each. The purpose is not a theory of causation in general, but a standard for judging whether ordinary singular causal explanations in history ('it happened because of X') can be defended once overdetermination is common.

In particular, I want to see how your list handles the following, if you agree they belong on it:

(1) an outcome for which two or more causes were each sufficient, so that the but-for test fails for each individually;

(2) a cause that was not sufficient on its own but was a necessary part of the only available sufficient combination;

(3) a condition that merely enabled the action of the causes we cite and is normally excluded from the explanation;

(4) the difference between explaining that the event happened and explaining its timing;

(5) the fact that which single cause we pick as 'the' cause often depends on the contrast the historian has implicitly chosen.

For each, say why it earns its place as a requirement rather than a stylistic preference.

Then I want two further things. First, mark which requirements are non-negotiable constraints on any acceptable account and which are really preferences of particular schools of history-writing. Second, say whether any subset of the non-negotiable requirements is jointly unsatisfiable; my suspicion after this evening's argument is that the but-for test and the requirement to explain overdetermined outcomes without arbitrariness are in direct tension, and if that is so I want it stated as a result about the list itself rather than as a victory for my friend. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most to rule out the familiar ways of doing causal explanation in history. Keep the weight on the requirements and their motivating cases; a short scorecard at the end will do.
