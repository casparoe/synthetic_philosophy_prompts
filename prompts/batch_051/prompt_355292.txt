Consider an account of the doctrine of the Trinity on which the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit are numerically distinct persons, yet each is numerically identical with the one God. Such an account is threatened by the principle that identicals are indiscernible: if each person is identical with the one God, then the symmetry and transitivity of identity make the persons identical with one another, and the indiscernibility of identicals forces every predicate true of any one person to be true of the others. Rather than rehearse this general problem in the abstract, give a specific, worked example of it by selecting a predication that is true of one of the three persons and not of another—for instance, a predication involving being begotten, proceeding, or becoming incarnate—and show, step by step, how the indiscernibility of identicals appears to transfer that predication to a person of whom it is not true. Then identify which premise or rule of inference in the argument is most plausibly rejected, and briefly assess one significant cost of rejecting it.
