For a seminar on practical rationality I have been trying to work out whether the hyperbolic-discounting model of preference reversal dissolves, or merely relocates, the problem of akrasia. Consider the following argument, which I find plausible but suspect contains an error.

(1) An agent is weak-willed only if, at the moment she acts, she acts against her own all-things-considered judgement about what it is best to do.
(2) In the standard hyperbolic-discounting reversal, an agent who at a distance prefers a larger-later reward (say, £130 in thirteen months) over a smaller-sooner one (£100 in twelve months) comes, as the earlier reward becomes imminent, to prefer the smaller-sooner reward.
(3) In such reversals the shift in preference is most naturally read as a shift in the agent's all-things-considered judgement: at the moment of choice she judges the imminent smaller reward better than the delayed larger one.
(4) It follows that, at the moment she chooses the smaller-sooner reward, the agent is acting on her present all-things-considered judgement.
(5) Therefore, to the extent that a person's conduct fits the hyperbolic-discounting pattern, she is not weak-willed; she is merely acting on a time-indexed judgement of what is best.

Assess this argument. Is it valid? If not, identify the earliest step at which it fails and explain how that step is doing illicit work. If it is valid, state which premise must be accepted for the conclusion to follow and assess whether it is true on its strongest reading.
