In the 1611 preface "The Translators to the Reader," the King James translators answer critics who objected to the marginal notes that give alternative readings and renderings. The passage I keep coming back to runs: "Some peradventure would have no variety of senses to be set in the margin, lest the authority of the Scriptures for deciding of controversies by that show of uncertainty, should somewhat be shaken. But we hold their judgment not to be so sound in this point .... doth not a margin do well to admonish the reader to seek further, and not to conclude or dogmatize upon this or that peremptorily?" I want to use this passage in a debate over whether a translated text can be inspired. The arguments I already know and don't need repeated are these: (1) inspiration attaches primarily to the doctrine or message, and alternative wording in the margin does not touch the substance; (2) the Septuagint precedent: New Testament authors quote a Greek translation as Scripture, so a version with known divergences from the Hebrew can still bear divine authority; (3) the margin is not an admission of error but a teaching device, training readers against false precision; (4) a translation can be inspired derivatively, the way a faithful sermon can be God's word in a secondary sense; and (5) the Holy Spirit's providential preservation of the church's received Bible includes even its marginal variants. I want one more argument that is not on this list and not a rewording, special case, or combination of anything on it, one that draws on the translators' own words in the passage. Then tell me honestly whether that new argument is any good, and whether it strengthens or weakens the case for the stronger KJV-onlyist claim that the KJV itself is uniquely inspired.
