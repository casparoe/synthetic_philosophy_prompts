I have been working through the distinction at the center of the modern Stoic revival — the dichotomy of control, the division between what is up to us and what is not up to us — and an argument I keep building from it seems to show that the distinction is either empty or useless. I need help finding the faulty step.

(1) Epictetus divides everything into two classes: what is up to us, which he lists as judgment, impulse, desire, and aversion, and what is not up to us, which he lists as body, property, reputation, and office. (2) For this division to do the work the Stoics ask of it — telling us which things to care about and which to relinquish — being up to us must mean something like being under our control. (3) A thing is under our control only if whether it occurs does not depend on factors outside us. (4) But our judgments, impulses, desires, and aversions arise from impressions, temperament, habit, and the way the world presents itself to us; they are causally dependent on factors outside us, whether or not they also involve our assent. (5) So by that standard nothing falls cleanly on the up-to-us side; the first class is empty. (6) If we retreat to a weaker sense of up to us — say, susceptible to influence by our choices — then much of the second class, such as health, finances, and career, also turns out to be up to us, since our choices clearly influence those too. (7) Therefore the dichotomy either empties the first class or swells it until the boundary disappears; in either form it cannot justify the counsel to concern ourselves only with what is up to us. (8) But that counsel is precisely what gives the modern revival its practical appeal. So the central distinction of the revival is either false or practically useless.

I would most like to keep the idea that there is a real line between what is ours and what merely happens to us, and the practical advice that follows from drawing it; I am less attached to the causal standard in step (3). But I do not want to simply declare that step the problem and walk away. Which step should I reject, why did it look so natural, and what exactly does rejecting it cost for the rest of Stoic practice?
