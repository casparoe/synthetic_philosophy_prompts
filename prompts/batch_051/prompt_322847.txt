Here's a messy one I've been chewing on in my philosophy of religion seminar, and I'd like a second pass at it rather than a survey of the literature. I've been doing a unit on whether 'religion' is a coherent category—half the students want a checklist, the other half want to say it's all family resemblance and politics—and I've found my own verdicts on particular cases dont hang together. I'd like you to (a) try to extract a principle (or a small set) that captures my verdicts, (b) tell me explicitly which verdicts it can't capture and which you'd overturn, and (c) apply it to three new cases I'll list.

The cases and my honest reactions:

1. Theravada Buddhism as lived in rural Thailand—monks, alms rounds, merit-making, temple festivals. Obviously a religion. If this isn't one, nothing is.

2. A Unitarian Universalist congregation: no creed, no required supernatural belief, but Sunday services, hymns, moral community, lifecycle ceremonies. I want to say yes, but only just. The form does a lot of work when the content is thin.

3. A solitary American who sits two hours of vipassana a day, reads the Pali canon seriously, and flatly disavows rebirth and karma. I don't call this a religion. I call it a practice, or a philosophy. But doctrinally it's got more going on than case 2, which makes me uneasy.

4. Confucian ancestor rites kept by a family in Taiwan: food offerings, bowing, divination blocks, but when you ask they say they don't know whether the dead are really there—it's about remembering and being a family the right way. I find myself saying 'religious practice without religious belief.' So yes in one register, no in another, and I refuse to say it's not a religion full stop.

5. American civil religion—the Fourth of July, Lincoln Memorial as a site of veneration, the founding documents treated as sacred, inaugurations as civic liturgy. I'm fine calling it a civil religion, but if you ask whether it's a religion in the plain sense, I dodge.

6. State Shinto in imperial Japan: state-run shrines, emperor veneration, a ritual calendar, with the government officially insisting it was not religion but civic duty. I think the government was wrong, maybe deliberately. It's a religion.

7. A tiny sun-worshipping group: twelve people, no scripture, no moral code, just songs at dawn, offerings of flowers, and a calendar of feast and fast days. I say yes—thin, but unmistakably a religion.

8. A hardcore soccer fandom: chants, relics (match-worn shirts kept like relics), pilgrimages to away games, an origin story, intense belonging. I refuse to call this a religion, but I can't say what the sun group has that this lacks. That specific failure bothers me most.

9. Alcoholics Anonymous: twelve steps, a 'higher power' you may interpret, confession-like sharing, regular meetings, explicit language of powerlessness and dependence. Pulls me apart. Functionally it feels religious; taxonomically I want to say no, or maybe 'para-religion.'

My half-formed principle is: religion requires a community orienting life around something treated as sacred, through ritual that isn't purely instrumental. That handles 1, 5-ish, 6, and 7, but it makes 8 hard to exclude, and it makes 3 hard to exclude only by leaning on 'community'—and then what do I do about a genuine solitary mystic?

So: give me a principle or small set that treats these verdicts respectfully, say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and which one you'd overturn first. Then apply it to three cases I haven't listed: a parody religion with a real congregation that disavows its own claims (Pastafarianism-style), a strict vegan community with food taboos, communal feasts and a strong sense of obligation, and a secular moral movement with heroes, regular gatherings and a mission. What does the account say about each, and does that feel like the right result? If the honest answer is that no principle works and 'religion' is just a political artifact, im prepared to hear that—but I want it argued, not announced.
