Immanuel Kant’s sixth preliminary article in Perpetual Peace argues that a state at war must not permit acts of hostility that would make mutual confidence in a future peace impossible; he lists as examples the use of assassins, poisoners, breach of capitulation, and incitement to treason in the opposing state. What he does not extend the prohibition to is every form of deception: ordinary military practice and international law allow ruses such as feints, camouflage, misleading radio traffic, and dummy positions, while treating assassins, poison, false surrender, and treacherous misuse of a truce flag as perfidy. This is a striking asymmetry in the norms of war. In both categories, a combatant seeks advantage by inducing false beliefs in the enemy; yet one is considered a legitimate exercise of military competence and the other a war crime. Kant’s stated rationale is that the forbidden acts destroy the precondition for any future peace: the parties must be able to trust one another enough to negotiate and keep agreements. But a state defeated by a clever feint might equally complain that its trust has been abused; and a state subjected to an isolated poisoning might nevertheless prefer to restore normal relations for prudential reasons. The question is whether there is a principled distinction between permissible ruses and forbidden perfidy, or only a customary line drawn to keep wars predictable and profitable for the officers who expect to be treated well. Give the strongest possible defense of the asymmetry, whether drawn from Kant or supplied by you, and test it against borderline cases: deceptive ceasefire negotiations, false reports of surrender, double agents, concealment of troop positions, and the misuse of a hospital ship. In exactly five points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, state whether the asymmetry should be kept, revised, or rejected and why.
