Had a long argument with a friend last night about whether forgiveness can be unconditional or whether it only really counts if the wrongdoer does something first—apologize, confess, make amends. She kept saying unconditional forgiveness lets people off the hook, and i kept saying making it conditional turns it into a transaction: youre not forgiving a person anymore, youre accepting a performance. Then i realized the South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission is basically her position turned into an institution, since amnesty was conditional on full disclosure. But i dont think that settles it in her favor, and i want someone to think it through carefully. Can you analyze the TRC through the lens of that unconditional-vs-conditional forgiveness debate? Specifically: is the commission forgiving anyone at all, or is it just the state choosing not to prosecute in exchange for testimony? If it is forgiveness, who is doing it—the state, the commissioners, the individual victims who sat through the hearings? Can a state even forgive on behalf of victims, and does the conditional structure (truth first, then amnesty) commit the whole project to the view that forgiveness has to be earned? And what does the TRC show about whether forgiveness can be a duty—does it vindicate my friend's worry that unconditional forgiveness lets people off the hook, or does it show that once you make forgiveness conditional it stops being forgiveness and turns into something else, like a legal deal?
