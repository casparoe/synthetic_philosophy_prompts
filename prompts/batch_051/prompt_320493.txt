I am a journalist who covers economics and the philosophy of the social sciences, and I am planning a feature about what happens to the concept of utility when a government tries to purge it of introspection. I would like you to produce the analytical core of the piece, written in British English.

The counterfactual. Suppose that in 2035 a medium-sized, high-income parliamentary democracy with a strong technocratic civil service and an established practice of regulatory cost–benefit analysis passes a Welfare Measurement Act. The Act creates a Commission for Choice-Based Welfare and instructs it that, in all official assessments of how policies affect citizens' welfare, the only admissible evidence is choice behaviour: actual market transactions, time-use decisions, and choices made in incentive-compatible experiments in which participants face real consequences. Stated preferences, self-reports, life-satisfaction surveys, and any appeal to utility conceived as a felt state are inadmissible. The Commission may use the word utility only as a numerical index that represents a consistent choice ordering — the revealed-preference sense of the term.

Predict, with visible reasoning, how this Commission and the surrounding discipline look in 2060. In particular, trace the mechanisms by which the constraint reshapes what gets measured: which policy areas acquire rigorous welfare numbers and which quietly fall out of the Commission's remit; how researchers adapt through the design of choice-based elicitation methods; and where the subjectivity the Act banishes re-enters, for example in decisions about which choices count, how choice sets are framed, and how inconsistent choosers are handled. Give a considered view of whether the Act succeeds in making welfare measurement more objective, and of what the episode shows about whether revealed preference can carry the meaning that talk of utility once did.

Structure your response in the following sections, in this order:

1. The baseline (roughly 120 words): what the Commission inherits and what problem its founders were trying to solve.
2. First-order effects (roughly 250 words): the most predictable changes, treated as near-certain.
3. Second-order adaptations (roughly 250 words): research strategies, gaming, and unanticipated consequences, treated as more contingent.
4. Utility after a generation (roughly 200 words): the shift in what economists and policymakers mean by utility.
5. Where the prediction could fail (roughly 150 words): the link in your chain of reasoning most likely to break, and the specific empirical finding or theoretical argument that would most change your prediction.

Throughout the piece, label each predicted effect as robust, likely but conditional, or speculative, and state what background condition it depends on. The result should read as reasoned analysis for an informed general reader rather than an academic paper, but it should be precise enough that an economist would find it fair.
