I've spent a long time reading rationalist and EA-adjacent forums, and one recurring AI-risk argument never seems to resolve. One camp treats the profit motive — or capitalism more broadly — as the root cause of catastrophic AI risk. The shape of the claim is roughly this: competing labs are locked in a race where any firm that unilaterally spends heavily on safety loses funding or market position, so safety is a commons that market competition systematically underprovides; the Moloch-style dynamic, where every actor is forced into a destructive race that none of them individually wants, is doing the real work. On this view, solving technical alignment inside a for-profit race is like trying to fix a commons problem by appealing to individual virtue. The other camp treats that analysis as close to a red herring. They argue that a misaligned superintelligence would be lethal whether it was produced by a corporation, a state, or a nonprofit; that coordination failures and rivalrous actors appear under any plausible institution, because scarcity and power-seeking are not unique to markets — the Manhattan Project was built by a government during wartime, yet it was still a race. On this view, the root cause is the technology, or the unsolved alignment problem, not its ownership structure.

What I'm after is not a verdict about which side is right, but a diagnosis of why the debate is stuck. Is it a verbal disagreement about what "root cause" means — the condition you would remove to prevent the outcome, the deepest explanation, or the factor still present in every counterfactual? Is it a hidden empirical disagreement about which institutional counterfactuals are actually feasible, or about how quickly institutions can be changed relative to AI timelines? Are the two camps answering different questions — one about what sustains the race, another about what makes the artifact dangerous — under a single heading? Is there a values disagreement underneath it, about how to weight structural versus technical interventions when both are uncertain? My own suspicion is that it is mostly two different questions with a thin strip of genuine disagreement about institutional counterfactuals, but I keep second-guessing that. Diagnose the structure of the impasse using the moves each side actually makes rather than a strawman of them, and say what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal model, an empirical question that could be posed but isn't being posed, or a reason to expect no progress. If you think this framing is confused, or that the debate isn't stalled so much as slowly separating into two different fields, say that plainly instead of producing a diagnosis for its own sake.
