I've seen the Ship of Theseus done to death — replace the planks one at a time until none of the originals are left, then ask whether it's the same ship — and the rationalist move is usually to taboo the word 'same' and ask what property you're actually trying to track. But I hit a variant lately that I think breaks some of the standard moves, and its about letterforms.

Take a double-storey lowercase 'a' (the kind with the enclosed upper loop), and think of it the way a type designer would: as a bowl, a stem, and a terminal, each made of outline points. Now run a Theseus-style replacement: every week, swap one stroke for a geometrically slightly different one. Each new stroke plays the 'a'-role in the script just as well; no reader would notice the change. By the end you've got a glyph that looks like an 'a' from a completely different family, maybe even a single-storey one. Is the final letter the same 'a' you started with?

Here's the question I actually want to ask. I have the hunch that the puzzle is a different puzzle for letters, because a letter is an abstract role-kind: any mark that plays the 'a'-role is an 'a', and no particular outline or material is essential to it. If that's true, the standard answers to the original Theseus (material continuity, spatiotemporal continuity, 'both are the original', 'it's just verbal') might not all survive, or might map onto the letter case differently. Which of them survive the letterform version, and which just stop applying? And can you construct a letterform case where the answer flips — gradual drift so extreme that readers eventually classify the mark as a 'd', or as decoration rather than a letter — and does that tell us anything about the original ship case, or only about how far a role-kind can stretch before the role itself changes?
