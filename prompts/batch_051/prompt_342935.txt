I sit on a university animal-research governance board that assigns species to one of two categories. Full protection means that members of the species may not be used in procedures carrying more than minimal pain or distress; limited protection means that invasive procedures are permitted subject to our standard refinement, anaesthesia and analgesia requirements. The board has never adopted an explicit criterion. Looking back over our rulings, some of my colleagues think we have been sorting animals by the complexity of their behaviour, with brain measures serving only as evidence; others think we have been protecting animals with a sufficient neural substrate, with behaviour serving only as our best window onto it. The two readings of our record have not previously diverged. They diverge in the case I describe below.

Five of our recent rulings, with the facts as they were presented to us, are as follows.

1. Species A (a small rodent). Brain mass 0.5 g; around 60 million neurons in the cerebral cortex or its structural equivalent. Behaviour: simple associative learning and stereotypic routines; responses to injury but no anticipatory avoidance. Ruling: limited protection.

2. Species B (a bird of the crow family). Brain mass 12 g; around 1.2 billion cortex-equivalent neurons. Behaviour: flexible tool use, food-caching adjusted for the risk of pilfering, and evidence of planning for future contingencies. Ruling: full protection.

3. Species C (a large ungulate). Brain mass 500 g; around 300 million cortex-equivalent neurons. Behaviour: dominated by fixed action patterns, with no convincing evidence of flexible problem solving, although responses to injury are clear. Ruling: limited protection.

4. Species D (a small primate). Brain mass 85 g; around 1.5 billion cortex-equivalent neurons. Behaviour: anticipatory planning, social learning, and novel solutions to feeding problems. Ruling: full protection.

5. Species E (a small fish). Brain mass 0.1 g; around 10 million cortex-equivalent neurons. Behaviour: simple shoaling and habituation, with no evidence of flexible learning. Ruling: limited protection.

The new application concerns Species N, a small-to-medium terrestrial mammal. The evidence gives a brain mass of 150 g and around 1.4 billion cortex-equivalent neurons. Its behavioural repertoire is, however, exceptionally narrow and apparently inflexible: it never modified its behaviour after repeated failure, has never been observed to use tools or to plan, and after being injured it showed a brief withdrawal but no subsequent avoidance of the place where the injury occurred. The applicant argues that Species N therefore belongs in the limited-protection category.

Please give me a recommendation that best coheres with our own precedents, rather than an independent essay on animal ethics. In doing so: (i) set out the candidate principles that are jointly consistent with the five rulings; (ii) state what each surviving principle would require for Species N; (iii) identify the closest analogue among our precedents and explain why it is the closest; (iv) state what principle we would be adopting if we assigned full protection and what principle we would be adopting if we assigned limited protection; and (v) give a reasoned recommendation between the two. If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the decision, say what further evidence or further case would best discriminate between the surviving principles.
