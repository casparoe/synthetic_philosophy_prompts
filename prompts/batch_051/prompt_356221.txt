I am a high school junior preparing a twenty-minute presentation for my school's Philosophy and Science Club defending the claim that the knowledge argument (Mary's room) refutes physicalism about consciousness. My thesis will be: a complete physical description of a color scientist like Mary—every physical fact about her brain, body, and environment, plus all the laws of physics—does not entail what it is like for her to see red; because the missing item is a genuine fact about the world rather than merely a new skill, physicalism is false. I plan to put the argument this way so that I can distinguish it from the familiar reply that Mary merely gains an ability, not a fact: if Mary's new knowledge is only know-how, it should be the kind of know-how a complete physical description could still predict she would eventually acquire and use, whereas the thought experiment is supposed to show something about facts left out beforehand.

The audience is about twenty members of the club, mostly students from AP Physics, AP Biology, and the computer science track, plus the faculty advisor, a chemistry teacher who calls herself a methodological materialist. They are not professional philosophers, and I do not expect them to use technical vocabulary from philosophy of mind. They think of the mind as an information-processing system implemented by the brain, they are comfortable with the idea that a sufficiently complete simulation of a person would reproduce every outward behavior, and they are suspicious of any argument that turns on introspection or on whether a state "feels like" something. I expect their objections to be framed in terms of information, computation, prediction, and what a completed neuroscience would actually let you do.

Prepare me for the question period. I want a prep sheet, not a restatement of my talk: the six or seven objections I am most likely to get from this audience, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each phrased roughly the way one of these students would say it; a two-minute reply to each; a note on where that reply is soft; and the one question I should most hope nobody asks, with the beginning of an answer if it comes anyway. Also tell me what, if anything, I should concede up front, and which single claim I cannot give away without losing the whole argument.
