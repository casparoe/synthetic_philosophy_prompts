I'm a backend software engineer. I have no formal training in statistics or philosophy; most of what I know about experiments I've learned in metrics reviews. One of those reviews last week ended in a standoff that I still cannot resolve. Below is the exchange I listened to, and I want a point-by-point adjudication of it.

We ran an A/B test on a change to our recommendation carousel, with weekly retention as the success metric. Because the release system rolled the new version out on Android first and only later reached iOS, the treated arm ended up about 80% Android, while the control arm stayed near 50% Android. Within each platform, users had been assigned essentially at random. Our actual user base is roughly 60% Android and 40% iOS. Within each platform, the new version looked better: it improved weekly retention by 1.0 percentage point on Android (40.0% versus 41.0%) and by 1.0 percentage point on iOS (70.0% versus 71.0%). But the raw cross-platform comparison showed the new version 8 points behind: 47.0% weekly retention in the treated arm versus 55.0% in the control arm. Everyone in the room accepted those numbers.

The disagreement was about which number should decide whether we ship.

Maya argued from the raw pooled number: "The raw overall retention is the only number that doesn't bake in a guess about the future. It's what actually happened in the test. If we ship to a user base that looks anything like the test, we're forecasting a retention loss. I don't see how you ship a feature when your own unadjusted number says it loses eight points."

Dario argued from the within-platform numbers: "The pooled number is confounded by platform. Android users were far more likely to receive the new version, and Android users retain much worse for reasons that have nothing to do with the carousel. Within each platform, the change is a full point better. If you standardize to the 60/40 mix we actually have, the new version wins overall by a point."

Maya came back: "But the 60/40 mix isn't a law of nature. It moves between quarters. Once you allow standardization, you've introduced a modeling choice, and the answer will shift as the choice shifts. The raw pooled number doesn't require me to pick weights. And if the result can be flipped by the choice of weights, which number is the decision statistic?"

Dario: "The raw pooled number also depends on weights—the accidental weights of a bad rollout. Nobody believes 80/20 is our population, so you're the one smuggling in an implausible target. The within-platform effects are the only stable piece here; the rest is arithmetic over a chosen population."

Maya: "The within-platform effects are just one stratification. You could also cut by OS version, device age, or country and get different pictures. What makes platform privileged? At some point we have to aggregate to the users we'll actually affect, and the raw pooled number is the closest thing to that without a model."

Dario: "Platform is privileged because it predicts both retention and the probability of receiving the treated version. That is exactly the condition under which an unbalanced rollout poisons an aggregate. If you refuse to condition on that, no gradual rollout ever gives a usable overall number. You condition on it, and then you reweight to the population you're deciding about."

Maya: "Even so, shipping is an aggregate act. If our mix shifts toward Android, then a small Android gain matters more than an iOS gain. The pooled number has decision meaning for that aggregate; the per-platform number doesn't."

Dario: "I'm not denying that shipping is an aggregate decision. But an aggregate decision needs a population distribution, and the rollout's 80/20 is not a candidate. If we want the future mix, we should state it and reweight. You can't let a broken rollout's weights become the forecast just because stating a future mix feels like a choice. Some choices are better than others."

At this point our product manager said this was "just Simpson's paradox." Maya and Dario both agreed with the label, but each insisted the standard lesson cut the other way.

I stayed quiet. I'd like someone to go through that exchange line by line: who is right about which specific claim, why, and what, if anything, both of them are missing that would settle or at least clarify the dispute.
