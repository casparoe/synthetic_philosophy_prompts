For several years I have decided which cause areas to take seriously by scoring each candidate from 1 to 10 on importance, tractability, and neglectedness, multiplying the three numbers, and then directing most of my attention and donations toward whichever causes have the highest products. I treat this as a deliberately mechanical epistemic shortcut, because I do not trust my unaided judgment and I want to prevent myself from dismissing a cause merely because it is unfamiliar or unfashionable. Recently, though, someone pressed me on the rule in a way that has made me unsure whether it is shoring up my reasoning or corrupting it. The first objection is that multiplying the three scores double-counts importance: tractability matters only because importance does, and neglectedness functions partly as a signal about where other people already think importance is concentrated, so the same underlying consideration gets fed into the calculation more than once. The second objection is that my 1-to-10 scores are noisy guesses, and multiplying noisy guesses compounds their errors. A cause whose three scores are each overestimated by a point will look dramatically better than a cause scored accurately, so I may systematically select whichever cause my uncertainty happened to flatter most rather than the cause with the highest expected impact. I have limited time to investigate causes myself, which is exactly why I adopted the rule. Is my multiplication rule defensible as a policy for forming views about which causes are worth supporting, or is it false precision dressed up as rigor? Is there a repaired version of the rule that would keep the benefit of forcing trade-offs to be explicit while avoiding the double-counting and error-compounding? And under what assumptions about how the estimation errors on the three scores are correlated would a lexicographic policy—first require high importance, then compare tractability only among causes that clear that threshold, then use neglectedness as a tie-breaker—actually track expected impact better than multiplying the three scores does?
