I work on a team that maintains a fairly complex distributed system, and a recurring situation is this: two senior engineers, with comparable experience and comparable track records, look at the same evidence and reach different conclusions — say, about whether a particular refactor will reduce incidents, or whether a new queueing architecture is worth the migration cost. There is no obvious asymmetry between them: neither has access to information the other lacks, and neither has a systematically better record on past calls. My colleague claims that, in such cases, the only rational response for each engineer is to move substantially toward the other's estimate, and that holding your ground without being able to name a specific flaw in the other person's reasoning is just stubbornness. I find myself wanting to push back: that sounds either like a temperamental preference, or like a claim that is untestable in principle, or both.

But I am not sure my skepticism is warranted, so here is what I want to get clear on. Is the claim that you "should" revise toward someone equally qualified who disagrees with you the kind of thing that could be tested at all — for example, by tracking many such disagreements where the truth is eventually revealed, and comparing the calibration of engineers who converge against those who hold firm? Design the strongest concrete test you can. Be specific about what would have to be measured, what result would favor each policy, and — this is the part I care most about — exactly what a positive result would and would not settle. In particular, if converging forecasters turn out to be better calibrated on average, have we shown that holding your ground in a single disagreement is irrational, or only that a particular policy does better under a particular scoring rule across many cases? And if no such difference shows up, have we shown anything about what an individual ought to do?
