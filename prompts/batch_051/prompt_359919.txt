I am a novelist, and I am trying to settle a question before I finish my current book. The book has two sisters: the elder resents the wealthy family that inherited the house her late mother cleaned for forty years; the younger envies a friend whose novel won a prize that, by any serious literary judgment, should have gone to the younger sister's own novel. In draft after draft, the resentful sister reads as dignified and principled, while the envious sister reads as small, though the object of each emotion is the same kind of thing: another person's advantage, undeserved in each case. I want to know whether that asymmetry in my own judgment, and in my future readers', is actually defensible.

State the asymmetry in general terms. Ordinary moral judgment treats resentment at an advantage produced or protected by injustice as a warranted moral response, even a kind of witness, while it treats envy of another's good as a private vice, something to confess rather than act on. Yet both are a painful awareness that someone else has more. Is there a principle that generates both verdicts, or only a difference in social styling?

Before answering, take one pressure case seriously. A tradition of thought, which I associate with Rawls though the idea matters more than the attribution, holds that even envy can be excusable when inequalities are severe enough to erode a person's self-respect: the envy is then not a sin but a symptom of arrangements that make it impossible for the envious to regard themselves as equals. If that is right, the line between respectable resentment and contemptible envy may track not the moral quality of the emotion itself but the background structure of the society in which it is felt.

Give me the strongest candidate justification for the asymmetry, then test it against hard cases: the envious sister, the resentful sister, an office worker who envies a colleague's unearned promotion, a taxpayer who resents a fraudulently obtained government benefit, a person whose self-respect has been worn away by a lifetime of unequal chances and whose envy has no clear target, and a person who envies a friend's equal and entirely deserved success. Then say whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up, and close by telling me what a novelist is entitled to feel toward each of the two sisters, given your verdict.
