I've been trying to get clear, on my own, about whether a person can assign probabilities to their own choices, especially while they are in the middle of making one. I don't have a background in philosophy, so I'll state things in my own terms. I'd like you to check whether the following set of beliefs hangs together. Please don't defend any of them individually; treat them as a system and tell me where the strain is.

1. In principle, my choices are already fixed by what came before. There is a fact about what I will choose in a given situation, settled by facts about my brain, my history, and my circumstances before I choose, whether or not anyone knows it.

2. That doesn't take away my agency. I still choose for reasons, I can be praised or blamed, and the possibility that an outside observer with enough information could predict me doesn't make my choices less mine.

3. In ordinary life, I take predictions about my own future actions at face value. I assign probabilities to things I myself will do—say, an 80 percent chance I'll skip a planned run tomorrow—and I regard these as real estimates, not figures of speech, that I should revise when I learn something new about myself.

4. But genuinely deciding seems to rule out assigning probabilities to my own choice in that moment. When I'm actively working through a decision, the act of standing outside myself and estimating what I will do feels like a different activity from deciding, and the first seems to undermine the second. If I'm in the middle of a choice and I say "I'm 60 percent likely to pick A," I've stopped, for that moment, treating the matter as open.

5. There is something one-sided about this. Another person can sensibly assign a probability to what I'm about to choose even while I'm deciding, and I can do the same for her. If there is a difficulty, it seems specific to my own choices, from the inside, in the present.

6. Yet my own track record seems usable even while I'm deciding. Suppose I'm choosing whether to order dessert and I know I've ordered dessert on most similar nights in the past. Reminding myself of that and taking it as a reason to think I'll probably order it again doesn't feel like a failure to take the decision seriously; it feels like using evidence well. I wouldn't want to say a person in that situation must ignore their own history.

7. But full certainty would settle things. If I somehow knew with certainty what I was going to choose—not because I'd already made up my mind, but on the basis of overwhelming evidence about my brain—then there would be nothing left for me to decide. The choice would be a formality.

Some of these thoughts seem to push against each other, but I can't tell whether the strain is real or whether it comes from words like "open," "predict," and "decide" being stretched over different cases. Go through the list and tell me: which claims are actually in tension, and how severely; which apparent tensions dissolve once the key terms are made precise; for each real tension, what the cheapest repair is and what it would cost; and which single change to the whole set would remove the most strain.
