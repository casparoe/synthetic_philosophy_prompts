Last week I overheard two colleagues in my department carrying on an argument about Benjamin Libet's experiments, and I came away genuinely unable to decide who had the better of it. The neuroscientist laid out what he took to be the force of the classic design. Subjects sit watching a moving dot on a clock-like display and are told to flex a wrist or finger at a time of their own choosing, then report where the dot was at the moment they first felt the urge or decision to move. A scalp-recorded build-up of brain activity, the readiness potential, begins several hundred milliseconds before that reported moment, and the reported moment itself comes only about two hundred milliseconds before the movement. His conclusion was that consciousness arrives late: the conscious decision is a report of a process the brain has already set in motion, not the thing that starts it. The philosopher answered that the comparison depends on subjects reconstructing the precise time of an inner event whose onset is vague, using a clock task whose reliability for timing an inner event is itself contested; and a gradually accumulating brain signal, she added, is not yet an intention or a commitment to act.

At that point the argument turned. The neuroscientist replied that even on the more recent accumulator models, in which noisy neural activity drifts upward and the movement occurs when it crosses a threshold, the crossing itself is not an act the person consciously performs. Libet himself, he noted, took the data to establish that the initiation is unconscious, and only tried to salvage a role for consciousness afterward, as a veto that could still withhold the action in the final fraction of a second. The philosopher called this veto an ad hoc repair: if the original worry is that a consciously reported decision has a neural precursor, then a conscious decision to veto should have one too, and the problem is merely pushed back a step. The neuroscientist's final reply was that this very objection cuts both ways, because the philosopher was now assuming that freedom would require the absence of any neural precursor, which is a further incompatibilist premise rather than something the temporal gap establishes; the data show at most that consciousness does not start the sequence, not that it does no work, and withholding an act may not be the same kind of event as initiating one.

I would like an adjudication of the argument as it actually proceeded. Go through each move in turn, say for each specific point who has the better of it, and then say what, if anything, both of them are missing.
