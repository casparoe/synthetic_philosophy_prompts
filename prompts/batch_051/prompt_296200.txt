I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've been thinking about why certain mathematical definitions feel like they capture the "right" notion of something. In Science and Hypothesis, Henri Poincaré says roughly that mathematicians study relations between objects rather than the objects themselves, and that they are indifferent to replacing those objects as long as the relations are preserved. I want to know how far this thought actually goes, because my own verdicts about definitions are inconsistent.

Here are my verdicts. A group, defined by closure, associativity, identity, and inverses, feels exactly right to me even though it forgets what the elements are. A topological space, defined through open sets, also feels like the right notion: it captures continuity without a metric, and I wouldn't want to use neighborhoods as the primitive idea. But a sheaf, defined as a presheaf satisfying gluing and locality, felt artificial to me for a long time; I only came to accept it because of what it lets you prove. And when mathematicians define dimension in several non-equivalent ways that agree on nice spaces, I don't think there is a single right notion at all — the choice seems partly pragmatic. Yet I do think the open-set axioms are better than the equivalent definitions through closure operators or limit points, even though they all define the same objects.

Is there a coherent principle behind these verdicts, and does it really follow from Poincaré's picture of mathematics as the study of relations? What would that principle say about defining a manifold without a metric, or defining continuity without points? And which of my verdicts does it force me to revise?
