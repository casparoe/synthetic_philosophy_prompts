I cover philosophy and the human sciences for a general-interest outlet, and while preparing an explainer on moral realism versus anti-realism I kept noticing something that bothered me: the two sides often seem to be arguing past one another, trading competing intuitions about whether morality feels independent of us. In response, I have been tinkering with a framework I call the Invariance Criterion. The central claim is that a moral judgment is objective to the extent that it remains stable across every internally coherent practical standpoint that takes the interests and reasons of all affected parties seriously. On this account, a claim such as "it is wrong to punish people for traits they did not choose" is objective not because it corresponds to a free-floating moral fact, but because no coherent practical standpoint can dismiss it while genuinely counting everyone affected as a source of practical authority; a claim such as "it is wrong to eat certain foods" is not objective in the same way, because its force depends on standpoints one is not rationally required to adopt. I suspect this reframes realism as a modal thesis about invariance under a specified range of perspectives rather than a metaphysical thesis about special entities, and it seems to me to preserve what realists want while avoiding what anti-realists reject. That said, I can see it may be courting an idealism, an ideal-observer theory, or a subtle relativism.

I would value an honest assessment. What are the three strongest objections to the Invariance Criterion, and is any of them fatal rather than merely in need of repair? Also, is there an existing position in the realism/anti-realism literature that this proposal is effectively restating?
