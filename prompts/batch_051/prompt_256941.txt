Explain the Wilt Chamberlain argument from Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia. Be precise about the distinction between patterned, end-state, and historical principles wherever it matters.

The student wrote:

Nozick asks us to start from any distribution D1 that the reader thinks is just, for instance a perfectly equal one. Wilt Chamberlain, a famous basketball player, negotiates a deal where a quarter of each ticket sold at his games goes straight to him. Say a million fans voluntarily buy tickets over the season. At the end Wilt has about a quarter-million dollars more than he started with, and each fan has a quarter less than they would have had. So we get D2, which is massively unequal. But D2 came from D1 by freely chosen transfers, nobody was lied to or forced, so D2 has to be just too. If justice required holding to a pattern like equality or need, D2 would be unjust by that pattern, yet we have already accepted the steps that produce it. The lesson Nozick draws is that free transfers upset patterns: you cannot keep a patterned distribution going without constantly interfering with people's free choices, which he finds unacceptable. The stronger lesson is that a distribution is just whenever it arises from free exchanges. Patterned principles are basically end-state principles, in that they look only at the final distribution at a given time rather than at how it came about. That is why Nozick says they are wrong, and that historical principles like his entitlement theory are the only serious option.
