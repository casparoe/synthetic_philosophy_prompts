My colleague in moral philosophy has made a series of calls on inherited-guilt cases that I can't quite reverse-engineer. Over the years she's said: a German born in the 1980s has no guilt for the Holocaust and no special personal duties, though the German state itself does have reparative obligations; a man who learns his grandfather helped round up Jews for deportation has no inherited guilt and owes nothing to the victims' descendants; a family that finds a looted painting in the attic must return it, and if they'd already sold it they'd owe the proceeds; the US government owes reparations for slavery, but no living American is guilty of slavery; and the doctrine of original sin, understood as babies being born guilty of Adam's sin, is a conceptual and moral mistake, though she's sympathetic to the thought that we're born into a damaged moral order we didn't choose.

Now I need her predicted verdict on this new case. A man discovers that his great-grandfather was a guard at a concentration camp and used that position after the war to get his son a scarce apprenticeship at a bank; the family's savings, still sitting in the man's account, are entirely the product of that downstream opportunity, not stolen money. He never met the great-grandfather, didn't know any of this, and is now in his sixties. Does she think he owes anything to the descendants of the people his great-grandfather guarded — the full value of the savings, some portion, nothing, or something else? I'm less interested in the dollar amount than in the rule: is she running benefit-tracing, institutional continuity, no-inheritance-except-stolen-property, or something else? Which of her earlier verdicts would each candidate rule have to explain away, and what further case would cleanly separate the two best-fitting rules?
