People keep using Fearon's rationalist explanations for war to talk about AI races, and I can't tell if the analogy is doing real work. The original setup, as I understand it: war is destructive enough that there's always ex post some bargain both sides would have preferred to fighting, so the puzzle is why rational actors don't find it beforehand. Fearon's three answers are private information about capabilities or resolve plus incentives to bluff, commitment problems (you can't credibly promise not to exploit a favorable shift in power later), and indivisibility of the thing at stake.

Now transpose that to an AI race: several labs, maybe state-backed, sprinting toward a frontier model where the winner captures most of the market and a decisive strategic advantage, the downside of sprinting is mostly the safety work everyone knows they're skipping, and a serious slowdown might mean loosing the race. The claim you see thrown around is that this is exactly Fearon's logic, so coordination just fails for the same reasons wars happen.

But I suspect the settings are different in ways that matter. Labs aren't unitary states with a government that bears the cost of fighting; they're organizations with thier own internal principal-agent problems. The 'war is costly' assumption is what guarantees a bargaining surplus in the original argument, but in the AI race the cost you're trying to avoid is itself generated by racing, and the prize is a one-time winner-take-all thing that maybe can't be split or compensated ex post. So the question is: does the rationalist argument survive the move to AI races, or is it doing mostly metaphorical work? Which premise carries over, which one breaks first, and is there a repaired version that keeps what's useful without pretending labs are just small states? I want a verdict, not a survey.
