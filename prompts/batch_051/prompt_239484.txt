I keep running into what feels like a single distinctive puzzle, and I want to know whether it has an established name and a settled literature, or whether it is only a recurring half-formed observation.

Here is the pattern. Outside observers can sensibly have probabilities for what I will do: someone who knows my habits can be quite sure I will order the fish. I can even take that stance toward my own past choices, or toward a future choice I am treating as a fact about myself rather than as something still to be settled. But while I am actually deliberating — standing in the restaurant, genuinely torn between the fish and the steak — the question "what is the probability that you will order the fish?" does not simply feel hard; it feels misplaced. It is not for lack of information. If I were told more about my own past choices and my current preferences, that information would itself become another consideration I could either defer to or override. If I conclude I will probably order the fish, and I take that conclusion seriously, it looks as though I have stopped deciding and started predicting myself; if I do not take it seriously, it is unclear what work the probability was doing. Meanwhile, treating my own choice as merely likely seems to conflict with the stance deliberation seems to require, namely that the options are still up to me.

The same tension shows up in weightier cases: a judge deciding whether to grant bail is not expected to answer "how probable is it that I will grant bail?" as if her ruling were one more event to be forecast, and a student torn between two offers can find the question "how likely are you to accept it?" nearly unanswerable during deliberation, even though an observer with good information about her would have had a perfectly reasonable forecast all along.

So the phenomenon has three parts. Third-person and past-tense predictions about the very same act appear unproblematic. First-person, present-tense prediction while the matter is still open appears to undermine or conflict with deliberation rather than merely being harder. And this conflict is supposed to be more than psychological discomfort, because deliberation seems to require regarding one's options as open in a way that assigning an outright probability to one path may rationally preclude.

My question is whether this exact configuration has a name. I suspect it lies near discussions of Newcomb's problem and of the different direction of fit between beliefs and intentions, but I do not know which label, if any, picks out this precise tension between predicting oneself and deciding. If there is an established term, tell me what it covers, what the main positions are on whether the tension is real or merely a feature of one metaphor for belief and desire, and how the phenomenon is distinguished from those neighboring debates. If the nearest term fits only imperfectly, or no term exists, say so and give me a precise characterization of the phenomenon and its boundaries — for example, whether it applies when I predict my own choice without deliberating, when I am betting on my compliance with a plan I have already made, or when someone asks me to forecast a trivial choice about which I do not care. I will not be satisfied with a bare label; I want the carving.

Lead with the bottom line in the first sentence — whether the phenomenon has a name and what you take that name to be — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating the question.
