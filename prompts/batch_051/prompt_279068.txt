I have spent the last few election cycles defending the practice of voting for the least-bad major-party candidate, and I have begun to suspect that the argument I have been giving assumes away the thing that now worries me. The standard lesser-evil argument, in its expected-value form, treats the candidates on the ballot as given: one of the two viable candidates will win, the expected difference between their victories is enormous, and even a small probability of breaking the tie therefore justifies choosing the less bad of the two over abstention, a protest vote, or a third-party endorsement. The familiar objections attack the pivot probability, the epistemic confidence in the difference between the candidates, or the account of democratic citizenship the argument presupposes. My worry is different. Modern campaigns run randomized experiments on messages and microtarget voters long before election day; they can observe whether a bloc holds its nose and turns out for a candidate who offers it little, and they can adjust the next cycle's candidates and platforms accordingly. If a bloc reliably votes for the lesser evil without extracting policy concessions, the candidates offered to it in the next cycle should be expected to drift. The menu from which the lesser evil is selected is therefore not exogenous; it is partly produced by the strategic response of parties to how blocs like mine have chosen in prior cycles. Does the lesser-evil argument survive this change of assumption? If it does, identify which premise actually does the work and explain why the endogeneity of the candidate menu does not displace it. If it does not, say whether it can be repaired into a weaker but still defensible version, and what that weaker version would have to concede. I am asking about the fate of the argument under endogenous candidate menus, not about which candidate anyone should vote for.
