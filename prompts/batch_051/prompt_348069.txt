I am a retired experimental scientist—for most of my career I worked in a physical chemistry laboratory—and since leaving the bench I have been reading the philosophy of technology with a mixture of fascination and impatience. A recurring claim in that literature is that artifacts can be political in themselves, and not merely in the uses to which people put them: that the way a device is designed, the constraints it bakes in, and the patterns of action it permits or forecloses can settle questions of authority, access, and social order before any explicit debate happens. I find the thesis attractive, but as someone trained to insist on operational definitions, I keep asking what would count as evidence for it.

Consider a concrete case. Two municipal housing authorities adopt different software systems for assigning maintenance requests. In the first, tenants submit requests into a queue that only supervisors can reprioritize, and the status of a request is visible only to the tenant who filed it. In the second, all tenants can see the full queue, priorities are set by public rules that tenants voted on, and any reprioritization is logged and explained. The philosophical claim would be that the second system does not merely serve a more egalitarian administration; the egalitarian arrangement is, so to speak, built into the machine itself, while the first system has an authoritarian politics of its own, whatever its administrators intend.

I would like you to design the best empirical test you can of that kind of claim, without assuming that any single study could settle it. What would you measure, over what period, and with what comparison? What pattern of results would incline you to say that the artifact, rather than the surrounding practices or the users' intentions, carries the political content, and what pattern would incline you against it? Finally, say precisely what such a test could never settle even in principle—what part of the question would remain conceptual after all the data are in, and why.
