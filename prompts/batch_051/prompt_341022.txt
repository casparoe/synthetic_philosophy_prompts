my friend and i just spent the whole evening arguing about whether there is a fact of the matter about when latin became italian. he pushed the continuity line hard: every generation of vulgar latin speakers learned the language of the generation before them, so from cicero's rome to a present-day roman there's an unbroken chain with no principled place to draw the line, and any choice of "italian starts here" is just literary prestige or nationalism. i feel the pull of that picture — it treats languages as clusters of idiolects instead of ghostly objects, and it makes sense of dialect chains where everyone understands their neighbours but the ends can't understand each other. but i also cannot make myself believe latin and italian are the same language: the case system went, articles showed up, and an italian who opens plautus is not reading her own language the way im reading this sentence. i don't want a list of first texts or dates. i want to know what the no-sharp-boundary view actually commits me to. does it force me to say "latin and italian are different languages" is just false, or only that the difference lives in the accumulated endpoints rather than at any single step in the chain? if the theory and the intuition are both tracking something real, what does a reading where they coexist look like? and if one really has to go, what would it take for it to be the intuition, and what would it take for it to be the theory?
