I retired a few years back from geochemisty and have been reading philosophy to fill the gaps, which is how I wound up reading Bishop Berkeley on money — the idealist bishop, not the town. The way I understand him, he thought money is not really wealth at all; it is more like a ticket or a counter, a purely social device for moving claims on real goods and labour around. Gold and silver get treated as if they were wealth itself, but for Berkeley that is something close to a prejudice: what matters is that the tokens are accepted, circulate, and set people to work. He even thought Ireland would be better off with a national bank issuing paper than with everyone hoarding coin.

I'd like you to act as a philosopher of economics and use that Berkeley picture to analyse the Nixon shock. In August 1971 the US ended the dollar's convertibility into gold, and the Bretton Woods system of fixed exchange rates unravelled into floating fiat currencies. A lot of sensible people expected some sort of crisis, but the dollar kept its reserve-currency role and trade went on, though the 1970s also brought inflation and a new kind of central-bank management. I know the history well enough — what I want is the careful mapping. What would Berkeley's view have predicted about closing the gold window? Which features of the episode does the ticket-or-counter account explain nicely, and which does it misdescribe or stay silent on? Does the episode end up confirming his view, refuting it, or something more awkward?

Then seperate out what the episode shows about the theory itself: for instance, where the it's-only-a-convention idea has to be thickened with institutions, central-bank credibility, or the discipline that the gold link arguably provided. Please start by brainstorming the commitments of Berkeley's picture of money, then work through the analysis step by step, and be willing to say where the theory breaks instead of stretching it to fit.
