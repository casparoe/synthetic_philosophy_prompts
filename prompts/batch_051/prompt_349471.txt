I'm in a grad seminar on the philosphy of Bayesian statistics, and last week two people in my study group got stuck on the slogan that "with enough data, the prior washes out." They went around and around, and I honestly couldn't figure out where each person was right, so I'm hoping someone can go through the exchange move by move.

Mara opened by saying the slogan hides a lot. She said sure, Doob's theorem gives posterior consistency at the true parameter for almost every parameter value under your prior, but the "almost all" is measured by the same prior, so a prior that puts zero mass on the truth never gets corrected, and anyway that's an asmptotic statement. She added that Bernstein–von Mises needs regularity conditions and fails in high-dimensional settings or when the truth is on the boundary, so the prior doesn't always wash out even inside a fixed, pleasant-looking model. Dev pushed back that those are pathologies. In the ordinary setup—continuous prior, well-identified model, truth in the support—multiplying by the likelihood will swamp any fixed prior, and he claimed there's a merging-of-opinions theorem saying two people with mutually absolutely continuous priors will eventually make the same predictions with probability one. Mara came back with Lindley's paradox: when you're comparing models rather than estimating a parameter inside one fixed model, the prior over models stays in the answer no matter how big n gets, so a narrow null can beat a diffuse alternative even with unlimited data. Dev said that's a different problem—the slogan was only ever about parameters inside a fixed model, and nobody claims the prior over discrete model choices washes out. Mara replied that inside a fixed model the two priors can still fail to be mutually absolutely continuous in pretty ordinary-looking cases, and once you go nonparametric or high-dimensional, different priors can lead to different posterior contraction rates, so the prior keeps doing real work at every sample size. Dev sort of conceded that last bit but said she'd changed the subject; under the conditions the slogan actually assumes, it's true.

So who's right about which specific point? Is "the prior washes out" best read as a theorem with conditions, a heuristic that's true in spirit, or a false general claim? And are there clean counterexamples that show exactly where it fails? I'd like a point-by-point verdict on the exchange, not just a winner.
