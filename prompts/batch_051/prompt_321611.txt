I spend a fair amount of time on LessWrong-adjacent forums, so I have absorbed the standard anthropics debates — Sleeping Beauty, the Doomsday argument, and so on — but I have never managed to keep the SSA/SIA distinction steady enough to use it myself. A friend recently sent me an explainer that finally made the stakes concrete, and before I let it become my cached view I would like someone who actually knows the technical positions to audit it. Here is the gist of the explainer as I remember it; treat the wording as approximate, since this is a reconstruction rather than a verbatim quotation:

"The Self-Sampling Assumption says you should reason as if you were randomly drawn from all the observers who could possibly exist. Because the set of possible observers is fixed and independent of any particular theory, SSA stays neutral between theories that predict different numbers of actual observers; the mere fact that one theory says the universe is more crowded does not give that theory a boost. The Self-Indication Assumption says the opposite: your own existence is evidence in favor of hypotheses under which many observers exist, roughly because you are more likely to be born into a crowded universe than into a sparse one. That is what drives the presumptuous philosopher result. Suppose one cosmological theory says humans are the only intelligent life, while a second, equally data-fitting theory says the universe also contains a trillion other civilizations. SIA says you should be almost certain the second theory is true, since it provides vastly more 'slots' among which you could have been an observer. Since you clearly cannot settle empirical cosmology from the armchair, SIA must be wrong, and SSA is the only sane principle left."

My prior is that this summary is doing something unfair to SIA — the forums I read tend to treat the presumptuous philosopher as a serious cost to be weighed rather than a knockdown refutation.
