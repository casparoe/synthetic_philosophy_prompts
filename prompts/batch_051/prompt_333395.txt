I'm a physics PhD student, and there's a practice in my own field that I can't figure out how to read philosophically. In everything we compute — helium atoms, quantum chemistry, scattering amplitudes — we treat particles of the same kind as unlabeled. The two electrons in a helium atom are never 'electron A' and 'electron B'; the wavefunction is antisymmetrized (or symmetrized for bosons), and the code I write literally has no variable for which electron is which. Even in principle, no observable lets you say that the electron over here is the same one that was over there a moment ago. My question: what view of identity does this practice presuppose? Is it presupposing that quantum particles genuinely aren't individuals, that there's no fact of the matter about which is which? Or is it just an epistemic limitation about tracking, the way I might lose track of two identical-looking twins who are nevertheless distinct individuals? And what would have to be different — in the formalism, in any predicted quantity, in what we're allowed to say — if electrons were distinct individuals that we merely couldn't follow? I'd like an answer that seperates what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and that says where it's philosophically underdetermined.
