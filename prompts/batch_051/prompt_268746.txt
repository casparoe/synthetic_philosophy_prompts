You are a professional philosopher of mind who has been brought in to facilitate an adversarial collaboration between two camps that have been arguing past eachother for years about original vs. derived intentionality. Think carefully about this one — start by brainstorming the points of contact and the likely verbal disputes before you commit to any wording.

Camp A holds the Searlean position: there is a real difference between original (intrinsic) intentionality, the kind human conscious mental states have, and merely derived intentionality, the kind books, road signs, computers and other artifacts get only because some mind interprets them. Camp B holds the Dennettian position: the original/derived distinction marks no real joint in nature; all ascriptions of intentionality are relative to an interpretive stance, and the aboutness a simple mechanism has is the same kind of thing our minds have, only vastly simpler.

The two camps have especially argued past each other over Dennett's two-bitser — the coin-slot mechanism of a vending machine that takes quarters and rejects slugs, which Dennett discusses as if it knows the difference between a quarter and a slug and wants to be paid. Write the joint document these two sides could both sign at the end of a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

Structure it as (1) a numbered list of agreed claims — specific enough to be non-vacuous, including claims about what the two-bitser can and cannot do, what the dispute is not about, and which parts of their old argument were probably just about words; (2) a disagreements table, where each row states one precise remaining disagreement, phrased so that each side would accept the wording of its own position; (3) a list of cruxes — sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one side — and for each crux the concrete case, experiment, or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

Do the actual drafting; don't just summarize the debate. Watch out for definitions that settle things by fiat: Camp A must not get to define original intentionality as whatever humans have and machines lack, and Camp B must not get to define intentionality as whatever the intentional stance tracks. If some residual disagreement turns out to be verbal after all, say exactly which part and give the translation scheme both sides would accept. And because this is a document both camps have to sign, don't stack the deck — each side should walk away feeling its view got stated in its strongest form.
