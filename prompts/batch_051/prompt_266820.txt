I have been reading Albert Einstein's essay "Why Socialism?" from the first issue of Monthly Review in 1949 for a course on political thought, and two central claims in it seem to me to be in tension, though I suspect they can be reconciled in ways I am not fully seeing.

Early in the essay, Einstein argues that the human being is at once a solitary creature and a social creature. On his account, a person can think, feel, strive, and work on his own, and yet remains dependent upon society in nearly every aspect of his existence. He treats the abstract idea of society as, for each individual, the sum of his direct and indirect relations to his contemporaries and to earlier generations, and he describes the damage done to the individual's social consciousness as one of the fundamental harms of the existing economic order, with socialism being the project that overcomes this damage.

Later in the same essay, however, he is careful to say that a planned economy is not itself the goal. A centrally directed economy, taken simply as such, might bring with it the complete subjugation of the individual, and he insists that preventing that outcome is among socialism's most urgent tasks. This second line sounds almost like a warning against the very collective control he appeared to endorse earlier.

Can these two strands of the essay be reconciled cleanly? What would the strongest readings be: different senses of "the individual," a distinction between social interdependence and centralized state power, a distinction between socialism as an ethical ideal and planning as a mere instrument, or something else? Which reconciliation requires the least special pleading, and does Einstein leave us with a stable position or a genuine unresolved ambiguity between individual freedom and social organization?
