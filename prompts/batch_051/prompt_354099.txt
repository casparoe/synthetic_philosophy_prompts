I'm taking a moral psychology seminar this term, and after our class on disgust and moral judgment I ended up in a long argument with a friend about a rule from her house. Her dad makes anything that has touched a dead pet stay outside for three days before it can come back inside; they call it letting the death "burn off." Our professor had framed the session around the idea that purity works as its own moral foundation, seperate from harm and fairness, and my friend jumped on that. She says the three-day rule isn't just queasiness — it's how her family does respect for the dead, and that it's in the same family as rules against hitting people, because both are about not letting the worst of something contaminate who you become. I pushed back that this is excactly the problem: disgust is just a feeling we happen to have about certain things, it doesn't tell you anything about right or wrong, so ethics can't be built on it.

But I've been turning it over since, and I'm stuck. If she's right that a purity rule like hers is a genuine moral rule, then doesn't the emotion of disgust have to be a reliable guide to what's wrong? And if it isn't, what's left to seperate a real moral rule from a strong bit of fastidiousness — apart from the fact that some people happen to feel very strongly about it?
