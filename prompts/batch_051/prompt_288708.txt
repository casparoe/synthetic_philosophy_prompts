I have come to accept a broadly Metzingerian picture of the self, and I would like to know what would have to be true for me to be wrong about it. The picture is this. There is no self over and above the representational processes that produce the experience of being one. The phenomenal self—the felt body, the sense of being a unified locus of agency, the first-person perspective, the impression of being directly acquainted with one's own mental states—is the content of a continuously updated self-model. The reason this model yields the feeling of an immediate, self-intimating subject rather than the feeling of a constructed representation is that the self-model is phenomenally transparent: the system represents its own states, but it does not represent the fact that it is representing them. It looks through the model and mistakes the content of the model for an unmediated datum. On this view, the felt self is not a window onto a self beyond the model; it is what transparency does to a self-representation.

I want a serious map of this view's exposure. Which observations, possible cases, and arguments would genuinely count against it? Please distinguish among evidence that would be decisive or close to fatal, evidence that would be merely awkward or require revision, and phenomena that look threatening but that a committed defender could dismiss or absorb without bad faith. To keep this tractable, separate the view's phenomenological commitments from its information-processing commitments and its ontological commitments, and say which level a given piece of evidence bears on.

In particular, address the phenomena a defender of the view should feel some pressure from: depersonalization and derealization, out-of-body experiences, experimentally induced shifts in felt self-location and body ownership, anosognosia (unawareness of one's own deficit), and experiences of ego dissolution or selflessness in meditation or under psychedelics. For each, say what pattern of findings would be a real problem as opposed to something the transparency framework can re-describe. Also consider more abstract challenges: whether the view is committed to thinking there could be transparent self-models without any phenomenal experience at all, or phenomenal experience of subjecthood without a self-model; whether transparency explains the felt self or merely restates it; and whether the view collapses under pressure into nearby positions that take consciousness to consist in one mental state's targeting another, or that take the self to be a useful illusion. Finally, tell me honestly whether the transparency thesis has been made elastic enough to accommodate any possible outcome, and, if it has, whether that is a vice for a theory of this kind or a tolerable feature of a research program.
