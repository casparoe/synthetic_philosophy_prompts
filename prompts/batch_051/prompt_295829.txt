In my teaching and writing I have defended a strongly intellectualist, neo-Stoic account of the emotions, according to which an emotion is not caused by an evaluative judgement, nor merely accompanied by one: it is identical with one. Fear is the judgement that something at hand is dangerous; grief is the judgement that something one values has been lost. On this view, once the evaluative content is fully specified there is no affective residue left to explain. I am not ready to abandon the position, but I want an honest account of how exposed it really is. Please give me a map of what would count against it. Which observations, phenomena, or theoretical pressures should move me, and which would a committed defender reasonably absorb or redescribe? Please rank them from decisive to merely awkward, and say in each case what a defender of the identity view should concede as genuinely counting against it rather than what only critics allege. Pay particular attention to the following hard cases: recalcitrant emotions, in which the emotion persists while the corresponding judgement is explicitly rejected; the fear and grief of non-human animals and pre-linguistic infants; the felt bodily character and valence of an emotion, which can seem to outrun any propositional content; and the risk that, in conceding that the evaluative content may be non-propositional or non-conceptual, the view ceases to be a judgement theory and collapses into a perceptual or feeling-based rival. Tell me whether the position can absorb these difficulties without qualifying itself into unfalsifiability, and, if it cannot, whether that insulation is itself a defect. Please keep your response to no more than 600 words.
