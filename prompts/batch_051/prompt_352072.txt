In my philosophy of religion seminar this term, I have watched the same thing happen whenever we turn to the problem of divine hiddenness. Students divide almost immediately: some treat the fact that a loving God would not be more manifest as a serious challenge to theism, while others treat hiddenness as something a loving God could have good reason to permit and perhaps even to prefer. But almost no one first states what a satisfactory theistic account of hiddenness would have to accomplish, so the discussion runs on unexamined intuitions. Before I engage with any particular response in my own work, I want a principled specification of the target itself.

Act as a professional philosopher of religion. Begin by brainstorming widely for candidate requirements, then refine them into a disciplined set of desiderata that any adequate theistic account of divine hiddenness must meet. For each one, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say whether it is non-negotiable or merely desirable. Engage at least the following demands: the account must make sense of sincere seekers who do not arrive at belief without writing them off as culpable or cognitively defective; it must remain consistent with God's perfect love for each individual; it must distinguish forms of nonbelief that seem blameworthy from forms that do not; and it must explain why the experience of hiddenness is so uneven across lives, temperaments, cultures, and historical periods. Flag any pairs of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and identify which single desideratum does the most work in excluding candidate accounts. End with a brief scorecard of three familiar strategies — free-will, soul-making, and cognitive limitation — against your list, keeping the emphasis on the criteria rather than on the scoring.
