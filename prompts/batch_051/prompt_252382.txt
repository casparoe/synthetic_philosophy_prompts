A friend of mine has this pet theory about a rule in our building's co-op: any motion has to be posted on the noticeboard for a full seven days before it can be voted on. Nobody on the board can explain why anymore; the person who apparently introduced it moved away years ago, and members mostly grumble that it just slows everything down. My friend insists the rule "embodies a rationale that no individual co-op member holds." His story is that the delay functions to stop a small, fired-up group from calling a snap vote and reversing a decision before the people who care most have a chance to show up and push back. The stabilising effect is real, he says, but it isn't anyone's plan, and if you asked every current member to justify the rule you'd get a pile of after-the-fact nonsense or shrugs. I find the phrase "free-floating rationale" suspiciously tidy. I can't tell whether he has discovered something about the practice or just found a flattering description of a pointless bit of bureaucracy and talked himself into it.

So here's what I want: design the best test you can of the claim that a social practice (like this seven-day rule) has a free-floating rationale — a point or function it serves that isn't held as a reason by any participant — as opposed to the rationale being (a) a projection by the observer, or (b) something participants do tacitly hold but can't quite articulate. Be really concrete about the setup, what result would count for and against each reading, and say honestly what part of the claim no test of this kind could settle even in principle. Also, where exactly do you think the line blurs between "a tacit reason someone has" and "a rationale nobody has"?
