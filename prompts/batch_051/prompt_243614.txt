I'm teaching a graduate seminar in philosophy of language on presupposition, and I want a decision tree that sorts a theory — or a student's inchoate commitments — into one of several live views about what happens when a sentence carries a presupposition that is not already in the common ground. The students know the difference between entailment and presupposition and have seen basic projection facts, but they still answer with labels rather than commitments. The tree should force them to answer diagnostic questions before they know which position they are walking into.

Please construct the decision tree under the following constraints.

Leaves. The tree must end in these five positions, named in plain terms:

1. Falsity view: an utterance whose presupposition fails is simply false (or otherwise fails as an assertion), and the failure cannot be repaired by the conversation.
2. Truth-value gap view: such an utterance is neither true nor false, and again nothing repairs it.
3. Accommodation-as-repair view: hearers treat a speaker's presupposition as a proposal to adjust the common ground; they quietly accept it if it is consistent and unobjectionable, so the shared background can grow by this repair even when new information is imported.
4. Scorekeeping view: the conversation maintains an abstract scoreboard, and presuppositions are one component of the score; a standing rule of accommodation adjusts that component when a speaker uses a presupposition trigger, so the presupposition can enter the score even when no participant would have independently endorsed it beforehand.
5. Dynamic context-change view: sentence meaning is a rule for updating a context; presuppositions are definedness conditions on that update, and the update can succeed by satisfying the presupposition in a local context (inside a conditional or another embedded constituent) rather than only in the global shared background.

If you judge that some of these are not rivals on a single axis but positions on different axes, do not hide that. Let the tree mark where they cross-cut, and use the branch structure to separate them only where they genuinely make different predictions.

Diagnostic cases. Every internal node should be phrased as a concrete question, using these cases wherever they genuinely discriminate. Each question must be answerable without already knowing the name of the position it leads to.

Case A. B arrives at a party and A says: I see your sister is late again. In fact, B has no sister. What is the status of A's utterance — false, neither true nor false, a proposal to add a sister to the shared background, a scoreboard move, or a definedness failure at some level of update?

Case B. A asks: Have you stopped leaving your wet towel on the bed? The addressee has never left a wet towel on the bed. Does the question have a yes/no answer, what kind of defect does it exhibit, and what is the addressee entitled to say in response?

Case C. A says: If the mayor has a son, his son should resign. Nobody in the conversation knew whether the mayor had a son. Does this conditional commit the conversation to treating the mayor's having a son as part of the shared background, or is that information supplied only within the conditional?

Case D. A chairs a meeting and says: Since the new policy has already been approved, we should move to discussing implementation. No such approval has occurred. B replies: Wait — the thing I am objecting to is exactly what you are quietly treating as settled before I can object. Is B obstructing a cooperative conversation, enforcing a norm A violated, or both?

Logical space. Build the tree from the space of possible answers before naming positions. Explicitly state the cross-classification of answers your questions generate, note which combinations are empty, and give one clause for each empty combination explaining whether it is empty because it is incoherent, plainly unattractive, or merely unoccupied. Then locate the five named positions in that space. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate, and say what additional case would be needed to tell them apart.

Length and audience: one to two pages of prose plus the tree itself, pitched at advanced graduate students. Do not include a bibliography or citations. Prefer plain-term labels for positions over proper names, except where a proper name is standard enough that a plain label would be ambiguous.
