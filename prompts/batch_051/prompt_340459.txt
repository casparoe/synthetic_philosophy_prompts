I have been trying to assess the following argument and cannot find the point at which it fails.

(1) Tranquilism is the view that only the absence of craving and suffering has intrinsic value; every other candidate good—pleasure, knowledge, achievement, love—is good only insofar as it reduces craving or suffering. (2) Imagine a procedure that reliably removes the capacities for craving and suffering while leaving a person conscious but inert: they feel no pain, form no desires, and take no interest in anything. Grant that the procedure works exactly as stipulated. (3) By (1), the resulting state is exactly as good as the state of a sage who reached the same absence of craving and suffering through years of discipline, because the two states share the only feature that matters intrinsically. (4) If that is right, tranquilism cannot distinguish its ideal from a chemically induced stupor, and the practices it recommends—meditation, renunciation, examination of desire—are merely one causal route among many, with no special standing. (5) That conclusion seems to drain the view of the normative content that made it worth discussing.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If instead the argument goes through, what is the least revisionary way to restate tranquilism so that it keeps its original insight without implying that the inert person and the sage are equals? I am more interested in the structure of the available options than in denying the thought experiment.
