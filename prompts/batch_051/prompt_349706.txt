I teach a philosophy of biology seminar, and every year I run into the species problem and end up more annoyed than enlightened. I want a working definition of 'species' — not a survey of Mayr vs. phylogenetic vs. ecological species concepts, but an actual definition you'd be willing to put in a glossary. Then run your definition through the cases below, tell me where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and either revise it or defend the surprising verdict.

Here are the cases I care about:
- Humans and chimpanzees: seperate species, obviously.
- A Chihuahua and a Great Dane: same species, obviously.
- Dogs, wolves, and coyotes: they can interbreed, but they're usually treated as distinct; what does your definition say about dog vs. wolf, and is the dog a species or a subspecies?
- Grizzly bears and polar bears: they hybridize in the wild and produce fertile offspring, but they're still listed as separate species.
- The Ensatina salamanders around California's Central Valley: a ring species, where neighboring populations interbreed but the endpoints meet and don't.
- Bdelloid rotifers: famous for going without sex for a very long time. Does your definition leave them without species, or force a special case?
- A fossil lineage that grades gradually from something Homo erectus-like to something Homo sapiens-like: where's the boundary, and should there even be one?
- Oaks, where the named species happily exchange genes across species boundaries while staying morphologically distinct.

I dont need the definition to be perfect, but I need it to have a clear rule, not just 'it depends.' If you think the right answer is that 'species' does two or three jobs and no single definition will work, say so — but then give me the best single working definition anyway, the one you'd teach.

At the very end, put your final definition in one sentence as the last line, starting with 'Summary:'.
