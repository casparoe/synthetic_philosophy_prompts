Set me straigt on a Spinoza problem. You're a professional philosopher who knows the Ethics inside out; please think through it slowly and tell me exactly which move is bad and what I'd have to give up by rejecting it. I'm a grad student and I've got an argument that seems to show Spinoza's necessitarianism turns the intellectual love of God into something that can't be an ethical achievement. Here's the passage I've been worrying at, from the opening of Part V of the Ethics, where Spinoza is attacking the Stoics and Descartes:

"Yet the Stoics have thought, that the emotions depended absolutely on our will, and that we could absolutely govern them. But these philosophers were compelled, by the protest of experience, not from their own principles, to confess, that no slight practice and zeal is needed to control and moderate them : and this someone endeavoured to illustrate by the example (if I remember rightly) of two dogs, the one a house-dog and the other a hunting-dog. For by long training it could be brought about, that the house-dog should become accustomed to hunt, and the hunting-dog to cease from running after hares."

Now the argument I've built:

1. Spinoza holds that every state of a finite mind, including its emotions and its passage to reason, follows necessarily from God's nature plus antecedent causes.
2. If the passage to the intellectual love of God is necessary in that way, then whether any given person reaches it is not genuinely up to them.
3. But Spinoza writes as if the intellectual love of God is an achievement of understanding, something the wise reach and the ignorant don't—not just an event that happens through them.
4. Since every mind is a mode of God, God expresses himself just as much through an ignorant mind's inadequate ideas as through a wise mind's adequate ones. So the love in question can't be something God has in the wise but lacks in the ignorant.
5. Therefore either the intellectual love of God is not an achievement (which makes the whole ethical path of Part V look like advice for a process you can't affect), or Spinoza's necessitarianism is false, or both the achievement claim and necessitarianism are true only because 'the wise' and 'the ignorant' are not real distinctions at the level where the love occurs.

I don't think Spinoza just missed this, so where does the argument slide? Does premise 2 maybe confuse necessity with lack of agency, or does premise 3 load too much into 'achievement'? And if the only way out is to reject premise 3, what does that do to the ethical payoff—can Spinoza still say the wise and the ignorant differ in a way that matters?
