A friend of mine who's really into programming language design tried to explain to me why mutation makes programs hard to reason about, and I want to know how much of what he said I should actually believe. His summary went roughly like this: "The whole problem is that mutation forces you to keep seperate three things the layperson runs together: the name (or variable), the value, and the identity of the object sitting in memory. In the imperative languages most of us actually use, two names can point at the same object, so a change made through one name shows up through the other; that's aliasing, and it's why you can't reason about a line of code in isolation, because its meaning depends on what other lines have done to the same memory. Pure functional languages dodge all this by having no assignment, only definitions, so a name just is a value and you never have to ask whether two things are 'the same object.' And he added that in imperative languages the equality operator is basically a design accident: sometimes it means same address, sometimes same contents, and the language just picks one, so there's no principled answer to the question of what equality really means." I'm suspicious of the last couple claims—the 'no assignment, only definitions' part and the claim that equality is arbitrary—but I dont know enough to say where he's right and where he's overreaching.
