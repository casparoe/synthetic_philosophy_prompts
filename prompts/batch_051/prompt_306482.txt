I have been trying to reconstruct what I actually believe about the line between health and disease, rather than what I think I should believe, by taking a set of concrete cases and looking at which way my gut goes. The philosophical debate is usually framed as naturalism versus normativism, but I keep finding that neither camp predicts my verdicts cleanly. I want you to reverse-engineer the principle I seem to be running, defend it, and tell me where it breaks.

Here are the cases and my settled or nearly settled verdicts.

1. Mild primary hypertension, around 140/90 on repeated readings, with no symptoms. I call it a disease. The fact that it is common and silent does not move me.
2. A child in the first percentile for height with no endocrine or systemic abnormality, whose shortness causes real social distress and whose parents want growth hormone. I do not think the child has a disease.
3. An adult with lifelong, severe problems of attention and impulse control that are now wrecking his work and marriage. I call it a disease.
4. An autistic adult of roughly normal-range intelligence who is employed and mostly independent, but whose sensory overload, social misreading, and need for routine cause recurring friction in daily life. I resist calling this a disease, and I cannot yet say why the ADHD-like case in item 3 feels different.
5. Daily heavy drinking with loss of control and early liver damage. Disease, clearly.
6. Heavy tobacco use that the person has repeatedly and unsuccessfully tried to stop. Disease, though it feels less organic than the liver damage in item 5.
7. Intense, disabling yearning and preoccupation eighteen months after a spouse's death. I currently call it a disease, but this verdict is recent and I suspect it may be deference to the diagnostic manual rather than my own view.
8. A demolition worker diagnosed with adjustment disorder after developing anxiety and an inability to function for several weeks following a near-fatal accident at work. I do not think this is a disease; it looks like an ordinary, if miserable, response to circumstances.
9. Months of exhaustion, cynicism, and reduced efficacy in an office worker, commonly labeled burnout, which remit fully after she changes jobs. I lean not-a-disease, because the condition seems tightly bound to the environment.
10. Widespread chronic pain and fatigue with no agreed lab finding or lesion, of the kind often called fibromyalgia. I call it a disease, and the absence of a biomarker does not shake that.
11. Menopause. I do not call it a disease, even though it involves symptoms and loss of function. But I cannot give a principled reason it differs from mild hypertension: both are statistically common, both reduce some capacities, both raise some long-term risks, and both are routinely managed medically.
12. Homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia, which causes very high cholesterol from childhood and early heart attacks if untreated. Disease, obviously.
13. Being a carrier of a single recessive pathogenic variant, with no symptoms and no effect on one's own health. Not a disease.
14. A long-standing pattern since adolescence of lying to, manipulating, and harming others without remorse, in someone who says he has no interest in changing. I am genuinely torn. Some days I call it a disease because it is a stable, early-onset, impairing pattern; other days that feels like letting him off the hook, though I cannot justify that response.

My tentative principle, which I keep reaching for and then abandoning, is that a disease is a part or process of the body or mind failing to perform its species-typical function, where that failure tends to harm the individual. It handles some cases well but has obvious trouble with others. Menopause looks like lost function and can cause harm, yet I do not want to call it a disease. The pattern in item 14 seems to fit if mental capacities have species-typical functions, but my hesitation suggests I may be smuggling in moral judgment or noticing that the harm is mostly to other people. The usual repair, that a disease must involve subnormal function relative to a reference class of age and sex, makes menopause and prolonged grief come out differently but feels ad hoc.

What I would like from you:

- State the principle, or a small set of competing principles, that best captures my verdicts, and say exactly what does the discriminating work in each hard pair: menopause versus mild hypertension, ADHD versus the autistic case, prolonged grief versus the demolition worker's post-accident anxiety, and fibromyalgia versus burnout.
- Identify which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and say whether I should drop or revise those verdicts or complicate the principle to save them.
- Apply the principle to several cases I have not listed and do not have settled intuitions about: (a) myopia severe enough to require correction; (b) pregnancy; (c) intense grief two months after a spouse's death; (d) persistent distress about one's sexed body; (e) memory and executive decline in a 75-year-old that exceeds age norms but causes no functional difficulty; and (f) chronic insomnia that appears after a move to permanent night-shift work and remits when the person moves back to day work. For each, give the verdict the principle yields and note whether it matches your own sense or points to a flaw in the principle.
- Finally, say whether the underlying project is viable: can there be a fact-based, value-free line between health and disease that tracks my verdicts, or do the cases show that the line is partly social evaluation, in which case I should scale back my ambitions rather than keep patching the principle?

I would rather you tell me which of my verdicts are incoherent than preserve all of them with a theory that has an exception for each case.
