I run a federation of tenant unions — a staff of about twenty, a few hundred active members — and we take public positions several times a month: endorsements in city elections, statements on rent control and eviction policy, calls for action by other organizations. We are not a political party and we do not run candidates, but I have been rereading Simone Weil's attack on political parties and it has made me uncomfortable about how we arrive at our positions. As Weil describes it, the defining property of a party is not that it contests elections but that it is a collectivity held together by shared opinion. On her account, the party gathers and intensifies collective passion, treats its positions as property to be defended, trains its members to deliver the line rather than to think, and in doing so replaces the search for what is true with a contest of pressures; she thinks the logic of the thing points toward totalitarianism even when the party's platform is democratic. The contrast she draws is with a collectivity organized around a concrete shared interest or task, within which individual thought can still operate. I suspect a tenant federation is exactly the kind of interest-based association she thought could stay healthy, but I can no longer tell whether our actual habits — rapid position-taking under news pressure, internal loyalty tests, message discipline, a culture where dissent reads as disloyalty — are distinguishable from the thing she warned about.

Turn that distinction into a standing procedure we can use before we adopt any collective public position: we need to keep acting as an organization without becoming a party in her sense. I want a checklist or ordered set of tests, short enough that staff who have never read Weil can run it under deadline pressure, to determine whether a given position is the product of individual thought — which is what we want — or the manufacture of collective passion — which is what we are trying to avoid. Build in the hard cases: when our members' material interests pull one way and an honest staff analysis pulls another; when effective action seems to require exactly the unanimity and message discipline Weil attacked; when a position is adopted by majority vote and yet still feels, from inside, like the kind of pressure she described. Apply the procedure to two or three worked examples, including at least one where it tells us not to take a position we would otherwise have taken. Be explicit about what the procedure cannot fix — whether, for instance, it can keep an outside observer from seeing us as just another faction, or neutralize the incentives that push any organization toward party-like behavior. Mark each step that is a judgment call rather than a mechanical test, because I need to know where my own responsibility begins. I am not asking for a summary of Weil's views; I am asking for the translation of those views into the running of an organization.
