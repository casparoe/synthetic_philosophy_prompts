I host a small philosophy-of-mind podcast, and next week I'm interviewing a philosopher who defends a view she calls 'currentism' about the physical. Her line is that Hempel's dilemma is a real fork, but the right move is to take the current-physics horn on purpose: define physical in terms of what present-day physics actually posits, taken at face value, and accept that physicalism is an a posteriori, revisable doctrine that might be partly false. I don't want her to just restate the view. Draft eight to ten interview questions that get into the details. Include at least one question on each of the following, and for each one a follow-up keyed to the reply she's most likely to give: (1) the content worry — does this just make physicalism whatever current physics says there is and drain it of the content that made it interesting; (2) successor theories — if the physics of 2150 posits an irreducibly phenomenal field or some proto-mental fundamental stuff, is she forced to count that as physical, and how is that not a bait-and-switch; (3) physics-chauvinism — why should physics be the science whose ontology sets the standard rather than chemistry, or whichever science is most predictively successful at the time. She'll probably answer (1) by pointing to the determinate ontology current physics actually posits, (2) by biting the bullet and saying physicalism was always an empirical bet, and (3) by claiming physics is the most general science of what everything is made of; make the follow-ups target those replies. Also work in one or two questions about the via negativa alternative (defining the physical negatively as not-fundamentally-mental) and press her on why she doesn't go that route instead of betting on today's physics.
