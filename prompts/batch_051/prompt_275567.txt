I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have been working through a view in the philosophy of value associated with Samuel Scheffler. He argues that our projects and achievements depend, far more than we usually notice, on a background assumption that humanity will continue to exist after we are gone — on what he calls a collective afterlife rather than any personal survival. One way to sharpen the thought is through this scenario: a benign biological accident makes every human alive now permanently infertile. No one dies prematurely; the children already born grow up; but within roughly a century the species is gone. My question is not whether that scenario is bad in some broad sense. It is why, and for whom. I want to put a credence on a precise claim:

P: In the infertility scenario, the lives of people alive now are made significantly worse than they would otherwise have been, in a way that is not exhausted by the ordinary deaths they will eventually undergo, because the value of their work, relationships, and other projects is partly parasitic on the expectation that there will be future people to carry something forward after them.

Please do not give me a bare number. Give me your credence in P, decomposed into the sub-questions you actually rely on: for example, whether people in fact form this background expectation and would revise their valuations if it were withdrawn; whether such dependence is rational or merely a psychological habit; whether events after a person's death can retroactively alter the value of that person's earlier activities; whether the loss is best framed as bad for particular present people at all, rather than as an impersonally worse world; and whether the scenario confounds the absence of future people with the slow decay of institutions before the species ends. Assign a rough probability to each sub-question and show how they combine into the final number, then identify the sub-question to which the final number is most sensitive. I have raised this with a philosopher who puts his credence at about 0.7 and a colleague at the magazine who puts hers at about 0.25; on your decomposition, where is their disagreement most likely to live, and what evidence or argument would move your own number the most?
