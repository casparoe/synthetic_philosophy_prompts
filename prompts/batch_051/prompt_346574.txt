So I've just started as a programmer at a company that makes brain-computer interface software, and in every other meeting someone says 'the brain is just a computer' as if it's obvious. I've nodded along for months, but when I actually tried to say what the claim amounts to I realised I couldn't. Before I read any more of the debate, I want the spec.

Write the desiderata: what any adequate account of the claim that the brain (or nervous system) is a computer has to deliver. I want the list of requirements — which cases it has to get right (say, ant navigation vs spinal reflexes vs doing long division in your head), which distinctions it can't collapse (implementation vs simulation, digital vs analogue, being describable as computing something vs actually doing it), and which empirical facts it can't flatly contradict (neurons are slow, noisy, and graded, with no central clock — if those even matter). For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it.

Then mark which of these are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of particular camps. And tell me if any pair can't be jointly satisfied. The bit I most want to hear about: the standard move for dodging the 'everything trivially computes' worry (that any physical system can be treated as computing something if you're allowed to pick the mapping freely) seems to make it hard to also require that the system can genuinely miscompute rather than just being redescribed as computing some other function. Is that a real tension?

And please seperate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who know this area, and don't present a contested view as if it were the consensus. Bullet points are fine.
