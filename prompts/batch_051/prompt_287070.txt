I am a retired experimental physicist who has lately been reading in the philosophy of science, and I have provisionally come to accept a position that would have scandalized my younger self. The position is this: fundamental physics can be rationally pursued, and in some cases provisionally believed, on the basis of non-empirical considerations — the absence of credible alternatives, explanatory unification, internal mathematical coherence, and the unexpected convergence of independent lines of theory — rather than on novel empirical confirmation alone. Its principal objects are string theory and the multiverse it has been argued to imply. I want to know what would count against this position.

Treat me as someone provisionally convinced but persuadable. I would like a map of the position's exposure: which observations, formal results, arguments, or sociological findings would have to obtain for the position to be false, ranked from decisive to merely awkward, together with a statement of which of these a sophisticated defender would themselves concede as counting against them.

To make the request concrete, consider these questions. Would a rival programme outside the string or multiverse paradigm that made a risky, falsifiable prediction and then saw it confirmed be fatal, or only an embarrassment? How should I weigh the landscape problem if it turns out the framework can accommodate virtually any low-energy physics: does that count against the theory, or is it precisely what non-empirical assessment was designed to absorb? Which failed expectations in adjacent physics — no supersymmetry at the LHC, no proton decay, no dark-matter detection, and no primordial B-mode signal at the levels once hoped — genuinely count against string theory itself, and which count only against particular phenomenological models built upon it? Could the no-alternatives argument be deflated by showing that alternatives existed but were prematurely dismissed, making the claim an artifact of institutional momentum rather than an epistemic fact? Are there formal results in Bayesian confirmation theory that would show the non-empirical arguments rest on priors that already encode the conclusion? And finally: has the position been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that a defect in a view about scientific method or an honest acknowledgment of where the burden of proof lies?

I should disclose one personal bias so that you can correct it. I was trained to distrust appeals to a lack of imagination, so the no-alternatives argument has always struck me as the weakest plank in the position. Tell me whether that suspicion is well founded, what evidence would vindicate it, and where it should sit in the ranking of defeaters.
