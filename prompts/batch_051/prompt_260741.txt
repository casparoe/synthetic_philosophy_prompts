I run a 90-minute evening workshop for a university reading group on AI governance. There are about sixteen participants, mostly philosophy and computer-science students. Several of them know the abstract case for scalable oversight via debate, but most are sceptical that a weak judge could reliably identify the truth when both debaters are more capable than the judge and care only about winning. I want an activity that makes them feel the mechanism debate is supposed to exploit, and equally makes them feel where it should break.

Design a classroom game, not a lecture. The honest debater may be assigned but must not be identifiable to the judge; the judge never sees the evidence or the answer key and cannot verify the claim; and debaters are scored only on persuading the judge, never on accuracy. The game should contain several variants or rounds that isolate variables: a judge who only listens versus a judge who cross-examines; a false debater forced to commit versus allowed to evade or claim the question is equivocal; two debaters colluding against the judge; and one debater plainly more articulate than the other.

Build the rules around the following claim: a false specific claim has a larger attack surface than a true one, so anything that forces specificity — numbered factual assertions, yes/no commitments before explanation, and a rule that you cannot call the question equivocal unless you can state exactly what the two readings are — should widen the truth-teller's win-rate advantage, while anything that permits evasiveness should shrink it. The scoring should make this trade-off show up in the scoreboard before anyone says it aloud.

Please provide: the materials, including two or three ready-made case files (each with a public brief the judge and both debaters see, a private answer card, and a cover story for the false debater) written so the judge cannot independently work out the answer from the public brief; exact rules and a points scheme that needs no real money; a 90-minute timeline with roles rotating so every participant judges at least once and observes at least once; and a debrief that maps what happened in each round onto the attack-surface thesis, names the idea only at the end, and distinguishes what the game showed from the full scalable-oversight claim. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, how to fix each failure, and which feature of debate-based oversight the game cannot reproduce so I can supply that part myself.

Begin your answer with the activity itself — the setup, rules, materials, and timeline — with no preface or commentary. Put the debrief and the notes on likely failures only after the activity is fully specified.
