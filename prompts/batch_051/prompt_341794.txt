Lately I've been tasked with drafting an internal memo at work defending the claim that the alignment problem is solvable in principle, and I've discovered that the words "in principle" are carrying far more weight than I initially thought. The position I want to defend — and to have stress-tested — is roughly this: there exists at least one finite, human-specifiable objective or training procedure such that, if implemented on an appropriate learning system, that system would robustly do what its operators actually intend, including under distribution shift substantially beyond the training environment; it would not merely optimize some proxy that correlates with intent in easy cases and diverges from it in hard ones. The claim is deliberately existential, not constructive: it does not assert that we know how to find such a procedure, that we could recognize or verify one if we found it, or that any realistic engineering effort would succeed. It is closer to saying that a proof exists whether or not anyone ever discovers it.

What I want is a map of this position's exposure. Please take the role of a philosopher of computer science, think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the different readings of "solvable in principle" and specifying which one my statement commits me to — existence in the design space, constructive solvability, tractability, or safe discoverability. Then present what would count against the view, ordered from fatal to merely awkward. I can imagine several categories, but I'm unsure how heavily to weigh each: mathematical impossibility results and no-free-lunch-style theorems, self-reference and embedded-agency arguments, empirical patterns in which proxy optimization reliably produces unintended behavior, and conceptual objections to the effect that "the operators' actual intent" is too indefinite to settle which behaviors count as aligned in the first place. For each candidate defeater, say whether a committed defender of the existential claim would concede that it counts against their position, or would dismiss it as a change of subject. Also give an honest assessment of whether the position risks anything at all: has it been qualified so heavily that no conceivable observation or result could move it, and if so, is that a problem for a claim of this kind? The evidence I suspect would move me most is a naturally occurring case where competent operators were highly confident they had specified the objective correctly and nevertheless got systematically unintended behavior despite reasonable safeguards — though I can't tell whether that refutes existential solvability or merely documents human incompetence. Please sort that out.
