Put yourself in the role of a philosopher of science who also works with the foundations of machine learning. I want you to think slowly, start by writing out a charitable reconstruction of the strongest version of the analogy before criticizing it, and resist the temptation to declare the parallel deep or shallow before it has been stated precisely.

I keep noticing what looks like the same negative lesson coming from two directions, and I want to know whether the resemblance is structural or merely verbal.

On one side is the no-free-lunch theorem in supervised learning. As I understand it, the theorem says that if you average over all possible input–output functions, every learning algorithm has the same expected performance on unseen examples, and in the usual binary classification setting this amounts to random guessing. An algorithm only gets an edge when the class of functions is restricted in a way that matches the algorithm's built-in bias. The bias cannot be earned from the data alone, because from the data's point of view every equally accurate hypothesis is as good as any other.

On the other side is the problem of induction as it appears in the skeptical tradition. An inference from observed to unobserved cases cannot be justified a priori, because a change in the course of nature is always conceivable, and it cannot be justified empirically, because any appeal to past regularities already relies on the very assumption that regularities will continue. The inference seems to work only if one already assumes some principle of uniformity, or a substantive prior over possible futures, and that assumption cannot be certified by the inferences it enables.

The shared pattern I think I see is this: in both cases, generality buys nothing. A learner that is competent over all possible worlds is competent in none; an inference rule that is supposed to be justified with respect to all possible worlds is justified in none. In both cases the real work is done by a substantive assumption that the world is one way rather than another, and in both cases that assumption has to be supplied from outside the machinery it supports.

But the cases are also visibly different: one is a mathematical theorem about expected performance under an averaging distribution, and the other is a skeptical argument about whether a certain inference can be justified at all. I want to know whether this is a deep identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion. Do the following. State the strongest version of the parallel you can, with an explicit mapping between the two sides: what corresponds to the learner, the target function, the training data, and the average over all functions on one side, and what corresponds to the reasoner, the truth about the unobserved, the evidence, and the space of possible courses of experience on the other. Then identify where the mapping breaks, and say for each break whether it is an inessential difference of vocabulary or a load-bearing disanalogy. Finally, say what would have to be true for a result to transfer from one domain to the other, and give at least one concrete prediction of the parallel that turns out false when spelled out, or one you would defend as correct.

I want to keep this grounded, so do not settle for the bare observation that both involve assumptions; the question is exactly how far the isomorphism goes and where it comes apart.
