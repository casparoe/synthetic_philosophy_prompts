I have recently had to think hard about what it means to understand a field, because my own work as a physician has come to lean heavily on a diagnostic AI, and I now supervise younger clinicians who have never worked without one. So let me put a case to you and ask for a definite verdict, not a survey of positions.

Here is the account of understanding I have been using. I treat a person as understanding a domain, in the sense relevant to professional responsibility, only if the following four things hold to a substantial degree:

1. Articulation: the person can explain why their judgments are correct in the domain's own terms, and can answer focused why-questions; the explanation is not a mere display but something they can defend under pressure.
2. Transfer: the person can extend their performance to novel cases, ones that differ in the underlying features rather than merely in superficial details.
3. Error-sensitivity: the person has a dependable sense of when they might be wrong and knows what further evidence or check would resolve the uncertainty.
4. Grounded reconstruction: if the external aid were removed, the person could still reconstruct at least the core of the reasoning from a smaller stock of internalized knowledge. I take this fourth criterion to be what separates understanding from reliably operating a powerful instrument.

Now consider a hospitalist I will call R. For several years she has worked through a diagnostic decision-support system: she enters the findings, reviews the ranked differential and suggested workup, and then writes the plan by editing the system's output. Her retained knowledge of pathophysiology and pharmacology has contracted; without the system she can safely handle only textbook-straightforward presentations. With the system, however, she is excellent. Recently it listed a pulmonary embolism as the leading hypothesis for a young patient whose only finding was calf pain after a long flight and whose screening blood test for clot was negative; R flagged the ranking as wrong before checking a second model, and could not say exactly why until a colleague noticed that the system had entered the wrong number of days between the flight and the onset of the pain. She gives fluent, accurate explanations of the final plan to patients and trainees, though those explanations are assembled from the system's reasoning and lightly edited by her. She is also unusually good, when the data conflict, at knowing which further test would discriminate between the leading hypotheses.

My question is whether R understands clinical medicine, judged by my four criteria or by the best defensible revision of them. Work through the criteria one at a time, and say plainly where they are only half-satisfied or where they come apart. Then take a definite position: does she understand the domain, or does she merely operate a reliable instrument with an unusually good quality-control sense? Finally, say whether her case shows that one of my four criteria is doing too much or too little work, and whether I should change the account. I would rather you commit to a clear verdict with reasons than remain balanced.
