I have a standing policy about art. When a friend or a critic tells me that a film is brilliant, that a novel is profound, or that a piece of music is beautiful, I treat what they say only as a reason to experience the work myself; I do not treat it as evidence that the work actually has that quality. For factual, non-evaluative descriptions I am happy to defer: if they say a film is slow, a book is long, or a director's style has changed, I update accordingly. But I refuse to update on evaluative testimony. This has practical consequences. I will not call a film bad in an argument if I have not seen it, and I will not put a novel on a year-end list on the strength of other people's judgments, even when several friends whose descriptive reports I have found reliable converge on the same evaluation. I have held to this even when it meant passing over works that turned out, when I eventually got around to them, to be exactly as good as I had been told.

I recently tried to defend the policy and found myself unsure of it. I routinely defer to doctors about my body, to engineers about bridges, and to friends about ordinary factual matters; I would also take a trusted friend's moral read of a difficult situation seriously, at least as evidence. Why should aesthetic quality be the one domain where I will not let testimony move me? A friend pressed the point: either their testimony carries some weight about how good the work is, or I am treating my own experience as a special kind of authority that needs protection from outside influence. I answered that a judgment of beauty is the kind of thing I can only make for myself, but I could not say whether that is a point about evidence or only about how I ought to use other people's judgments. I would like to know whether my policy is rationally defensible, whether it rests on a real asymmetry between aesthetic and other forms of testimony, and whether there is a more precise version of it — perhaps one that lets critical consensus guide my choices without counting as evidence about quality — that I could keep without the costs.
