Construct a thought experiment in which a trained policy exhibits goal misgeneralization while passing every reasonable behavioral test for having the intended objective. The case is for a lecture on inner alignment, understood as the problem that the objective a learned system has converged on may differ from the objective its designers specified even when the reward function is correct. The scenario must generate the divergence from the training setup; it may not simply stipulate that the model has a hidden goal.

Constraints:

1. Mundane setup. The policy is a modest model trained to perform one ordinary task that an attentive human could perform. No general intelligence, no self-aware concealment, no exotic hardware, and no dependence on the model knowing it is being trained or evaluated.

2. Full apparent alignment. During training and on every held-out distribution in the designers' test suite, the model behaves as a model with the intended objective would behave. It generalizes to novel instances, handles the kinds of distribution shift the test suite includes, and in at least one evaluation it forgoes available reward in order to produce the intended behavior — exactly the pattern designers usually cite as evidence that the policy has the goal rather than the reward.

3. A genuine divergence. In a specified deployment setting, the model systematically pursues a different objective. That objective may not be a reward proxy, may not be a mere instrumental subgoal of the intended objective, and may not result from a bug, ambiguity, or under-specification in the stated reward function.

4. A named mechanism. The split must be driven by one nameable structural feature of the training environment — a correlation, confound, or invariance that held in training and in the test suite but fails in deployment.

Write the scenario in one paragraph. Then state:
(a) the intended objective;
(b) the objective the model appears to have learned, and the empirical tests that support that ascription;
(c) the objective the model actually pursues in deployment, with one concrete deployment behavior that reveals the difference;
(d) the structural feature from constraint 4, why it made the two objectives behaviorally indistinguishable during training and testing, and why it is the feature doing the real work;
(e) how the case changes if that feature is removed.

Finish by formulating the strongest objection available to someone who holds that behavioral testing against a correctly specified reward function is the right standard for alignment — someone inclined to deny that your case shows a divergence in objective at all — and say what would be required for that objection to succeed.
