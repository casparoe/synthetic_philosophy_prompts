I've been trying to pin down what I actually think about the wrong kind of reasons problem, and I keep ending up at a view I don't fully trust because it feels too tidy. The usual gloss is that some reasons bear on whether an attitude is correct or fitting — the right kind — and others just bear on the practical benefits of being in that state of mind — the wrong kind. The demon who threatens to hurt you unless you admire him is the stock case: it gives you a reason to admire him without giving you any reason to think he's admirable. Fine. But here's the view I'm tempted by: there's no principled distinction there at all. Every alleged wrong-kind reason is either (a) a right-kind reason for some nearby attitude (a reason to want to admire him, or to wish you admired him, or to prefer the world where you admire him), or (b) an ordinary instrumental reason to do something — to bring about the admiring, to practice self-conditioning, whatever — that gets reported sloppily as a reason for the attitude itself. On this view the distinction is a philosophers' artifact built from a few vivid cases, and it would dissolve once we force every claim into the form 'a reason for attitude A toward content C.'

I want you to stress-test this against me rather than give me a survey. What would have to be true for this view to be wrong? I'm after a map of its actual exposure: which wrong-kind cases are hardest to recast in the way I just did, and what exactly goes wrong with the recasting — does it change the subject, lose the original normative force, or smuggle in an implausible de dicto/de re claim about the content of the attitude? Are there structural features of reasons that would protect the standard distinction — for example, reasons that stay put when the object of the attitude is swapped for something equally good, or reasons that don't transmit to instrumental reasons in the usual way? And be explicit about which of these would be decisive rather than merely awkward. The thing I'd personally find most persuasive is a case where every available redescription either changes which attitude is being evaluated or drains the original reason of its force, yet the original reason still seems to attach to the attitude itself. If you think that case exists, describe it exactly; if you don't, then say so, and tell me whether the insulation my view gets from its redescription strategy is a virtue or a problem. I don't care about who said what in the literature, and I don't want a list of names or papers.
