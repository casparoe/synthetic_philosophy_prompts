I'm writing a short paper for a philosophy of mind and language class about whether machine translation understands anything, and I keep getting tangled up. Could you act as an expert on the philosophy of cognitive science and AI — someone comfortable with Hofstadter, Searle, and the translation/understanding debate — and think through this with me carefully? Please don't just give the first answer that sounds plausible; actually start by brainstorming two or three ways the argument could go before you settle on one.

Here's the setup. Hofstadter's core complaint was that statistical machine translation is hollow: a system that hoovers up a huge bilingual corpus and learns to predict plausible target-language strings is doing a refined kind of pattern matching, not translating in the full sense. Real translation, on his view, requires understanding — having concepts, mental imagery, a feel for nuance, humor, register, and what the author actually meant. A purely statistical system has none of that; it can be fluent and still be clueless, and it will fail exactly where genuine comprehension is required. So its successes are shallow. That arguement was originally aimed at systems like the neural Google Translate of the late 2010s.

But the relevant development since then is this: modern LLMs are, at bottom, still statistical next-token predictors trained on enormous corpora (with some human feedback on top). Yet they translate far better, keep track of long context, transfer idioms and register, and they'll even solve cross-lingual reasoning tasks — read a puzzle in one language, answer in another, or explain a pun rather than just render it. A lot of people now say understanding is an emergent property of scale, or that whatever gap remains is just a benchmark problem, not a difference in kind.

So my question is: does Hofstadter's argument survive this development? If it does, which premise is doing the heavy lifting? If it doesn't, is there a repaired version that still says something true and important — for example, 'statistical systems don't understand in the way human translators understand' survives even if 'they don't understand at all' is dead — or should we instead reject his claim that translation requires full-blooded understanding? I care less about the practical question of whether we should use machine translation than about what actually happened to the argument.
