I serve on a research ethics committee, and I have noticed that 'clinical equipoise' does an enormous amount of moral work in our deliberations while meaning visibly different things to different members. The background worry, as I understand it, is this: randomising a participant to an arm that the investigator suspects is inferior looks like using that participant chiefly to produce knowledge for future patients, and equipoise is supposed to be the condition whose presence makes that randomisation ethically permissible. The standard distinction between individual equipoise (the treating clinician's own uncertainty) and clinical equipoise (uncertainty in the relevant expert community) seems important to me, but I do not want to build it into the definition without argument; I want you to tell me whether a working definition needs that distinction, and how the definition handles the following cases.

Here are the cases.

A. A phase III trial compares two newly licensed antibiotics for a serious infection for which no comparative data yet exist; a survey of 200 relevant specialists splits roughly 50/50 on which they would prescribe first-line. I regard this as a central case in which equipoise obtains.

B. A trial randomly assigns patients with bacterial meningitis to antibiotics or to placebo, even though untreated meningitis carries high mortality and antibiotic treatment is known to be life-saving. I regard this as a central case in which equipoise does not obtain.

C. The expert community is genuinely divided (a formal survey suggests a 60/40 split) over whether surgery or conservative management is better for a condition. The trial's senior investigator, however, is firmly convinced on the basis of her own case series that surgery is better, and she tells every prospective participant as much. Does the trial satisfy your definition?

D. A placebo-controlled trial of a new antihypertensive is planned in a country where effective antihypertensives exist but are frequently unavailable because of cost. The same trial would probably be rejected in the drug's licensing country, where effective treatment is routinely available. Does equipoise obtain, and how should the 'relevant community' be identified?

E. An adaptive platform trial compares three doses of a drug for depression. At the first planned interim analysis, one dose has an 87% posterior probability of being the best, but the pre-specified rule requires 95% before the trial may stop for superiority, so recruitment continues. Has equipoise been lost at the 87% point, or is this simply a high degree of uncertainty?

F. A cluster-randomised trial compares two widely used sepsis protocols across hospitals. Every participating hospital is internally convinced that its own protocol is superior and would refuse to switch outside the trial; the expert community is split exactly along institutional lines. No individual clinician is genuinely uncertain, but the community as a whole is. Does clinical equipoise obtain?

G. A trial compares two anticoagulant strategies, one of which reduces strokes but increases major bleeding. Fully informed patients with different values would rationally choose different arms. Does the very idea of a single 'preferred treatment' break down here, and can the trial still satisfy an equipoise-type condition once patient-specific values are taken into account?

Please propose a working definition of clinical equipoise, apply it to each case, and explicitly flag any case where the definition gives the wrong result or no result; then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. I am more interested in a definition that does real work on cases C–G than in a sentence that merely restates the term.
