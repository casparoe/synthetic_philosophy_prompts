I'm still turning over a long argument I had last night with a friend, and I want to see the best version of the side I was defending. The issue concerns what it means for a policy change to be a Pareto improvement when the outcomes are uncertain. An ex ante Pareto improvement is a change that leaves no one's expected welfare lower and raises at least one person's expected welfare. But such a change can still leave a specific person worse off once the uncertainty resolves. My friend argued that the ex ante standard is the right one: people can accept risks, and a standard that forbids any policy that might harm someone under some contingency would make almost every real decision impossible. I defended a stricter standard: in contexts of deep uncertainty about how costs and benefits will fall, the only defensible Pareto-type standard is a safe Pareto improvement, understood as a change that makes at least one person better off and no one worse off under every seriously possible resolution of the uncertainty, not merely in expected terms. Please make the strongest case for that stricter standard, and answer the objection that it would paralyze policy. Please write in American English.
