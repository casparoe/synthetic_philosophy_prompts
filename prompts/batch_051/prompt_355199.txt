I am drawn to expected utility theory because it is the only account of practical rationality I know that is fully general: it puts every consideration on a single scale, yields a determinate verdict in every case, and rests on axioms that each seem almost impossible to deny. But when that theory is combined with two further commitments I also find hard to abandon — that there is no principled upper bound on how good a possible outcome can be, and that a claim I cannot strictly rule out should not receive probability exactly zero — it follows that a barely possible scenario with an astronomically large payoff should dominate my practical reasoning, and that a “Pascal’s mugger” can manufacture a decisive reason by naming a big enough number. I regard that conclusion as deeply counterintuitive, not as a discovery about what I value. I am not asking for a verdict. I want a map of the possible resolutions: under what conditions would the right move be to debunk the intuition and retain the theory; under what conditions would the right move be to amend or restrict the theory; and is there a plausible reading of the relevant “ought” — or of the standing of merely possible outcomes — on which the theory and the intuition do not really conflict?
