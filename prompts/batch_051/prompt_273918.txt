Here is an asymmetry in ordinary moral judgment that I would like you to examine closely. Consider two cases with the same underlying structure. In the first, a private citizen deliberately breaks a promise, or tells a falsehood to someone who trusted her, because she judges that this is the only way to prevent a serious harm to a person she cares about; the harm is prevented, and the breach later comes to light. In the second, an elected official deliberately breaks a pledge made in office, or misleads the body to which she is accountable, because she judges that this is the only way to prevent a serious harm to the public; the harm is prevented, and the breach later comes to light. In the private case we are, on the whole, comfortable with a single verdict: the citizen wronged the person she deceived, the good outcome does not cancel the wrong, and had she refrained we would not have blamed her for the restraint—if anything, we would have credited it. In the public case we are not comfortable with a single verdict in the same way: we are disposed to say the official did what the office required, and that an official who would not dirty her hands in this way was putting her own conscience above the people she was elected to protect, while also wanting to say that something morally significant was lost in the doing. So in the private case the wrong tends to dominate; in the public case wrongdoing and benefit remain in suspension, and the refusal to commit the wrong becomes itself a ground of criticism.

Is there a principle that generates both sides of this asymmetry, or is the difference merely a psychological and political fact about how we respond to power? Give the strongest candidate justification you can find—the special responsibilities of office, some idea of authorization or consent, the different scale and stakes of private and public decision-making, or something else—and test it against nearby cases: a physician who breaches confidentiality to prevent a suicide, a parent who deceives a child for the child's own good, a corporate officer who misleads a regulator to save the firm. Then say whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up, and why.
