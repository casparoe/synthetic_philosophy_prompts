I sit on the board of a housing association, and we are drafting an internal review after a large renovation programme ran catastrophically over budget and had to be halted partway through. During our deliberations, several board members have begun to speak as though the failure were obvious from the very beginning: they point to early contractor reports, note that certain risks were recorded in meeting minutes, and argue that anyone who had studied the projections properly should have seen that the scheme was bound to fail. I find this way of talking troubling, but I cannot articulate exactly why, and I worry that if the review absorbs it, we will draw the wrong lessons. I should stress that I am not trying to excuse the managers responsible. What I want to know is whether there is a principled, intellectually defensible basis for resisting the claim that a past failure was foreseeable in advance, even in cases where the surviving documents, read now, seem to point towards it. If there is, how should that principle shape the way the review frames its findings, assigns responsibility, and makes recommendations?
