This has been bugging me since a conversation with a friend. He thinks it's sentimental nonsense to say a language dying out is a loss when the last speakers themselves don't care: if the only people whose business it is are indifferent, he says, then nothing bad has happened. I tried to give him a step-by-step argument, and now I'm not sure whether it's sound or whether one of the steps is carrying more weight than it can bear.

(1) A language is a specific stock of words, grammatical distinctions, idioms, and stories. Some of these encode ways of slicing up the world that are hard to restate exactly anywhere else — think of languages that force speakers to mark, every time they report an event, whether they witnessed it, heard about it, or inferred it.
(2) No other language packages that exact same set of distinctions and their associations in the same way.
(3) So when a language stops being spoken and passed on, the world loses something unique.
(4) Losing something unique is bad in itself, not merely bad because the people involved are unhappy about it.
(5) Therefore a language's disappearance is bad even in the case where the last speakers genuinely don't mind.

I can't pin down whether the trouble is in the move from (2) to (3), or in (4) just being asserted, or maybe the argument is fine and the conclusion follows from a premise my friend will simply reject. Could you go through it and say whether the argument is valid, and if it's valid but unsound which premise exactly fails or needs changing, and what the corrected version would be?
