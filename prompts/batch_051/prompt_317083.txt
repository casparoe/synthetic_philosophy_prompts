I have been trying to reconstruct the central argument from the last part of Kurt Gödel's 1949 essay A Remark About the Relationship Between Relativity Theory and Idealistic Philosophy, where he discusses his rotating-universe solution to the Einstein field equations. Please treat the reconstruction below as my paraphrase, not as a quotation from Gödel.

Here is the argument as I understand it:

1. According to general relativity, spacetime geometry is not an inert background; it is dynamically determined by the distribution and motion of matter-energy.

2. Gödel discovered a class of cosmological solutions to the Einstein field equations in which the matter of the universe rotates uniformly and the distribution of matter is homogeneous.

3. In such a rotating universe, there are worldlines that always move in what is locally the forward direction in time and yet curve back on themselves. A traveler following such a worldline could in principle return to a point in spacetime that lies in that traveler's own causal past.

4. If a physically possible universe contains such closed timelike curves, then the relation earlier-than cannot be a single global ordering that applies to all events in that universe.

5. Since the only relevant difference between the rotating Gödel universe and a non-rotating, temporal-order-friendly universe is the motion of matter, whether time has a global direction depends entirely on how matter is distributed and moving.

6. Therefore the ordinary notion that time itself flows, or that there is an objective advance from past to future, is not a necessary feature of the world. At best it is a contingent feature of certain matter configurations.

7. Since our actual universe shares no absolute boundary or external frame by which to distinguish itself from a Gödel-type rotating universe a priori, we cannot infer from experience that time objectively passes. The experience of passage is compatible with its being an ideal or mind-dependent feature.

I half-suspect that step 4 or step 5 does the real work, and that step 6 slips in a notion of time itself that the earlier steps have not actually eliminated. Is the overall argument valid? If not, say exactly which step fails and what the strongest corrected version of that step would be. If the argument can be repaired, does its conclusion still follow?
