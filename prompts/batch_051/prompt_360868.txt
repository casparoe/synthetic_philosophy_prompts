Six months ago I moved into a strategy role where our team's offers are shaped by a model that predicts, before we make an offer, how the other party will respond and what we are likely to do. The experience has made the Newcomb problem feel less like a parlor puzzle, and I have been trying to work out what principle I actually accept when an accurate but causally inert predictor is informed about my choice. Below are my reactions to a series of cases. I would like to know whether one principle captures them, or failing that, which of my verdicts I should revise.

Case 1, the standard opaque case. A predictor with a 90 percent success rate fills Box B with one million dollars if it predicted that I will take only Box B, and leaves it empty otherwise. Box A contains a visible one thousand dollars. I would take only Box B.

Case 2, transparent Box B. The case is exactly the same as Case 1, except that Box B is transparent and I can see before choosing whether it is full or empty. I would take both boxes no matter what I see. The contrast with Case 1 troubles me, and I cannot quite say what the transparency does to my reasoning.

Case 3, the inverted target. The set-up is as in Case 1, except that the predictor places the one million dollars in Box B if it predicted that I would take both boxes. I would take both boxes. I include this because whatever principle explains Case 1 should also explain why the rational act here is the one that happens to be rewarded.

Case 4, weak and cheap. The predictor is right only 51 percent of the time. Box B contains ten thousand dollars if the predictor guessed that I would one-box, and nothing otherwise; Box A contains five hundred dollars. I would take both boxes. This feels continuous with Case 1, with the parameters merely running the other way.

Case 5, the duplicate. An exact duplicate of me faces the same choice in another room. Our reasoning is perfectly correlated, but our acts are causally independent. The payoffs are the usual prisoner's-dilemma payoffs: if we both cooperate, we each receive one million dollars; if I defect while the duplicate cooperates, I receive one million one thousand dollars and the duplicate receives nothing, with the reverse if I cooperate while the duplicate defects; and if we both defect, we each receive one thousand dollars. I would cooperate.

Case 6, the actuarial predictor. In the standard case, I am told that the predictor has no information about me as an individual. It assigned predictions to the whole population by a fixed rule: 90 percent were predicted to one-box and 10 percent were predicted to two-box, and the population happened to contain exactly 90 percent one-boxers, so the predictor's historical accuracy is 90 percent. My prediction was drawn from that distribution before I chose, and my choice cannot alter it. I would take both boxes.

I can almost articulate a principle behind these verdicts: choose the act such that, conditional on my choosing it, the unknown facts are in expectation as favorable to me as possible, taking account of how strongly the act is associated with those facts and of how much is at stake. But I do not trust myself to apply this to Case 2, where the relevant fact is not unknown, or to Case 6, where the association is a population base rate rather than a model of me.

I want a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough to apply to new cases. For each candidate, tell me which of my six verdicts it preserves and which it rejects. Then apply the principle you think best to one further case I have not listed and report its verdict. If no single principle gets all six, say which of my verdicts should go and why.
