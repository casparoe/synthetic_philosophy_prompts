I've been thinking about a policy proposal for farmed fish and crustaceans, and I want you to game it out for me rather than tell me whether it's a good idea in the abstract. Suppose a country that produces a lot of farmed salmon and shrimp sets up a cap-and-trade system for what it calls conscious-slaughter-equivalent units (CSEUs). Here's the scheme. Every farmed fish or decapod crustacean killed without a verified electrical stun beforehand creates a liability, scaled by a species weight set by a government panel — salmon 1.0, tilapia 0.6, shrimp 0.3, and the weights are revisable every five years. Verified stunning gives zero liability; using a method the panel regards as unproven generate half a unit. Farms have to hold permits equal to their total quarterly liability. Permits are auctioned four times a year, freely tradable, and bankable for up to three years but can't be borrowed. The national cap starts at last year's total and falls 8 percent a year. Farms can also earn sellable credits for verified welfare improvements — lower stocking density, no eyestalk ablation, better water quality — each worth some fixed fraction of a CSEU. Audits are random, but the farm pays for its own audit, and an auditor who catches underreporting gets a bounty paid out of the fine.

Walk through the incentives this creates for the farms, the auditors, the species-weight panel, and the retailers buying the fish. What does it do to species choice and to where slaughter actually happens? Where does the scheme get gamed first, and would the obvious fixes (independent audit funding, freezing the species weights, an import tariff on fish from countries without the scheme) actually fix the problem or just move the gaming around?
