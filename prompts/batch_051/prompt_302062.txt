Write a dialogue between two equally skilled metaphysicians addressing Max Black's two-sphere universe: an otherwise empty world containing exactly two qualitatively identical spheres at some fixed distance from each other, with no further objects, events, or asymmetries.

The first character defends the Principle of Identity of Indiscernibles by arguing that it should be formulated in terms of weak discernibility. On this view, the spheres are not genuinely indiscernible because they stand in an irreflexive qualitative relation: each is at a nonzero distance from the other, while neither is at that distance from itself. That relation, the first character claims, is enough to confer distinctness without appeal to primitive identity facts or bare substrata.

The second character rejects this defense as either trivial or circular. If any irreflexive relation counts as a discerner, she objects, then any two distinct objects are weakly discernible through the relation of non-identity, and the principle loses all substantive force. If the relation must be qualitative, she argues, the proposed relation still presupposes the two relata it is meant to distinguish, so it cannot ground the numerical diversity of the spheres.

The dialogue should press both positions on their real commitments: whether relations should count as properties for the purposes of the principle, whether a symmetric irreflexive relation can confer individuality, whether the possibility of Black's universe is a primitive intuition or must be derived from a theory of worlds, and whether the first character's view can avoid collapsing into the claim that quality facts ground identity facts. The exchange should be a genuine back-and-forth in which each character restates and answers the other's strongest version of the argument; neither should be a strawman, and the disagreement should sharpen rather than dissolve into an easy synthesis.
