Suppose an independent mathematics institute runs an internal prediction market in its own research. For each of a small number of open conjectures, members may buy and sell contracts that pay 100 points if the institute's verification panel certifies a proof of the conjecture by a fixed deadline, and 0 otherwise; the institute subsidizes a market maker that must quote a price for each contract whenever the market is open. To stop insiders from quietly proving a theorem and then trading ahead of the announcement, trading in a conjecture's contracts halts the moment a proof is deposited with the panel and resumes only after certification or rejection, and a depositor who traded in that conjecture's contracts in the ninety days before depositing forfeits any payout. Analyze how this mechanism would actually behave. What would it incentivize members to work on, and to say in public about partial results? How should prices behave, given that the mathematical facts are already fixed and the only uncertainty is the community's uncertainty about what follows from the axioms? Where would it be gamed first—near-proofs, false proof sketches, selective leaks, deliberately delayed deposits—and do the two anti-insider rules fix those failure modes or mainly create new ones?
