Work thing turned philosophical today. We have a review committee where three senior engs vote on two things seperately — whether a change is minimal, and whether it actually fixes the bug — and then on whether to ship the thing. Each person's votes are internally consistent (if you think it's minimal and it fixes the bug, you vote ship). But if I tally each question by majority, sometimes I get: majority says minimal, majority says fixes-the-bug, majority says don't-ship. Which is a combo nobody on the committee voted for. I looked this up and apparently it's called the discursive dilemma, and people treat it like a real problem for group decision-making. I don't get it. The committee isn't an agent with beliefs; it's just a tallying procedure. Why does a majority vote over individual propositions need to add up to a "consistent group mind"? Can't we just accept the per-question majorities and treat the contradiction as a harmless artifact, like a floating point thing? What actually breaks if we ignore it?
