Write the desiderata that an adequate theory of play and leisure as ends in themselves must meet, before one could responsibly choose among the existing accounts (for instance, one in the spirit of Huizinga, Pieper, or the standard view that play is autotelic activity). Give the requirements as a list, with the case or consideration that makes each one a requirement, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are merely preferences of one tradition. Make sure the list covers at least the following: boundary cases (daydreaming, idle scrolling, professional sport, gambling, ritual, a child's make-believe, a hobby pursued with near-professional seriousness); distinctions that cannot be collapsed (play as an activity versus play as an attitude; leisure as freedom from work versus leisure as a distinctive kind of activity; 'valued for its own sake' versus 'valued as recuperation for further work'); and findings one would expect a theory not to contradict, such as the presence of play in non-human animals and its developmental role in children — but say explicitly how much work each of these should and should not be asked to do. Then flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied without weakening one of them (for instance, a definition of play precise enough to exclude paid work may struggle to include professional play). Finally, state which single requirement does the most to eliminate the common views, and why. Keep the emphasis on the criteria themselves and their motivation rather than on a survey of the literature.
