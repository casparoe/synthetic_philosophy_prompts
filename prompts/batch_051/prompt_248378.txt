In the Preface to his New Essays on Human Understanding, Leibniz criticizes Locke’s comparison of the mind at birth to white paper or an empty tablet and offers a rival analogy: the mind is like a block of marble whose veins already trace the figure of Hercules, so that the form is present in the stone before any chisel touches it, though hard work is needed to make it visible. He uses this to argue that ideas and necessary truths are innate not as fully formed thoughts but as inclinations, dispositions, or prefigurations that experience merely activates or makes distinct.

Audit Leibniz’s veined-marble analogy. Give an explicit mapping: what corresponds to the veins, the Hercules shape, the marble, the chisel work, and the quarry or block of stone; what exactly is supposed to be innate and what is contributed by experience. Then identify where the analogy breaks. Pay attention to features the marble image quietly imports: that the mind comes in relatively few pre-cut shapes, that its later content is predetermined by its initial structure, that the sculptor’s work is merely revelatory rather than formative, and that the material is passive. Which of these features can an innatist rationally want to keep, and which does Leibniz’s own account not actually need? Finally, say whether the analogy, once disanalogies are on the table, can support the conclusion Leibniz wants against Locke, or whether it mainly shows something weaker. If there is a better analogy for the position Leibniz is trying to state, give it and say what it fixes.
