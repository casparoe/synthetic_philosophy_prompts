I've been chewing on an asymmetry that shows up in both tiny and huge cases, and I can't decide whether it's defensible or just status quo bias wearing a suit. Take two equally qualified workers in the same town. Al's plant closed and he's been unemployed for a year; Bea has been unemployed for a year too, because the plant that would have hired her was never built, even though the plans were ready and the money was approved. Ask people which one has been harmed by the way things went, and Al gets treated as someone who suffered a real loss — benefits, sympathy, maybe claims on the company — while Bea gets shrugged off: she never lost anything. But a year later they're in exactly the same position; the only difference is whether we count a loss or a foregone gain. The same thing shows up with a free mug: people demand more to part with it ten minutes after getting it than they'd have paid for it ten minutes before. I want to know whether the big case and the little case are the same thing, and whether there's a principle that justifies weighting losses more heavily than foregone gains — or whether the mug case is a mistake and the big-case asymmetry should go with it. Give me the strongest principle you can find, then try it against the mug, against a version where Al loathed his job and was about to quit anyway, and against a version where Bea would only have gotten the job if she'd moved her family across the country. Where do you land, and what should I keep or give up?
