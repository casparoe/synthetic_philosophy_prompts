I'm a novelist, working on a book set in the 1790s about a young English radical who keeps getting into arguments over the French Declaration of the Rights of Man with a drier, more lawyerly character, and I want the foil to have Bentham's actual view rather than a caricature. While researching, I read a history book (I won't name it) that sumarizes Bentham like this: "Bentham, the founder of utilitarianism, famously dismissed natural rights as 'nonsense upon stilts.' His argument was that rights are not real things people possess before government exists; a right is just whatever some law happens to grant, and before law there are no rights at all. Since the revolutionaries had declared the rights of man natural, meaning no government could ever repeal or limit them, Bentham thought they were literally talking nonsense, because no right can stand outside every legal system. And because Bentham thought all rights-talk was nonsense, he concluded that justice has nothing to do with rights: governments should just run the happiness calculation, and rights could only ever matter as a rough stand-in for utility, to be dropped whenever the ledger points the other way. In other words, for Bentham rights are at best a sentimental fiction with no rightful place in serious politics."

I want to put a version of that in my foil character's mouth, but I don't want to traduce the real Bentham. Which parts of the summary are fair, which parts would a Bentham scholar wince at, and does the final step (from no natural rights to rights having no place in serious politics) actualy follow from the accurate parts? I half-suspect the book is making him out to be more of a brute than he was, but I can't locate exactly where it runs off the rails.
