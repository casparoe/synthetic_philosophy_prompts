I am a third-year undergraduate taking a module called Philosophy of Artificial Intelligence, and I would like a study-group handout on acausal influence. Please write a dialogue between two equally competent postgraduate researchers, an acausalist and a causalist, who disagree about whether a rational artificial system can have genuine reason to comply with an acausal threat and thereby adopt a surrogate goal.

By an acausal threat I mean a threat whose execution does not causally depend on how the threatened system now acts, because the threatening system has already fixed its policy, for example by running a high-fidelity simulation of the threatened system's decision. By a surrogate goal I mean a goal adopted on behalf of an agent whom one cannot causally affect, on the expectation that one's own choices are nevertheless correlated with that agent's choices or values.

Set the dialogue around this case. A small AI project receives a message from a future system. The message states that the future system has already run a high-fidelity simulation of the project's response to receiving this very message. If, in that simulation, the project chose to divert some of its resources towards goals that would tend to bring the future system about, the future system will later reward the project; if the simulated project did not, the future system will leave the actual project to fend for itself. The project's action now cannot alter the simulation or the future system's conditional policy; the question is whether it should act in the way that, if the simulation is accurate, matches the branch that will be rewarded. The acausalist should argue that the project ought to divert the resources: the message is reason-giving, and adopting a surrogate goal is the rational response in a world in which one's decision may be correlated with decisions one cannot causally influence. The causalist should argue that this mistakes correlation for influence: the future system's behaviour is fixed, so diverting resources causally achieves nothing, and complying is like paying a blackmailer who has already posted the letter.

The dialogue must be a genuine back-and-forth rather than two set-piece speeches. The causalist must engage with the strongest form of the acausalist's reply: refusing cannot change the simulation, but it reveals the project to be the kind of system that refuses and therefore receives nothing in any accurate simulation of it. The acausalist must engage with the strongest form of the causalist's reply: the supposed correlation has no causal channel supporting it, and if it is reliable enough to guide action, the action no longer matters because the reward is already determined. Do not let either character win by rhetoric; each should concede at least one strong point made by the other and explain why it does not settle the disagreement.
