I'm on the finance committee of a small private foundation that supports community arts, and we're revising our spending policy. Our new investment consultant has proposed we adopt a "total return" rule. His pitch is that since the Federal Reserve expands its balance sheet, the nominal value of our endowment rises, and that increase is real wealth we can spend. I can't shake the feeling he's double-counting something, but I don't have the vocabulary to say exactly what.

I was rereading Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations, Book II, chapter 2, and this passage stopped me:

"The gross revenue of all the inhabitants of a great country comprehends the whole annual produce of their land and labour; the net revenue, what remains free to them after deducting the expense of maintaining- first, their fixed, and, secondly, their circulating capital; or what, without encroaching upon their capital, they can place in their stock reserved for immediate consumption, or spend upon their subsistence, conveniencies, and amusements. Their real wealth, too, is in proportion, not to their gross, but to their net revenue."

If Smith is right, the consultant is confusing gross revenue with net revenue, or maybe money with wealth. But I'm not an economist and need a clear memo for our commitee. Our next meeting is in two weeks. Please draft a short decision memo: should we adopt the total-return policy? In it, give us an actionable recommendation, explain whether nominal gains from monetary expansion count as "net revenue" in Smith's sense or are just a change in the measuring rod, and treat the consultant's strongest argument fairly.
