I train the chairs of local public bodies — planning commissions, licensing boards, school boards — who run public question-and-answer sessions during hearings. They take dozens of questions a night with only a few seconds to respond, on the record and before an often hostile room, and the questions are frequently loaded: they presuppose a controversial proposition, sometimes the very matter up for a vote. I want a reusable, one-page decision procedure for the chair, grounded in what philosophy of language actually knows about presupposition, accommodation, and conversational score, that someone who has never read the theory can run in real time. For each question whose presupposition the chair can identify, the procedure should determine whether to accommodate the presupposition (answer as if it were accepted), repair the question (strip the presupposition and answer what remains), challenge it (name the presupposition and decline to accept it), or handle it some other way, along with the test that decides each time. It should flag where the chair's own views are most likely to corrupt the call, and where the procedure cannot decide and a person must.

Then apply the procedure to three questions from the public-comment period of a hearing on a proposed housing development: (1) "Given that this project will increase traffic, how many new crosswalks will you require?" (2) "What is the board's plan for the school overcrowding this development is going to cause?" (3) "When will the board stop treating the developer's traffic study as credible?" At least one application should return an answer the chair will not like; say which and why the procedure gives it anyway. Throughout, separate what is settled in the theory from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested position as the consensus; where the procedure depends on a contested claim, say so and say what the main alternative would imply.
