So my brother has locked-in syndrome—brainstem stroke, fully awake but only able to move his eyes—and he's on the waitlist for one of those implanted speech BCIs. We keep arguing about whether the implant would be reading his mind or just picking up motor commands he's trying to send to his mouth. I used to think the answer was obvious: current devices mostly sit over motor areas and decode intended speech, so calling it mind reading is mostly hype. But then I started thinking about inner speech, the little voice in your head. A lot of people with locked-in syndrome keep a rich inner monologue, and once a decoder gets good enough to turn that into text, the line between motor decoding and actual thought reading gets blurry fast. And I think the distinction matters, because if it's thought reading then mental privacy starts to look like a distinct ethical problem—maybe cognitive liberty territory—rather than just another data-security problem.

So here's the conceptual claim I want help testing: a speech BCI that decodes covert inner speech is genuinely reading a person's mind, in the sense that matters for mental privacy, whereas a BCI that only decodes motor/articulatory planning is not. I want a concrete experimental design—or a sequence of tests—that would help seperate these. I have some half-baked ideas: compare decoding accuracy for sentences people silently read under articulatory suppression (their mouth is occupied, so they can't subvocalize) versus sentences they imagine saying aloud versus sentences they just think about for their meaning without hearing the words; or train a decoder on overt speech and test it on inner speech versus on remembering a scene; maybe even use bilinguals switching languages to see whether the decoder tracks meaning or motor code. Use, combine, or replace those as you like. For each test, say what pattern of results would favor the mind-reading interpretation, what pattern would favor the motor-decoding interpretation, and why the two can or can't be cleanly seperated. Then the hard part: say what a positive result—decoder reconstructs semantic content even when motor simulation is suppressed—would and would not settle about mental privacy and cognitive liberty. Would it actually show mental privacy is a distinct right, or just that the data are unusually sensitive and need stricter handling? What residue is left over that no experiment can reach?
