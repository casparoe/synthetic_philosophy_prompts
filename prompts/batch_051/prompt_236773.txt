A colleague of mine, Mara, has over several years expressed a strikingly consistent set of verdicts about small promissory lapses, and I would like to work out what principle she is running before she is asked about a new case next week. Her earlier rulings:

- Promised to call B at 9:00, called at 9:40 while B waited by the phone: wrong.
- Promised to attend B's concert, skipped it for a casual dinner, and texted an hour before; B had planned around the promise: seriously wrong.
- Promised to return a borrowed umbrella on Thursday, brought it back on Sunday, after B had already said not to rush: not wrong.
- Promised to email B a document by noon, sent it at 4:00 while B waited at a print shop: wrong.
- Cancelled on Friday evening a promise to help B move on Saturday, because a better social offer came up; B found replacement help with one text: wrong, but not very serious.

The new case: Dan promises Priya to meet for lunch at 1:00, starts a non-urgent conversation on his way out of work, arrives at 1:25, and finds Priya entirely unbothered — she had brought a book and says she honestly did not mind the wait. Has Dan wronged Priya, or merely been inconsiderate?

Two quite different principles seem able to fit the earlier verdicts. One grounds the wrongness of broken promises in setback and abused reliance; the other grounds it in the promisee's temporary right over the promisor's action, so that a deliberate breach for one's own convenience can wrong even without any setback. There may be further candidates.

Predict which verdict Mara will give, identify the closest precedent and say why it is closest, give your best assessment of which principle is actually doing the work for her, and specify one further case whose verdict would cleanly discriminate the live principles.

Structure your response in these sections, in this order:

Candidate principles (about 120 words): state the two or more principles that fit the five earlier verdicts about equally well, and pin down exactly where they diverge.

The new case under each principle (about 200 words): say what each candidate principle predicts for Dan and Priya, and why.

Prediction for Mara (about 150 words): give your best guess of her verdict on the new case, identify the closest precedent, and say which principle you think she holds and why, while noting the strongest consideration against it.

Decisive further case (about 100 words): describe one further case whose verdict would cleanly tell the live principles apart, and say what each possible answer would establish.
