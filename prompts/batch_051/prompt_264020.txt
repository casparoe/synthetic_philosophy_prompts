I am organizing the Q&A for a colloquium in philosophy of biology. The speaker defends a chastened adaptationism: she grants that developmental constraints, phylogenetic inertia, and trade-offs are real, and that not every trait is an adaptation, but she maintains that natural selection is the only available explanation of complex functional design and that Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique therefore leaves the adaptationist research program intact. Her standard response to objections is that citing constraints and byproducts never actually tests the program, because serious adaptationists already accept them. I need eight to ten questions that block that response. They should press on at least these fronts: the distinction between a trait's being useful now and having originated by selection, and what bridges the gap; the further distinction between adaptive maintenance and adaptive origin, and whether evidence for the former licenses the historical inference to the latter; whether exaptations should be counted as victories for the adaptationist program or as evidence that the program needs an independent account of constraint and co-option; how a developmental or architectural constraint can be positively established rather than invoked as a residual explanation after an adaptive story fails; and the familiar charge that adaptationist hypotheses are little more than just-so stories because a revised selection narrative can always be supplied. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give. The final two questions should ask her to specify, in advance, what comparative, experimental, or model-based finding would lead her to lower her confidence that a particular complex trait is an adaptation.
