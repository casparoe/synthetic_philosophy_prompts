I'm teaching a module on the ethics of deception and I want to give my students a short adversarial-collaboration document between two positions that have been talking past each other for ages. The question is whether misleading is morally different from lying. Position A holds that lying is in a different and usually worse category: a lie is an assertion offered under a kind of warranty that the speaker believes it, so the liar betrays the practice of assertion itself; a misleader lets the hearer draw a false conclusion themselves and nowhere vouches for the falsehood, so even when the outcome is the same the interpersonal wrong is different, and the deniability is part of what makes it sleazy but in a different way. Position B holds that this is moral bookkeeping, not a real moral difference: if you intentionally maneuver someone into the same false belief through the same essential means, the few bits of true language left scattered around the maneuver make no ethical difference, except that misleading is often worse because it adds planned deniability and offloads the work of deception onto the victim. Both sides should be presented as serious, careful, and open to being partly wrong.

Write the final document they could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Include: (1) a numbered list of agreed claims about intention, assertion, implicature, deniability, and when each type of deception is wrong; (2) a disagreements table with the exact residual disagreement, phrased so each side would accept the wording of its own view; (3) two or three cruxes, sub-questions where settling them would actually move at least one side; and (4) for each crux, a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. I suspect one crux is whether the wrong of lying is best modeled on breaking a promise or on harming someone's relationship to the evidence, and another is whether the extra deniability in misleading is itself a moral cost or just a prudential advantage. But push on those rather than assume them, and flag any part of the dispute that looks merely verbal.
