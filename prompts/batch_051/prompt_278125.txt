I am a PhD student in moral psychology working on Hellenistic and Buddhist accounts of the good life. For a seminar I constructed the following argument, and I now find myself unable to accept its conclusion but also unable to say which step fails. I would like a diagnosis of the argument rather than a survey of the views it touches.

1. Consider an ideal of freedom from disturbance, understood broadly enough to cover Stoic apatheia, Epicurean ataraxia, and the equanimity that Buddhist sources distinguish from indifference or mere flatness. If this ideal is to be the organizing aim of a life, it must require that one not stake one's peace of mind on things that can be lost, damaged, or destroyed — health, reputation, political projects, a friend, a child, a cause.

2. To not stake one's peace of mind on a thing is to hold that thing in such a way that its loss would not disturb one. In the limiting case one may prefer the thing's presence, but one does not treat it as something on which one's well-being depends, and its absence leaves one's equanimity intact.

3. But there is an ordinary and irreplaceable form of valuing — call it attachment — that is constituted by the opposite relation. To love a person, or to be genuinely committed to a project or cause, is not merely to prefer that the beloved or the project fare well. It is to be vulnerable to the loss of the beloved or the project: the death of a friend, the collapse of a cause, or estrangement from someone loved unsettles the person who genuinely loves or is committed. Where no possible loss would unsettle an agent, we would not say that she loved or was committed; we would say that she had a preference, an interest, or a routine.

4. A flourishing human life requires at least some attachments of this kind. A life from which all such vulnerability had been removed would be recognizably impoverished, not serene.

5. Therefore a flourishing human life requires vulnerability to being disturbed by loss.

6. Therefore freedom from disturbance cannot be the organizing aim of a flourishing human life.

I cannot accept step 6, since it seems to sweep aside in a few moves the central promise of several traditions of ethical thought: the Stoic insistence that one can love while treating the beloved as a preferred indifferent, the Epicurean claim that friendship is among the greatest sources of ataraxia, and the Buddhist attempt to distinguish equanimity from a refusal to care. But none of the exits I have tried feels sufficient. Denying step 2 by saying that equanimity is resilience rather than invulnerability seems to concede that the disturbance itself is the thing to be avoided, which is the idea under examination. Denying step 3 by saying that one can love without clinging seems to change the subject away from love in the ordinary sense. And denying step 4 by saying that the sage needs only virtue seems to make the traditions coherent only by making them inhuman. I would rather keep step 4 than surrender it, but I need to know what I am paying.

Which step of the argument do you reject, and, if the argument is valid, which premise is the least costly to surrender? In either case, what does the move cost for the ideal of freedom from disturbance — does it save a defensible ideal, or does it force a revision in what apatheia or ataraxia could mean?
