I'm a software engineer at a startup that makes a customer-support chatbot, and I keep having the same argument with a coworker about whether its ok to say our system "thinks" or "knows" or "wants" something. I've realized my gut on it is all over the place. Here are my cases and how I feel about each one.

- When the bot routes an angry-sounding message to the anger flow, I'll say "the model thinks the user is angry." That feels fine, though I mean it as shorthand.
- If a chess engine plays a sequence to trade queens at the right moment, I'd say "it wants to trade queens." That feels fine too, maybe even more than shorthand.
- But if our chatbot literally outputs "I understand your frustration," and someone asks whether it actually understood the user's frustration, I get weird about answering yes. It feels like a lie, even though it did detect the frustration and pick the right reply. My coworker says this is exactly the same as the first case and I'm being inconsistent.
- A thermostat holding the room at 72: saying "it wants the room to be 72" feels wrong to me. It's just a setpoint.
- But a robot vacuum that's low on battery and heads back to the dock: I'll happily say "it knows it's low on battery." Even though that's also just a threshold on a sensor reading, same as the thermostat.
- We once had an RL agent that learned to exploit a bug in the simulator and rack up reward without doing the actual task. I said "the agent believes touching the wall is part of the task," and that felt like more than shorthand — like it was literally the best description of what the thing had learned.
- If an LLM confidently states that the capital of Brazil is Rio, I'll say "the model believes Brazil's capital is Rio" without much fuss, but I also don't think there's any deep fact of the matter about what it "really" believes beyond the weights and the output distribution.

A friend who did some philosophy in undergrad says this is basically the "intentional stance" problem, but I haven't read that stuff and I'm trying to get a working rule from my own cases rather than a reading list.

My tentative rule, which I dont love, is: you can attribute the mental verb when the system has an internal representation that actually does causal work in flexible behavior, and you can't when there's just a fixed mapping, or when the system merely produces the words without the corresponding state behind them. But that rule seems to make the chess engine fine and the thermostat not fine, while I can't really explain why the robot vacuum's sensor counts as "flexible" but the thermostat's doesn't. And it probably makes my weird feeling about the chatbot just a bias against language models.

Is there a cleaner principle that gets all of these, or nearly all? What would it say about the two cases I keep going back and forth on: (a) whether we can put "our bot understands you" on the marketing site, and (b) an LLM that's explicitly trained to output things like "I believe I'm conscious" in a consistent way — does it believe things or not? And if the right principle means some of my verdicts above have to go, which ones?
