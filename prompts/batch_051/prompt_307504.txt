So I just spent way too long arguing with a friend about whether someone with no brain function on a ventilator is really dead. He kept saying the dead donor rule says you can't take organs from someone who isn't dead, and we do take organs from brain-dead patients, so that's what death is. I kept saying that sounds backwards—like the defintion got built around what makes donation work. We never got past it, so now I want to work out what the practice actually commits you to.

The thing that bugs me is that the same dead donor rule gets applied in two pretty different situations. In donation after brain death, someone is declared dead while their heart still beats, their body stays warm, and a ventilator keeps them going; what's dead is the whole brain. In donation after circulatory death, someone is declared dead a few minutes after their heart stops, even though in a lot of cases the heart could be restarted—what makes the stoppage permanent is that everyone has agreed not to restart it. So one rule, but one idea of death seems to be about the brain and the other seems to be about circulation, and the second case quietly treats permanent as if it were the same as irreversible.

Reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories) of death this practice presupposes. Specifically: (1) What view of death is doing the real work, and is it the same across the two cases? (2) Which parts of the practice strictly presuppose that view, and which parts merely fit well with several views? (3) Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, so a rival account of death would license the same rules? (4) What would have to change in organ donation if you swapped in a higher-brain or personhood view of death, and what would have to change if you insisted on a strict no-restartable-heart view? And go after the irreversible/permanent distinction—I have a feeling the whole setup leans on it without ever having to say so.
