I have been arguing with a colleague about what heritability studies of complex traits actually show. His position, as best I can reconstruct it, is this: if a trait such as reading ability is about 60% heritable in a population, then roughly 60% of any given person's reading ability is caused by their genes and the remaining 40% is within our power to change through schooling; he further concludes that a uniform improvement in schooling should substantially shrink achievement differences regardless of family background. I suspect this chains together several distinct mistakes, but they are tangled up with one another: heritability is a population-level variance decomposition rather than an apportionment of causes inside an individual; the estimate is specific to a particular population and a particular range of environments; raising a trait's mean through an intervention is not the same as reducing its genetic variance; and high within-population heritability places no constraint on the causes of differences between environments or between groups.

Within each of two very different rearing environments the offset is nearly constant, so almost all of the within-environment variance is genetic and heritability is high; but the offset differs greatly between the two environments, so the mean difference between them is almost entirely environmental. Explain how this can be so, why it is compatible with high within-environment heritability, and what it reveals about using heritability as a guide to intervention or to explaining group differences.
