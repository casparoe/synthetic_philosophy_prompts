I'm in this Model Congress thing at my high school and I got put on the judicary committee. I'm writing a bill and I want it to be something people actually argue about instead of the usual "we should ban plastic straws" stuff. The idea I've been kicking around is to get rid of fixed statutes of limitations for crimes and replace them with a kind of running score system. Here's the mechanism: for every full year between the offense and the start of trial, the maximum sentence a judge can impose automatically shrinks by 5%, compounding. But the shrink only counts for "clean years" — a year doesn't count if the defendant concealed evidence, threatened a witness, or got convicted of anything new. And to keep the state from letting old cases sit forever, the prosecution has to file a notice of continued interest every three years, and each notice costs the state a flat fee that goes into a victim compensation fund. Cases where the victim was under 14 at the time, or where the harm is still ongoing, are completely exempt.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a position you're defending. Walk me through how it would actually behave if some state adopted it: who does it help and hurt, how do prosecutors and defendants each try to game it, what predictable failure modes show up by like year five, and which of those can be fixed without gutting the whole point? And one more layer I keep getting stuck on: is the passage of time itself doing any moral work in a law like this, or is time just a rough proxy for evidence going stale and people moving on with their lives? I want to know what my bill accidently assumes about that.
