Before I go any further down the logical uncertainty / logical induction rabbit hole, I want the spec pinned down before I compare accounts. What does a theory of logical uncertainty actually have to do? I mean the problem of how a computationally bounded reasoner should assign degrees of belief to sentences that are already determinately true or false — math claims, logic claims, claims about what some fixed piece of code outputs — but that it hasnt established yet. Give me the desiderata, with the motivating case behind each, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of one camp. Then flag any pairs of desiderata you suspect can't be jointly met — thats the part I most want, the actual trade-off, not a scorecard of named theories.

Concretely, a good theory should be forced to say something definite about at least these cases, and you can tell me which of them are real requirements as opposed to things people over-fixate on: (1) a huge random tautology the system hasnt checked, so it's determinately true but the agent doesn't "see" it; (2) a trivial arithmetic fact like 1+1=2 after the agent derives it, vs that same fact a moment before it derives it; (3) an open problem like Goldbach or P vs NP, where a flat 0.5 feels like a cop-out and the credence should track the weight of partial heuristic evidence; (4) the case where the agent already assigns high credence to A and high credence to A→B but hasnt put them together, and would currently report a low credence in B; (5) the case where someone proves B next week, and we look back and ask what the theory says should have happened to the agent's credences in the meantime; (6) the self-referential one — a sentence about the agent's own future outputs, or the claim that its own reasoning system is consistent.

I have a hunch the real knife-fight is between something like not-being-Dutch-bookable-over-a-computable-set-of-obvious-logical-relations and not demanding logical omniscience, but I want you to lay out exactly what has to give and why, under what notion of "coherence." If the honest answer is that there are two different notions of logical uncertainty doing different jobs — one about assigning credences to propositions we happen to know are mathematical, and another about bounded, approximating reasoners — then say which desiderata belong to which, and where they actively conflict.
