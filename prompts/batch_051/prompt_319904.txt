I chair my university's research ethics board. A proposal before us concerns a two-year field trial, run by our public-health faculty, in a district where tuberculosis screening rates have been stagnant for a decade. In the trial, households assigned to the treatment arm would receive monthly home visits from a trained peer navigator, while the control arm would receive only the district's standard screening invitation. An elected community research council in the district has already held several months of ward-level consultations and issued a written finding that the trial is morally permissible and consistent with residents' interests. The researchers now ask us to accept that finding as sufficient ethical clearance and close our review. Our board is split. Some members argue that when another body is better placed to make the moral judgment, accepting its verdict is not laziness but humility: the council knows this community as we cannot, and our re-litigating the matter from a distance adds delay rather than protection. Others argue that moral judgment cannot be handed over like a factual report: however good the council's reasons, if we put our name to an approval without making our own assessment of the trial's fairness, we have failed to act as an ethics board at all. Draft the short written decision memo I will circulate before Friday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—and it should give the strongest case for the losing side its full weight before explaining why we should not follow it.
