I sit on the formulary and coverage committee of a national health service, and the distinction we keep trying to use is treatment versus enhancement. Our working rule is that we fund interventions which restore or protect normal, species-typical functioning and decline interventions which exceed it. The rule has the advantage of letting us say no to things we instinctively regard as optional self-improvement, but it is quietly falling apart. Prevention already looks like enhancement carried out early; normal aging changes with the population and with what medicine can do; and I suspect "normal functioning" is doing moral work we have never had to state out loud.

I want you to write me a decision procedure my committee can actually use, not an essay defending or attacking the distinction. Assume there is something worth preserving in the distinction but that it has to be rebuilt so it can survive contact with cases. The deliverable should be a reusable sequence of questions we ask about each request, in order, with enough specification that a generalist can tell what counts as a sufficient answer to each. It should say where we are entitled to stop with a no and where the procedure has to hand the decision to a person because the question has become one of values rather than fact. Be explicit about which steps are judgment dressed up as procedure and where the procedure is most likely to misfire.

The practical constraints are real. We meet monthly, the agenda is long, and no one on the committee is a philosopher; each case gets perhaps twenty minutes of discussion, plus whatever the procedure contributes. Our decisions become precedents for other requests and are public, so we need the procedure to be explainable to a patient who has been refused and to a minister who thinks we are rationing by vibes.

Run the finished procedure on three requests and show the reasoning at each step. The three are: growth hormone for an eleven-year-old boy projected to reach roughly five foot two with no identified endocrine deficiency; modafinil for a junior hospital doctor who wants it to manage alertness across twenty-four-hour shifts; and a medication intended to slow normal age-related memory decline in an otherwise healthy fifty-eight-year-old. Make sure at least one of these applications produces an answer the committee will not like, and say what in the procedure forces that answer rather than our instincts.

When the procedure is finished, tell me what would change your mind about where the line is drawn: which case or argument should cause me to revise the procedure, and which step in your chain of reasoning you would bet is the most fragile once we use it on the next fifty requests.

Format: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. Write the entire response as continuous prose, like a letter or an email to me; if you need the sequence to be clear, write it in sentences rather than as a displayed list.
