A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about a political career neither of us could classify, and by the end we had narrowed the disagreement to a question about Machiavelli's distinction between virtù and fortune. Rather than settling it, we decided to make our criteria explicit and then pressure-test them on a constructed case. The passage we kept returning to is from Niccolò Machiavelli, The Prince, chapter 25, in W. K. Marriott's public-domain translation:

“But a man is not often found sufficiently circumspect to know how to accommodate himself to the change, both because he cannot deviate from what nature inclines him to do, and also because, having always prospered by acting in one way, he cannot be persuaded that it is well to leave it; and, therefore, the cautious man, when it is time to turn adventurous, does not know how to do it, hence he is ruined; but had he changed his conduct with the times fortune would not have changed.”

From this we extracted four criteria for attributing a ruler's long success to virtù rather than fortune. (1) Causal contribution: the ruler's own conduct, not external forces alone, made the decisive difference to the outcome. (2) Preparatory foresight: the ruler built protections in advance of the turn of events, as one builds barriers and canals before a flood rather than merely coping after it. (3) Congruence: his mode of proceeding fit the spirit of the times during his career. (4) Non-coincidence: the fit between his nature and the times was not a bare accident; he either adapted when circumstances changed or showed a real capacity to.

Now consider this case. A prince seizes a commercial city by one impetuous stroke at a moment when exactly that boldness is what the situation rewards. For the next fifteen years every major crisis has the same shape: speed and audacity win, deliberation loses. He rules in a single unvarying key, builds no reserve funds or durable institutions, refuses the slow work of alliances, and dismisses advisers who urge caution. He dies in a riding accident at the height of his success; the era of caution and patience that would have undone him arrives a few years later, so he never faces a time that doesn't suit his temperament. My friend says criteria (1) through (3) are clearly met and the ruler has virtù. I am not sure criterion (4) is met—and if it is not, I do not know whether the ruler's success is virtù, fortune wearing the mask of virtù, or a sign that our criteria need repair.

Take me through the case criterion by criterion and give an argued verdict. Does this case show that one of the four criteria should be added, weakened, or dropped? End by saying whether the quoted passage itself decides the case, or whether Machiavelli's text leaves it underdetermined.
