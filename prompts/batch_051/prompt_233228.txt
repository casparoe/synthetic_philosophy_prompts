I keep noticing a pattern in ordinary decision-making that I do not have a word for, and I am hoping you can tell me whether it has an established name and, more importantly, help me see its exact boundaries.

The pattern is this. A person faces a long sequence of small choices, each of which moves her one step along some dimension. At each step, the change is small enough that it makes no felt difference on its own, except perhaps for a small gain of some kind. None of the individual steps looks like a mistake: if two conditions feel exactly alike, it seems unreasonable to refuse the one that is slightly better in some other respect. But the steps accumulate, and after enough of them she reaches a condition she would have refused outright if it had been offered to her as a single change at the start. The pain that began as a dull ache has, through tiny increases in a dose each of which felt no different from the last, become severe side effects; or a workload that grew by one minor commitment at a time, each trivial on its own, has swallowed every free evening.

I have seen the same shape in more than one setting, so I believe it is a general structure rather than a quirk of one story. What I want to know is what this structure is. Does it have a name? Is the mistake in the move from thinking that each step is acceptable to thinking that the entire journey is acceptable, or is the mistake earlier, in treating two conditions that differ imperceptibly as though they were the same? How is this different from ordinary weakness of will, where a person knows at the time that she is choosing the worse thing? Here no single choice seems worse. And does accepting this description force one to say that a person's preferences can run in a circle without her being irrational at any particular step? If the pattern has no settled name, tell me what you would call it and how you would define its edges. I am not looking for a bare label; I want the structure separated from the nearby phenomena it is often confused with.

Please write your answer as continuous prose, the way one would write an email or a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
