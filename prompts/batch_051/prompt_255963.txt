Consider the following argument, which someone might offer in defense of Otto Neurath's project of an international picture language — the system associated with his name and known as ISOTYPE, developed in interwar Vienna to present social and economic facts through rows of standardized pictures rather than prose.

(1) A word refers to what it refers to only by an arbitrary convention, and that convention must be learned separately for each natural language.
(2) A picture refers to what it depicts by visible resemblance, so a competent viewer can recognize what it depicts without first learning the arbitrary sound–meaning conventions of any particular natural language.
(3) Therefore a picture is a more neutral sign than a word: its meaning depends less on the local choices of a particular linguistic community.
(4) A sign whose meaning depends less on such local choices is better suited than one whose meaning depends on them to communicate matters of fact across linguistic and cultural boundaries.
(5) Therefore a standardized picture language of Neurath's kind is both more neutral and better suited than prose for communicating matters of fact across linguistic and cultural boundaries.

Is the argument valid? If not, say exactly which step fails, and under what reading of the key terms. In particular, distinguish the respect in which a picture really is less tied to the arbitrary sound–meaning pairings of a particular natural language from the respect in which a picture may still embody interpretive choices — of selection, simplification, stylization, and emphasis — made by its designer. If the argument can be repaired by weakening the conclusion, state the strongest conclusion the premises actually support.
