I'm a PhD student in religious studies working on sacrificial systems, and I keep going back and forth on ritual substitution. On one side, the scapegoat mechanism seems basically sound as a theory: communities under stress — plague, drought, a run of internal feuds — converge on a marginal substitute (a goat, sometimes a person), load it up with the accumulated guilt or impurity, and drive it out or kill it. And it appears to work. The aggression really does seem to get discharged into the substitute instead of tearing the group apart. It's a tidy mechanism, it unifies a huge range of otherwise bizarre sacrificial practices, and it predicts the calm after the killing. On the other side, I have a stubborn intuition that guilt is not the kind of thing that can be transferred. The goat didn't do it; if the scapegoat is a person, the community just murdered an innocent person, whatever the social payoff. People tell me the victim bears the community's guilt, as if that settles it. It doesn't.

So here's what I want: does the theory actually contradict the intuition, or does it only contradict the metaphysical gloss participants put on the ritual? If they genuinely conflict, which should give — should I debunk the intuition as moralistic squeamishness about a mechanism, or amend/abandon the theory? Or is there a reading on which they're compatible, and how would I tell that from just blurring the claims? I want a committed answer: pick a move and defend it, but be explicit about what evidence or argument would justify each of the moves, not just the one you favor.
