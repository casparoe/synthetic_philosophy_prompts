I am a novelist, and I have never found a satisfying account of when past investment is a reason to continue and when it merely feels like one. I would like a working definition of the sunk-cost fallacy — not the slogan, but a criterion precise enough to sort the following cases. I am confident that (1) and (7) are clear instances of the fallacy and that (2) clearly is not. On (3) through (6) I have no settled view. The deliverable is the definition itself; the cases are its test.

1. I keep writing a novel I have known since around page 300 to be unsalvageable, solely because I have already written three hundred pages.
2. I lay aside a manuscript after forty pages because a better idea arrives; the forty pages do not strike me as a reason to continue.
3. A novelist continues a series she has come to dislike because her readers are invested in the characters, though she has no interest left and no financial need to continue.
4. A writer keeps submitting a much-rejected manuscript because each revision has made her a better editor of her own work. I lean toward saying this is not the fallacy, but I cannot say exactly why the past is doing legitimate work here and not in (1).
5. A general refuses to withdraw troops from a failed campaign on the ground that the soldiers who have died must not have died for nothing. I cannot tell whether this is the fallacy in a uniform or a different kind of reason altogether.
6. I finish a novel I no longer believe in because I want to be the sort of person who finishes what she begins, and I suspect that my next book will go better if I know I can finish.
7. A man eats a restaurant meal he dislikes because he has already paid for it.

Propose the definition, run it through all seven cases, and say where it gives a surprising verdict, where it forces me to abandon or revise a leaning, and where it leaves the matter genuinely unsettled.
