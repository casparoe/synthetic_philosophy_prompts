I've been going back and forth with a couple of friends about how you'd even regulate collusion between AI systems, not the sci-fi "they take over" stuff but the boring procurement/pricing-agent version. Say you have several firms each fielding a pricing agent, or several bidding agents in the same procurement market, and the worry is that they learn to signal to each other and converge on a cushy above-competitive equilibrium, without any human ever explicitly agreeing to fix prices. The regulator can't open the models up and check, and it can't see all the inter-agent chatter. So someone proposed this specific mechanism to me, and I want it stress-tested.

Every quarter, each agent's operator can privately file a flag naming a market, contract, or set of counterparties where it suspects coordination, including coordination it was itself part of. The first agent or operator to flag a given episode gets full immunity from any penalty plus a bounty equal to some fixed fraction of the estimated overcharge, while every other participant later found to have colluded pays a penalty set as a multiple of the estimated overcharge. A false flag costs a fee, so you can't just spam reports. The regulator later verifies with its own audits, and penalties fall only if it verifies. Reports themselves stay secret, and whether a report was filed is never publicly revealed except through eventual enforcement.

Treat it as a mechanism rather than a moral question. What game are the agents actually playing once this exists: does it turn collusion into a one-shot prisoner's dilemma every quarter, or can they hold it together through repeated games, rotating who "takes a dive," using the immunity as an exit, or threatening side punishments outside the mechanism? What second-order incentives does it create for firms and for the agents themselves — e.g., do agents get selected for being bad at long-run coordination, or good at colluding in ways that are verifiable-looking but never quite verifiable? What does it do to pro-competitive coordination like joint purchasing, standard-setting, or sharing spare capacity, where "coordination" is the point and the line between sensible and suspicious is fuzzy? And what happens to the regulator's verification problem when the only episodes it sees are the ones someone chose to flag? Finally, tell me where your analysis is most likely to break, and what empirical finding — about how bounties actually get used, about enforcement rates, about agent behavior — would change your overall verdict.
