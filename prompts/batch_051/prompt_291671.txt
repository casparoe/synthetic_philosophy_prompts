I keep coming back to a bit in Simone Weil's *On the Abolition of All Political Parties* where she says a party is essentially a machine for making a certain kind of pressure, not a place for thinking:

"A political party is a machine to manufacture collective passion. A political party is an organisation designed to exert collective pressure on the minds of all its individual members. The first goal and the ultimate goal of any political party is its own growth, and that without limit."

I'm less intrested in her full abolitionist conclusion than in this definition, because I can't tell how far it's supposed to stretch. Suppose, for example, a group of people inside a larger party form a "tendency" or loose faction. They have no dues, no membership list, no leader exactly, just a shared analysis paper circulating among perhaps twenty people, and they coordinate a bit on how to speak at branch meetings. They call themselves an anti-party caucus: their explicit aim, written into their founding note, is to stop the party acting like a machine for collective passion, and to force more genuinely deliberative meetings. They themselves act collectively — they plan who will raise which point, they pool information, they support each other when speeches go badly. Does Weil's passage apply to them anyway? Is an anti-party caucus still a political party in her sense, and if so, is that a problem for her view, or just for the caucus?
