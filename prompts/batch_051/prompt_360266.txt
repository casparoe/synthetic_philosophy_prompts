You are a philosopher of mind with expertise in perception, concept acquisition, and the empiricism–innatism debate. Think the question through slowly; start with a brief brainstorm of the live positions, then give your considered answer and say precisely which premises force which predictions.

Consider Molyneux's problem in roughly its classical form. A person blind from birth can distinguish a cube from a sphere by touch; if her sight were suddenly restored, could she say by sight alone, before touching the objects, which was which? Associationist empiricists standardly answer no, on the ground that the connection between visual and tactile ideas must be built from correlated experience; proponents of innate or amodal spatial representation are more inclined to answer yes, on the ground that shape knowledge is not confined to any single sense.

Now vary the case. The subject has been blind from birth and has never touched or seen the shapes. Instead, for years she has worn a sensory-substitution device: a forehead camera encodes visual edges, shading, and perspective into a continuous stream of sound through headphones. By sound alone she can reliably identify a cube and a sphere from a distance and from unfamiliar angles, and she can guide her hand to grasp each object without fumbling. After surgery restores her sight, she sees a cube and a sphere on a table and is asked, before touching either, which is which.

Work through what each side in the original dispute is committed to predicting here, and whether the old disagreement survives the variation. If modality-specific association is doing the work, the device has taught her only a sound–shape mapping, not a sight–shape mapping; if innate or amodal spatial representation is doing the work, the sounds may have been merely another route to it. Which premise of the original debate now does the controlling work, and do the two camps converge, or does the variation split one of them internally?
