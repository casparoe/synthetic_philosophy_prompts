In the St. Petersburg game, a fair coin is flipped until the first heads; if that happens on flip n, the player receives 2^n dollars. The expected payoff is the sum over all n of 2^n times (1/2)^n, which is an endless sum of ones, so it diverges, even though the game ends in finitely many flips with probability 1. I think I have found the same structure in the codebase I maintain. Our API client retries failed requests, starting with a 2-second timeout and doubling after each failure. If each attempt has an independent 1/2 probability of success, the probability that the first success occurs on attempt n is (1/2)^n, and the cumulative time spent through attempt n is 2^(n+1) minus 2. The expected total time is therefore the sum over n of 2 minus 2^(1-n), which also diverges, even though the request eventually succeeds with probability 1. This looks to me like the St. Petersburg paradox with payouts measured in seconds instead of dollars, but I cannot tell whether that is a real identity of structure or just a numerical coincidence. If the parallel is real, does it give me anything usable for setting retry budgets, reasoning about tail latency, or deciding how much compute to provision? And is a team paying for that divergent expected compute making the same kind of error as a gambler paying an arbitrary sum to play the game?
