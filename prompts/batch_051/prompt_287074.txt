A friend showed me an argument that no felt state is "luminous" — where a state is luminous iff, whenever you're in it, you're in a position to know you're in it. He thinks the same move also puts pressure on the KK principle (if you know that p, you know that you know that p), but I'm mainly stuck on the luminosity part. I hate the conclusion but I cant find the bad step. Here it is.

Imagine a run of moments t0 to t100. At t0 you're definitely cold; at t100 you're definitely warm. Each adjacent pair is so close in how it feels that, from the inside, you couldn't tell ti apart from ti+1.

(1) Suppose feeling cold is luminous: whenever you feel cold, you're in a position to know you feel cold.
(2) You feel cold at t0, so by (1) you're in a position to know you feel cold at t0.
(3) If you're in a position to know that you feel cold at ti, then you feel cold at ti+1. Reason: ti and ti+1 are indiscriminable from the inside, so the very same basis that would support your belief at ti would be available at ti+1, where the belief would be false if you weren't cold. A basis that's that compatible with falsehood is too unsafe to count as knowledge, so you couldn't have known at ti, and therefore couldn't have been in a position to know.
(4) So you feel cold at t1 (from 2 and 3). By (1) again, you're in a position to know at t1. By (3), you feel cold at t2. Keep going.
(5) Eventually you feel cold at t100, contradicting the setup. So feeling cold is not luminous.

I keep feeling like (3) is the cheat — it seems to slide from "I can't discriminate the cases" to "my basis is the same," and maybe my being in a position to know at ti doesn't require my basis to be safe in that way. But maybe I'm just trying to save the conclusion. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails and what's the corrected version? If it is valid, what should I give up, and does KK actually go down with it?
