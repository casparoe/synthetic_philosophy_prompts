I run a small removals business, and last year a court decision reinterpreted a piece of health-and-safety guidance in a way that made something we did in 2023 retrospectively non-compliant. I've been mildly obsessed with the rule of law ever since, and there's one thing I can't get straight.

Everyone seems to agree that retrospective legislation is basically the hallmark of tyranny. A parliament that passes a law today criminalising something you did last Tuesday is doing something obviously wrong, and constitutions all over the world ban it. But when a court changes the law through a new interpretation and applies that interpretation to the parties in front of it — and to past conduct more generally — nobody calls that tyranny. We call it the common law developing, or just judges doing their job. In my own case, the rule I supposedly broke didn't exist, as far as anyone could tell, until the judgement that said I'd broken it.

So here's the asymmetry: retrospective law-making by legislatures is treated as an affront to the rule of law, but retrospective law-making by courts is treated as normal, even admirable. Is there actually a principled difference between the two, or are we just used to one and not the other? I can think of some candidate justifications — courts only "find" law that was already there, legislatures are elected and courts aren't, judges have to decide the case in front of them somehow, parties expect the common law to evolve — but each of them feels leaky in it's own way when I push on it.

You're a legal philosopher who works on the rule of law. Start by brainstorming the candidate justifications and say in a sentence or two why each fails or half-works, then give me the strongest version of the best one, test it against the hardest case you can think of, and give an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept or given up. Keep it under 400 words, and please don't give me a reading list — I want your actual reasoning, not references.
