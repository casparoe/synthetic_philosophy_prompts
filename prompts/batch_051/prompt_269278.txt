I have noticed a pattern in how modern institutions work, and I would like to know whether there is a name for it. In older stories about power, someone with authority gives an order and punishes disobedience. But in the places I have worked and studied—schools, hospitals, offices—control often does not look like that. Instead, people are constantly recorded, measured, and ranked: patient files, student transcripts, performance metrics, incident reports. Information is collected not only after something goes wrong but continuously, and the people being observed can usually see the records or at least know they exist. Over time, they begin to regulate their own behavior before anyone says anything, because they assume they are, or might be, under observation. What strikes me is that power seems to operate through visibility and classification more than through direct commands. The result is not just that people comply, but that they become their own first line of supervision: they check their own habits against a standard they did not set, correct themselves, and sometimes even produce information about themselves that helps the system classify them further. I have seen this happen in a school that made students fill in self-assessment forms for behavior, and in a workplace where employees logged their own time and activity categories. Is there an established concept for this exact shift—from being punished after the fact to being shaped by the way one is seen and sorted? I am not asking about surveillance in the ordinary sense, because it is not only cameras or watching; it is the way the mere possibility of being seen, plus a system of categories, moves the work of control inside the person. What is the nearest established idea, how does it differ from what I am describing, and where are the edges of the concept?
