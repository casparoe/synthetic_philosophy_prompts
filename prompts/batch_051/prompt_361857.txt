You are a philosopher of economics who works on the structure of entrenched policy disagreements. Take your time: begin by brainstorming the distinct axes on which the parties might be disagreeing, then commit to a diagnosis and defend it.

Last night I spent two hours arguing with a friend who works in energy policy. He kept insisting that a carbon price — a tax or a cap with tradable allowances — is the one serious lever, that performance standards and industrial policy are second-best politics, and that degrowth is a mood rather than a program. By the end I found myself defending much more regulatory and sufficiency-oriented positions than I would have predicted, and I honestly cannot tell how much of that was because the arguments were good and how much was reacting to his confidence. The argument stopped in frustration, not disagreement resolved. It pushed me to think about the wider impasse this mirrors. The carbon-pricing side argues that setting a price equal to the social cost of carbon lets dispersed emitters find the cheapest abatement and gives every technology an equal incentive, with minimal picking of winners. The regulation side replies that pricing is politically diluted, delayed, regressive unless deliberately engineered, and poorly matched to how states actually act, so standards, bans, mandates, and public investment are needed. The degrowth or post-growth side says both camps are fighting over how to decarbonize an economy whose deeper problem is its commitment to aggregate growth and throughput; on their view, treating GDP growth as a feasibility constraint rather than a variable to be contested already begs the question.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is less about expected abatement elasticities or decoupling rates than about three deeper mismatches: what the policy instrument is supposed to control (a marginal incentive, a directly mandated outcome, or the scale of the economy); what baseline counts as realistic (a textbook market, an actually existing state with lobbyists and short electoral horizons, or biophysical limits); and whether growth is a neutral measure of resources for welfare or a substantive normative commitment. But I worry this diagnosis is too convenient — the kind of "it's all conceptual" story that lets everyone keep their priors — and that a real empirical or formal question may be doing the work underneath, such as how abatement responds to non-marginal price changes or whether absolute decoupling is physically possible at the relevant scale.

What I want is a diagnosis of the disagreement itself — why, after decades of serious work, it remains stuck, and what kind of disagreement it really is — not a survey of climate policy positions and not a verdict on which suite of policies to adopt. Is this a verbal dispute, an empirical dispute, a normative dispute, a methodological dispute about whose models count, or a compound of these with different layers depending on the issue? Which components could in principle be moved by evidence or formal results, and which are fixed starting intuitions the sides cannot argue each other out of? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a distinction, a natural experiment, a model, a reformulated question — or should we expect no progress on certain fronts, and why?
