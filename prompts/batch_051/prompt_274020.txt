I've spent way too long in a seminar devoted to indicative conditionals, and we're stuck on a single question: what do the triviality results actually show? The frustrating part is that we all accept the same mathematics and the same textbook presentation of the theorem, and yet nobody's view moves an inch.

Here's the shared ground. Suppose an indicative conditional like 'if it rains, the game is off' expresses a proposition with ordinary truth conditions, so it sits in a Boolean algebra like any other sentence. Suppose also that the probability of such a conditional always equals the corresponding conditional probability: P(if A then B) = P(B | A). Lewis's theorem, in one standard version, then shows something disastrous: for any A and B where both A-and-B and A-and-not-B have positive probability, P(B | A) is forced to equal P(B). In other words, the conditional probability can't actually depend on the antecedent; the probabilistic connection that motivated the thesis in the first place survives only in degenerate settings. Since people plainly don't reason that way, something in the package has to go.

The fight is about which thing goes. One camp says the conditional doesn't express a proposition at all; its job is to express conditional belief, and the probability equation is the fixed point any account has to preserve. For them, triviality is a proof that the propositional framework was the mistake. A second camp says conditionals do have truth conditions—a closest-world style account, maybe—and the probability equation is at best an approximation or a special-case rule; for them, triviality is a refutation of the unrestricted equation, and that's that. A third camp says the theorem proves less than advertised because it stacks the deck: it assumes that the phrase 'the probability of the conditional' picks out one proposition that stays fixed across all those different probability functions. If the conditional's content shifts with context, or with the probability function itself, or if it only has a propositional content relative to a given probability space, then the whole setup doesn't get going.

My own suspicion is that the real conflict isn't about the logic at all; it's about what gets to count as data. The no-truth-conditions people treat our plain numerical judgments about the probability of a conditional as hard data that a semantic theory is answerable to in exactly the way it's answerable to truth-value judgments. The truth-conditions people treat those numerical judgments as loose talk—as really tracking assertability, or acceptability, or something other than the literal content of the sentence. If that's right, the theorem can't move anyone, because each side has already decided which class of speaker-judgments its theory has to fit.

So my question: is my diagnosis right? Or is there a substantive formal or empirical issue underneath that I'm missing—a new triviality-style result with weaker premises, or a body of ordinary examples where the two camps make different predictions about speaker judgments, or a distinction between semantic content and assertability that splits the difference? What kind of disagreement is this, really, and what would actually count as progress? I want your best diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, with reasons, and an honest answer about whether we should expect the impasse to continue. Don't give me a literature survey; I've already read plenty.
