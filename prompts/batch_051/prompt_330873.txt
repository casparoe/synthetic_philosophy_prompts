Here is an argument I built for a seminar on Berkeley, and I now suspect it proves too much, but I cannot locate the failure. It is an expanded version of the master argument, aimed at the familiar conclusion that a sensible object cannot exist unperceived, yet the same moves seem to show that no one can think about anything as existing apart from being thought about. That result cannot be right, so something must give. Here is the reasoning:

(1) Try to represent a tree as it is when no mind perceives it. To represent that state of affairs, you must bring the tree before your mind. But then the tree is before a mind — yours — and so it is not unperceived. The state of affairs you attempted to represent is therefore contradicted by the very act of representing it.

(2) If a state of affairs cannot be represented without contradiction, it cannot obtain.

(3) Hence the tree cannot exist unperceived; and since the tree was arbitrary, no sensible object can exist unperceived.

(4) The reasoning generalizes to every representation-denying state of affairs. It appears to show that you cannot represent the early universe as it was before any mind existed, because in representing it you have it in mind; that you cannot think about a thing as it is at a time when no one is thinking about it; and that you cannot even mention something no one has ever mentioned, because in mentioning it you mention it.

(5) But some of these consequences have to be false. It is perfectly coherent to think about the early universe before minds existed, to suppose the last dodo died unobserved, or to wonder what happened in a room no one entered. If the master argument commits us to the denials in (4), then it proves far more than Berkeley's conclusion, and it proves what we already know to be false.

Where does this argument go wrong? I would prefer to keep (1), since I find the master argument's opening move hard to give up, and I am reluctant to deny (2), since some link between representation and possibility seems right. But I want to know exactly what rejecting each option costs. Please identify the step or inferential move at which the argument fails, explain why it looked legitimate, and say what remains of the master argument once the failure is exposed.
