I've been going in circles on the deterrence paradox, and I want a negotiated verdict rather than a side. Here are the two things I'm holding.

On the one hand, the policy-level argument looks nearly airtight to me. A threat only deters if the other side expects you to carry it out, and the other side can watch what you're actually committed to. So you cannot evaluate the retaliatory strike the way you would evaluate an unprompted attack. Conditional on deterrence failing, retaliation stops being defensive and starts looking like revenge or pointless escalation; but if you let that ex post judgment govern your ex ante posture, you make the attack more likely. From that standpoint the ugly features aren't bugs: launch-on-warning automation, pre-delegated authority, deliberately cultivating a reputation for being unpredictable or half-mad, a willingness to leave the outcome partly to chance. They're how you make the threat credible. And I get that this is the same logic as brinkmanship: if you're not willing to risk the fall, standing near the edge is just theater.

On the other hand, my firm intuition is that none of that can make it permissible, at the moment after the attack has already happened, to incinerate a city full of people who didn't choose there government's action. It feels like the theory is saying the only way to prevent the worst thing is to genuinely become, not just seem, the sort of agent who would do the worst thing — and maybe even to raise the chance of doing it by accident. That can't be acceptable, but every attempt to soften it seems to leak credibility back into the system.

I want to know which thing should yield. Is the intuition one that should be debunked and dropped once I see what deterrence requires, or has the theory amended itself into a reductio, or is there a reading on which "being" and "credibly seeming" come apart enough to keep both? Walk through what would justify each move — intuition debunked, theory amended, theory abandoned, compatibilist reading — not just pick one. Also say what would change your mind: which specific argument or consideration would flip the verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
