I've been trying to get a handle on the philosophical zombies debate and I keep running into the same wall. The setup is familiar enough: imagine a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of me, physically identical in every respect, behaving exactly the same, but with no phenomenal consciousness—nothing it is like to be that duplicate, just dark inside. One side says this is conceivable, and if it's conceivable it's possible, and if it's possible then the physical facts don't exhaust the conscious facts, so physicalism is false. The other side says the whole thing is a trick of the imagination: you can't actually keep the entire microphysical story fixed in your head while subtracting experience, so what you're really imagining is a coarse cartoon of the physics, or you're failing to notice a contradiction. On that view the conceivability premise is just an intuition dressed up as modal logic, and the argument gets its force from the way the thought experiment flatters the imaginer.

What strikes me is how stuck this debate has been. Both sides know each other's moves, nobody thinks a future neuroscience result will settle it, and yet the argument just keeps circulating without anyone moving. Its not that the participants are stupid; it's that they seem to be talking past each other in some deep way.

My working theory is that the word "conceivable" is doing a bait and switch: one side means something like "I don't notice a contradiction when I try the exercise," while the other means something like "it would survive ideal rational scrutiny," and the argument transfers the burden from one of those senses to the other as needed. But I'm not sure that's the real diagnosis. Maybe the disagreement is actually about whether first-person imaginability is any kind of evidence about possibility at all, or about what "metaphysical possibility" is supposed to be, or about who has the burden of proof in explaining consciousness. Or maybe the two camps are answering different questions: one about what follows from our concepts, the other about what the world is like.

I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, not a survey of the literature and not a verdict on who is right. What kind of disagreement is this really—verbal, substantive, a clash of starting intuitions, a hidden methodological difference? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a better account of the link between conceivability and possibility, some kind of formal result, giving up the zombie scenario for a different argument, or just accepting that no progress should be expected? Be honest if you think the impasse is permanent.
