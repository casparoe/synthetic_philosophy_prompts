I am a doctoral student in political philosophy, and I have spent the past year following an exchange between longtermists and their critics that now seems to me to have stopped moving. The critics argue that longtermism, understood as the view that an action's moral value is determined chiefly by its effects on the very long-run future, has a structural affinity with authoritarianism. Their thought is this: once near-term harms are allowed to be dwarfed by astronomical far-future stakes, it becomes difficult to resist the conclusion that one may, or even should, impose sweeping surveillance, restrict the political speech of groups thought likely to obstruct technological progress, concentrate power in some technocratic body, or curtail democratic accountability, provided any of this raises, by even a small amount, the probability that humanity survives and that its eventual values are locked in well. The longtermists reply that this is a caricature. On their account, the same uncertainty and cluelessness that make far-future stakes so large also make one's grip on those stakes fragile; hence longtermism, worked out carefully, favors robust institutions, experimentation, dispersed power, civil liberties, and public legitimacy over concentrated coercion, not merely as a moral compromise but as the epistemically safer bet. Both sides appear to share much of the factual picture (the future is vast and our influence on it is poorly understood) and much of the ethical framework (an impartial, broadly consequentialist orientation). And yet neither side moves.

My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily a factual disagreement about the far future, but a disagreement about what longtermism commits one to, and in particular about how a view that aggregates large expected value should treat the deontic constraints on coercing people in the present. But I can see rival diagnoses: it might be a substantive disagreement about whether expected-value reasoning may override rights and side constraints; a political-theoretic disagreement about whether concentrated power can ever be trusted with a far-future mandate; a verbal disagreement about how much of the longtermist package, its axiology, its epistemology, and its practical recommendations, is part of the view itself; or a difference in bedrock intuitions about whether tiny probabilities of astronomical value can license present coercion that neither side can argue for. I would like a diagnosis of the structure of this impasse, engaging what each side actually says, and an account of what, given that diagnosis, would count as real progress: a formal result in decision theory, a sharper distinction between the axiological claim and its political or practical interpretation, an empirical finding, a change of question, or an argument that none of these should be expected to move the two sides. I am interested in the anatomy of the disagreement rather than in a verdict about who is right.
