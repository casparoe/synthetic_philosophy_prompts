I'm a novelist, and I'm stuck on a subplot that needs a moral argument at its center, but I'd rather you design the case than send me off to the literature. Heres the brief.

Build me a realistic one-paragraph thought experiment where a woman, call her Mara, blows the whistle on her longtime mentor, and two considered views of loyalty come apart.

View one is a virtue-ethics take: loyalty is a settled disposition to act for the good of the person you are loyal to, which sometimes means refusing to help them hide from a truth. View two is a deontological partialist take: you have special obligations to people with whom you share a deep history, and those obligations function as side-constraints, so some acts are forbidden no matter how much good they do.

The specs: in the case you build, View one must say Mara's action was the more loyal act toward the mentor himself, not just toward the public or the institution. View two must say it was a betrayal of the mentor, even though the mentor is not a villain and Mara still loves him. Set it in a ordinary workplace or community institution, not a spy service or a courtroom. No sexual abuse and no corpses. The mentor's secret should be a single badly motivated choice rather than a pattern of corruption, and Mara's decision to go public has to be genuinely voluntary, not legally required.

After the paragraph, walk me through what each view says about the action and why, and be explicit about which feature of the case is doing the work to pull the two verdicts apart. Then tell me what would happen if that feature were removed—would the case collapse back into a standard conflict between loyalty and honesty? End with the strongest objection someone in View two could make to the claim that View one is loyalty at all, and how a View one person could answer them.

This is for the novel's moral climax, so the scenario can't just stipulate the verdicts; it has to make them fall out of the two views.
