Below are my considered verdicts on seven cases about promises. I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

1. I promise my neighbour to water her plants while she is away. She has no way of knowing whether I do it, and she will not be back for a week. I take myself to be bound.
2. At a conference, a stranger says she admires my work, and I say, purely to be polite, that I will send her comments on her paper by Friday. She then turns down another scholar's offer of comments so that she can wait for mine. I now take myself to be bound to send something, even though I never meant to commit myself.
3. Someone threatens to reveal a secret of mine unless I promise to pay him $500. I make the promise. I do not take myself to be bound.
4. I promise my dying mother that I will visit her grave every year. After she dies, I still take myself to be bound, even though she can no longer know, be harmed, or release me.
5. When my co-founder and I dissolve our company, I promise not to open a competing shop in town. She then fails to pay me the buyout she had promised. I do not take myself to be bound by the non-compete clause.
6. As an obvious joke, I "promise" my neighbour to paint my house bright pink by June. Everyone involved recognizes it as a joke. I do not take myself to be bound.
7. I promise a friend that I will tell his wife he was with me last night, when he was not. I do not take myself to be bound; if anything, I take myself to be obligated not to do it.

Is there a single principle of promissory obligation that delivers all seven verdicts? Two of them seem especially hard to reconcile: the conference email binds me because someone relied on it even though I did not intend to promise, while the deathbed promise binds me although no one can now rely on it or be harmed by its breach. If no single principle works, I want the cleanest reconstruction of my view and an honest statement of where it gives way.

Organize your reply in four titled sections, in this order. In "The principle," state one principle or a small set of candidate principles as precisely as you can (around 150 words). In "What forces each clause," go through the cases and say which case, or which aspect of a case, forces each part of the principle (around 200 words). In "Further cases," apply your principle to two cases I have not settled: a promise I made to my father as he entered dementia and no longer remembers, and a promise to keep a friend's secret where keeping it would let a third person be seriously harmed; say what the principle predicts and whether I should accept that prediction (around 200 words). In "The cost," say which of my seven verdicts, if any, your principle cannot accommodate, and whether you would overturn it or complicate the principle to save it (around 150 words).
