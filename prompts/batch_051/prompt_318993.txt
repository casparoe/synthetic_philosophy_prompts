Section 21(1) of the UK Theft Act 1968 defines blackmail this way:

"A person is guilty of blackmail if, with a view to gain for himself or another or with intent to cause loss to another, he makes any unwarranted demand with menaces; and for this purpose a demand with menaces is unwarranted unless the person making it does so in the belief—
(a)that he has reasonable grounds for making the demand; and
(b)that the use of the menaces is a proper means of reinforcing the demand."

I want this passage to be the fixed point. The paradox of blackmail is usually put as follows: it can be lawful to threaten to reveal someone's secret, and lawful to reveal it, yet it is blackmail to demand payment as the price of silence. Two privileges seem to combine into a wrong, and this statute locates the wrong in the single word "unwarranted" — a word that does its work only if there is already an account of when a demand with menaces is warranted. Note also a strange clause: the question whether the demand is unwarranted is answered by the maker's beliefs (reasonable grounds; proper means), not by whether the demand actually was warranted. Any theory of blackmail has to make sense of that subjectivization.

Before I read any further theories, write me the specification of what an adequate theory of blackmail must get right. Give the desiderata: the requirements it must meet, with the case or consideration behind each. Include at least these, and add any others that are doing genuine work:

1. It must dissolve or explain the two-privileges paradox rather than restate it — it must say exactly what is added when a lawful threat and a lawful act are combined into a conditional demand.
2. It must respect the statute's structure: the wrong is in the "unwarranted demand with menaces," the gain/loss element is agnostic about what is asked for, and section 21(2) makes the nature of the act or omission demanded immaterial. So the theory must handle demands for lawful performance, and demands where the maker may in fact be entitled to the thing demanded.
3. It must explain the subjectivization of the warrant element — why the statute cares about the maker's belief that there are reasonable grounds and that the menaces are a proper means, rather than requiring the demand to be objectively reasonable or the menaces objectively proper — and what that implies about whether blackmail is a wrong of conduct, of belief, or of something else.
4. It must separate blackmail from legitimate hard bargaining: a union threatening a strike, a lawyer's demand letter, a creditor threatening to sue, a consumer threatening to take business elsewhere. Whatever criterion marks off blackmail must be statable without assuming the cases it is meant to distinguish.
5. It must classify the menaces that can count: lawful ones, true disclosures, reputational and emotional pressure, threats of lawful omission. It must say whether all menaces count once the demand is unwarranted, or only some kinds.
6. It must explain what work conditionality does. "I am about to publish this" together with "I accept donations" may not be blackmail, while "pay me or I publish" is. Is the wrong in the threat, the demand, the link between them, or the disproportion between what is demanded and what is withheld?

The main families to score: paradox-dissolvers, wrongful-threat or rights-based views, exploitation views, and warrant-based readings of the statute.
