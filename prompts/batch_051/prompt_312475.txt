I've been reading up on Lakatos on my own, no background in any of this, just got curious about how scientific ideas hang together and eventually get dropped. The way I understand him, a research programme has a hard core of central claims that its people won't let evidence count against, plus a protective belt of auxilary assumptions they're willing to adjust every time some result doesn't fit. A programme is doing fine while the belt tweaks keep it predicting novel facts; it starts degenerating when the tweaks are mostly after-the-fact fixes and nothing new is being predicted.

I'm a programmer, and this sounds exactly like the difference between a healthy codebase and a legacy one. You have the core API or architecture that nobody will rewrite no matter what, and around it a belt of adapters, feature flags, and compatability shims that get patched whenever a test fails or a customer reports a bug. A healthy codebase keeps shipping genuinely new features; a dying one keeps passing tests only because somebody keeps adjusting the tests or tacking on another shim. So: hard core = core API, protective belt = adapters and shims, novel predictions = new features that don't collapse when they hit production.

Is that a real structural identity or just me pattern-matching? If it's real, what would have to be true of both sides for things to transfer—for instance, would technical debt tell us something accurate about when a research programme should be abandoned, and would something like rival programmes (you can abandon one codebase for a competitor's) break the mapping? And if it's only a loose analogy, what's the first thing it would predict about science that comes out false?
