I teach philosophy of language, and over the last few years I've been playing with something I call geometric semantics, and I'd like an honest verdict from someone who knows the territory better than I do. The central claim is this: in a well-trained semantic embedding space, a concept is not a point but a convex region. If two things fall under a concept, then any item whose embedding lies on the straight line between them should also be an instance, or at least a borderline instance. Put 'dog' and 'wolf' close together in the space, and the interpolated vector between them should land on something doggish — a coyote, a mutt, a jackal — not a toaster. A few things fall out of this, if it's true. Graded membership is just distance from the region's center, which is why a robin is a better bird than a penguin. Synonymy is overlap of regions, hypernymy is containment of one region inside another, and metaphor is a rotation or projection of one region onto another. I know this sounds like prototype theory with a linear-algebra costume, and I know thats a real risk — I just don't know whether someone has already built exactly this, or whether the convexity claim even holds for real embedding spaces rather than being an artifact of how they get trained.

So here's what I'd like. Tell me whether the theory is coherent, and whether I've reinvented something that already has a name — and if so, whether my version has anything the existing version lacks, or whether it just has extra confusions. Then hit me with the three or four hardest cases. I can already feel trouble with negation ('not a cat' is not an obvious region), with antonyms that sit close together, and with concepts that seem perfectly good but have no natural convex shape. Tell me which of these are fatal and which I can absorb by making the theory vaguer. And please answer in American English, since I want to use the response in a draft without having to convert the style.
