In a seminar on the metaphysics of modality, we have been working through arguments that modal properties—what a thing could or must have—can be surprising. I keep returning to a version of the necessity of origin that seems to prove more than it should, and I cannot locate the faulty step.

Here is the argument as I have reconstructed it. (1) If an organism such as me could have developed from a gamete other than the one I actually developed from, then it is possible for an organism to have a different origin than its actual one. (2) But the identity of a particular organism is fixed by its developmental origin: once you specify the originating gametes and the particular biological history through which they produced this organism, you have specified this organism rather than any other. (3) So no organism can have a different originating gamete than the one it actually had. (4) Therefore I could not have developed from a different sperm or egg. (5) But I can easily imagine a situation in which my parents conceived a child a month later than they actually did, and the child in that situation has a slightly different set of traits because of a different genetic recombination. (6) It is natural to say that in that situation, I would have been conceived later; but by (4) that is impossible. (7) The only way to reconcile the intuitions behind (2) and (5) is to say that the later child is not me, though I can imagine him being me. (8) So apparently our ability to imagine such a situation is not a reliable guide to what is possible. (9) But if our modal imagination systematically misfires in this case, then origin essentialism undermines one of the main sources of evidence we have that any ordinary modal claim—that the table could have been somewhere else, that the lecture could have started later—is true. (10) That conclusion seems too broad to accept, but I cannot see which premise it forces me to give up.

I am especially unsure whether the pressure falls on (2), because the “fixes identity” language might smuggle in the conclusion, or on (6), because the natural reading of “I would have been conceived later” might be a way of thinking about an alternative history rather than a strict identity claim. But I also wonder whether accepting the argument just means we should be more moderate about the role of imaginability in modal epistemology. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? And whichever diagnosis you give, I would also like to know which further commitment it costs, and what consideration would most likely make you change it.
