I work as a legal advisor to a small country's cabinet office, and after a flood disrupted the state opening of parliament a few years back (nobody died, but it was much closer than the public knows), there's now a serious proposal on the table for a designated-survivor rule. The idea is that at every state opening, budget speech, and other event where the whole constitutional line of succession is expected be in one room, one minister would be held back at an undisclosed location with a small staff and the authority to form an emergency government if the worst happens. The working group keeps treating it as a logistics problem: comms links, updated line-of-succession rules, how to brief the survivor. But I keep coming back to the ethical structure of the thing, and I don't think we've named it honestly. We would be asking one person, every year, to sit in a bunker while their colleagues are killed, and then to become head of government over a country that is simultaneously in mourning and in crisis. Whatever their ordinary democratic mandate, that person would govern under circumstances no election ever considered. And there's real disagreement among officials about whether the survivor should even be told in advance that they've been selected, which raises the weird question of what it would mean to consent to a role whose entire content is "you survive; everyone else may not."

Could you lay out what you see as the most important ethical implications of designated-survivor arrangements, and what a defensible version of the policy would have to include or rule out in light of them? I'm particularly interested in the normative anatomy: legitimacy, consent, moral luck, what we owe the person we ask to survive, and what we owe the public about who would govern them after a catastrophe. I have to bring something to the working group next month, and I'd rather come in with the uncomfortable questions than with a clean policy draft.
