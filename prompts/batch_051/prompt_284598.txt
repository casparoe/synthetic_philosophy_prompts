I've been kicking around a distinction I'd like checked out. I call it 'relevance by default' versus 'relevance by touch'. The frame problem, as I understand it, is the problem of how an agent knows which facts are unaffected by an action without enumerating all of them, and which effects matter without following every causal ripple. My hunch is that the problem is an artefact of representing the world as a big store of sentences plus inference rules. If you instead represent the world as a compressed, situated model that only gets updated where the action 'touches' it, then relevance is built into the representation: there are no unrepresented non-effects to list, and questions about unchanged facts never arise because the unchanged facts aren't tokened as separate sentences at all. The model just re-stabilises around the action, the way a map is corrected by redrawing one altered road rather than by reconsidering every other road. I'm aware this might just be relocating the problem into how the agent picks the right model or knows where the touches go. So: is this distinction a real contribution or just a restatement, and is the frame problem dissolved, dodged, or pushed deeper? Give me the strongest case you can think of where even a compressed, situated model still runs into the original problem — where the agent has to think through the unmentioned to know what it should care about. And be honest about whether the distinction is doing any work.
