Suppose your country's consumer protection agency floated a rule for anything with a public-facing AI chatbot: the company is liable for false or deceptive statements if an ordinary reasonable customer would have taken the bot to be asserting something, and courts are instructed to reconstruct what the bot asserted using something like Dennett's intentional stance — treat the bot as a rational agent whose words are the expression of whatever beliefs and desires would make those words a sensible move in that conversation. The company can't get out of it by showing the bot is just a text-prediction system with no beliefs, unless the bot told the customer, in the same message and before the contested claim, that it has no knowledge and is only predicting text.

So its a legal mechanism, not a moral claim about whether the bot really believes anything. I want to know how it actually plays out. What does it incentivize among chatbot makers, ordinary customers, grifters, and the agency its self? How does the language bots use change — do they get more honest, or do the just drown everything in hedges and pre-disclaimers? Do customers learn to prompt around the warnings? What happens to bots that are designed to be playful or character-like? Where does the reasonable-customer test strain, given that the intentional stance was built for prediction, not for assigning warrantable claims? And is there a way to patch the rule's failure modes without gutting it?

Work through it like a mechanism-design problem. Then give me the result as a JSON object with this schema:

- bottom_line: a one- or two-sentence answer to whether the rule mostly works, mostly gets gamed, or changes nothing.
- actors: an array of objects, each with fields actor, incentive_created, likely_adaptation, and second_order_effect.
- failure_modes: an array of objects, each with fields name, who_exploits_it, how_it_works, fixable (yes/partly/no), and a suggestion for a fix.
- strains: a few sentences on where the intentional-stance reading of chatbot output gets weirdest as a legal standard.
- success_metrics: an array of things you'd measure to tell whether the rule actually made customers better-informed rather than just better-hedged.
