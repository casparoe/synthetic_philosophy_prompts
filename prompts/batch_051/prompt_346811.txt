I've noticed something in my own attitudes and I want to know whether it's defensible or just a glitch.

If my dentist tells me I need a root canal next month, I'll dread it, pay extra for sedation, rearrange my week. But if I found out I'd had the exact same root canal last month and simply couldn't remember it, I'd mostly shrug. Same tooth, same nerve, same procedure, same hour of pain — the only difference is which side of now it sits on.

The philosophical backdrop: presentists say only the present is real; the past is gone and the future isn't yet. Eternalists say past, present, and future are all equally real, just at different dates, like towns along a road. On presentism, neither the past root canal nor the future one exists right now, so my treating one as "coming at me" and the other as "over" looks like a trick of psychology. On eternalism, both are equally real, so my asymmetry seems even less moored.

The obvious candidate justification is that I can still do something about the future one, while the past one is settled. But here's the wrinkle that worries me. Suppose the future root canal is genuinely unavoidable — no cancellations, no postponing, and the anesthesia will be exactly the same as last time — and the past one was equally out of my hands. I suspect I'd still prefer the past one to the future one. If that's right, "I can affect it" isn't doing the real work, and the asymmetry might be a raw bias in favor of whatever isn't ahead of me.

So: is there a better principle — something about anticipation, about the direction of a life, about the different kind of unreality the future has versus the past — that justifies caring more about future pain than past pain? Which theory, presentism or eternalism, makes the least mess of this? And should I keep the asymmetry, weaken it, or deliberately try to correct it?

Answer in American English please.
