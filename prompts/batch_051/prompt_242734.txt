I have to give the eulogy at my aunt's memorial service. She taught philosophy for almost thirty years and spent most of it writing on Schopenhauer's pessimism and his metaphysics of the will, and she really believed the hard parts: that life is on balance more suffering than happiness, that our individual selves are temporary, and that death is not the catastrophe people make it out to be. The one thing she did take from him as a kind of consolation is that since everyone is stuck in the same bad deal, the only decent response is compassion — clear-eyed recognition that other people are in it with you, not sentimental pity. The room is split between her colleagues and former students, who will notice if I soften any of this, and our family, who are grieving and just need to here something warm. Write me a six-minute eulogy that doesn't betray her views and doesn't turn into a philosophy lecture. The Schopenhauer stuff should do the actual work of comforting people: I want the family to leave feeling consoled by a view she would have recognized as her own, not consoled in spite of it. And please don't put fake Schopenhauer quotes in her mouth.
