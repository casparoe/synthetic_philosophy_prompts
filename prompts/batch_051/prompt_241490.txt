I keep returning to a reconstruction of Milton Friedman's F-twist, and I want to know whether it is a good argument or a sleight of hand.

(1) A scientific model is, at bottom, an instrument for generating predictions. (2) An instrument is evaluated by whether it does its job well, not by whether its components resemble the objects it is used on. (3) So a model is evaluated by its predictive performance, not by the descriptive accuracy of its assumptions. (4) An assumption's being unrealistic can therefore count against a model only when that unrealism is implicated in a predictive failure. (5) Hence no report that a model's assumptions are false, by itself, is a reason to reject the model.

My suspicion is that the argument equivocates on "realistic": it slides between an assumption's being idealized in the way a frictionless plane is idealized and an assumption's being flatly false about the part of the world the model is meant to represent. But the inference from (3) to (5) also looks as though it gets its force from treating a model as though it had no representational content at all. Does the argument go through? If it does, which premise is carrying the whole argument and should I accept it? If it does not, which step fails, and what does the strongest repaired version of the argument actually show?
