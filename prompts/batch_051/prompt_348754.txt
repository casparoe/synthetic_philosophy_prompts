I write about science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm working on an interactive feature about why the word "dignity" does so much work in arguments over things like organ sales, shaming punishments, and embryo research, given that a lot of working bioethicists think the word is just a fancy stand-in for autonomy. I want a decision tree that sorts readers into the theory of dignity they actually hold, based on their reactions to cases rather than whether they like the label. The tree should route through at least these four cases, and you can add one or two more if they earn their keep: (1) a bar runs a dwarf-tossing contest between consenting adults; (2) a healthy adult with no other way to pay her debts sells a kidney to a stranger; (3) a judge sentences a shoplifter to stand outside the store with a sign reading "I stole"; (4) a fertility clinic creates embryos for research and destroys the leftovers. The leaves should include at least the Kantian picture (dignity as inner rational nature, no price), the deflationary view associated with Macklin (dignity is redundant with autonomy, so it isn't a separate foundation of rights), dignity as equal social status or rank rather than an inner capacity, and a bare species-membership view.

Build the tree from the logical space of answers, not from the list of names: make each branch node a question a reader can answer without knowing which position it leads to, locate the actual positions in that space after the questions do their work, and flag any combinations of answers that are empty or that collapse into each other. In particular, don't pretend the tree can cleanly seperate a Macklin-style deflationist from a liberal autonomy theorist if it can't—say where it can't and why. And don't just present a balanced menu of options for me to choose from. Commit to a definite structure: the order of the splits, which distinction does the most work, what the leaves end up called, and a short closing note defending the choices you made. I'd much rather have a structure I can push back on than a survey that treats every option as equally good.
