I was arguing late into the evening with a close friend who is training to be a therapist. She kept insisting that a patient who struggles daily with unwanted appetites and wins only through sheer white-knuckled effort is no worse off, from the standpoint of character, than someone who no longer experiences the appetite with any real force. I found this impossible to accept and kept returning to Aristotle’s distinction, in Book VII of the Nicomachean Ethics, between the continent person and the temperate person: the continent person has bad appetites but the rational part of the soul is still sufficiently in control to resist them, whereas the temperate person has appetites so aligned with reason that there is nothing painful to resist. That distinction felt obviously important while we were talking, but the more she pushed me, the less clear I became about what, exactly, it is supposed to track.

Part of what unsettled me is a strange moment in Aristotle’s own treatment. After defending the claim that continence, though inferior to temperance, is still a kind of psychological health, he pauses to ask whether the continent person can be called self-controlled in an unqualified sense, or only in a qualified way. He writes, in Book VII:

“But is the continent man the same as the man who abides by his calculation, or any and every man who abides by his calculation? Or must the former have knowledge of what is good, and the latter abide by any calculation, so that a man who abides by a false calculation is not continent? Must we not say that the continent man abides by a true calculation and by his choice, but the incontinent man does not abide by his choice and his true calculation?”

I had read this passage before without much reaction, but my friend’s challenge made it feel newly puzzling. If continence is essentially a matter of sticking to one’s rational plan under pressure, then why does Aristotle here seem to make correctness of the plan part of the definition? That would make continence partly an epistemic achievement rather than a purely volitional one. It also raises an awkward possibility: someone with a false or perverse rational plan who sticks to it unflinchingly might resemble the continent person in terms of the inner struggle, yet Aristotle appears unwilling to grant that person the name of continent at all. Is he quietly assuming that only true practical reasoning generates the right kind of rational authority, or is the word “continent” just a success-term that packages a normative claim about the content of the plan?

I would like you to interpret this passage slowly and explain how it bears on the broader question of what continence is a virtue of. In particular, does the passage show that Aristotle thinks the value of continence depends on the goodness of the calculation one abides by, or does it merely restrict the term to its central case while leaving room for a form of self-control that is structurally identical but directed at a false end? And if Aristotle really does build correctness into continence, what should I say to my friend, who thinks the psychological struggle itself deserves the same respect regardless of what the struggle is for? My intuition is that the dignity of resistance depends partly on what is being resisted and on the truth of what is being obeyed, but I need a clearer account of why that might be so—or a reason to give the intuition up.
