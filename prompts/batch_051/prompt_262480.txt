I am a doctoral student in moral and political philosophy, working on the ethics of risk, and I have become frustrated with how the debate over nonconsensual risk imposition is usually staged. A delivery van, a drone corridor, or an industrial process imposes a tiny probability of serious harm on large numbers of people who never consented, and the literature oscillates between two positions: that ex ante compensation can make the imposition permissible, and that the unlucky person who actually suffers the harm retains a complaint no payment beforehand can extinguish. What I rarely see is anyone trying to build an actual institution around one of these positions and then watching it run. I would like you to do exactly that for the following proposal.

Suppose a city adopts a consolidation clause in its public-safety ordinance. Any activity that would impose on non-consenting residents an annual risk above a de minimis threshold—say, a 1-in-1,000,000 chance of death or of equivalent morbidity-weighted harm—must be brought under the threshold or barred. If the operator cannot reduce the risk below the threshold through safer design, it may instead concentrate the residual risk: it must keep the total expected harm roughly constant while redistributing it so that no non-consenting person exceeds the threshold and the excess probability is physically transferred to a small number of consenting risk-bearers. The transfer must be real and traceable—achieved through spatial or temporal segregation, restricted corridors, closed facilities, or redesign—not merely simulated by paying people while the same strangers remain exposed. Risk-bearers must be adults who pass a comprehension test, undergo a mandatory cooling-off period, are screened for financial duress, and consent under a contract that specifies fixed ex ante payments plus scheduled ex post payments if the harm occurs, backed by insurance. Activities whose risk cannot be causally traced to identifiable risk-bearers are prohibited until safer methods exist.

Walk through how this scheme would actually behave if it were enforced seriously. What does it do to who bears risk and who captures the benefits of risky activity? Who becomes a professional risk-bearer, and do the screening requirements make that class more or less vulnerable than the system's rhetoric suggests? Where does the scheme get gamed first—through the definition of de minimis risk, through the measurement of expected harm, through the comprehension or duress screenings, or through operators slicing activities into pieces that each fall just under the threshold? Does concentrating risk make consent more meaningful, or does it merely move the moral problem into a labor market where poor people sell their exposure? And what becomes of diffuse, latent, or threshold-effect harms whose probability cannot be estimated ex ante, or whose causal pathway cannot be confined? I am less interested in whether the clause is a good idea in the abstract than in a disciplined account of its incentives, its second-order effects, and whether its likely failures are local and fixable or symptoms of a deeper problem in treating individual consent as the currency of permissible risk.
