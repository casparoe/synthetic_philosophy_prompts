I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am preparing a piece on whether it is fair to bind people to legal rules they could not realistically have known. The starting point: in the United States the body of enforceable federal rules is far too large for any ordinary person to learn, yet the general doctrine is that ignorance of the law does not excuse violations. At the same time, the law recognizes narrow exceptions—for example, a person who relied on a mistaken official statement of the law may escape liability—which suggests the system does care about fair notice. A defensible theory of this practice must explain both why the general rule is fair despite nearly universal ignorance of much of the law, and why the exceptions are the right ones rather than arbitrary carve-outs.

Please give me exactly five desiderata that such a theory must meet to count as fair and non-arbitrary. Format the response as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion. Each item should state one requirement and the case or consideration behind it, and mark the requirement as negotiable or non-negotiable. Somewhere in the list, identify the requirement that does the most work in excluding candidate theories, and note whether any two of your five requirements pull against each other. As a parenthetical appended to one of the items, state how confident you are in the list overall and the single requirement you are least sure is genuine.
