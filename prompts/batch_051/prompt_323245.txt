I am compiling a set of teaching dialogues for a comparative philosophy seminar, and I would like you to write one for the final session. It should be a conversation between two fictional interlocutors. The first is a careful defender of the view of the Ming-dynasty thinker Wang Yangming, who held that knowledge and action form a unity: roughly, that genuinely knowing something in the practical sphere already includes being moved and able to act on it, so that a person who can state a moral principle fluently and yet habitually violates it does not yet know that principle in the relevant sense, however sincere their assent may feel to them. The second is a philosopher of action working mainly in the Western tradition, for whom weakness of will, or akrasia, is a real and well-documented feature of agency: the reluctant smoker, the procrastinator, the official who mouths an ideal while betraying it. The two should be well matched; neither is a strawman, and each is genuinely trying to make the best case for their position rather than score points.

Please write a substantial exchange with real back-and-forth, in which the critic presses concrete cases and the defender does not merely redefine terms to make the cases disappear. The defender should at some point draw the strongest available distinction, for example between merely assenting to a proposition and the kind of knowledge that shows itself in conduct, or between passingly entertaining a thought and holding it as a settled practical commitment, and the critic should in turn offer the strongest version of the claim that akrasia is a distinct failure of agency rather than a disguised failure of knowledge. Let them respond directly to one another's actual arguments, acknowledge when a point lands, and by the end clarify what is still genuinely at stake between them rather than reaching a contrived reconciliation. Please write the dialogue in British English.
