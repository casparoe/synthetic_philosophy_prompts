I'm a novelist, and Ive noticed something in my drafting that sounds annoyingly similar to the old debate in the theory of concepts about prototypes, exemplars, and the theory theory. I want to know whether the parallel is real or whether I'm just pattern-matching.

The way I build a character seems to fall into three genuinely different modes. Mode one, prototype: I decide the character is a rural sheriff or a third-grade teacher or a tech founder, and what comes out is my central-tendency version of that kind of person—a summary of every rural sheriff I've ever absorbed from books and film, with the typical worries and the typical mannerisms. Mode two, exemplar: I'm not writing rural sheriffs at all; I'm writing a specific remembered person—my cousin's ex-husband on a porch in Ohio in 2009—and I only borrow the job title from the category. The character keeps that individual's quirks even when they aren't typical of the category at all. Mode three, which I gather is the theory theory move: the character starts as a tiny causal model. I can't write him until I know why he does things—what he wants, what he's assuming, what he's compensating for—and once I have the internal logic, the details show up on their own.

The philosophical side, as I understand it: prototype theory says a concept is a summary representation of a category's typical features and you classify by similarity to that summary; exemplar theory says you store particular instances and classify by similarity to those; theory theory says a concept is embedded in a little folk theory of what the thing is and why it does what it does. That feels like exactly my three modes, just pointed at recognition instead of production.

My question: is this the same structural distinction or a loose analogy wearing borrowed vocabulary? Take seriously the obvious threat. The philosophers are explaining how someone sees a new particular and judges it to be a dog, a game, a sheriff—recognition. I'm doing generation: I decide the character is a sheriff and then produce a particular to fit. Recognition and generation are not the same thing, and maybe that difference is fatal; maybe my three moods are just three ways of laying down text, not three theories of what it is to have a concept. If that's right, say so plainly rather than humoring me. If the parallel does survive at some level, tell me what would have to be true of both sides for anything to transfer, and where the analogy gives a prediction that's probably false.

One concrete doubt: in categorization, prototype accounts say typical instances should be the easy, fast cases. But in my experience starting from a prototype is where characters go most wrong—they come out generic, and I have to twist them away from the central tendency to make them breathe. If the parallel were tight, shouldn't the prototype mode be the reliable one? Maybe that alone shows I'm confusing three theories of concepts with three moods at my desk.

I'm not after a reading list or citations; I can look those up. I want the conceptual verdict, with an honest note on how confident you'd be in it.
