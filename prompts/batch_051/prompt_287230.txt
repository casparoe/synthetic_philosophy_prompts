One of my friends keeps leaning on this analogy to defend the principle of indifference: he says assigning equal probability when you have no evidence is just like cutting a cake into equal slices when you don't know who'll get which slice. It comes up mostly in arguments about priors in AI-risk and anthropics, where he uses the analogy to license moving from no information straight to equal probabilities across whatever possibilities happen to be on the table. I get the intuitive pull, but I suspect the cake is doing more work than he thinks — especially once you move from a fixed list of outcomes to continuous magnitudes, or to cases where there are multiple equally natural ways of carveing up the same space. I want an audit of how far the analogy holds. Give me the correspondence table explicitly (a table is fine): what's the cake, what are the slices, what is the not-knowing-who-gets-what? Then tell me where it breaks in ways that matter. In particular, where does the analogy quietly import something that possibility-space lacks, like a pre-existing notion of equal volume or equal portions, and how does that connect to Bertrand's paradox? Then rule on the conclusion: is no-information-implies-equal-probabilities fine so long as the possibilities form a discrete, privileged list, and does it only break when the space has to be parameterized — or is the problem more general than the Bertrand case makes it look? If it doesn't survive in general, tell me what the closest version is that does, or whether there's a better analogy altogether.
