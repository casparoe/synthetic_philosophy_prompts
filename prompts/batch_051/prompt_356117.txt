I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am working on an essay about the ethics of eating and of intervening in the natural world. My difficulty is that my verdicts on concrete cases feel firm one at a time but do not sit together under any principle I can state. I want to find the principle, state it precisely, see which of my judgments it cannot accommodate, and hear what it says about a few cases I have not yet settled.

Here are my verdicts.

I regard buying factory-farmed chicken as wrong and do not do it. The life of a broiler chicken — short, crowded, engineered for rapid growth — seems to me a life not worth living, and in buying the breast I take myself to be paying for more such lives.

I also regard feedlot beef as wrong, but at a catered event where the only choices are chicken and beef I choose the beef. My thought is that a serving of chicken is a large fraction of a bird's body, while a serving of beef is a tiny fraction of a steer's, so beef spreads one death across many more meals. Yet I also believe that beef is worse per plate along the other dimensions I care about: land cleared, habitat lost, emissions imposed, wild animals displaced. I cannot tell whether I am weighing two different currencies sensibly or merely switching currencies to let myself take the beef.

Eating mussels or oysters does not bother me at all. I doubt there is anything it is like to be a mussel, which suggests my scruple tracks felt suffering rather than the destruction of life as such.

I know that growing wheat and soy kills mice and other field animals as a foreseeable byproduct of harvest, and I do not think buying ordinary bread is wrong. But I cannot say why buying the bread is importantly different from buying the chicken, except by gestures at intention and side effects that I do not fully trust.

Venison from a deer shot by a responsible hunter who eats the animal strikes me as less objectionable than factory chicken, perhaps not objectionable at all. The deer lived something like a natural life; but I also know natural lives include starvation, disease, and being eaten alive, so a natural death is not a clean thing to compare against.

I believe the suffering of wild animals matters as much as that of farm animals. If I found a starling in my garden dying slowly, I would think I should kill it quickly. Yet I do not accept that I am obliged to do anything general about the vastly larger sum of predation, starvation, and parasitism in the woods around me, and I cannot locate the boundary between the starling and the woods.

Treat my empirical comparisons — fewer individual deaths per plate of beef, more land and climate damage per plate of beef — as fixed for the sake of the exercise; I am trying to settle the normative structure, not the numbers.

Is there one coherent principle that captures most of these verdicts, and which of my judgments does it force me to give up? What would that principle say about eating insects, about eggs from a small flock whose male chicks were surely killed at the hatchery, and about culling an overpopulated deer herd to prevent slow winter starvation?
