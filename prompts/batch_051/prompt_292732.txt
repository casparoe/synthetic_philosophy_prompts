I am a PhD student in the philosophy of induction, and I have an epistemic policy I follow whenever someone presents me with a successful algorithm. When I am shown a new model that outperforms my familiar default on a benchmark or dataset, I leave my default largely unchanged, on the ground that the no-free-lunch theorem tells us that no algorithm is uniformly better than any other across all possible tasks. A colleague in our machine-learning group objects that the theorem assumes tasks are drawn uniformly from all logically possible tasks, whereas our actual problems come from a structured distribution, so a win on our problem class is evidence rather than noise. I accept the formal point but keep the policy, because I rarely know the true distribution well enough to trust that the benchmark samples it. Last week this meant I did not abandon a simple default classifier when a colleague's complex model beat it on two benchmarks. Is this policy defensible, or is it a piece of inductive scepticism dressed up as a theorem? Under what conditions should a few observed victories justify abandoning a default, and is there a restated version of the rule that keeps its caution without its rigidity? Please answer in British English.
