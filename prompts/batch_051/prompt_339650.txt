I keep returning to the question of when thinking about one's own thinking helps a person and when it undermines the very activity it is meant to improve. My verdicts about particular cases feel stable, but I cannot state the principle behind them, and I suspect at least one pair of my verdicts cannot both be right. I would like you to treat the following cases as data and tell me what principle, or what small set of principles, best captures them.

Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. After a slow chess loss, I spend an hour reconstructing each critical position: what I was trying to do, what I considered, what I failed to consider, and which patterns I misclassified. This has noticeably improved the candidate moves I generate in later games. Verdict: clearly helpful.

2. A violinist in the practice room slows down and attends to the angle of her bow and the placement of her left-hand fingers, and this corrects an uneven passage. Days later, in performance, she attends to the same mechanics in real time, and the now-automatized passage falls apart. Verdict: helpful in practice, harmful in performance.

3. At a party, I monitor my contribution as it happens: whether I am speaking too much, how my last remark landed, what I should say next to seem at ease. The effect is that I become wooden and stop hearing the other person. Verdict: harmful.

4. While reading a difficult article, I pause after each section and force myself to state, in full sentences, what the argument was and where I might object to it before moving on. My later recall and reconstruction of the article are far better than when I simply reread it. Verdict: helpful.

5. When I work on an insight problem of the sort that seems to require restructuring rather than step-by-step search, saying my strategy aloud helps me with the early analytical moves, but once I am near the solution, attending to my own thought process in words seems to make the restructuring recede. Verdict: helpful early, harmful at the moment that matters.

6. After an unpleasant interaction, I repeatedly replay the event and ask why I felt as I did and what it reveals about me. This is clearly thinking about my own thinking, but it produces no new option and no corrected belief, and it leaves my mood worse. Verdict: harmful.

I can report the three comparisons that bother me most. First, the chess post-mortem and the party self-monitoring are both attempts to examine one's own cognition, yet I call the first helpful and the second harmful. The obvious difference is that the chess reflection happens after the fact and concerns a bounded task, whereas the party monitoring happens in real time and concerns the self as a social object; but I do not know which of these differences is doing the work. Second, self-testing while reading and think-aloud in an insight problem are both, in their way, attempts to check one's own cognitive state in real time, yet I call self-testing helpful and the insight monitoring harmful precisely when it matters. Third, the violinist's attention to mechanics is nearly the same attention in both settings, and I call it helpful in the practice room and harmful on stage, which suggests my verdicts track something about the context rather than the mere content of the metacognitive act.

My tentative hypothesis is this: metacognition helps when it is aimed at a product of thought that is already fixed—the move actually played, the argument actually reconstructed—and when it tests that product against a standard outside the thought process; it harms when it is aimed at the running process itself, as when self-focus disrupts an automatized skill, or when self-focused attention has no external task to constrain it, as in rumination and social anxiety. But I know this is rough. It does not cleanly explain why attention to mechanics helps before a skill is automatized and harms after it is, and it does not explain why the insight problem resists being made explicit.

I would like you to do four things. (1) State the best principle or small set of candidates as precisely as you can, and say which clause is motivated by which of my cases. (2) Apply the principle to two further cases I cannot settle: (a) a depressed person keeping a daily thought record of automatic negative thoughts, assessing the evidence for each one and generating alternatives; and (b) an athlete mentally rehearsing a particular movement and its mechanics while waiting to perform. (3) Tell me honestly which of my six verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether saving them would require ad hoc complications. (4) Tell me whether the result forces me to revise my tentative hypothesis, to abandon the hope of a single unified explanation, or to give up one of my case verdicts.
