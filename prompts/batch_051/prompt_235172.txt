I was recently told that several of my blood-pressure readings put me just over the current line for hypertension — readings that under earlier guidelines would have been labelled normal or high-normal. My body did not change; the boundary did. Since then I have been trying to understand the old dispute in the philosophy of medicine between naturalism and normativism about health and disease, and I keep noticing what looks like the same structure in a dispute from my own field, software engineering. I would like help deciding whether the parallel is deep or merely a case of pattern-matching.

On the medical side, naturalism says, roughly, that health and disease are value-free facts about an organism's functioning: a part is diseased when it fails to make its species-typical contribution to survival and reproduction, or when its state is a statistical outlier relative to an appropriate reference class. Normativism says that judgments of disease inevitably import values: which departures from typicality count as disease, and which states count as bad for a person, cannot be settled by biology alone. On the engineering side there is a parallel disagreement about whether a program's being buggy is an objective property of the program. One can take an objectivist line: given a specification, a program either matches it or does not, and "bug" names the mismatch. Or one can take a pragmatic line: the specification is itself a choice made by someone for some users, and the interesting part of labelling a behavior a bug — rather than an unsupported edge case, a tolerated quirk, or a feature — is exactly the value-laden part.

The mapping seems suspiciously neat. Test results correspond to observed program behavior; the reference class or normal functioning corresponds to the specification; a diagnosis corresponds to a bug report; treatment corresponds to a patch; and the guideline change that made me a patient corresponds to revising a spec and thereby changing the truth-value of "this behavior is correct" while leaving the behavior itself untouched. If the parallel is real, then the naturalism/normativism dispute and the objectivist/pragmatist dispute about bugs should rise and fall together: whatever settles one should, suitably translated, settle the other.

But I can also see reasons to suspect it is superficial. Organisms have no author, and their proper functioning is read off from evolutionary history or statistical typicality rather than from a written spec; software has an intentional designer, and a spec can be checked against something outside the system. The fact that a spec was written by someone may make bug attributions relative to an authored standard, whereas biological norms, if they exist, would have to be authorless. I suspect that is either the hinge of the analogy or the crack in it, but I cannot tell which.

Is this a genuine identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion? Be specific about where the mapping holds and where it comes apart: what would have to be true of biological functions on one side and specifications on the other for results to transfer between the two disputes, and what prediction made by the parallel actually fails?
