Rousseau argues in the Social Contract that the general will emerges when each citizen, sufficiently informed, votes on the common interest without communication among citizens, so that the many small differences in judgment cancel rather than combine into factional wills; he adds that partial associations replace the citizens' own judgment with a corporate will that is general within the group but particular to the state. His claim that the refractory citizen may be compelled to follow the general will—forced to be free—rests on this account of how the general will is known. Now suppose that the relevant civic deliberation happens not in an assembly but on a single digital platform whose recommender system optimizes for each user's engagement: every citizen sees a personalized feed and is silently sorted into homogeneous clusters that rarely encounter one another's strongest arguments. Does Rousseau's argument survive this change? State which premise the development actually pressures—the independence of judgments, the absence of partial associations, the transparency of the common interest, or some other—and say whether the general will could still be formed under these conditions or whether the platform generates a new kind of partial association that the argument cannot handle.
