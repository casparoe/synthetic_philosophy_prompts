I've been reading around the First World War and its aftermath, and I keep returning to the way the peace settlement tried to compress the question of blame into a single legal clause. Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles — usually called the war guilt clause — affirmed, in essence, that Germany and its allies accepted responsibility for all the loss and damage the Allied governments and their citizens had suffered in a war imposed upon them by the aggression of Germany and its allies. I know the article was drafted in part to ground reparations rather than purely as a moral judgment, but many Germans heard it as exactly that: a verdict that Germany as a nation was guilty of starting the war, and that grievance did real historical work over the following decades. I want to know whether an attribution like that can be made coherent, not just as diplomatic language.

I'd like a case analysis that runs in both directions. First, analyze Article 231 through the main philosophical positions on collective or national responsibility: the individualist view that only persons are morally responsible, so talk of a guilty nation is at best shorthand; the view that nations are themselves agents with intentions, policies, and a life that outruns any particular generation; and any middle positions, such as responsibility grounded in shared intentions, political institutions, or a chain of collective omissions. For each position, say what exactly the clause would be attributing, to whom, and whether the attribution can be made defensible. Second, bring in the Fischer controversy. As I understand it, the historian Fritz Fischer argued from the early 1960s that Imperial Germany's leadership had actively pursued a European war in the years before 1914 and regarded war as an instrument of German hegemony; his thesis was hotly contested by historians who preferred to see the war as a failure of all the European powers. To the extent that Fischer was right, does his account vindicate the attribution made in 1919, or does it still stop well short of blaming the German nation as a whole? Finally, tell me what the Versailles episode shows about the theories themselves: which of the positions handles this kind of case without strain, which one has to be quietly amended or hedged to survive contact with it, and whether the episode is ultimately a point for or against attributing responsibility to nations.
