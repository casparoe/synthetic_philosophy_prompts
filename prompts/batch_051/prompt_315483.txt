I've been struck by a structural parallel between two ways of dealing with the fact that the people bound by rules don't know them. In the rule-of-law tradition, a standard justification for the maxim that ignorance of the law is no excuse is that law is not a private command but a public, promulgated standard that must be knowable in principle; the system presumes knowledge so that everyone is on a common footing even though no one is. In rational-expectations economics, an equilibrium is defined as a state in which agents' expectations are generated by the model itself, so agents are effectively assumed to know the rules of the system they inhabit — and a substantial literature then asks whether they could learn their way to that equilibrium without starting out with such knowledge. Both fields seem to face the same underlying predicament: a system of binding rules whose coordinating function seems to presuppose that rule-followers know the rules, when in fact none of them do. The law answers with a fiction of knowledge plus a knowability requirement; economics answers with a modeling assumption plus a learning literature.

Is this a real identity of structure, or just pattern-matching? Would results about convergence to rational-expectations equilibrium — or failures of convergence — transfer to the legal question of when the presumption of knowledge is defensible? Or do the two sides come apart because law's knowable-in-principle ideal is a normative one while economics' common-knowledge assumption is a descriptive modeling condition? If I'm leaning on a mistaken picture of either the law or the economics, I'd rather you say so plainly than force the analogy into shape.
