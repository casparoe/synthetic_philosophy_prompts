I keep running into the same difficulty. Truth-seeking, tradition, and purity all present themselves to the people who hold them as non-negotiable — worth maintaining even when they produce nothing further. Yet when I press on concrete cases, I cannot always tell whether this feeling is evidence that the thing is genuinely valued for its own sake, or simply the way certain instrumental values are packaged. I would like a working definition that helps me make that cut.

Give me a working definition of ‘valuing something for its own sake’ — that is, a criterion for counting a value as intrinsic rather than instrumental — that classifies the following cases correctly.

1. A woman keeps her savings in a current account solely so that she can pay her rent at the end of the month; she has no further interest in the balance. I take this to be plainly instrumental.

2. A terminally ill man, who will not publish or tell anyone, spends his final weeks trying to establish whether a minor historical claim he has wondered about for years is true, though the answer bears on nothing else he will ever do. I take this to be plainly truth-seeking for its own sake.

3. An academic says he values truth for its own sake, but when a colleague corrects an error of his in private he is indifferent; he minds only when the correction is public, because his reputation suffers. I think this is reputation rather than truth.

4. A monastery keeps part of its liturgy in a language none of the monks speak, though a translation is available, and can give no practical reason for retaining the old language other than that the practice has come down to them in that form. I have no settled view here.

5. A woman refuses to let certain foods touch on her plate even when she is alone, though she no longer believes any harm follows from their mixing; when pressed, she says only that the contact would feel like a stain. I lean towards saying this is purity valued for its own sake, but the word ‘stain’ is doing work I do not understand.

6. A town continues to fund a festival that loses money and bores most residents; when asked why, they say only that it is what the town does, and that cancelling it would mean the town had changed. I suspect this is tradition valued as something more than a means, but it may be inertia, or identity, rather than value.

7. A lawyer refuses to tell a minor lawful lie that would spare a client embarrassment, where the client asks for it and no third party would be harmed; she says simply that she does not lie. I think this is truthfulness held as a kind of purity, but it may only be a prudent rule about habit-formation.

Propose the definition, run it through all seven cases, and say where it surprises you. In particular, I need to know whether your definition forces you to reclassify either of my two clear cases, and whether it can distinguish valuing something for its own sake from merely having a strong habit, identity, or self-presentation around it. Please write your answer in British English.
