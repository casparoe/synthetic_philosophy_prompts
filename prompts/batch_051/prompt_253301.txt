At my company, the head of forecasting is pushing us to replace our panel of domain experts with an internal prediction market. Her case rests on a picture I keep meeting in popular writing about markets: a market is a kind of distributed computer, or collective brain, in which every trader uploads a small private piece of information by buying or selling, and the price is the machine's continuous output — a running best estimate of the true value, assembled from fragments no single person holds. From this she concludes that the more freely people can trade, and the more liquid the market, the more information gets pulled into prices, so the prediction market will beat any committee we could convene.

A friend who works in decision theory told me this picture is much too simple, and mentioned two things I only half followed. The first was the no-trade theorem, which he summarized something like this: if everyone is rational and starts from the same prior, then purely speculative trade on differences in private information should unravel, because each side ought to treat the other's willingness to trade as evidence that the other knows something unfavorable. The second was a paradox from the efficient-markets literature: a perfectly informationally efficient market would destroy the incentive to gather costly information, since everyone could read it off the price for free — in which case the information would never reach the price.

I'd like you to audit the distributed-computer analogy rather than settle the economics from outside. Spell out the correspondence first: what is supposed to be the nodes, the inputs, the connections, the local updates, and the output? Then say where the mapping holds, where it breaks, and which breaks actually matter for the conclusion my colleague wants to draw. Once the no-trade theorem and the information-incentive paradox are in view, is a more active and liquid market a better epistemic machine, or is that the wrong inference? If the analogy misleads, give the most defensible replacement: what is a market doing, epistemically, when it is working well?
