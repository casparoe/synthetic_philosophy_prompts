I am a retired chemical engineer who came to philosophy late, and I have lately been working through the debate about causation by omission: whether an absence can be a cause, what the distinction between doing and allowing is really doing in causal judgment, and where normativity enters the picture. A bill now before my state legislature puts these questions into a concrete institutional design, and I would like it analyzed as a mechanism rather than as a moral position. By training I tend to judge a rule by its dynamics once installed, not by the intentions of its drafters.

The bill creates a limited civil Good Samaritan liability. Its core provision is this: whenever a person suffers serious bodily injury, and a preponderance of the evidence shows that an identified adult bystander could have prevented that injury at a cost, in money or time, no greater than the lesser of $400 and 8 percent of the bystander's monthly income, and without substantial physical risk to themselves and without violating a duty they owed to someone else, the bystander is liable for 20 percent of the injured person's economic damages. The qualifying omission is statutorily deemed a cause of the injury for purposes of this liability; no prior relationship, professional role, or background legal duty is required. There is no criminal penalty. Insurance against this liability is permitted, with premiums keyed to an audited "bystander exposure score" reflecting the insured's residence, occupation, and rescue or first-aid training. A state board publishes quarterly, anonymized case summaries for use by judges and litigants.

I want a rigorous walk-through of how this institution would actually behave. Please use the following sections, in this order, with the indicated lengths:

1. Intended logic (one paragraph): what behavior the drafters are trying to produce, and which commitments about the metaphysics and ethics of omissions are built into the statutory language.

2. Direct incentives (two paragraphs): who becomes more likely to act, who becomes warier of being in a position to act, and how the income-indexed threshold alters behavior at different points in the income distribution.

3. Gaming and avoidance (two paragraphs): strategic ignorance of risk, deliberate failure to acquire or maintain helping skills, arranging not to be the best-positionioned bystander, witness collusion, and nuisance or retaliatory claims.

4. Second-order effects (one to two paragraphs): the market for omission-liability insurance, the effect on ordinary helping and social trust, and what the anonymized case summaries do to public knowledge of the rule and to litigation strategy.

5. Where the metaphysics leaks in (one paragraph): where the statute's stipulation that qualifying omissions "count as causes" does and does not settle the philosophical question whether omissions are genuinely causes, and which disputes about causation by omission would resurface in judicial interpretation.

6. Defects and fixes (two paragraphs): which failure modes can be repaired by adjusting the cost threshold, the damages fraction, the insurance provisions, or the evidentiary standard, and which failure modes are intrinsic to any liability rule built on counterfactual prevention.

7. Bottom line (one paragraph): the single most important consequence of the bill, and whether on the whole it selects for rescue or for something else.
