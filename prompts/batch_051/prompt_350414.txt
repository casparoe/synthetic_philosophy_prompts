I'm in a philosophy of mind course and we've just finished panpsychism. A freind in my tutorial has an argument he thinks kills it, and I can't find the flaw, but I also can't accept where it leaves us.

(1) Panpsychism says consciousness is fundamental: the basic physical constituents each have some extremely simple form of consciousness, so we never have to get mind from completely mindless stuff.

(2) I'm conscious, and I'm one subject. My experience isn't a loose heap of separate little experiences; it's unified.

(3) On panpsychism, my unified consciousness has to be either (a) constituted out of the many micro-consciousnesses of my parts, or (b) something that emerges when those micro-conscious parts are arranged the way mine are.

(4) Option (a) — the subject-summing problem, my tutor calls it — looks impossible. However many tiny subjects you pack together, you still have many subjects, not one. A relation among many subjects doesn't by itself make a new single subject, any more than a crowd of people is a person.

(5) Option (b) gives away the store. If you allow a unified subject to strongly emerge from a bunch of separate subjects, then the materialist can say the same thing about consciousness strongly emerging from non-conscious neurons. Panpsychism was supposed to avoid that kind of brute emergence, so it looses its whole point.

(6) So either I'm not really a single subject (which is mad, since I directly know I am) or panpsychism is false.

I'd most like to keep (2): I really am a single subject, not a bundle. And I'd like to keep panpsychism's motivation from (1) if I can, because the idea of brute emergence from mindless stuff genuinely bothers me. My tutor muttered something about cosmopsychism as a way out — the view that the universe as a whole is the fundamental subject, and our minds are limitations or aspects of it rather than combinations of smaller minds — but I don't see how that helps, whether it still counts as panpsychism, or whether it just relocates the problem to how one big subject divides into many. Which step should I give up, and what does giving it up cost? And if the argument is actually valid, which premise is the least bad to lose?
