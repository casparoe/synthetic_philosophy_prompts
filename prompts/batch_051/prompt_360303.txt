I've been trying to see whether the maximum entropy principle actually delivers a uniquely rational prior, and I wrote the following argument down. I'm half-convinced by it, but step 3 keeps feeling like it does two jobs under one word. Walk through the argument and tell me if it's valid; if not, say exactly which step fails, give the strongest reading of that step on which it's true, and check whether the conclusion still follows on that reading.

(1) A rational prior shouldn't assume more than the evidence supports.
(2) If a prior assigns different probabilities to two hypotheses when the evidence doesn't distinguish between them, then the prior is assuming something the evidence doesn't support.
(3) The maximum entropy distribution compatible with your evidence is the least informative such distribution, because entropy just measures how much information a distribution contains; maxent therefore makes only the distinctions your evidence forces and no others.
(4) So the maximum entropy distribution is the unique prior that makes no distinctions beyond the evidence.
(5) Therefore, if there is any rational prior given your evidence, it is exactly the maximum entropy one.

Now here's what worries me. Suppose all I know is that a parameter θ is between 0 and 1. Maxent gives a flat prior on θ. But if I'd written the same problem in terms of η = θ² instead, maxent would've given a flat prior on η, which corresponds to a non-flat prior on θ. So the "uninformative" prior seems to depend on how I choose to describe the parameter, even though the evidence didn't change. Does this show a specific step is false, or just that "information" and "distinction" are being smuggled between two senses somewhere?
