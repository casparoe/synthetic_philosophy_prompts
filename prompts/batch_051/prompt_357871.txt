I work as a translator, and although I have no formal training in linguistics, my work keeps throwing up grammatical puzzles that I end up thinking about on my own. Last month two colleagues in my office argued about a single word, "ago," and I still cannot decide what to make of their exchange.

Daniel opened by saying that calling "ago" an adverb is indefensible. In "ten years ago," he said, "ago" is the word that determines how the noun phrase "ten years" functions in the sentence; it marks a temporal relation measured back from the present, much as "in" marks one in "in ten years." The only difference is that "ago" follows its complement rather than preceding it. English happens to favor prepositions, but there is nothing in the notion of a relation word that requires front placement, and languages with postpositions get by perfectly well.

Sima replied that Daniel was classifying by meaning at the cost of the only thing that keeps categories usable, namely behavior. By his test, she said, "worth" in "worth the price" would have to be brought into the preposition class too, and before long the class would have no edges at all. The school label "adverb" at least tracks something real: the whole phrase "ten years ago" does the same work as a simple adverb such as "previously." A category based on function alone, she argued, stops telling a learner anything about how a word actually combines with others.

Daniel answered that "does the work of an adverb" is the same escape hatch that turned "adverb" into a wastebasket category in the first place. We do not call "in" an adverb when "in the garden" modifies a verb; we classify the word by its relation to the noun phrase it governs. The same reasoning should apply to "ago," which assigns a temporal role to its complement even though it comes after that complement. Every grammatical category, he added, has central and peripheral members, and a grammar that demands crisp boxes is confused about what grammatical categories are.

Sima said this reply was doing more work than Daniel admitted. Once membership is settled by best overall fit across conflicting tests, and different tests can be weighted differently, there is no longer an objective fact about which box "ago" belongs in. But then Daniel cannot say the textbook's "adverb" is wrong; he can only say he prefers another convention. His initial charge, she said, had collapsed into a stylistic preference.

Daniel accepted that conclusion but said it was not a small one. If the parts-of-speech table is, in such cases, a convention rather than a discovery, then treating a learner's classification of "ago" as an error is teaching a convention as though it were a fact about the language. That, he said, is the more important point—more important than which label wins.

They turned to me to referee, and I could not. Please go through their exchange move by move and adjudicate it: for each of the claims they made, say whether it is sound, defensible but overstated, or mistaken, and briefly justify each ruling. Then say what, if anything, both of them are missing, and give a considered verdict on whose overall position is stronger.
