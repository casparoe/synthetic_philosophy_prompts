I've been reading the why-some-nations-are-rich debate for years now, mostly through rationalist-adjacent blogs and econ stuff, and I genuinely cant tell whether it has stalled because the empirical work is hard or because the three camps are answering different questions and the words make it look like one question. The institutions people, the geography people, and the culture people all nod along when someone says "it's multicausal" and then go right back to arguing that their factor is the deep one. What's frustrating is that none of the stylized facts are actually in dispute: the reversal-of-fortune result, the settler-mortality instrument, the two Koreas, the tropics-being-poor gradient, the work-ethic correlations. But each side can absorb the other side's flagship finding into its own story. Institutions people say geography matters only through which institutions it made possible, and culture changes pretty fast when incentives change. Geography people say institutions are endogenous all the way down, so pointing at them explains nothing. Culture people say both camps leave an unexplained residual: the same formal institutions produce different equilibria in different populations, and that residual is what actually does the work.

My pet diagnosis is that the three sides want three different kinds of explanation. The institutions side wants a manipulable lever, since policy can in principle change institutions. The geography side wants a fixed constraint you can't wish away, since you can't move the tropics. The culture side wants an account of how a population coordinates, since it's trying to explain why rules that work in one place fail in another. If that's right, each side is running different controls and picking different samples, and each side's factor wins by construction. The debate wouldn't be fixable by another growth regression or a cleverer instrument; it'd need a prior decision about what an adequate explanation of national wealth has to do — explain levels or growth, within-country or between-country variation, policy-actionable causes or historical-inevitable ones. But maybe theres a real empirical question underneath: whether institutions are mostly downstream of geography and culture, and how much independent variance institutional changes actually explain. I can be talked out of my pet diagnosis, but I want a diagnosis that engages what the sides actually say and doesn't just gesture at "they're all partly right."
