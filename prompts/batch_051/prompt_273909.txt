Mill makes a good test case because he held both halves of this thing with total confidence. He is the one who argued that the only ground on which society may interfere with a person's liberty is to prevent harm to other people, and that a person's own good is never a sufficient warrant for coercing him. He meant that as a fully general principle about adult human beings. But he also spent thirty-five years as an official of the East India Company and defended its rule of India, and in his own political theory he said flat out that despotism is a legitimate way to govern peoples he considered not yet ready to benefit from free and open debate, as long as the despot's aim is their improvement. So the same principle that treats paternalism toward an English adult as tyranny treats paternalism toward a whole Indian population as a civilizing mission. The asymmetry is the thing I want cracked open: is there any principle that generates both halves, or is Mill just running two theories and switching between them? Give me the strongest candidate you can find, but make it do the work — it can't make the difference turn on race or national origin or naked power, and it has to handle to case where a population meets whatever standard of 'readiness' Mill lays down and is still denied self-government. Then tell me honestly whether Mill's liberalism should be read as narrower than it pretends to be, and whether the asymmetry should be kept, restricted, or abandoned.
