I have been thinking on my own, without any academic background in this area, about whether there is a real moral difference between lying and merely misleading someone, and I have drafted a short essay about it. I would like detailed comments on the draft itself rather than a survey of what others have written. The draft is summarized below, and then I list the questions I would most like help with.

The essay begins with the familiar thought that an outright lie is worse than a misleading statement, because the liar asserts something false while the misleader only lets the other person draw a false conclusion. My draft argues that, for an important class of cases, this gets the moral situation backwards.

The centerpiece of the essay is a pair of examples. In the first, a manager is asked directly whether there will be layoffs and says there will not be, knowing that there will be. In the second, the manager is asked the same question and says that nothing has been finalized yet, which is technically true, while knowing that the only open question is how many people will be let go. The draft claims that the second case wrongs the employee twice, not once. The employee is led into the false belief that their job is safe, and is also recruited into producing that false belief themselves, through the perfectly reasonable assumption that the manager's answer was responsive to the question. Later, when the truth comes out, they cannot pin the deception on anything the manager said, and they may even blame themselves for having read too much into a careful answer.

From these examples the essay draws its main claim: what is often more serious about misleading is not that it deceives more, but that it is designed to preserve the speaker's deniability. The deception includes a plan for its own defense. The draft then concedes that this cannot be the whole story. A lie can also be told in a way that leaves no trail, and some misleading is harmless or even kind because it protects the other person's freedom to remain unaware. The essay ends by proposing that the morally important line is not between lying and misleading at all, but between treating the hearer as someone who can check what they are told and managing the hearer so that they cannot.

I would like comments on three points. First, does the layoffs argument actually show that misleading is sometimes the graver wrong, or does it only show that deception plus built-in deniability is worse, leaving the original comparison between bare lying and bare misleading untouched? Second, do the two manager examples differ along the line the essay needs, or do they differ along some other feature, such as how much blame the manager can later shift or how much harm either answer causes? Third, if the essay's final claim is that the lying-misleading distinction is not itself the morally weighty one, does that undermine the essay's opening contrast, and would the argument be stronger if it set aside the attempt to rank lying and misleading and instead defended the wider claim about hearers as checkers from the beginning?
