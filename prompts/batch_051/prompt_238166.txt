Write the joint statement that a committed gene-level selectionist and a committed multilevel selectionist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The gene-level selectionist position is that natural selection is ultimately and causally a matter of differential replication of replicators (genes, paradigmatically), and that individual organisms and groups are vehicles whose apparent selective significance must ultimately cash out in terms of allele-frequency change. The multilevel selectionist position is that selection can act simultaneously and causally at several levels of the biological hierarchy, and that group selection often does explanatory work that a gene's-eye redescription merely recounts after the fact.

The document should have two parts. First, a numbered list of agreed claims both sides can sign, covering at least: the minimal requirements for evolution by natural selection, namely heritable variation in fitness; what both sides accept about the formal apparatus, including why any selection process can be represented in the covariance form of the Price equation, and what that representability does and does not establish about causality; what both sides accept about phenomena neither treats as central to the dispute, such as selfish genetic elements and meiotic drive; and the conditions under which a between-group difference in composition is agreed to be causally relevant to evolutionary change, stated so that neither side feels it has conceded the argument.

Second, a disagreements table with four columns: the issue, the gene-selectionist's precise formulation of the disagreement, the multilevel selectionist's precise formulation, and a neutral statement of what the two formulations have in common. Each side's column must be worded so that side would accept it as a fair statement of its own position.

Then identify the cruxes on which the dispute actually turns, where a crux is a sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side. For each crux, give one concrete model, experiment, or natural case both sides would accept as bearing on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance. Your crux analysis should address at least the following, though you may identify others: whether a trait-group model with altruism is best described as individual-level selection in a structured population or as genuine group selection; whether group selection requires a group-level fitness that is not merely a statistical aggregate of individual fitnesses; and whether the gene's-eye view explains the relevant facts prospectively rather than only redescribing them after they are known.

Throughout, do two things. First, flag explicitly which apparent disagreements you judge to be verbal rather than substantive, and say what both sides would have to accept for those disagreements to dissolve. Second, keep the agreed claims non-vacuous: each should be a claim a reasonable third party could deny, not a truism both sides can sign because it is empty.
