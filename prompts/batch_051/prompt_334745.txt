I was talking with a friend about whether there is any real moral difference between lying and merely misleading, and how Grice's distinction between what is said and what is implicated seems to do a lot of work in that debate. The Clinton deposition in the Paula Jones case feels like a cleaner natural experiment than most of the made-up examples. The rough facts as I remember them: in his January 1998 deposition, Clinton was asked about Monica Lewinsky and denied having had sexual relations with her; earlier in the deposition the term had been given a narrow legal definition, and after the truth came out his defenders argued that his answer was literally true under that definition even though it was obviously meant to sound like a blanket denial. I'm not asking for a legal account of the deposition or a verdict on Clinton. I want a careful Gricean analysis of what happened, and in particular whether the framework can actually handle it.

Walk me through what the sentence said versus what Clinton most plausibly intended his hearers to take away, and say whether that takeaway is definitly a conversational implicature in Grice's sense — something calculable from the Cooperative Principle and the maxims — or whether it's a different phenomenon (audience assumption, exploiting a prior definition, whatever it is) that Grice doesn't have a clean category for. Then say what the framework gets right about why ordinary listeners felt lied to even if the sentence came out true, and where it starts to creak: the decisive move happened before the answer, in the definition of the term, and the misleading content may not be inferable in the maxim-based way Grice requires. Finally, tell me what the case shows about using the saying/implicating distinction to ground the lying/misleading distinction — does it vindicate Grice's machinery for that job, or does it show the machinery was never built for it?
