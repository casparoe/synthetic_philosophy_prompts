My friend is convinced the whole Lucas–Penrose argument comes down to one picture: a machine is like a character inside a novel. The character can follow the rules of the story, but it can never step outside the page to see that the whole thing is consistent from the outside — whereas we, the readers, can. From that he jumps straight to the conclusion that the human mind can't be a Turing machine, since we can see the truth of a Gödel sentence the system itself can't prove. I cant shake the feeling the picture is doing more work than the actual argument. Audit the analogy for me: what maps onto what (novel, character, reader, the rules, that moment of seeing-the-cover), where the correspondence breaks, and what the outside-the-book image quietly imports about us having a God's-eye view of a formal system while being finite processes ourselves. Does the anti-mechanist conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the whole thing? And if it's a bad picture, what's a better one?
