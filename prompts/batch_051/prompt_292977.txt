I am a journalist covering science and ideas, and I am preparing a feature on the renewed academic interest in mystical experience, particularly the question of whether psychedelically induced states can be studied as episodes of insight or merely as unusual neural events. At a recent interdisciplinary symposium, a philosopher of mind concluded his remarks by citing the old slogan "Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent" to rule out the possibility of a cognitive science of mystical insight: if the experience is ineffable, he suggested, then no attempt to measure, model, or even describe its content can get off the ground.

I have encountered this sentence before, but I suspect it is doing more work in this argument than it can bear. I would like your help in separating out the different precise claims that "whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent" could be making, so that I can report accurately on what such appeals actually establish.

Please act as a professional philosopher working in the philosophy of language and the philosophy of mind. Before you settle on a single interpretation, take the time to brainstorm the various candidate readings of the slogan, including those that would fit the early twentieth-century logical context from which it has become detached as well as those that make sense in debates about mysticism. Then present the readings as a menu, from the relatively weak or even trivial to the strong or radical.

For each reading, specify:

- the precise thesis being asserted;
- whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or something likely false;
- whether it would actually license the philosopher's conclusion that a science of mystical experience is impossible; and
- what additional assumptions would be needed for the conclusion to follow.

In particular, I would like you to address distinctions such as: unutterability in a given language versus in any possible language; failure of description versus failure of knowledge; whether the slogan concerns propositions, feelings, or abilities; and whether "cannot speak" means "would be meaningless to utter" or "cannot adequately convey to someone who has not had the experience". I want to know which of these readings is doing the work when the slogan is used to stop an empirical research programme in its tracks, and whether the slogan, so read, is true. If none of the readings both is true and supports the philosopher's conclusion, say so explicitly.

I would also like the menu to flag which reading a careful philosopher probably ought to have in mind when discussing mystical states, if only to avoid the impression of equivocation.

This is for background I can use in my feature, so I would prefer analytical rigour over broad historical survey.
