I am giving a colloquium next month at a philosophy of science department with a strong causal-inference group, and I am defending a Russell-inspired eliminativist thesis about causation. My claim is that causation is not a fundamental feature of the world: at the fundamental level there are no causal relations, and "cause" is best understood as a pragmatic, interest-relative device that earns its keep in the special sciences and everyday life but does not pick out a joint-carving relation. My argument has three parts. First, what counts as "the cause" shifts with contrast classes, default states, and explanatory interests across and even within sciences. Second, causal judgments track these background interests in ways that are the signature of a pragmatic notion rather than a natural kind. Third, fundamental physics gets by with symmetries, conserved quantities, and dynamical laws with no causal vocabulary, so causation does no indispensable work at the bottom level.

The audience matters. It includes two interventionists who work with structural causal models and think of causation as invariance under interventions; a philosopher of biology who thinks causal specificity and selection are doing real explanatory work; a metaphysician sympathetic to powers and dispositions; and my host, an old Russellian who is worried I have not gone far enough. Most of them will care more about what my view does to causal modeling and causal explanation in science than about the metaphysics itself.

Prepare my Q&A prep sheet. Give the objections I will actually get from this audience, not the ones from a metaphysics conference, ordered by roughly how likely and how damaging they will be. Phrase each objection the way this audience would put it. For each, give a two-minute reply, note where the reply is softest, and say what I should simply concede. Identify the premise in my argument that is most vulnerable and that I should plan to defend rather than hope they miss. End with the question I am hoping nobody asks.
