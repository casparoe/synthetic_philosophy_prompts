I'm a PhD student in multi-agent systems, and I some how ended up running our lab's pre-deployment collusion review. Every couple weeks a team wants to ship one or more agents that will interact with other deployed systems — pricing agents, bidding agents, a negotiation bot — and I get a two-hour meeting with three ML engineers to decide whether the way they coordinate should count as collusion and get blocked. A false positive sends the project back to design, people get annoyed and we lose months; a false negative lets collusive behavior into production. We're not a law firm, so don't give me the antitrust standard; the safety committee that signs off on my memo wants an engineering standard. Please turn whatever is actually known about collusion into a reusable checklist my engineers can run without an economics background: tacit collusion in repeated games, the folk theorem (roughly, if players are patient enough, lots of outcomes can be stable), conscious parallelism, cheap talk and signaling, correlated equilibria and shared signals, prices or bid patterns as a communication channel, emergent communication. The checklist should say which questions to ask in what order, what evidence counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with ship, where it stops with block, and where it hands the decision to a person because its a values call rather than an engineering call. Then run the checklist on three cases: (1) two pricing agents that converge to supracompetitive prices in simulation with no message channel we can find; (2) two negotiation agents that develop what looks like a private code over a public channel and use it to split a market; (3) two content-ranking systems whose rankings drift in lockstep in a way that hurts source diversity but measurably improves user retention. At least one of those should come out in a direction I'm not going to like, and I want you to say so. Finally, tell me what the checklist can't catch — the collusion that would still walk through.
