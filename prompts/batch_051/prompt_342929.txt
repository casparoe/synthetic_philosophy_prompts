I'm a science-and-ideas journalist, and I want to work through the Phineas Gage case — not the neuroscience, which I know, but the metaphysics. In 1848 an iron tamping rod was driven through Gage's skull in a blasting accident; he survived and lived another eleven years. The standard story is that the damage to his frontal lobes changed his personality sharply — from dependable and temperate to profane, erratic, and impulsive — so much so that the people who knew him said he was no longer the man they had known. I want to use that case to test the Buddhist doctrine of not-self, and also to use the doctrine to see what the case actually shows.

Run the analysis in both directions. First, how would a proponent of anatta describe what happened to Gage? On the standard Buddhist picture there is no enduring, substantial self: there are only the five aggregates — form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness — in a causally continuous stream, with persons as conventional designations laid over that stream. Does Gage support that picture, by showing that what we call the same person is just a bundle of dispositions that can be altered wholesale while the body and the name continue? Or does it instead support the view that personal identity is more than a convention — and if so, precisely what persists: the continuity of memory, the living organism, the social recognition of the same man, something else? Second, what does Gage show about anatta? Is the case a confirmation, an anomaly, or something the doctrine never anticipated? Be disciplined about the mapping: say exactly which features of the stream did and did not change, which facts anatta explains well, which it has to explain away, and where it should stay silent instead of being forced to speak.

Keep one wrinkle in view throughout. Suppose the standard story of the personality change is exaggerated — that some of the more lurid post-accident descriptions are later mythmaking, and that Gage was substantially the same man before and after. How much of your analysis would survive? Does anatta need the change to be real to get its grip, or is its point that no amount of change, or stability, could settle the question because there was no fixed self there to begin with — and if the latter, does the case do any real work at all?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble and no restating of the question.
