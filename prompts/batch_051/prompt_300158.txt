In our philosophy of psychiatry reading group we have been stuck for two sessions on an argument that seems to show that the standard two-factor theory of delusions, together with a functionalist account of belief, collapses the distinction between delusions and a much larger class of states. None of us can agree on where the argument goes wrong, and we would like a diagnosis.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) S believes that p just in case S is in a state that plays the belief-role: S is disposed to avow p sincerely when asked, to use p as a premise in theoretical and practical reasoning, and to act in ways that would make sense given S's desires if p were true.

(2) Paradigmatic delusions play this role. A person with Capgras delusion sincerely reports that a spouse has been replaced by an impostor, uses this to explain the spouse's otherwise familiar behavior, and in many cases acts on it — searching the house, refusing contact, involving the police.

(3) The two-factor theory says a delusion results from two factors: a first factor supplies the content (an anomalous experience or prediction error, such as the feeling of unfamiliarity that meets a familiar face), and a second factor explains why the content is not rejected — a failure of the system that normally evaluates and filters out improbable contents.

(4) The second factor does not change the kind of state it is; it just explains how a belief with a strange content is acquired and retained. Ordinary belief formation is full of the same kind of local evaluation failures — the gambler who keeps his system, the jealous partner who explains away disconfirming evidence, the scientist who continues to favor a hypothesis the data have already embarrassed.

(5) Therefore, on the best reading of the two-factor theory, delusions are beliefs: beliefs formed and maintained by abnormal or biased processes, but beliefs in the ordinary functional sense.

(6) But the same reasoning appears to apply, step for step, to other states: a confabulated memory in a Korsakoff patient, a post-hypnotic suggestion the person later avows and acts on, the as-if conviction of a child with an imaginary companion who is treated as real across many contexts. Each is produced by a content-supplying process plus a failure to reject that content; each is avowed, reasoned from, and acted on in the relevant circumstances.

(7) If (1)–(6) are sound, then all these states are beliefs in exactly the same sense as the delusion. But that seems to prove too much: it erases the line between believing, confabulating, acting under suggestion, and pretense — a line the theory of delusions was supposed to illuminate.

(8) Hence at least one premise must be false.

Which step should we reject, and why did that premise look as secure as it did before we had to pay its price? I would like, if it is coherent, to keep both the functionalist account of belief in (1) and the two-factor theory in (3), but I am no longer sure either survives contact with the rest of the argument. Please explain what the main positions in the debate over whether delusions are beliefs each have to give up to escape this argument, and which rejection leaves the two-factor theory with the most theoretical work still to do.
