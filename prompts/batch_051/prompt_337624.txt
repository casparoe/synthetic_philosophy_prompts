I have been rereading Bertrand’s paradox while preparing for a seminar on probability and symmetry, and I want to get clear on what it actually shows about the principle of indifference. The standard example asks for the probability that a randomly chosen chord of a circle is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle. Depending on whether one chooses two endpoints on the circumference, a radius and a point along that radius, or a midpoint uniformly in the disk, the answer comes out as 1/3, 1/2, or 1/4. The obvious reply is that random chord was never well defined. But I want to know whether that reply dissolves the philosophical problem or only restates it. The principle of indifference tells us to assign equal probability to alternatives that are symmetric or equipossible, yet in a continuous parameter space there is no canonical way to enumerate the alternatives. Is the right diagnosis that the principle is underdetermined, that it requires an additional symmetry constraint beyond mere equipossibility, or that it only works when a unique invariant measure exists?

Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. The first three points should explain the three classical parametrizations and why each one appears to satisfy the principle of indifference; the last two should give the strongest philosophical diagnosis and say what, if anything, a suitably restricted version of the principle could still justify.
