I have no formal philosophical training, but I have been thinking on my own about reward, effort, and desert, and I have arrived at a principle I call the "half-life of desert." My claim is that a person's desert for a past performance is not a fixed quantity that, once earned, simply persists unchanged. It decays over time unless it is renewed, and it decays at different rates depending on the kind of relation between the person and the one who is in a position to reward or acknowledge her. An engineer who designs a bridge has a strong claim to payment and credit on opening day; thirty years later, even if the bridge is unchanged, the claim seems weaker; after she is dead, it seems weaker still, and not merely because she is not around to receive anything. I think this is a structural fact about desert rather than a failure of memory or generosity, and that it explains why seniority pay outruns current contribution, why reputations fade, and why a purely backward-looking meritocracy eventually stops looking meritocratic. I want an honest assessment. Is the notion coherent, or am I mixing desert together with gratitude, memory, and incentives? Has something like this already been worked out under another name? And where does the principle break — what does it wrongly count as deserving or undeserving?
