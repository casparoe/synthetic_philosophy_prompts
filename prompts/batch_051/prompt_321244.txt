I'm preparing a workshop handout on the doomsday argument for a philosophy of science class. Please write a decision tree, titled Which position on the doomsday argument do you actually hold?, that sorts a student by what they say about concrete cases, not by which label they like. Anchor the exercise in Richard Gott's 1993 Nature article, Implications of the Copernican principle for our future prospects, specifically the passage where he argues that an observer at a random point in a phenomenon's lifetime should make a 95 percent prediction interval for total duration from observed past duration, roughly from one thirty-ninth of that past to thirty-nine times that past. Do not rely on exact wording; work from the argument structure.

The tree should begin with a question about whether self-locating information—such as finding yourself alive now, or learning your approximate birth rank among humans—can be evidence about the total number of humans who will ever exist. Build the rest of the tree from the logical space of answers, with each branch node phrased as a question or a concrete case a student can answer without knowing any named position. The distinctions I want the tree to test are:
- whether the relevant reference class is humans, all possible humans, all sentient beings, all observers, or all observer-moments;
- whether the fact that you exist at all is itself evidence that larger total populations are more likely, and how that interacts with birth-rank evidence;
- whether unbounded hypotheses about total future births are allowed, and what happens to the likelihoods if they are;
- whether the doomsday update is strong enough to override prior information about extinction risks;
- whether one's response to the argument changes when the phenomenon is artificial, such as the run of a Broadway show, rather than humanity.

The leaves must include at least: a Leslie-style Bayesian who takes the argument seriously; a Gott-style delta-t interval user; a self-indication deflationist who thinks the relevant update cancels; a reference-class skeptic who denies that any precise conditional probability is defined; a prior-dominated position that absorbs the anthropic shift into a long future; and an unnamed leaf for someone who accepts the self-sampling premises but rejects the conclusion without a principled reason. For each leaf, include one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate. If an answer combination has no known defender, mark it as unoccupied and diagnose whether the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Keep the instrument to ten to twelve questions. Write each question with answer options, and give at least one concrete case per question (for example: you learn you are the 60-billionth human born; you learn an alien civilization has a trillion members; a critic says the argument would also predict a short future for a one-week-old marriage). The final product should be a reusable sorting instrument, not a prose summary of the debate.
