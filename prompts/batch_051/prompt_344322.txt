I teach an upper-level ethics seminar, and every year someone who's spent time in effective altruism circles defends the same move. They say donating to existential-risk work — asteroid deflection, pandemic prevention, AI alignment, whatever — is like buying a lottery ticket. The price is a few hundred dollars, the odds of your donation being the one that actually prevents catastrophe are tiny, but the jackpot is the entire future, so the expected value is vastly positive and the donation is rationally required, not just nice. The analogy is doing a lot of work for them: it turns an outlandish-sounding belief (a 1-in-a-billion chance matters more than a certainty of feeding the hungry) into something that sounds like plain arithmetic, and it makes critics of 'pascal's mugging' look like they're refusing to multiply.

I'm not satisfied, but I want to be able to say exactly where it breaks rather than hand-wave at vibes. Could you audit the analogy? I'd like an explicit correspondence table — ticket, price, odds, jackpot, drawing — and then the disanalogies, sorted into ones that matter for the intended conclusion and ones that don't. Press especially on the features a real lottery has that the longtermist case quietly borrows: a finite contractually specified jackpot, published odds with a known denominator, a regulator who actually pays out, and a market of neighboring tickets that reveals how much risk costs. Which of those features, if any, is doing the real argumentative work? And then the verdict: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion survive — that this kind of giving is rationally mandatory — or is all the analogy can really support something weaker, like 'its not obviously irrational'? If there's a better analogy for what longtermist giving is actually like, I'd like that too.
