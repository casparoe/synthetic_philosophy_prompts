I've been chewing on a parallel I noticed, and I can't decide if it's deep or just me pattern-matching. The old Weber story about the Protestant work ethic treats long, disciplined work as an individual virtue — a sign you're in a state of grace. Karoshi culture seems to do something similar from the other direction: working yourself to death reads as proof of loyalty. But the French 35-hour week treats work as neither virtuous nor vicious, just a quantitiy with spillovers that the state has to cap by law. That makes it look like a multiperson prisoner's dilemma or rat race: everyone stays late to signal commitment or avoid being the one seen as a slacker, no one can unilaterally cut back, and everyone ends up with an equilibrium nobody would have chosen if they could all coordinate. On that read, the 35-hour week is just an enforceable cooperative agreement, and the Protestant work ethic and karoshi-loyalty are moralities that turn defection into virtue so workers don't resent the bad equilibrium. Is that a genuinely deep way to read these three cases, or does the game-theoretic analogy break because work isn't only a cost people want to escape and some people actually want to be the one who works hardest? What would have to be true for the coordination-failure story to be more than a loose metaphor?
