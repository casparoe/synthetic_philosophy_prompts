I'm building a discussion for a reading group on free will, and I want you to make me a very specific thought experiment. You probably know the old story behind the word sphexishness: a digger wasp stings a cricket, drags it to the entrance of its burrow, leaves it on the doorstep, goes inside to inspect the tunnel, comes back out, and then pulls the cricket in. But if someone moves the cricket a couple of inches while the wasp is inside, the wasp drags it back to the doorstep and runs the entire inspection routine again — and it'll keep doing that forever if you keep moving the cricket. The wasp looks like it's reasoning, but it's actually stuck in a fixed subroutine and can't fold back on itself enough to notice that it already checked the burrow.

I want a case that tests whether our ordinary idea of free action as flexible and responsive to reasons is missing something. You're a professional philosopher who works on action theory and thinks best in careful case-by-case terms. Construct a scenario in which an agent passes every ordinary flexibility test a reason-responsive compatibilist would set — it can do otherwise, updates on new information, improvises, and would act differently across lots of counterfactual circumstances — but the agent is still sphexish one level up: at the level of its standing goals or higher-order policies, rather than its means. The case should make a reason-responsive compatibilist say the agent acts freely and is fully responsible, while a sourcehood incompatibilist (someone who thinks free action requires being the ultimate source of your ends, not just your means) says the agent is not acting freely in the relevant moment. Constraints: ordinary workplace or household, no sci-fi, no hypnosis, no mental illness, no outside controller; the rigidity has to come from a sincere policy the agent adopted and could revise in principle. Keep the scenario to one paragraph, and don't just stipulate the verdicts — they should fall out of how the case is built.

After the scenario, walk through what each view says and why, and identify which structural feature of the case is doing the work — the feature that, if you removed it, would make the two views converge or swap. Then say how a defender of the view that comes out badly would attack the case, and whether the other side has a reply that dosen't just beg the question. Don't worry about citing anyone; I care about the mechanics of the case, not the bibliography. Take your time, brainstorm two or three candidate scenarios before settling, and pick the one where the disagreement is real rather than verbal.
