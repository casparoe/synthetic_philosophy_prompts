I teach philosophy of archaeology, and every year the seminar gets stuck on the same word: 'ritual.' In ordinary life I'm confident about it — a wedding, a funeral, a graduation, those are rituals, full stop. And an ordinary trash midden obviously isn't. But the archaeological record is full of stuff in between, and I've noticed that 'ritual' often functions as the label we stick on a deposit when we can't figure out what its for, which makes me suspect the word is doing two jobs: picking out a type of action, and papering over an explanatory gap. I want a working definition of 'ritual' (or 'ritual behavior,' if you think that's the better target) that would let an archaeologist look at a mute deposit and decide whether she's seeing a belief-laden practice or just the archaeology of getting by. The cases I want it to handle: a human burial with a flint blade placed at the feet; a pit full of deliberately smashed but still usable pottery; a hoard of bronze axes sunk in a bog with no sign anyone meant to retrieve them; a house floor scattered with animal bones carrying butchery marks; an infant buried beneath a doorway; a patch of preserved footprints where several people walked in a rough circle; and a midden of everyday food waste. I'd call the first clearly ritual and the last clearly not, and I honestly don't know where the middle ones land. Propose the definition, run it through all seven, and tell me where it forces you to revise or to defend a surprising verdict. If you think the word really is doing two jobs, give me both definitions and say which cases switch between them.
