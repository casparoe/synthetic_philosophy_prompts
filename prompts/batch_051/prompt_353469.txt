I'm a novelist, and a subplot in my current book has gotten completely away from me. The book is set in a hospital, and there's an "explainable" triage AI that, for every decision it makes, prints an elegant little arguement justifying the decision. The engineers are adamant the system has no beliefs, no goals, no point of view — it just samples text conditioned on its outputs — but the rationales are often better than what the attending physicians come up with on the spot, and the protagonist, a surgeon, begins treating them as the machine's reasons. The thing I can't resolve is whether "reasons without a reasoner" is even a coherent idea, and it stopped being abstract when a friend read the draft and we fell into it, and another friend piled on.

Mara thinks the book is built on a category mistake. She said a reason has to be somebody's reason: a state of a mind, or at minimum something anchored to a mind that can weigh it. Text can encode an argument, but an argument becomes a reason only when a thinker takes it up; before that it's just marks. Her standing example: a correct proof of some major conjecture is sitting unread in a dead mathematician's drawer. The theorem is true, but nobody has a reason to believe it yet — the proof is a possible reason, not a reason. So the machine can't have reasons without reasoners; it can only emit strings that a human, by taking them up, promotes into reasons. Dev's word for it is psychologism, and his point is that the force of a reason lies in the logical and evidential relations it stands in, not in whether a particular skull ever uses them. The proof in the drawer is a good reason for the theorem before anyone reads it — that's the difference between discovering a reason and inventing one. And the machine's printouts are assessable as better or worse rationales regardless of what the machine "meant" or couldn't mean, so the same argument can't be a real reason when a tired resident scrawls it and mere text when a model emits it.

Mara's response was that he'd swapped support for reason. Support, she granted, is mind-independent: whether some evidence supports a hypothesis, given background assumptions, doesn't depend on anyone noticing. Reasons, by contrast, are moves in the game of deliberation — things an agent could cite and that normatively bear on what to believe or do. Data in a database can support a conclusion in Dev's sense; we call the data "a reason" only because a possible reasoner is being projected into the picture. Strip away every actual and hypothetical agent, and talk of reasons stops doing its work. Dev answered that this is exactly enough for my novel: free-floating relative to actual minds is all the premise needs, because a rationale's authority lives in the argument, not the arguer. Mara's last move was to push on the hospital scene itself: the machine produces marks, and it's the surgeon's uptake — plus the reader's — that manufactures the ghost of a reasoner; the creepiness is in the audience, not in the model. So by her lights the book is really about reasoners who hallucinate a mind behind text, not about reasons without reasoners. I've gone back and forth ever since and genuinely can't call it. Could you adjudicate the actual dispute? I'd want a point-by-point verdict on which claims each of them got right, what either of them is missing, and who's more right overall. It'll feed straight into the subplot, so don't be polite.
