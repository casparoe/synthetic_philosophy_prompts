Construct a thought experiment in which a person gives genuinely informed consent to each of several disclosures of information about herself, considered one at a time, and yet suffers a privacy violation that is generated by the combination of those disclosures rather than by any single disclosed item. Constraints: use an ordinary setting (a workplace, a medical practice, a school, or the like — not a surveillance state or a science-fiction scenario); no one in the case may lie, coerce, watch covertly, or step outside the stated terms of any individual disclosure; and each item disclosed must be mundane enough that its sensitivity appears only when it is combined with the others. After presenting the scenario, do four things. First, explain why a disclosure-by-disclosure control account of privacy — on which privacy is violated only when information about a person is accessed, used, or shared outside what she has consented to — would judge that no violation has occurred. Second, explain why a contextual-integrity account of privacy — on which privacy is violated when an information flow breaches the norms appropriate to the context, regardless of whether the subject consented to each discrete step — would judge that a violation has occurred. Third, identify the precise feature of the case that is doing the work, and say how the verdicts would shift if that feature were removed. Fourth, consider how a defender of the control account could reply: for example, arguing that the subject should have anticipated the combined inference, or that the combination itself should count as information subject to her control. Say whether that reply succeeds without abandoning the disclosure-by-disclosure standard.
