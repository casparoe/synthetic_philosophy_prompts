I've been trying to get my head round an idea I came across from Daniel Dennett about free will. As I understood it, he reckons the kind of free will people usually argue about is a sort of impossible ideal where your action would have to be a little miracle that interrupts the causal chain, and if thats what free will means then it isnt worth wanting. The varieties that are actually worth wanting, he says, are the everyday kind: being able to deliberate, revise your views when new evidence comes in, learn from your mistakes, and have what you do flow from who you are. I want to read the film through that lens, and I could do with some help.

Here's what I'm stuck on. In Arrival Louise picks up the heptapod language and starts experiencing time in a non-linear way, so she knows she'll have a daughter, Hannah, who will die young, and she still goes ahead and has her, even knowing how it will hurt her and Ian. On one reading the film looks fatalistic: the future is already sitting there and she just goes along with it. On another reading, knowing your future is not the same as being controlled by something else, and her choice still comes from her, from actually wanting that life with Hannah in spite of the grief. Which of these readings fits Dennett's idea better? Does the film show a kind of free will worth wanting, or does it quietly smuggle in the impossible kind by making Louise seem like a spectator of her own life?
