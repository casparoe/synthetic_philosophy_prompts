I was recently appointed to my university's research-integrity panel, and my first case has forced me to think about a question I had always waved away as settled: what actually makes a promise binding once honoring it becomes costly. I would like you to draft a policy memo for the panel, roughly 800 to 1000 words, that recommends how we should decide the case and gives us criteria we can apply and defend.

Three years ago, a university research team running a longitudinal study with a patient-advocacy community made a written promise to the community's coordinating council that no manuscript would be submitted for publication until the council had received the draft, been given forty-five days to respond, and the team had made good-faith revisions to any requested changes in framing or terminology. The promise was made to secure the council's help in recruiting participants and interpreting data, and the council has since used the promise to justify its cooperation to its own members.

The team now asks the panel to release it from the promise for the next two manuscripts. It argues that circumstances have changed: its continued funding now depends on establishing a publication record before the next review, a competing lab is working on similar data, and the council can point to no concrete, measurable loss it would suffer if the promise were not kept. The council responds that the promise was a condition of its cooperation and that the team would wrong it even if it were made no worse off, because what the council received was the commitment itself.

The panel is divided in a way that has made me realize how much turns on the right account of promissory obligation. Some members think a promise binds because it knowingly creates expectations and invites reliance; for them, if the promisee cannot identify any harm beyond disappointment, little of the promise's force remains. Others think a promise binds because the promisor voluntarily takes on an obligation to the promisee and thereby gives the promisee a claim to performance; on their view, a breach can wrong the promisee even without harming them, and the fact that keeping the promise has become expensive is precisely the kind of consideration the promise was meant to settle. I need the memo to take a position on this disagreement rather than treating it as a side issue.

Please draft the memo so that it contains: a clear recommendation, with any conditions attached; a statement of the test panel members should use to decide when a promise binds and when it may permissibly be broken; an honest treatment of the strongest consideration in favor of releasing the team; and an indication of which facts, if they were to come out differently, would change the recommendation and in which direction. The panel is asking for an ethical judgment, so please do not base the recommendation on legal enforceability or on the university's internal policies, and do not include citations or a reading list.
