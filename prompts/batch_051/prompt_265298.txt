Here's a thing I've never been able to get straight. If a novelist says in an interview, "when I wrote that scene I meant X," most readers treat that as basically final: the author's later gloss settles what the text means. But if a legislator says after a statute is passed, "what I meant when I voted for it was Y," courts and legal scholars mostly say that's irrelevant; the text alone is law. A party to a contract who insist's she meant something different from what the words say gets almost nowhere, yet if I send a friend an ambiguous text and then clarify what I meant, the clarification counts as real evidence about the message, often decisive. Same underlying question — what does a text mean — but wildly different rules about whether the person who produced it gets to settle that. Is there one defensible principle that makes all of these practices come out right, or are we just ad hoc?
