I help run a small internal forecasting group in a research lab. Once a month, each member records subjective probabilities in a shared spreadsheet for events that affect our planning—whether a grant will be funded, whether a paper will clear review at a particular venue by a certain date, whether our main experimental instrument will need a major repair before the next audit. We aggregate each item by taking the arithmetic mean, and we treat the resulting number as the lab's working estimate when we allocate time and contingency funds. Two of our members have economics training and have begun warning that the sheet is probably incoherent in the Dutch book sense, because the columns are updated independently and no one checks them for consistency; another member replies that a group is not a single betting agent, that no one person is offering to accept all the bets implied by the averaged numbers, and that Dutch book arguments assume a precision we simply do not have with coarse, once-a-month estimates.

What are the most important implications of Dutch book arguments for our situation specifically? I would like a careful treatment of which parts of the Dutch book critique apply to an individual reasoner's degrees of belief and which parts do or do not carry over when the "book" is an aggregate of several people's independently updated judgments. In particular, I would like you to address how the usual exposure works for failures of additivity, complementarity, and conditional coherence, and to explain which of our current habits are most likely to leave the group exploitable by someone who sees the spreadsheet and can offer bets consistent with it. Please also indicate what a realistic minimal reform would look like for us, given that we want to keep the process lightweight and are not trying to run a prediction market or maintain an epistemically exhaustive model.
