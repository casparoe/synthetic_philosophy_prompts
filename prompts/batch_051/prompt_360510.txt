I've been trying to get my head round the bit in Sidgwick where he says practical reason seems to split in two — rational egoism on one side and utilitarianism (or rational benevolence, as he sometimes puts it) on the other — and he can't find any way to make them consistant. I've turned the problem into a numbered argument, partly to force myself to see where the trouble is, and I'd like someone to go through it step by step for me. Here it is:

(1) A principle is a genuine demand of practical reason only if the verdicts it issues are binding all-things-considered.
(2) Both rational egoism and the principle of universal benevolence are genuine demands of practical reason.
(3) In some cases the two principles issue conflicting verdicts: egoism says I ought to do an act that benevolence says I ought not to do (my favourite example is a choice between a lucrative career that benefits me and a less lucrative one that does far more for strangers).
(4) If two genuine demands of practical reason conflict in this way, then practical reason both requires and forbids the very same act.
(5) If practical reason both requires and forbids the very same act, then its verdicts are contradictory, and there is no univocal answer to the question what I ought to do.
(6) Since such conflicts happen, either one of the two principles is not after all a genuine demand of practical reason, or practical reason is contradictory.

I half-suspect the move from (4) to (5) is the cheat, because 'requires' might mean something stronger in (5) than it does in (4), but I can't make that thought precise. Is the argument valid as written? If not, say exactly which step fails and what the corrected version of that step would be. If it is valid, tell me which premise I should reject and what follows — e.g. does Sidgwick's dualism just collapse into the claim that there is no all-things-considered ought in conflict cases? Please write the whole answer in British English.
