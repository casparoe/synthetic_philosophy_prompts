You're an expert who advises heritage bodies on the philosophy of preservation. Think carefully, and maybe start by brainstorming the different ways a building can matter historically before you settle on a recommendation. I'm on the board of a small heritage commision, and we've got an application from a local group to delist and demolish the old Harbour Street Jail. It's an 1880s prison with original cellblocks and a walled execution yard, so it clearly meets our test for historical and architectural significance, and the building isn't falling down. But basically everyone associates it with the cruelty that happened there: after the 1912 dockworkers' strike the city tried and hanged three organizers in the yard, and before that it was used as a debtors' prison. The group's case is that keeping it standing is a monument to the city's own cruelty. The traditionalists on the board say demolition is exactly the willful forgetting we exist to prevent. Last night I had a long argument about it with a friend, and she said I was just hoarding the past, the sick antiquarian attitude Nietzsche describes, and should let the building go; I said maybe tearing it down is the critical attitude, but then I don't know what a heritage commission is for. Other board members want to keep it as a site where the labour movement's martyrs can inspire people now, the monumental mode, I guess. I have to circulate a short decision memo before Thursday's meeting. Draft me that memo: recommend whether to delist and allow demolition, refuse and preserve, or something in between, with a time frame and concrete conditions, and make sure it genuinely engages the strongest argument for the side you don't recommend.
