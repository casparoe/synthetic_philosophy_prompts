I'm putting together a handout for a philosophy of law course on discrimination and equal protection, and I want a "which theory of suspect classifications do you actually hold?" quiz. Please write it.

The quiz should have ten questions. Each question gives a ground of differential treatment — height, say, or a felony conviction — and asks whether the respondent thinks courts should treat it as suspect (needing a really strong justification), quasi-suspect, or not suspect. The answer options should be tied to the case (what makes *this* trait more or less like race), not the abstract criteria they gesture at. No question should just ask "do you think immutability is what matters?" — you sort people by what they say about cases, not by which label they recognize.

Use at least these cases: height, religious dress, a felony conviction, obesity, citizenship, current smoker status, left-handedness, having a heavy foreign accent, pregnancy, and a genetic predisposition to a serious illness. Swap one or two if you have better ones, but keep the spread between "can't change," "could change only at great cost," and "chosen but stigmatized."

Build the scoring key from the logical space, not from a list of views. I mean: first work out the diagnostic splits on which the positions genuinely come apart, then say which combinations of answers constitute which view. Leaves should include a monist immutability view, a history-of-subordination view, a "no relation to legitimate state purposes / anti-stereotype" view, a political-powerlessness view, the classic multi-factor suspect-class test, and a realist view that the factors are just window dressing for value judgments. I'm especially curious whether the history-of-subordination view and the political-powerlessness view can actually be made to come apart in your questions, or whether they always travel together — if they always travel together, say so and merge them rather than pretending they're separate. Flag any other pair your quiz can't really separate too. Also flag any answer combinations that come out as empty cells, with a quick diagnosis for each (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored).

The questions should be ones people could genuinly hesistate on, not ones where only one answer is respectable. Audience is undergrads who haven't read the case law.

Finally, say how confident you are in the way you've carved up the positions, and single out the diagnostic question you're least sure actually discriminates between the views you map it to.
