I keep going back and forth on the old aesthetic testimony debate. The pessimist says you can't form a genuine aesthetic judgment from someone else's word: if I describe a film to you shot by shot and tell you it's devastating, you still haven't seen it, and it would be a mistake for you to treat yourself as having an aesthetic view of it. The optimist says that's gatekeeping dressed up as epistemology—testimony licenses beliefs in loads of domains, so why is whether a poem is good special? People sometimes call the pessimist's position the acquaintance principle, but I don't care about the label. What I want is to know whether the slogan is a substantive claim about how aesthetic judgments work, rather than just a norm about not talking out of turn.

Here's one operationalization: a judgment formed only from rich testimony is measurably different from one formed from first-hand experience—less stable over time, less confidant, worse at predicting the person's own later aesthetic reactions, worse at tracking consensus among people who have experienced the thing, more flippable by how the question is framed. Design the best test of the pessimist claim you can, using any artform and any concrete materials you want. Say what pattern of results would push you toward pessimism and what would push you toward optimism, and then say what the whole experimental program would leave unsettled no matter how it comes out—for instance wether we should retire the acquaintance principle or just reinterpret it. Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion.
