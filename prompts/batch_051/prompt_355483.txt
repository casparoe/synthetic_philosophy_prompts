I run the catalogue team at a mid-sized streaming service, and we've been going in circles about when two audio files should be merged under one "song" or "work" and when they should stay seperate. It isn't just metadata aesthetics: the grouping drives search results, composer credit lines, and — through how we map tracks to publishing rights — how mechanical royalties get divided. Right now the calls are made by gut, so the same pair of files can be read by one cataloguer as "same song, new recording" and by another as "a new work that borrows from the original," and that inconsistency already triggered one dispute with a publisher.

In a normal day each cataloguer makes maybe forty of these comparisons, with a couple of minutes per pair, and nobody on the team has a philosophy background; anything genuinely tangled gets escalated to a rights specialist, but I'd like to keep that queue short. Could you write us a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the distinction between a musical work, a performance of it, and a recording of a performance (the type/token stuff), that our cataloguers can apply without the theory? Give us the tests to run in order, and then work through: (a) a 1960s studio recording of a pop song versus a recent bedroom ukulele cover; (b) a live bootleg that changes some of the lyrics; (c) a remaster of the same original tape. Then do at least one hard case from our weekly pile — a DJ remix that replaces the backing track, a mashup of two songs, a jazz standard with no canonical original recording, or an ambient generative file with no score and no single performance. Along the way, flag the points where the procedure stops being mechanical and a person has to decide, and include one case where the procedure returns an answer our team will definately not like.
