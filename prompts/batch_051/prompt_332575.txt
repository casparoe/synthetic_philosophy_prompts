Zahavi's handicap principle is often summarised by saying that honest signals must be costly. Explain what the handicap principle actually claims, and explain why a signal's cost is not by itself enough to make it honest. Use an example.

Here is the student's answer:

Zahavi's handicap principle claims that in order for a signal to be reliable, it has to impose a cost on the signaler. The peacock's tail is the standard example: it makes the male more visible to predators and harder to fly, so only a genuinely high-quality male can afford to grow and carry it. The logic is that cheaters cannot pay the cost, so the cost itself guarantees honesty. This means any sufficiently costly display will be an honest signal of quality, becuase low-quality individuals just can't manage the extra burden. Grafen later proved mathematically that these handicaps can be evolutionarily stable. A related example is the stalk-eyed fly, where the eye-stalks are costly to produce but perfectly honest because they are expensive. So the main thing that determines whether a signal is honest is the total amount it costs.
