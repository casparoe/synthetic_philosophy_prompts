Several years ago I read a popular book on rationality that summarized Good's theorem in a way I still see repeated almost everywhere. The gist, close to the book's wording:

"In the 1960s, I. J. Good proved that a rational agent can never rationally refuse free information. The reasoning is close to a tautology: any new information can only narrow down the possible states of the world, and the fewer possibilities you must be uncertain among, the better your decisions will be. So the expected value of information is always nonnegative; indeed, for any observation that is at all useful, it is strictly positive. If someone nevertheless avoids finding something out, that merely reveals that they are weighting the anxiety the knowledge would cause them; a fully rational agent, whose utility attaches only to outcomes and not to feelings about uncertainty, would always accept the information."

The book then concludes that someone tempted to avoid a medical test result, say, is rationally at fault, with Good's theorem cited as the proof.

I suspect the word "tautology" is doing a lot of work, and the slide from "narrows the possibilities" to "better decisions" seems too quick. Could you go through the quoted reasoning claim by claim: which parts would a decision theorist accept, which are overstatements or false, and does the medical head-in-the-sand conclusion follow from anything Good actually proved? I would also like to know which assumptions the proof quietly needs, and what happens to the argument under the standard relaxations.
