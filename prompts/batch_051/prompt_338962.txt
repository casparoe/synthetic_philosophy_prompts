I'm a science journalist covering psychology and ideas, and I also sit on my magazine's editorial standards comittee. We're deadlocked and I've been asked to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's meeting. Six years ago our most senior long-form reporter was censured for inventing two quotations in a profile; he apologised, did a remedial accuracy course, and has published clean copy since. Now he's filed an eight-month investigation showing that a prominent psychologist whose work on moral judgment is taught in every intro course fabricated data in at least four studies. The evidence is about as close to conclusive as this kind of thing gets, but the psychologist's defenders are already saying the piece is a serial fabricator accusing someone else of fabricating, and that the magazine lacks the standing to point the finger. Half the board wants to run it under his byline and live with the criticism; the other half wants to reassign the story to a clean reporter or spike it, because they think the hypocrisy charge will stick and taint the finding. One colleague keeps insisting that fabricating a quote in a profile and fabricating data in a paper are different enough that the standing objection doesn't even get off the ground, and I honestly don't know whether that move works.

Draft the short decision memo I'd circulate. It should land on one of: run as is, run with a transparency note, reassign the story, or kill it—with an actionable recommendation and reasons. But the part I most need treated carefully is the philosophy of it: does someone's own past wrongdoing actually undermine their standing to make a true accusation, or does that just confuse the messenger with the message? Give the strongest version of the losing side first, so the committee feels it hasn't been waved away. And please write the memo in British English.
