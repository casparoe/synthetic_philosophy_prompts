I am a PhD candidate in philosophy of medicine, working toward a seminar paper on the conceptual foundations of the therapy/enhancement distinction. Please take the role of a philosopher of medicine with a strong background in the philosophy of science and in value theory, and think through this carefully; you may find it useful to begin by brainstorming several candidate justifications for the practice before settling on your reconstruction.

Consider the following practice, which I will present as a deliberately simplified composite of real drug-approval and reimbursement systems; please treat it as stipulated. A regulatory agency licenses a drug or device for an indication only when that indication is a recognized disease, disorder, or deficiency: it licenses erythropoietin for anemia but not for enhancing an otherwise healthy athlete's endurance; methylphenidate for attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder but not for improving normal concentration in a healthy student; a cochlear implant for severe-to-profound deafness but not for augmenting hearing beyond the normal range in someone with unimpaired hearing; growth hormone for a diagnosed deficiency but not for slowing age-related decline in an otherwise normal adult. Reimbursement bodies apply the same line: interventions that restore or approximate normal species-typical functioning count as medical necessity, while interventions that would raise functioning above that baseline are classified as enhancements and excluded from coverage.

On its face, this practice seems to presuppose a principled line between restoring normal functioning and enhancing beyond it. Yet the same systems cover several things that fit that story poorly: vaccination for healthy people, lipid-lowering drugs for asymptomatic high cholesterol, oral contraception, treatment of mild and largely asymptomatic hypertension, and pain relief. And once a drug has been approved for a disease indication, clinicians may prescribe it off-label for enhancement uses.

What philosophical view of health, disease, and the proper ends of medicine does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can. Then separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with: could the same pattern of approval, reimbursement, and exclusion issue from several different underlying theories of health, and if so, which? Does the practice genuinely presuppose that normal functioning has special moral significance, or might the baseline be doing merely administrative or epistemic work? Is the practice internally consistent, and where exactly are the tensions? Finally, spell out what would have to change if the implicit view were replaced by (a) a purely biostatistical account of health, (b) a welfarist account on which medical need tracks well-being rather than normal functioning, and (c) an account on which the end of medicine is the promotion of valuable capacities rather than the treatment of disease. Be concrete about which approval, coverage, and reimbursement decisions would flip under each replacement, and flag the places where the practice is philosophically underdetermined.
