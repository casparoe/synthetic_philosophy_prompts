For the past few weeks I've been turning over a version of the discursive dilemma — sometimes called the doctrinal paradox — that came up in a reading group full of readers of the same rationality blogs I read, and the easy moves everyone makes in forum debates aren't getting me anywhere. I've arrived at an argument that seems to prove too much, and I need a careful diagnosis. Set up a three-member panel voting on three propositions: P = the accused breached the contract, Q = the contract was valid, and P∧Q = liability follows. Member A accepts P, rejects Q, and rejects P∧Q. Member B rejects P, accepts Q, and rejects P∧Q. Member C accepts P, accepts Q, and accepts P∧Q. Each member's own judgments are perfectly consistent. Voting issue by issue, the panel accepts P 2–1, accepts Q 2–1, and rejects P∧Q 2–1.

The argument runs as follows. (1) A group's position on a proposition just is the majority position of its members on that proposition, where a no on X counts as a yes on ¬X. (2) If the panel's majorities accept P and accept Q, then the panel accepts P and accepts Q. (3) Any minimally rational agent that accepts P and Q is committed to accepting P∧Q and cannot coherently accept ¬(P∧Q). (4) The panel accepts P, accepts Q, and accepts ¬(P∧Q). (5) So the panel is committed to an inconsistent set of judgments. (6) This is not a contrived corner case: whenever a bottom-line judgment is a conjunction of at least two independent findings, this three-voter pattern can reproduce the same inconsistency, so majority rule will sometimes produce inconsistent collective judgments in exactly the multi-part verdicts and bundled policy votes that juries, boards, and electorates issue daily. (7) But a procedure that sometimes commits a body to contradictory judgments is not a rational way to aggregate judgments. Therefore either ordinary composite majority voting is systematically irrational, or a group's judgment is just a tally of votes with no rational constraints on it. Both conclusions feel stronger than anything I can actually accept, yet I can't find the exit.

I would most like to keep group rationality — so that a jury's verdict can genuinely be called consistent or inconsistent — and majority rule at the same time, though I expect a satisfying answer may force me to weaken one.
