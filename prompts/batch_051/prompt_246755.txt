I have no formal background in philosophy, but I have spent the past few years reading about the nonidentity problem on my own, and I keep returning to a puzzle that comes up whenever a local planning dispute is discussed in my town. The dispute concerns a flood-defense project whose timing and design will determine not just how well off the next generation is, but which particular people come to exist at all, because any substantial change to how people live, meet, and decide when to have children before the next generation is conceived changes who is born. I already accept the basic idea that if a policy changes which people exist, then the people who end up in the worse position may not be able to say the policy made them worse off than they would otherwise have been. What I want is a clean thought experiment that tests a specific disagreement between two ways of reacting to that fact. The first way, which I have seen called person-affecting, says a decision can be wrong only if it harms or wrongs some particular person. The second way, which I have seen called impersonal, says an outcome can be bad even when no particular person is made worse off. I want a case where the first view finds no individual victim while the second view condemns the decision, but where ordinary language keeps encouraging us to talk as if the same people were involved all along.

Here are the specifications. The scenario should be a single realistic planning or environmental decision — a dam, flood wall, mine, power plant, or something similar — whose effects on the next generation are serious but still compatible with each member of that generation having a life worth living under either option. The decision must change which particular people exist rather than merely changing the circumstances of people who would exist either way, and the mechanism that changes who exists must be ordinary: the timing of births, who meets whom, who stays or leaves, and so on. The scenario must also be built around a persisting group — a town, a valley, a neighborhood, an institution — that exists under both options, so that it remains natural to say, for example, that the next generation of the town is worse off, even though the individual members of that generation are not the people they would have been under the alternative. The relevant future generation should be the same size either way, and the wrongness must not rest on harm to people who already exist at the time of the decision. Do not settle the case by stipulating the verdicts; the verdicts should fall out of the mechanism. The scenario itself should be no more than a paragraph; the walkthrough can be as long as it needs to be. The point of the case is to show how a person-affecting reading of the phrase that no one was harmed can clear a decision that still seems wrong, and to expose exactly where that clearing happens.

After you build the scenario, demonstrate that it meets each specification. Set out the two options and say which feature of the case makes the person-affecting view find no victim while the impersonal view condemns the outcome. Then perform the control: reconstruct the case as a same-people choice, with the same individuals existing either way, and explain how the person-affecting verdict changes. Finish by saying how a defender of the person-affecting view would most plausibly attack the case, and give an honest note on whether the scenario genuinely generates the two verdicts or relies on an equivocation between a group and its members.
