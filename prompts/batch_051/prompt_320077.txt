In recent work on AI safety, a proposal has been advanced that structured debate between two more capable systems can allow a much less capable judge to reliably identify the truth about questions the judge could never answer alone. Let me reconstruct the argument for that proposal as I understand it, because I want to examine whether a specific new development breaks it.

The argument runs something like this:

(1) Verification is easier than generation. For many hard questions, a judge who could not have produced the correct answer can nevertheless check a presented answer step by step, the way a student who could not have discovered a theorem can verify a proof of it.

(2) Truth is resilient under questioning. A true case can in principle be unfolded into a chain of individually checkable steps, whereas any false case must somewhere rest on a step that fails under sufficiently pointed scrutiny.

(3) Cross-examination amplifies the judge. In a debate protocol, the judge can demand that either side unpack any claim it makes. This lets the truth-teller drive the questioning toward the false side's weak step; the false side, by contrast, cannot force the true case onto a step that collapses.

(4) Training scales the amplification. If the debaters are trained against the judge's verdicts, they improve precisely at making and exposing such arguments, so the judge's effective reach increases without any increase in the judge's own native ability.

The conclusion is that a weak judge, by running a debate, can learn to accept true answers and reject false ones far beyond the judge's own competence.

The development that troubles me is the collapse in the cost of producing locally plausible but misleading argumentation. A modern language model acting as a dishonest debater can generate an almost unlimited stream of superficially reasonable subarguments on demand; each one may take a judge real effort to see through, but can be produced at negligible marginal cost. The judge's checking budget — the number of claims they can actually examine before attention, time, and patience run out — has not scaled accordingly. The dishonest debater can then adopt a strategy of obfuscation by burial: concede nothing directly, answer the hard question obliquely, and, whenever pressed on a weak step, open several new branches of plausible-sounding reasoning. The truth-teller must refute each branch in turn, and if branches can be generated faster than they can be checked, the single decisive flaw never gets forced into view.

I want to know whether the reconstructed argument survives this changed fact about the economics of argument production. Please be precise about which step the development damages. Is it step (2), because truth's resilience is worth little once falsehood can be dressed in unlimited local self-consistency? Is it step (3), because cross-examination only amplifies the judge when the judge can recognize which thread is worth pulling? Or does the real damage fall on an unstated assumption behind step (4) — that the judge's verdicts remain a reliable training signal when the transcript is long, plausible on both sides, and too costly to inspect? And if a repair is available — for example, constraining the number of branching moves, making the judge rule at fixed depth, or having the debaters first debate about which claims are worth checking — does that repair restore the conclusion, or does it relocate the same problem to the meta-level?
