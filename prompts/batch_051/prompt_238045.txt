I retired a few years ago after a career in field biology — mostly birds and salmon, with a side of social insects — and I've been reading philosophy of biology for the first time. I keep coming back to one tension I can't resolve.

On the one hand, the gene's-eye view (Dawkins, Williams, and the people after them) seems to me about as close to a tidy scientific theory as evolutionary biology ever gets. It says the unit of selection is the gene, or more precisely the replicator, and organisms are just vehicles that replicators build to get themselves copied. A lot of things click once you say that: altruism toward kin, selfish genetic elements, why sex ratios do what they do, why we don't see many adaptations "for the good of the species." On paper, I find the arguments hard to resist.

On the other hand, forty years of watching whole animals has left me with an intuition I can't shake. When I watched a sockeye dig a redd or a tundra swan pull off a migration, the thing that seemed to be living or dying, succeeding or failing, was the whole integrated organism — one coordinated life history thrown against an environment. It didn't feel like gene-level bookkeeping; it felt causal. The genome is carried along, but the organism is the thing that actually meets selection. Phrases like "a gene for migration timing" or "a gene for territory defense" sound to me like the sort of post hoc abstraction I spent my career warning students against.

So I'm torn bteween a theory I find compelling and an intuition that feels like it comes from firsthand acquaintance with the phenomenon. I don't want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the theory to be the thing that goes or gets amended, and whether there's a reading of "unit of selection" on which both are right. Is my intuition the kind of thing a good reductionist should debunk — a byproduct of how a middle-sized primate parses the world into agents? Or is it tracking something, like the way an organism concentrates fitness variance in one causally integrated package? Give me the strongest version of both cases and then tell me which you would yield on, and why.
