For an English paper, I am reading Adventures of Huckleberry Finn alongside Aristotle's Politics. In the Politics, Aristotle argues that some people are slaves by nature rather than merely by law or convention: such a person, he thinks, can grasp another person's reasoning but does not deliberate on his own account, and so is better off being ruled, much as the body is better off being ruled by the soul. The novel centers on Jim, an enslaved man whom Huck has been raised to regard as someone else's property, but the story shows Jim reasoning about his circumstances, making plans, and choosing on his own judgment what he should do. I would like to read the novel through the lens of this Aristotelian claim. Does Jim, as the novel presents him, satisfy or fail Aristotle's tests for the natural slave, and which episodes matter most for that judgment? Does the book ultimately treat slavery as something that could in principle be natural and justified, or as a convention whose apparent naturalness is manufactured in Huck's own conscience? Where does the novel resist the clean anti-Aristotelian reading, and is there anything in Aristotle's account that the novel actually draws on rather than simply refutes?
