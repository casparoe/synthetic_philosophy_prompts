I'm putting together a handout for an intro philosophy of language class, and I want a "which theory of word meaning do you actually hold?" quiz, specifically about polysemy. The students have read some of Plato's Cratylus, and I want the whole thing to turn on this passage, where Socrates says:

"And can you say something of the same kind about a name? The name being an instrument, what do we do with it when we name? ... Do we not teach one another something, and separate things according to their natures? ... A name is, then, an instrument of teaching and of separating reality,"

The first branching question should be how you read that bit about "separating reality" for words like "bank" (riverbank vs. the place with tellers) and "light" (not heavy vs. a lamp). Something like: (a) these are two instruments that happen to share a sound/spelling; (b) one instrument with a standing meaning plus context-driven extensions; (c) the "standing meaning" is itself a fiction, and all we have is a family of uses; (d) a real but vague social rule that does different sorting work in different games. But I want the tree built from the logical space of answers, not from that list, so if that list misses a combination, give it a leaf or say why its empty.

The quiz should have maybe eight questions, each one a concrete case with two or three options, and no question should ask "do you believe in determinate meanings?" or mention the labels. Use cases like "book" (a novel vs. to reserve), "paper" (the material vs. a journal), "run" (the verb vs. a run in a tights), maybe a weird one like "start" (the noun beginning vs. the verb to begin), and at least one case where the senses are historically related and one where they're just accidental homophones. The scoring key should map answer patterns onto the leaves, and it should sort by what they said about the cases, not by which label they like.

At the end, flag any two leaves the tree can't cleanly separate, and add a short note on which parts of the tree are settled in semantics and which are genuinely contested, so I don't hand out a taxonomy that presents a contested view as consensus.
