I'm a PhD student working on personal identity, and I've been trying to extend Reid's brave-officer objection to Locke so that it also puts pressure on the standard neo-Lockean repair. The reconstruction I keep arriving at forces me toward a conclusion I don't want to accept, and I can't tell whether it has a hidden flaw or is valid and requires me to give up something costly. I would most like to keep the transitivity of identity and the claim that it is a determinate fact that the old general is the boy; beyond that, I'm open. Here is the argument.

1. Locke's criterion (L): a later person-stage P is the same person as an earlier person-stage Q just in case P has a direct, first-personal memory of at least one experience of Q.

2. Identity is transitive: if P = Q and Q = R, then P = R.

3. Brave-officer case: at t1, a boy is flogged for stealing apples. At t2, the same living body is a young officer who remembers the flogging from the inside. At t3, the same living body is an old general who remembers, from the inside, some of the officer's experiences but has entirely lost the flogging.

4. By (L), the officer is the boy, and the general is the officer. By transitivity, the general is the boy. But by (L), the general is not the boy, since he has no direct memory of the flogging. Contradiction; so (L) is false.

5. The neo-Lockean repair replaces direct memory with psychological connectedness: a relation, coming in degrees, that holds between adjacent person-stages when they share particular memories, intentions, beliefs, and similar states. Personal identity is then psychological continuity, defined as the ancestral of connectedness — the relation that links stages when there is a chain of overlapping connected steps between them. This makes identity transitive by construction.

6. But the chain criterion is only as definite as its two settings: the grain at which person-stages are individuated and the threshold of overlap required for one stage to count as connected to the next. Connectedness is graded, and within a single life psychological overlap thins out gradually, so no natural point presents itself for either setting.

7. If the grain is fine, adjacent stages in an ordinary waking life share almost everything, and chains propagate across practically any continuous psychological history, no matter how thin the retained overlap between the endpoints. If the grain is coarse, chains can break in gradual cases where the theory was supposed to yield continuity.

8. Likewise, if any nonzero overlap counts as a link, the chain relation becomes extremely cheap: the old general is tied to the boy even if, at each step, almost nothing was preserved as long as no link drops to zero. If a substantial overlap is required, then whether the general is the boy depends on a threshold that memory decay, fading intentions, and gradual character change do not supply.

9. But identity is supposed to be an all-or-nothing relation with the formal properties in (2), not a relation whose verdicts shift with an arbitrary choice of grain and threshold. So the neo-Lockean repair either trivializes personal identity over time or makes it arbitrary in the very cases it was meant to explain.

10. That conclusion is unacceptable: it is a determinate, non-arbitrary fact that the old general is the boy.

So the repair fails. Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? If it doesn't, which premise would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost for transitivity and for the determinacy of identity over time?
