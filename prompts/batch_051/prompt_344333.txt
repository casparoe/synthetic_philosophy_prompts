At a medical ethics seminar I attended, two of my colleagues got stuck in a dispute about the doing/allowing distinction and neither would concede. They were comparing two cases. In the first, a patient is stable on a ventilator whose battery is fully charged; a physician switches the machine off, and the patient dies within minutes. In the second, the same patient's ventilator battery has only five minutes of charge left; the physician decides not to replace it, and the patient dies five minutes later.

One colleague, Elena, insisted that the first is a killing and the second is a letting die, and that the difference matters morally. The other, Marcus, replied that this is hard to believe: the only difference is whether support is withdrawn now or allowed to lapse five minutes from now, and no moral description should turn on where, within the act sequence, the doctor happens to move.

Elena answered that the counterfactuals are not a detail. In the first case the patient would have gone on living, absent the physician's intervention; in the second the patient would have died within five minutes anyway, absent intervention. That, she said, is exactly the difference between making someone worse off and failing to make them better off.

Marcus came back that, by that test, a do-not-resuscitate order would count as killing whenever the patient could have been revived, since absent the decision not to do CPR the patient would have lived longer. But one presumably wants DNR to be a letting die. And if DNR is a letting die, so is switching off a ventilator: both are decisions not to continue an intervention at the moment it is needed.

Elena said DNR is different: the DNR patient is already in cardiac arrest, with the body's own lethal process underway. The ventilated patient is physiologically stable until the switch is thrown; in switching it off, the physician inserts herself into a process that was not otherwise going to kill the patient, whereas in DNR she merely refrains from interrupting a process that already is.

Marcus replied that the ventilated patient is not stable in the relevant sense. The machine is actively holding back a lethal respiratory process, much as CPR holds back cardiac arrest. If the fact that support comes from a machine rather than the doctor's own hands is what makes one withdrawal an allowing and the other a killing, that is a suspicious place for the moral distinction to live.

Elena then revised her view: maybe the real difference is in the reason the patient dies. When the physician switches the machine off, she makes herself the cause of death; when the battery is not replaced, the machine's depletion — a pre-existing causal process — is the cause of death, even though the hospital could have stopped it. What matters is what the agent causally initiates, not what she merely fails to interrupt.

Marcus said the battery's depletion only runs its course because the hospital chose not to replace it, and choices are not given in nature; they are sustained minute by minute. If the causal distinction just pushes the same decision back one step, nothing has been distinguished, only relabeled.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. Say who is right about which specific claim, which arguments succeed on their own terms, and which load-bearing assertion each side would need to establish in order for their overall position to follow. In particular, assess whether the dispute actually bottoms out in a prior question neither has isolated, and whether either side can state a version of the doing/allowing distinction that handles both cases without collapsing.
