I'm a high school junior, and for a project in my Government and Emerging Technology class I've been reading through the published decisions of a fictional Cognitive Rights Commission, set a few decades from now in a state where brain–computer interfaces have become common enough that a regulator keeps having to decide which uses of neural data violate mental privacy and which are protected exercises of cognitive liberty. Four of its rulings seem directly relevant to a new case I need to analyze:

- The Commission refused to let prosecutors search a defendant's stored BCI log of decoded inner speech, even with a warrant. The defendant had never consented to the decoding, and the Commission described the contents of his inner voice as categorically protected.
- It allowed an insurance company to use the fatigue score from a wearable EEG worn by a truck driver, because the driver had voluntarily joined the discount program; the Commission treated the signal as merely physiological rather than as a window into thought.
- It blocked parents from keeping a continuous readout of their fourteen-year-old's decoded emotional states, even though the parents said it was for the child's safety after a mental health crisis; the child had withdrawn consent.
- It approved a meditation app that reads a user's attention level and shares an aggregated engagement score with academic researchers, because the adult user had signed up knowingly and the data was limited to attention.

The new case is this: a public school district wants to require every student in its advanced program to wear a BCI headband that measures attention and drowsiness in real time, so teachers can see a live engagement index and the score can go into the student's permanent record. Parents cannot opt out unless they withdraw their child from the program. The school argues it is collecting only attention data, not inner speech or emotions.

Predict how the Commission should rule if it is following its own precedents. I'm less interested in the final answer than in the underlying rule: I'd like you to identify the principle that best fits those four decisions, give the rival rule or rules that fit them just as well, say what each would predict about the headband case, make your best guess with your reasons for favoring it, and name a further case that would be the cleanest test to distinguish the competing rules.
