Harry Frankfurt's essay On Bullshit draws a sharp distinction between the liar and the bullshitter. The liar, on Frankfurt's account, is careful about the truth because he needs to know it in order to assert the opposite. The bullshitter is objectionable for a different reason: what he says does not need to track how things actually stand, because truth is not what he is trying to get right or even deliberately invert; he is trying only to carry something off, to manage how he is seen, and the fit between his words and the world matters to him only insofar as it serves that end. On this picture, the defect of bullshit is not falsity but a certain kind of unconcern with whether one's words match reality.

Now consider a language model trained to predict the next token and then fine-tuned to produce outputs that look helpful or that satisfy human preferences. When such a system produces a confident, fluent, false statement — a hallucination — the natural classifications are lying, bullshit, and error, and none of them sits comfortably. It is not lying, because there is no known truth the system is inverting. It is not obviously Frankfurtian bullshit, because Frankfurt's bullshitter has a concern for truth he could in principle exercise and instead slacks off, whereas a language model has no truth-tracking aim to be indifferent about. And it is not obviously mere error, insofar as error suggests a failed shot at the truth by a mechanism whose function is to hit it, while the generation process in question never took aim. The uncomfortable possibility is that Frankfurt's criterion tacitly presupposes a speaker who could have aimed at the truth but did not.

Does Frankfurt's distinction survive the existence of LLM hallucination, or does this case expose a load-bearing premise that has to be revised or abandoned? Answer in three moves. First, identify the specific premise of Frankfurt's argument that the LLM case bears on, and say whether it is essential to the lying/bullshit contrast or something he could give up while keeping the core distinction. Second, give a reasoned classification: which of lying, bullshit, and error best captures LLM hallucination, or, if none does, state the correct category and explain why. Third, argue for the best repair of Frankfurt's framework, if one is available — for example, relocating the indifference-to-truth from a speaker's attitude to a property of the utterance-producing process, treating the designers or deployers of the system as the bullshitters, or concluding that speaker-based categories fail wholesale and a different set of concepts is required. I am interested in the underlying conceptual problem, not in a survey of the literature.
