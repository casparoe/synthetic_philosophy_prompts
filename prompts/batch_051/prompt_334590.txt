I'm on the science-and-ideas beat at a magazine, and I'm writing a piece about why misinformation seems to outrun corrections online. One source—an evolutionary anthropologist, not some pop-science guy—told me the gene/meme thing is more than a metaphor. His argument goes: cultural variants get copied from mind to mind, copying is noisy so they mutate, variants compete for finite attention and memory, and the ones better at getting copied spread. Put heredity, variation, and selection together and you've got Darwinian evolution. He uses that to license a prediction: misinformation will keep getting more engaging and harder to debunk, because truth isn't the trait being selected for; transmissibility is.

A cognitive psychologist I talked to said the whole business is just a metaphor. When people learn from each other they reconstruct an intended meaning, splice fragments from many sources, and retune the result for the current audience. Often there's no discrete unit being copied at high fidelity, so there's nothing for selection to act on. Some critics add that culture settles into attractors—different starting points converge on the same public shape—which makes the unit an effect of the process, not the thing that replicates.

For the piece I need you to audit the analogy. What maps to what between genes and memes (a table of correspondences would help), and where does the mapping break? Which disanalogies actually matter for the anthropologist's prediction, and which are just loose talk? One thing I can't shake: memes blend, recombine, and get deliberately edited in ways genes don't. Does that just change the mutation rate, or does it break the replicator idea altogether? Does cultural change need high-fidelity, particulate, gene-like replication to count as Darwinian, or can selection work on sloppy reconstruction? And does the prediction—misinformation evolves toward what spreads, not what's accurate—survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or does it depend on a tidy replicator that isn't there? If the gene analogy can't carry the weight, is there a better analogy, or should the Darwinian framing be dropped?
