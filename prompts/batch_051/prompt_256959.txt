Arrow's impossibility theorem is usually deployed as an argument that no method of aggregating individual preferences into a collective ranking can satisfy a short list of seemingly modest conditions: unrestricted domain (any profile of individual rankings is admissible), Pareto efficiency (unanimous preferences are respected), independence of irrelevant alternatives (the social ranking of two options depends only on how individuals rank those two options), and non-dictatorship (no one individual's ranking always prevails). A crucial background assumption is that the inputs are ordinal rankings: each voter orders the options but supplies no information about how strongly they prefer one option to another, and there is no basis for comparing the strength of one voter's preference with another's. Now consider a development that alters that background. In many of the settings where preferences are actually aggregated today, the inputs are not bare rankings but cardinal signals: five-star ratings, willingness-to-pay, time spent, likes, and other behavioral traces that appear to carry intensity information, often on a scale that is at least nominally shared across individuals, and these signals can be collected at essentially zero marginal cost. Does Arrow's impossibility still go through in this environment, does it need repair, or does it become inapplicable? I am asking about the fate of the theorem as a constraint on aggregation, not about which specific voting rule is best. Identify exactly which premise of the theorem the development bears on, and say whether that premise can be restored in some other form. Respond as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
