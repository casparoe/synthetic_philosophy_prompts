Since retiring from a career in the physical sciences, I have been working through the philosophy of probability, and I have found myself caught on a small argument about finite sequences that I cannot resolve to my satisfaction. In the laboratory I was perfectly comfortable calling a process random — a fair coin, a Geiger counter, a thermal noise source — without worrying about the particular string of outcomes it happened to produce. The difficulty begins when I try to carry that habit over to the outcomes themselves. Consider this argument:

(1) A fair coin is random, no matter what it produces; randomness is a property of the process, not of any particular outcome.
(2) If a process is random, then any finite sequence it actually produces is, in the ordinary sense, a random sequence.
(3) A fair coin can produce HHHHHHHH; that sequence is exactly as probable as any other sequence of eight tosses.
(4) Therefore HHHHHHHH is a random sequence.
(5) But HHHHHHHH is plainly not random: it is maximally patterned, and if I saw it I would doubt the coin rather than shrug and call it chance.

Something has to give, and I cannot tell which step is doing the cheating. My working suspicion is that 'random' means one thing when applied to a process and another when applied to a string — something like 'produced by an unbiased mechanism' versus 'incompressible, or free of regularities' — and that step (2) smuggles the process sense into a conclusion about the string sense. But I am not confident, because I also have the impression that the mathematical theory of randomness (Kolmogorov complexity, Martin-Löf tests, and similar ideas) treats randomness as a property that applies cleanly only to infinite sequences, and treats finite strings as merely more or less random. That would make step (5) too coarse as well.

Please tell me whether the argument is valid, and if it is not, exactly which step or steps fail and on what reading. Give the strongest reading of each premise under which it is true, and check whether the argument still goes through. I would also ask you to separate what is settled in this area from what is genuinely contested among people who work on it, and not to present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
