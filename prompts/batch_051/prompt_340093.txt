For most of my writing life I have treated causation in fiction as something irreducibly narrative: a matter of which detail the narrator chooses to enlarge, which earlier moment the shape of the story invites the reader to hold fixed, which possible alternative remains live in the prose. In recent years, however, I have found myself genuinely persuaded by Pearl's causal hierarchy and the apparatus of structural causal models. There is something clarifying about the discipline it imposes: a causal claim has determinate content only relative to a specified graph of variables and equations; the do-calculus tells me what follows when I intervene rather than merely observe; and the three rungs of association, intervention, and counterfactual map with surprising precision onto the questions I actually ask while plotting—what tends to co-occur in a character's life, what would change if I forced a circumstance upon her, and what would have happened had something been otherwise. I am even moved by the austere assumption that the equations are modular and remain invariant under the interventions I imagine; it seems to be what lets a story branch coherently instead of falling into ad hoc revision.

Yet I cannot rid myself of a contrary conviction, and it is not one I am eager to give up. In my practice the decisive causal work appears to happen in the choice of the model itself, before any query is posed. When I ask why a character left home, I can answer "she left because of the letter" or "she left because of her pride," and these do not feel like two reports of a single underlying counterfactual that I have merely described from different angles. They feel like two different stories built from the same events, each licensed by a different variable set and a different background held fixed, and neither feels merely pragmatic or merely epistemic. The formalism treats that selection as presupplied and therefore external to the causal facts, but for me the selection is the causal fact I care about. I can see three ways this might resolve: the intuition is a pragmatic shadow the formalism is right to externalize; the formalism must be amended so that model selection is itself a causal matter; or the two can be reconciled once "cause" is disambiguated. Do not simply choose among these. Give me the strongest statement of each, and then say what evidence or argument would justify discarding the intuition, revising the theory, or accepting the reconciliation.
