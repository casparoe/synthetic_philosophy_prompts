I've landed on a position about causal discovery and I want you to tell me what would count against it — not just what critics already say, but what would actually move a reasonable person who holds it. The position goes like this: when we infer causal structure from non-experimental data, we should prefer the minimal Bayes net compatable with the observed pattern of conditional independencies, and we're justified in doing that because of faithfulness. By faithfulness I mean the assumption that the only independencies in the distribution are the ones the graph implies, so none are just numerical accidents. Minimality is the weaker requirement: don't keep an edge if deleting it wouldn't change any independence facts. Together they're meant to explain why the simplest model that fits tends to track the causal truth (up to Markov equivalence, obviously).

I buy this. Now take it seriously and tell me where it's exposed. What cases would a defender of minimality-plus-faithfulness have to admit are genuine failures or at least awkward — exact cancellation between two causal paths, deterministic relationships, near-cancellations in finite samples, latent confounders, whatever else you can think of? Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones you'd expect the faithful-minimalist to bite the bullet on. Are there formal results or simulations you'd point to that would do more damage than a clever example? And be honest about whether the view has been qualified to death — weak faithfulness, adjacency-faithfulness, whatever — and whether that's a serious problem or just a reasonable retreat for an assumption that was only ever meant as a regulative ideal. Please answer in British English.
