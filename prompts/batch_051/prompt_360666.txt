Two friends of mine got into it last night about our friend Lina. Lina's still in love with her boyfriend Marcus even though, on her own account, he isn't the person she fell for anymore: he used to be warm and funny, and after a rough couple of years he's become cold, sarcastic, and mostly checked out. When I asked what she still loves about him, she said "just him—I can't put it into words."

Priya said Lina's love has become unjustified, maybe even irrational: the qualities that made Marcus lovable are gone, and "just him" isn't a reason, it's the thing that needs explaining. Tom said that gets love backwards. Love isn't a judgment about someone's qualities, he said; it's closer to a stance or a decision. Qualities might get you in the door, but once you love a person, they stop being replaceable by someone with better traits.

Priya came back: then love is never criticizable—a woman who loves a man who beats her would be just as "rational" on that view. Tom said no, you can criticize the relationship and the staying, but not the love itself; love isn't the kind of thing that's true or false. Priya replied that if a belief stayed put after all its grounds evaporated, we'd call it irrational, so why does love get a pass? She loved him for being kind, and that premise is now false. Tom said because love creates its own reasons: once you love someone, their well-being matters to you directly, not as a proxy for their list of good traits. Priya said that's worse—it means loving an awful person generates reasons tied to an awful person, which blindly blesses terrible relationships. Tom said yes, love does change the normative landscape; it doesn't need a prior certificate of worth.
