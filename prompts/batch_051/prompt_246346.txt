I’ve been poking at Mill’s late socialist turn, and there’s a passage in his posthumous *Chapters on Socialism* that feels like it’s doing more work than people usually notice. He writes:

“Now these are the very class which, in the vocabulary of the higher ranks, are said to have no stake in the country. Of course they have in reality the greatest stake, since their daily bread depends on its prosperity. But they are not engaged (we may call it bribed) by any peculiar interest of their own, to the support of property as it is, least of all to the support of inequalities of property. So far as their power reaches, or may hereafter reach, the laws of property have to depend for support upon considerations of a public nature, upon the estimate made of their conduciveness to the general welfare, and not upon motives of a mere personal character operating on the minds of those who have control over the Government.”

The context is Mill arguing that once manhood suffrage lets wage-earners vote as a class, the laws of property can no longer be propped up by the personal interests of the propertied people who happen to control government; property arrangements have to be defended on grounds of general welfare. It’s basically his version of the “public reason” move.

What’s bugging me is this. Mill is writing before the big spread of pension funds, 401(k)s, index funds, employee stock plans, and so on. In a modern rich country, a large chunk of the working population is technically a small shareholder, or at least owns a claim on diversified capital through retirement accounts. If you squint, the very class Mill says has no “peculiar interest” in supporting property as it is now has *some* personal exposure to equity and housing prices. Does Mill’s argument survive that shift? I don’t just mean the obvious point that the median worker still isn’t rich enough for it to matter much. I mean, when the personal stake is diffuse, tiny, and managed through institutions, does it restore the old pro-property motive Mill thought was gone, or does it just change the way the public justification has to work? What premise does Mill’s argument actually need, and is that premise still true in a world of mass asset ownership?
