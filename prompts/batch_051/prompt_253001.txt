I'm a PhD student in cognitive neuroscience and I keep tripping over a tension in how the clinic handles disorders of consciousness. The way we actually diagnose whether someone is conscious is almost entirely behavioral: on the CRS-R or the Glasgow, what matters is whether the person can follow a command, track a stimulus, orient to their name, that sort of thing. If they cant do any of that, they get slotted as unresponsive wakefulness syndrome, which used to be called vegetative state and is officially described as wakefulness without awareness — the person has sleep-wake cycles but shows no signs of being aware of anything. But that whole scheme seems to treat consciousness as what a philosopher would call access consciousness: information that is globally available in a way that lets the person report it or use it to steer action. If you can't produce report-like behavior, the clinical conclusion is basically that there's nothing it's like to be you.

Then there's the weird middle ground: patients who are behaviorally unresponsive but show command-following brain activity on fMRI or EEG, what some people call cognitive motor dissociation. If you take the access/phenomenal distinction seriously, that looks like a case where phenomenal consciousness might be present without the usual access-and-report machinery working — which is exactly the kind of dissociation the behavioral exam is built to rule out by fiat.

So here's what I want to know. What theory of consciousness does this diagnostic practice actually presuppose? Does it strictly require something like a global workspace view, where access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness go together or are the same thing, or is it better read as treating access as a fallible operational proxy while staying agnostic about the metaphysics? Which parts of the practice are underdetermined — for example, could someone who fully accepts the access/phenomenal distinction still run the same behavioral exams and just interpret a negative result differently, and which parts would they have to object to? And if we did take the distinction seriously, what would concretely change in how we diagnose these patients, and what would stay the same because the alternative is just epistemically or practically unavailable at the bedside?

Also, please flag wherever I'm leaning on something contested and dont present a contested view as the consensus. Separate what's settled, like the behavioral criteria being what they are, from what's genuinely up for grabs, like whether covert command-following really establishes phenomenal consciousness or could be explained another way.
