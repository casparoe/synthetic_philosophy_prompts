I am a retired geophysicist, and after many years away from philosophy I have been reading Poincaré's *Science and Method*. The passage in which he discusses how a very small cause that escapes our notice can determine a considerable effect, and that we then say the effect is due to chance, has been bothering me. As I understand it, Poincaré is not saying that such effects are random in any metaphysical sense; he is saying that our classification of something as chance is often the shadow of a real sensitivity that makes the world unforecastable in principle. I would like you to take that idea seriously and interrogate it through a counterfactual.

Imagine the following. Starting in 1900, the Earth's atmosphere is exactly as it actually was, except for one change in the underlying fluid mechanics: for every pair of initial states that differ by a small amount, the distance between the resulting trajectories grows at most linearly with time. Exponential separation never occurs. The equations of motion remain deterministic; storms, fronts, cyclones, and ordinary weather still occur; only the explosive amplification of very small differences is gone. An initial error of one part in a trillion in the pressure field therefore cannot become a synoptic-scale difference within a week.

What happens to the notion of an ensemble forecast when all members agree? How does Poincaré's remark look to scientists and philosophers in this world—would it sound like a curiosity or a deep insight? Which of your predictions are robust, and which depend on assumptions about how much of atmospheric unpredictability was really due to exponential sensitivity rather than to model error or unresolved physics?
