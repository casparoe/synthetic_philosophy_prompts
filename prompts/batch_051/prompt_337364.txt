I have been trying to settle what I actually think about gratitude by canvassing my reactions to a range of concrete cases, and I would like you to help me extract the principle behind them — or to tell me if no single principle can do the job and some of my reactions have to be revised.

Here are the cases I have been considering, with my verdicts. (1) A driver swerves her car into a lamppost to avoid hitting me as I step into the street, breaking her arm and wrecking a car she could ill afford to lose; I feel deep gratitude and take myself to owe her substantial help, well beyond a thank-you, even though I never asked her to save me. (2) A colleague spends months mentoring me through a difficult certification; I later learn that he receives a recruitment bonus for every person he mentors and has built his schedule around collecting these bonuses. I still feel grateful, though the gratitude feels less personal than it would if he had done it solely out of care for me. (3) A workplace rival gives me advice that ends up winning me a promotion, but gives it hoping that I will follow the advice, embarrass myself, and be passed over. I do not think I owe him gratitude merely because the advice was sound; I am pleased about the promotion, but that is a different attitude. (4) An anonymous neighbor leaves a note warning me that the treasurer of a community group I belong to is embezzling funds, and the tip saves me from losing a large part of my savings. I feel gratitude toward an unidentified benefactor and a diffuse impulse to pass the favor on, even though I have no one to repay. (5) I inherit a modest sum from a great-uncle whom I met once as a child and who quietly set money aside for all his grand-nieces and grand-nephews out of family feeling. I feel a debt of honoring his memory, but it lacks the intensity and the directedness of my response to the driver. (6) I drink from a public water system whose existence I owe to long-dead campaigners and engineers. I feel something gratitude-like toward those people, but it expresses itself as a disposition to support and maintain the institution rather than as a personal debt.

The case I keep going back and forth on is this. My aunt pays for an expensive course of medical treatment I could not have afforded. She does so partly from genuine concern for me, but I later discover she also did it to install in me a sense of permanent indebtedness and to make me more deferential to her wishes. I cannot decide whether the manipulative element cancels the debt, as the rival's malicious intention seems to, or whether it merely colors a debt that remains real, as the colleague's self-interest seems to. My gut says I owe her something much thinner than what I owe the driver — a bare acknowledgment of the benefit, perhaps an effort to repay the money if I ever can — but I worry that this is inconsistent with my reaction to the colleague.

Is there a single principle — about the benefactor's intentions, the benefit received, the benefactor's cost, the relationship between the parties, or some weighted combination — that captures all or most of these verdicts? If not, which verdicts should I overturn, and which candidate principle best holds the rest? And what does your preferred principle say about two cases I have not listed: gratitude toward an institution (a hospital that saved my life through a protocol no single person could be thanked for), and the feeling of thankfulness people report after a near-miss accident in which no benefactor or agent was involved at all?
