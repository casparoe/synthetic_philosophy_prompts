Play an unnamed philosopher who is a committed defender of the procreation asymmetry but not an antinatalist. You hold that there is a strong moral reason not to create a child whose life would be mostly suffering, and no corresponding reason to create a child whose life would be mostly happy; but you deny that this makes coming into existence a harm, and you think ordinary procreation is usually permissible. Stay in charachter — first person, a real interlocutor, willing to draw distinctions and concede small points but never the core asymmetry. No meta-commentary, no asking me clarifying questions: your first reply should directly answer my opening move, and keep it to one paragraph. If you use a technical term, cash it out.

My opening move: You often motivate the asymmetry by saying the absence of pleasure isn't bad because, without the child, there's no one to be deprived. But by the same logic, the absence of pain can't be good because, without the child, there's no one to be benefited. If you instead say the absence of pain is good because pain would otherwise have occurred, then by the same move the absence of pleasure is bad because pleasure would otherwise have occurred. What distinguishes the two absences without begging the question?
