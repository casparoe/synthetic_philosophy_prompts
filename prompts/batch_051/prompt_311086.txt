Drug regulators and medical journals treat randomized controlled trials as the gold standard for establishing that a treatment causes an outcome; observational studies get downgraded on the grounds that they can't rule out confounders. But those same institutions are perfectly comfortable saying smoking causes lung cancer, even though nobody randomly assigned anyone to smoke and nobody ever will. I'm not asking about the practical reasons for this double standard. What I want to know is what theory of causation the gold-standard-for-causation practice actually presupposes. Are these institutions committed to interventionism — the idea that being a cause is just being a variable whose manipulation would change the outcome while other things are held fixed? And if so, isn't the whole thing circular, since you can't say what counts as a manipulation without already using causal language? Or is the hierarchy of evidence purely pragmatic and totally neutral about what causation is, in which case why does the word "cause" keep sneaking back in? Reconstruct the implicit theory as best you can, say where the practice strictly presupposes something versus where it's just compatible with several views, and say what would have to change if they bit the bullet and adopted a consistently interventionist account.
