I got into an argument at a museum recently and I keep turning it over. A friend and I were standing in front of a large abstract painting; I had read a fair amount about the artist and studied high-resolution reproductions, so I offered an opinion about how the surface worked. My friend dismissed it by saying, in roughly these words, that I couldn't know the painting until I had stood in front of it. She was not saying I lacked facts about the painter or the movement. She was saying that something about the experience itself was missing, and that my opinion was somehow second-rate. When I asked what exactly I was missing, she just repeated herself. Since then I have noticed versions of the same move everywhere: you can't judge a song from a description, you can't know a place from photographs, you have to taste the dish yourself. I suspect that sentence is doing several different jobs at once. I would like you to lay out the distinct claims someone could be making when they say something like "you can't know a painting until you've stood in front of it." For each candidate claim, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or just false, and tell me which reading would actually support my friend's dismissal of my opinion as opposed to merely expressing her preference for first-hand experience.

I want the readings arranged as a menu I can use the next time this comes up, not a single preferred interpretation. I am especially interested in the difference between claims about knowledge (I literally cannot know the relevant feature from testimony or reproductions) and claims about justification or entitlement (I should not form confident aesthetic judgments that way), and in whether the slogan would apply equally to a paint conservator describing which pigments are present and to a friend describing how the painting feels. If some readings would support dismissing my opinion, say exactly what feature of the case they need to rely on—for example, a difference between propositional description and perceptual acquaintance, or the unreliability of other people's taste—and whether removing that feature would collapse the slogan back into something uncontroversial.
