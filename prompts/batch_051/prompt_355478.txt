I run a book club and we're doing Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day next month, with the Merchant-Ivory film as a fallback for people who didn't read it. I want to steer the discussion toward Hannah Arendt's idea of the banality of evil, but I don't want a shallow thing about Stevens being a Nazi by proxy, because he plainly isn't. I want a real reading through a lens, one that goes both ways.

As I understand Arendt from Eichmann in Jerusalem: she was struck that Eichmann was not a monster or a fanatic but an ordinary, shallow, career-minded sort of man, full of clichés and oddly incapable of thinking from the standpoint of anyone else; he committed himself to the system's tasks without ever really asking what he was doing. The evil of the enterprise was huge, but its agent looked small and unremarkable, and that mismatch is what she meant by banality.

Now take Stevens. He is no Eichmann, but he does hand over the question of what is worth serving to Lord Darlington, treats professional dignity as a matter of never being shaken out of the role, carries out the dismissal of the two Jewish maids without any visible moral wobble, and on the night his father dies he mostly keeps the conference running and later presents that as a professional success rather than a loss. Yet the novel treats him with far more sympathy than Arendt has for Eichmann, and a lot of his rigidity reads as suppressed feeling rather than plain thoughtlessness.

My question is how the book and the lens cut against each other. Does Stevens fit Arendt's account of how ordinary decent people become vehicles of evil by not thinking, or does he complicate it? Which parts of the novel would you point to as resisting the Arendt reading, where the book gives us something the theory doesn't? And what would you say the novel adds to the philosophical debate about banality that the essays tend to miss? Don't just do a list of parallels; I want a reading that stays faithful to the book and to Arendt.

Oh, and please write your answer in British English.
