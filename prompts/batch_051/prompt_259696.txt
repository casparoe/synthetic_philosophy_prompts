A colleague in my epistemology seminar laid out the following argument and said it shows that process reliabilism can be made to certify itself. I cannot locate the step at which the damage is done, and I would like a step-by-step diagnosis.

(1) Process reliabilism says, roughly, that a true belief is knowledge when it is produced by a reliable belief-forming process.

(2) Consider a subject whose color vision is in fact reliable, looking at a ripe tomato under good light. It looks red to her, and on that basis she forms the true belief that the tomato is red. By (1), that belief is knowledge, because a reliable process produced it.

(3) The subject also knows by introspection that the tomato looked red to her; introspection about one's own current visual experience is itself a reliable process in normal circumstances, so (1) grants her this second piece of knowledge as well.

(4) From these two known claims she can infer, and thereby come to know, their conjunction: the tomato looked red and the tomato was red.

(5) Over the course of a week she compiles hundreds of such conjunctive records — the traffic light looked red and was red, the shirt looked blue and was blue — all under the same good conditions and with no known misses.

(6) A long run of hits with no known misses is, by ordinary induction, strong evidence that the process producing the matches is reliable. Since induction is itself a reliable process, by (1) her inferred belief that her color vision is reliable counts as knowledge.

(7) So process reliabilism entails that a subject can come to know that her color vision is reliable by reasoning in which every first-order premise — every claim that some object has a certain color — was supplied by the very faculty whose reliability is at issue. At no point does she independently check any item; each hit is credited on the word of color vision itself.

(8) That is the too-easy result: the faculty has, in effect, been allowed to calibrate itself on trust alone. Worse, from the inside this reasoning is indistinguishable from what a subject with systematically bad color experience would run. She could compile the same list of apparent hits and perform the same induction, and nothing in the schema requires the hits to be genuine rather than merely accepted. A theory of knowledge that licenses self-calibration this cheaply cannot distinguish genuine calibration from confirmation bias, so the argument proves too much.

Where exactly does the argument go wrong, if it does? Which step should a reliabilist reject, and what does rejecting it commit her to? I am inclined to keep (2), the ordinary perceptual knowledge, and (6), the thought that induction from a long unbroken run of hits is normally knowledge-conferring, so I want to know what keeping those costs. Finally, say whether the fix for the reliabilist also handles the same remark made against a non-reliabilist evidentialist who wants to allow the subject's inductive step, or whether the problem simply relocates there.
