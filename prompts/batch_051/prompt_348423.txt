I'm a phd student working on the epistemology of expertise—how judgment gets trained and whether it transfers—and I keep coming back to an analogy that might be load-bearing or might just be a vibe.

Martial arts: traditional training is full of kata, solo pre-arranged sequences, plus cooperative drills where the partner feeds a punch and freezes so you can do the prescribed counter. The combat sports crowd has a line about this: techniques only become fight-relevant when trained 'alive,' i.e. against timing you haven't rehearsed, real resistance, and unpredictability; otherwise you get good at kata instead of fighting. Set aside whether that's fully fair as a theory of fighting—its a standard critique and I find it pretty plausible.

Now rationality training. The analog of kata seems to be calibration quizzes, base-rate problems about cabs and diseases, conjunction-fallacy vignettes, framing tasks. They're all done in a context where you know you're doing a reasoning exercise, the problem type is sort of telegraphed by the setup, and the correct answer is well-defined and waiting. The worry: this is the kata critique all over again. Someone can get great at calibration games and base-rate homework while it never touches how they actually update about their relationships, their finances, or their politics—the messy, high-stakes, self-serving cases we actually care about.

So my question: is this a real structural parallel or just loose pattern-matching? Spell out the mapping as you see it, and say what would have to be true of both sides for the aliveness critique to carry over to rationality training wholesale. Also say where the mapping breaks. I especially wonder whether the thing that makes aliveness possible in fighting—feedback that punches you in the face in something like real time—has any clean analog in reasoning, given that bad reasoning often doesn't visibly punch you, or punches you months later through confounds. Is that a disanalogy that matters, or just a practical obstacle? Bonus if you can name something the parallel seems to predict about rationality training that is probably false.
