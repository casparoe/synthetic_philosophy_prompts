Hey — I'm a PhD student in philosophy of music, and I've been trying to nail down my own view on musical expressiveness (the "how can sounds be sad" problem) before I write the prospectus. I've ended up with a list of commitments that each feel right to me individually, but I haven't tested them as a set, and I'd rather find out now than in a defense. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) The sadness of a piece is a property we perceive in the music itself — not an emotion the music literally has, and not just an emotion it happens to cause in listeners. A sad passage is sad in something like the way a drooping willow or a basset hound's face looks sad.

(2) But sad music does reliably make me sad — actual felt sadness, or something continuous with it — and I don't think thats just a biographical quirk; any account that says listeners merely recognize sadness without ever feeling it is leaving out half the phenomenon.

(3) The way I reconcile those two is roughly this: when I listen, I don't just detect sad contours in the sound; I hear the music as the expression of an imagined persona — an agent whose unfolding inner states I track and respond to. The felt sadness is empathy with that imagined subject, not contagion from the actual composer or performer.

(4) At the same time, I want to deny that instrumental music represents or refers. A sonata isn't about anything the way a sentence or a picture is; it doesn't have semantic content.

(5) And I think a performer can play something so that it comes out vividly sad while feeling nothing whatsoever; the expressiveness is in the music and the performance, not in the performer's occurant feelings.

(6) Finally, I think the expressiveness is grounded in real resemblance: tempo, contour, register, dynamics, and so on map onto the way the human voice and body actually move and sound in grief or joy, so the hearing isn't merely conventional or learned.

So: does this hang together? Go through these and tell me which pairs are actually in tension, which only look like they're in tension, and how bad each real tension is. Then tell me what the cheapest single revision would be and what it costs. I'd rather you rank the tensions than treat them all as equal, and I'd rather know where the problem is than have you be polite about it.
