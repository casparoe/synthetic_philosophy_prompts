Two people in my reading group got into an arguemnt about hedonism last week and I honestly couldnt referee it. One of them, Dan, was defending the idea that pleasure is the only thing that's good for us; the other, Ruth, kept saying pleasures don't actually have anything in common, so the word 'pleasure' is doing no real work. It went roughly like this.

Dan started with what he called the obvious bit: all pleasures are the same kind of experience at bottom — they all feel good. That felt-goodness is the single thing hedonism points to.

Ruth said that just relocates the problem. A headache feels bad and an orgasm feels good, but the pleasantness of solving a crossword, the pleasantness of a cold drink when you're thirsty, and schadenfreude at a rival's failure don't share a felt quality. Saying 'they all feel good' is just a way of not listing the differences.

Dan replied that pleasantness isn't meant to be an extra ingredient on top of each experience; it's a dimension any experience can have, like intensity or duration. You don't ask what the intensity of a smell and the intensity of a regret have in common — intensity isn't a separate sensation.

Ruth said that was exactly her point. Intensity and duration are formal dimensions, but calling an experience pleasant is evaluating it — saying it's the sort of experience you want to be in — rather than reporting a way it feels. So pleasure marks an attitude toward experiences, not a shared feel. And then hedonism becomes the view that what's good for you is whatever you're glad to be feeling, which is a very different theory.

Dan said she'd changed the subject. The phrase 'sort of experience you want to be in' smuggles in the idea of good; the whole point of hedonism was to explain good in terms of feeling. If pleasantness were nothing but being wanted, a person with a destructive urge would make an experience good just by wanting it, which is absurd.

Ruth said he was now admitting heterogeneity. If pleasantness isn't just being wanted and also can't be located in any shared felt quality across relief, sensory delight, and malice, what's left of the single thing he started with? Saying they all feel good has to mean something specific or hedonism's central claim is empty.

Dan's last move was to define pleasure by its functional role: it's the sort of state that, other things equal, we tend to seek and want to continue, and that tends to reinforce behaviour. That, he said, is one kind of thing, and it's what hedonists have meant all along, roughly.

Ruth's answer was that a functional definition gives up exactly what made hedonism attractive — the idea that there's a felt answer to the question what is good. If pleasure is whatever plays a reinforcement role, hedonism has become a placeholder for whatever the brain happens to reward, and it's not obvious that's intrinsically good at all.

I'd like a referee's report. Could you go through the moves in order and say who's right about which point, where each of them overreaches, and whether Dan's final functional move rescues the view or quietly changes it? And could you write your answer in British English, please?
