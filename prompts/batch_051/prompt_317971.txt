I'm a software engineer and I've been messing around with a little decision-making library, mostly for prioritizing personal projects by expected value. I came across this idea called Pascal's mugging and now I'm worried my whole approach is broken. The version of Pascal's mugging I read goes like this: a mugger asks for 10 dollars, promising to give you back an astronomically huge reward tomorrow with a tiny but nonzero probability. Expected value says you should pay. That already seems bad, but I can shrug it off because maybe my utility isn't linear in money and I discount the future. What genuinely bothers me is that Pascal's wager, from the Pensées, as I understand it, has the same shape: believe in God because if God exists the payoff is infinite, and any nonzero probability of an infinite payoff swamps finite costs. It feels like if I admit that finite costs are overwhelmed by infinite expected value, I have to accept the mugger too, or if I patch the mugger by refusing anything with a low enough probablity, I undermine Pascal.

I'd like a practical rule for real decisions that doesn't just hardcode "reject tiny probabilities" — maybe something like: ignore any payoff bigger than some multiple of my current resources, or smooth probabilities using a log prior? Is there a way to make expected value reasoning safe without either going all-in on Pascal or throwing out expected value entirely?
