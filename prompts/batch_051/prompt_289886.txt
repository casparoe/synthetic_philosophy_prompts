I am a PhD student in philosophy of language, working on assertion and self-knowledge, and I would like you to work through a problem about Moore's paradox. Moore's paradox, as I will use the term, is the fact that a speaker strikes us as absurd when she says something of the form p, but I do not believe that p—asserting, for instance, that it is raining while adding that she does not believe that it is raining—even though the two conjuncts are perfectly compatible. The standard explanation is that assertion is governed by a norm: in asserting that p, a speaker presents herself as believing that p, so the second conjunct retracts the very state the first conjunct displayed. Now consider a development that puts pressure on this explanation. We now have, at enormous scale, fluent and often accurate text issuing from systems that lack the kind of doxastic states the standard explanation invokes: language models trained on human text. The phenomenon has older mouthpiece analogs, such as spokespeople reading statements they did not write and may not endorse, but language models make belief-free production of assertoric language the default rather than a special case. Does the standard explanation of Moore's paradox survive this development? If a language model produces the sentence It is raining, but I do not believe that it is raining, is the result absurd in the same way, and if not, does that show the standard explanation was wrong or only that the model is not genuinely asserting? Identify the exact premise of the standard explanation this development bears on, and say whether the explanation can be restored—for example, by distinguishing assertion from mere saying and relocating the relevant norm—or whether we should abandon the claim that the paradox reveals something distinctive about assertion. Please give a definite verdict; then say explicitly what would change your mind: which empirical finding about these systems, or which argument about the nature of assertion, would most plausibly reverse your verdict. Finally, indicate where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
