I've been trying to apply the hedonistic imperative—the project of treating suffering as a biological engineering problem and eventually abolishing it, not just managing it—to the US opioid crisis, and I keep going back and forth on how much the episode damages the view. I know the standard history: in the 1990s American medicine was pushed to treat pain much more aggressively, pain got treated as a routine vital sign, Purdue Pharma marketed OxyContin as a long-acting opioid with low addiction risk, prescribers were told they'd been undertreating pain, and the result was a wave of addiction and overdose deaths, followed by a backlash that left lots of chronic pain patients undertreated again. I don't need a retelling of that history; its the analysis I want.

What would the hedonistic imperative actually have told the different actors—doctors, regulators, patients, pharma—to do in that situation? Which parts of the episode does it explain or justify, and which parts does it get wrong, miss, or actively obscure, especially addiction, tolerance, diversion, and the fact that the market was expanded far beyond end-of-life or severe chronic pain? Does the opioid crisis show that the imperative is wrong, that it was misapplied, or that it was never really built to handle cases where reducing one kind of suffering creates more of another? And running the other direction: what does the episode reveal about the assumption that hedonic state can be optimized in isolation from the social and psychological conditions that produce pain and addiction? I'm especially interested in whether the imperative can tell a good biotech intervention from a bad one without quietly importing values about autonomy, social justice, or naturalness that it claims to have demoted.
