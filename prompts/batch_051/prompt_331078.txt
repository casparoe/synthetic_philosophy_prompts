I direct a small high-risk seed-grant program at a private science foundation. Twice a year my screening committee reviews roughly thirty proposals that explicitly set out to overturn a consensus theory, method, or taxonomy rather than extend it. The committee members are competent scientists but rarely specialists in the field at issue, and they can spend no more than twenty minutes per proposal. Most of these proposals are almost certainly wrong, a few are probably right too early, and nearly all would be rejected by ordinary peer review as too risky. I need a reusable decision procedure, in numbered steps, for triaging such heterodox proposals. Ground it in the dispute between Popper and Kuhn over whether science can be rational during a revolution. Use Popper to build operational tests: whether a core claim is falsifiable, whether the proposers specify what observation would count against it, whether they offer a severe rather than perfunctory test, and whether the proposal protects itself with ad hoc qualifications. Use Kuhn to mark the limits of those tests: distinguish normal-science puzzle-solving from anomaly-driven revolutionary work, check whether the proposal engages the strongest current account rather than a strawman, and recognize that when the standards of a field are themselves at stake no neutral algorithm can certify in advance that a new paradigm will solve the puzzles it promises to solve. For each step, say what counts as a passing answer, where the procedure returns a clear no, where it returns a provisional fund or sends the proposal to adversarial review, and where it must hand the decision to a specialist or mark it as an irreducible judgment. State honestly where the procedure will misfire, and note where the Popperian and Kuhnian parts of the procedure can conflict and how the procedure handles that conflict.

Then run the procedure on these three proposals. First, a cheap but physically implausible cold-fusion variant whose team commits in advance to a preregistered calorimetry protocol and an explicit failure criterion. Second, a multi-laboratory preregistered psychology project predicting that several canonical effects in its subfield will fail to replicate, a proposal that mainstream reviewers describe as already settled against it. Third, a proposal from a prominent scientist for a new framework that names no observation that would falsify it, declines to engage the strongest current account, and promises that its puzzle-solving power will become visible only after the field converts. Make sure at least one case yields a decision the program director would not have chosen on prior commitments, and identify which case that is.
