For a paper I am drafting on moral luck, I keep returning to a passage in Adam Smith’s The Theory of Moral Sentiments, Part II, Section I, Chapter I. He writes:

“But it is quite otherwise with resentment: if the person who had done us some great injury, who had murdered our father or our brother, for example, should soon afterwards die of a fever, or even be brought to the scaffold upon account of some other crime, though it might sooth our hatred, it would not fully gratify our resentment. Resentment would prompt us to desire, not only that he should be punished, but that he should be punished by our means, and upon account of that particular injury which he had done to us. Resentment cannot be fully gratified, unless the offender is not only made to grieve in his turn, but to grieve for that particular wrong which we have suffered from him.”

I want to put this passage to work in an argument whose conclusion I have not seen stated sharply enough: that the retributive sentiments themselves have an irreducibly outcome-dependent structure, so a purely intention-based account of blame would omit something real from ordinary moral psychology, even if it may still be the soundest rule for the law. Could you argue for that position as strongly as you can? Please distinguish carefully between the claim that a completed murder deserves more punishment than an attempt and the claim that it is rationally fitting to feel more resentment toward the successful murderer. Then, because I worry the position merely dignifies an irrational instinct, please close by testing it against the following case: two agents fire with identical intention, skill, and malice; one victim is pulled out of the bullet’s path by a sudden cramp; the other is not. If Smith’s psychology is to function as an argument rather than as mere description, what should be said to the thought that the unsuccessful agent’s resentment could in principle be no less fully gratified than the successful agent’s? Please write your answer in British English.
