i'm a high school junior giving a talk next week to my philosophy club, and I want to be ready for the Q&A, not just give the talk. My thesis is about Seneca's *De Constantia Sapientis* (*On the Firmness of the Wise Man*). The passage I'm working from is near the beginning, where Seneca says the wise man can't be injured or insulted because injury requires damaging the person's good, and the wise person's whole good is internal. So beatings, exile, poverty, losing reputation, having your property taken, whatever: they can't touch your actual good, so they can't injure you. I'm defending a modern version of that: being genuinely uncorruptible means your good isn't the kind of thing the outside world can seize or buy, and Seneca's point is that this is actually possible, not just a nice aspiration. I'm arguing this is a helpful line for thinking about integrity: an uncorruptible person isn't someone with superhuman willpower who resists big bribes; it's someone whose deepest source of good is structured so that bribes and threats stop being *relevant* in the first place.

The audience: it's a school philosophy club with maybe six students and one faculty advisor. One kid is basically an incipient Objectivist and will think I'm saying people should be indifferent to their own lives and property, which she thinks is anti-life. Another kid is a Roman history nerd who knows Seneca was rich and politically compromised, and he'll go straight for hypocrisy or ideology. Two or three are debate kids who will just attack the argument's premises and definitions without caring much about Stoicism. The advisor is a gentle Socratic type who asks questions that seem soft but expose the weak joints.

Give me a prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from this crowd, in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way these specific people would phrase them. For each one: a two-minute reply, what I should concede, and where my reply is soft. Also include the question I should be hoping nobody asks, because I don't have a good answer for it yet. And if one of the debate kids presses me on whether Seneca's definition of injury isn't just rigged so the conclusion falls out, tell me what my worst possible answer would be and how to avoid it.
