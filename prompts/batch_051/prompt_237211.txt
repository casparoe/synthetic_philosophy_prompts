I write about science and ideas for a general but serious readership, and I am preparing a long reported essay on causal discovery algorithms: the methods that try to learn causal structure from purely observational data by supposing that the data are generated by a Bayes net. The standard story about why these methods work leans heavily on the faithfulness assumption — roughly, the claim that the conditional independence relations present in the observed distribution are exactly those entailed by the true causal graph. I have grown skeptical of that story. The usual justification is that unfaithful parameter choices are rare, but the measure-zero argument has always struck me as a mathematical convenience rather than a reason to expect nature to comply, and the cases in which faithfulness fails — exact cancellation between two causal pathways, deterministic mechanisms that introduce additional independences, and parameter values that satisfy extra independence constraints by accident rather than by structure — do not look like mere pathology. I am increasingly convinced that the real work is done by minimality instead: the assumption that the true graph contains no edges beyond those required by the Markov condition.

Please argue for that position. Specifically, argue that minimality, not faithfulness, is what explains why Bayes nets succeed at tracking causation, and that the practical success of causal discovery has been overinterpreted as evidence for faithfulness. As part of the argument, construct a concrete causal structure in which the same distribution is represented by a minimal Bayes net yet is unfaithful to that graph, and explain what a search procedure that assumes faithfulness would get wrong about it. Please also confront the obvious challenge: if minimality alone leaves too much orientation underdetermined, then a defense of minimality needs to say something about where the additional orienting information comes from, rather than simply helping itself to it.
