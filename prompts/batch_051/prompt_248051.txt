I'm a backend engineer and I never took a philosophy class, so bear with me if my vocabulary is off. Lately I've been chewing on the Church-Turing thesis because it keeps showing up in arguments at work about what software "can't" do: someone will claim a problem is impossible and by that they just mean no Turing machine can solve it, and that settles the design discussion. The position I've ended up holding is something like this: any procedue I'd actually call mechanical or effective can be carried out by a Turing machine, and — the stronger part — no physical device in our universe will ever compute a non-Turing-computable function, quantum computers included; those only change the time and resource profile, not the class of functions. Efficiency is a separate argument and I don't want it snuck in.

What I want is a map of what would count against that position. Treat the mathematical version (about what should count as an effective procedure) seperate from the physical version (about what real hardware can do), since I half-suspect the mathematical one isn't the kind of claim that can be refuted at all and the physical one is where the action is. For the physical version, give me a ranked list of threats: which observations, arguments, formal results, or gadgets would be decisive, which would just be awkward, and which a committed defender like me would actually concede rather than shrug off. Also dig into the escape routes — oracles, unbounded precision, a supposed halting-problem solver that turns out to be a modeling error or a hidden lookup table — and say which of those are live possibilities rather than merely logical ones. I'd also like an honest verdict on whether the physical thesis has been insulated by qualifications, and whether that insulation is a problem or just the position doing its job. Personally, the thing I'd find most persuasive is a working tabletop box that decided the halting problem for nontrivial programs, though my first move would be to open it up and look for a hidden conventional machine inside.

End by saying how confident you are in the overall picture and which single point you're least sure of. American English, please.
