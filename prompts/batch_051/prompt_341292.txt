I've been using a rule in arguments about racism for a few years now, and I honestly can't tell whether it's a principle or just a way of never being made uncomfortable. The rule is: I wont call a statement racist on effects alone. For me to call it racist, I need something in the words themselves or in the speaker's broader record that shows they either meant the harm or knew it was the likely result and went ahead anyway. I extend the same rule to dog-whistle claims: if someone says "that was a dog whistle", I refuse to accept it unless they can point to either an explicit marker or a repeated pattern from that speaker that the supposed target audience would plausibly pick up on; otherwise I treat the claim as speculation about hidden mental states.

The thing that's brought this to a head is a colleague who described a new client, a Black woman, as "so articulate." A friend said it was a textbook dog whistle, and I said under my rule I can't call it racist because I can't point to intent or a pattern; at worst it's patronising, maybe, but that might be about age or class rather than race. My friend says my rule is tailored to never have to credit the person on the receiving end, because effects are much easier to show than intent, and dog whistles are basically optimised to pass the test I've set them. He has a point. But I don't see what the alternative is: if I drop the intent requirement, a clumsy compliment and a deliberate slur get the same label, and if I accept dog-whistle accusations on testimony alone, then I'm agreeing to inferences I can't inspect at all. I'm not asking whether the colleague or my friend is right; I'm asking about the policy itself. When is it reliable, when does it fail, and is there a version that keeps the good parts without the blind spots?
