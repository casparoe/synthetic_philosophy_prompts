I've got a friend Mara with a well-documented track record on whats actually a failure of respect—not ordinary rudeness or hurt feelings, but the kind of regard that's owed to someone. We argue constantly about whether respect is mostly an attitude or mostly a kind of treatment, and for years I've been writing down her verdicts on small cases. She's weirdly consistent, but I can't state the rule. Here are the notes:

- A night nurse finds a long-stay patient repulsive, boring, and a drain on the ward, and privately wishes he would be transferred. Her care is flawless: she answers every call light, uses his name, explains every step, and the contempt never leaks, even in tone. Mara says no failure of respect—you can't owe someone a feeling you don't have.
- A project lead invites the newest team member to "push back" on a design, then listens and nods for twenty minutes. But the decision was final before the meeting, and no objection could be folded in. Mara says failure: the invitation was a performance, and the kid's say-so never actually counted.
- A colleague uses a trans co-worker's name and pronouns flawlessly for years, even in private, but by her own account has never once updated how she thinks of him; she's just avoiding awkwardness. Mara says failure.
- A daughter calls her mother every day, visits every Sunday, and handles her medication, all from pure obligation—she doesn't especially like her mother and would rather not be there. Mara says no failure: showing up is owed, warmth isn't.
- A professor writes a strong and entirely accurate letter for a student she privately finds self-important and prickly. Mara says no failure; the letter was true and no claim of the student's was shortchanged.

So the two rules I keep bouncing between are: (i) what's owed is that the person's claims actually count in your practical reasoning and treatment, not that you hold any particular private attitude; and (ii) what's owed includes something in your internal orientation—you have to genuinely recognize the person, not just produce the behavior, at least when their standing rather than merely their likeability is at stake. Both fit most of the notes, so I need a new case.

The new case: a senior surgeon has a resident she privately regards as not really doctor material—her standing belief is that he's there because of family connections and doesn't belong, and a year of his work hasn't moved it. But she trains him attentively, gives him real procedures, and last week he caught an error in her own medication order in front of the team; she stopped, checked, and changed it on the spot. The private file still didn't budge. What does Mara say—has the surgeon failed to respect the resident?

Predict Mara's verdict. Lay out the candidate principles that fit her track record, including any better than my two, say which earlier note is the closest analog and why, and give me the single best future case for distinguishing which rule she's actually running. I know more then one rule fits the notes; I want you to stick your neck out with a best guess about Mara anyway, and then tell me where that read is most likely to break and what would make you switch to the other rule.
