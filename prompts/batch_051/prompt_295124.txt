Okay so here's a position I keep coming back to and I want someone to actually stress-test it rather than just tell me it's smart. It's roughly Ian Hacking's thing about looping effects in psychiatric classification. The claim is that psychiatric kinds are not like natural kinds: an electron doesn't change its behavior because we call it an electron, but a person classified as, say, borderline or depressed learns the category, changes how they describe themselves, how clinicians screen for it, how insurance codes work, and that feeds back into what the category picks out. So the classification is an interactive kind, unstable in a principled way, and the medical-sounding picture of disorders as stable natural kinds waiting to be discovered is the wrong picture. I dont mean the crude social-constructionist version where it's all just made up and nothing is real; I mean the more specific loop version where the category changes its object and thereby changes itself.

What I want is a map of what would actually count against this. Like, what observation, case study, or argument should make me abandon it or seriously weaken it? Rank them from decisive to merely awkward. What would defenders of the looping view themselves admit would be a problem for it, not just what critics say? Is there a plausible psychiatric category that looks like a human kind but doesn't loop, or a case where the loop is predicted but nothing actually changes? Does the view make any predictions that differ from a boring old medical-realist view, or has it been built so that any stability counts as "temporarily settled" and any change counts as "looping"? And if it is insulated that way, is that a problem for this kind of claim or just what a feedback claim looks like?

The thing I'd personally find most persuasive against it: a case where a classification changes its object in the looping way but the category still stays stable and useful, because that would show looping and being a real kind aren't actually opposed. If you know one, tell me.
