Propose a working definition of "bias" as applied to individual decision-making, and test it against the following cases. (1) A person who has just been given a mug refuses to sell it for $10, though before owning it they would have paid at most $5 for one. (2) An investor holds a losing stock for years because selling would make the loss real, even though they would not buy the stock today. (3) A family renews its current health insurance each year because comparing alternatives would take hours, even though a cheaper plan is probably available. (4) A person with little savings declines a one-off gamble offering a 50% chance of +$110 and a 50% chance of −$100, because losing would mean missing rent while winning would only buy a luxury. (5) A negotiator rejects a concession they would have accepted had the identical outcome been framed as a forgone gain rather than a loss. I am confident that (5) is a bias and that (4), given the stakes, is not; I am unsettled about (1)–(3). I suspect the term "bias" runs together a descriptive claim about departure from some normative benchmark and an evaluative claim about poor reasoning; if that diagnosis is right, give two definitions and explain which cases change classification between them, and if not, defend a single definition. Finally, identify which of your classifications is least secure.
