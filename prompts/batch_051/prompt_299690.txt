I am a retired experimental physicist who has lately taken up philosophy, and I keep returning to a pattern that feels both familiar from ordinary life and more troubling than I can yet articulate. I would be grateful for a rigorous treatment of it.

Suppose someone lives with a persistent discomfort that can be adjusted in very fine steps, from wholly manageable at one end to genuinely terrible at the other. Offered a large sum of money to move, in one act, from the manageable end to the terrible end, she refuses, and no reasonable observer thinks her refusal was a mistake. Now offer her a long sequence of smaller payments instead: each payment requires her to advance exactly one step along the scale, and each step is small enough that, taken by itself, it makes no difference she can reliably notice, while the payment attached to it seems plainly worth accepting. She accepts every step with full knowledge of the entire schedule in advance and eventually finds herself in precisely the condition she refused at the outset, for the same total sum of money. She and those around her regard each individual acceptance as unproblematic, yet many of the same people also regard the final position as one she should never have allowed herself to reach.

The asymmetry I want to understand is between the single leap, which almost everyone refuses, and the stepwise route, which almost everyone accepts at each point. Is there a defensible principle that justifies treating a long chain of individually acceptable steps differently from accepting the endpoint outright? If there is, it must survive the case in which the steps are made arbitrarily small, she knows the whole schedule, and she is in every other respect a competent chooser. If there is not, then one of the two ordinary judgments has to be given up, and I would like to know which one should go and what a coherent alternative rule for choice would look like.
