I've been turning over an argument that came up in my philosophy of science seminar and I cant find the exit. It looks like it proves too much, but each step on its own seems fine:

(1) If a system is deterministic, its state at any later time is entailed by its present state plus the laws.
(2) If a future state is entailed by the present state and the laws, then a Laplacean demon with the exact present state, the laws, and unlimited computational power could in principle determine that future state; nothing about the system itself blocks it.
(3) The systems chaos theory deals with—the Lorenz attractor, the double pendulum, turbulent fluids—are deterministic in this sense.
(4) So no chaotic system is unpredictable in principle; its unpredictability comes entirely from our finite precision and limited computing power.
(5) But then the butterfly effect isn't a feature of the world at all. It's a fact about our epistemic situation, like not being able to do long division in your head.
(6) That seems to prove too much, because strange attractors and positive Lyapunov exponents are supposed to be facts about the dynamics, not about the observer.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I'd rather keep (1) and (3); I suspect (2) is where the trouble is, but if so I want to know exactly which notion of "in principle" or "determine" I'm surrendering.
