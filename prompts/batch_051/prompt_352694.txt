I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy, and at our reading group last week two of my cohort got into a long argument about Sidgwick's dualism of practical reason that none of the rest of us could referee. I took rough notes; I'll do my best to reproduce there exchange. I genuinely couldn't tell who was winning at any point.

Dana started by saying the dualism is the deepest unresolved problem in ethical theory. Roughly: we have two apparently rational methods, egoism — make your own good as large as possible — and utilitarianism — make the general good as large as possible. Most of the time they can be made to coincide, but in the hard cases, like a lucrative job versus an effective-altruist path, they issue flatly contradictory instructions. And there is no higher principle to rank them, since any higher principle would just reintroduce one of the two standpoints. So practical reason turns out to be divided against itself, and ethics can't be shown to be rational exactly where people actually struggle.

Ravi answered that Dana was treating egoism and utilitarianism as though they were on the same level. They aren't. Egoism only generates reasons for you if you already care about your own good; utilitarianism is grounded in impartiality — the idea that nobody's good matters more just because its yours. So there aren't two dictates of a single faculty; there are two different standpoints, and it's no surprise you can't merge them into one verdict.

Dana came back that this just relabels the conflict instead of dissolving it. The agent deciding about the job or the donation is still asking one question — what should I do, all things considered — and being told "from the standpoint of prudence do one thing, from the standpoint of morality do another" is not an answer to that question. Also, nothing in Ravi's move shows egoism is irrational; it only shows egoism isn't impartial. So the dualism survives: reason appears to sanction both.

Ravi said Dana was begging the question by treating "what should I do, all things considered" as well-formed. Once you notice that "should" has at least two senses — a prudential ought and a moral ought — the dualism doesn't look like a contradiction anymore. It looks like different questions getting different answers. The contradiction only appears if you insist there was a single undifferentiated ought waiting to be discovered.

Dana replied that the two-senses move doesn't help the actual agent, who doesn't get to live in one sense at a time: she has to perform a single action, and both oughts bear on that same action. And anyway, Sidgwick's whole procedure was to see whether one method could be subordinated to the other, and he found he couldn't do it without assuming the very thing he was trying to show. So calling the conflict "two senses" is a verbal fix for a real practical split.

Ravi said then the burden is on Dana to say what a common measure between my good and the general good would even look like. Any principle that ranked them would presumably go impartial — and collapse back into utilitarianism — or go partial — and collapse back into egoism. So the demand for an all-things-considered verdict is the culprit. The honest conclusion, he said, is that the dualism is an artifact of an over-demanding picture of practical reason, one that assumes reason must issue a unique verdict in every case and then declares a contradiction when it can't.

Dana said that's just accepting the dualism and renaming it. If reason goes silent precisely in conflicts between my interest and other people's, that isn't a refutation of Sidgwick — it's his conclusion, dressed up as a theory about why we shouldn't have expected an answer. The project was supposed to show that ethics is a rational enterprise; a "limit" that strikes exactly where self-interest and duty part ways is the problem, whatever you call it.

At that point we broke for coffee and never reached a resolution. Could you adjudicate their exchange move by move — who is right about which specific points, where each overreaches, and who is more right overall? And then go one step further: tell me what would change your mind about the verdict — which argument or consideration, if it had gone differently or been pressed harder, would most likely flip or weaken your conclusion, and where do you think your own reasoning is most likely to fail?
