So I help run a small community garden network, and last year a county planner warned us that if our area keeps growing the way it is, we'd hit a food access crisis in maybe ten years. We expanded plots, started a little co-op, but now some neighbors say he was wrong because it hasn't happened yet—actually one of our markets has had surplus every week this summer. My brother says this is exactly like Malthus. I had heard the name before but never read him, so I found An Essay on the Principle of Population, and in chapter one Malthus says:

"It is an acknowledged truth in philosophy, that a just theory will always be confirmed by experiment. Yet so much friction, and so many minute circumstances occur in practice, which it is next to impossible for the most enlarged and penetrating mind to foresee, that on few subjects can any theory be pronounced just, that has not stood the test of experience."

What I don't get is this. If Malthus's own test-of-experience rule says a theory can't be pronounced just until it has stood the test of experience, then doesn't that mean the people who say he was refuted are basically right? My brother says no, Malthus was just early, and the underlying idea was still basically sound even if his timing was off. I don't want to be the guy who keeps moveing the goalposts whenever a prediction doesn't pan out, but I also don't want to overcorrect and throw out a useful warning just because the calendar doesn't match. How do I tell the difference between a forecast that's genuinely been shown wrong and one that's just on a slower clock than the forecaster thought? And should I think about the county planner's warning the same way or differently?
