You are a professional philosopher and institutional designer who specializes in epistemic incentives: you are equally at home in argumentation theory, the design of scoring and rating systems, and the sociology of competitive communities. Think carefully and work through the problem systematically; begin by brainstorming the incentives and pressure points in the rules below before you reach any overall verdict.

I am developing a proposal for a spectator league for competitive argumentation, and before I commit resources to it I want a rigorous analysis of how the rules would actually function, not whether they are praiseworthy in the abstract.

Here is the proposed institution. Two arguers face off on a live contested proposition: a policy claim, an empirical forecast, or a philosophical thesis. Assignments are made by lot shortly before the match, so competitors cannot always argue for the positions they hold. Before seeing their opponent, each competitor must seal three things: a numerical credence in the proposition between 0 and 1; a list of the three strongest considerations for their assigned side; and the single strongest consideration for the opposing side.

Each match has four phases: a short opening statement; cross-examination; a mandatory steelman phase, in which each side must present the other side's case until the opponent certifies that the restatement is fair; and a closing in which no new claims may be introduced. During the match, either side may concede a point at any time, and each side states a final credence in the closing.

Scoring runs on two tracks. A technical panel scores argumentative craft on validity, relevance, burden-of-proof management, and fidelity to the opponent's actual position. A progress panel scores epistemic movement, computed as follows. First, convergence: both sides are rewarded for reducing the gap between the two initial credences, as measured from the sealed pre-registrations to the public final credences. Second, truth-tracking: after the match, an expert panel assigns the best-supported position on the proposition given the public evidence; each side gains points for having moved toward that position relative to their sealed initial credence, and loses points for having moved away. Third, a pair bonus: if both sides independently identify the same sub-question as the crux of their disagreement, the pair earns a combined bonus, even if their credences do not converge.

Competitors carry a public, Elo-like rating fed by their match scores, and the season prize pool is distributed in proportion to final rating. Finally, any judge or competitor may flag a rules violation — strawmanning, refusing to answer a direct question, introducing new claims in the closing, or misrepresenting the record of the match — and sustained flags deduct rating points.

Walk me through, in concrete detail, how this institution would behave.

Start with adaptation: what kinds of competitors would rise to the top of the ratings, and what behaviors would the rules select for? Then identify the earliest and most damaging ways the system would be gamed, paying special attention to how the sealed pre-registrations, the convergence and truth-tracking formulas, the pair bonus, and the expert panel's post-match adjudication would each be exploited. Then trace the second-order effects: what topics would get selected, what style of argument would be rewarded, what norms would develop among regulars, and what would happen to the meaning of winning in this community. Where the system could go in either of two directions, specify the condition that selects one path over the other.

For each major failure mode you identify, say whether it is fixable by a targeted rule change or whether it is a symptom of making argument competitive at all.

Conclude by addressing the underlying question directly: if this league became popular, through which specific mechanisms would it improve or degrade public reasoning, and what evidence about its early seasons would tell you which way it was going?
