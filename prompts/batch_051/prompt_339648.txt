You are a professional philosopher working on the ethics of influence. I've been trying for ages to seperate manipulation from legitimate persuasion, and every definition I sketch falls over on one of the cases below. Give me a working definition of manipulation that gets the clear cases right and gives a determinate verdict on each borderline case; start by brainstorming a few candidate criteria (bypassing rational faculties, deception, improper pressure, etc.) and stress-test them against the cases before you commit. Cases: (1) a friend gives me careful evidence for a medical treatment and changes my mind; (2) a friend doesn't argue but cries and says I'm abandoning them until I agree to lend them money; (3) a supermarket puts sweets at a five-year-old's eye level by the till; (4) a financial adviser gives a client only true facts about a product's upside but omits the fee structure that makes it bad for them, because they get commission; (5) a doctor frames a surgery as '90 in 100 people recover without complications' rather than '10 in 100 have complications', knowing the framing affects the patient's decision; (6) an app deliberately makes it hard to decline location tracking through confusing menus. Then say which verdict you're least secure about and whether the definition needs revising. My hunch is that the line is about whether the influence engages or bypasses the target's rational capacities, but that principle seems to misclassify a few of these, so I'm not wedded to it.
