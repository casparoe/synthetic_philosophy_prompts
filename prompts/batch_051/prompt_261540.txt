I am writing an essay on what an AI system could mean when it says it wants to act on a person's real preferences, and I keep going back and forth on whether the usual idea of idealization actually picks out one determinate target. My understanding is that the standard proposal is something like this: instead of optimizing for the preferences a person happens to have right now, an AI should optimize for what that person would want after two kinds of improvement — being given much better factual information about the world, and having their preferences made internally more coherent and more stable under reflection. This is roughly the idea people call coherent extrapolated volition.

Here is the claim I want a probability for, and please give me an actual number between 0 and 1:

Claim: For an ordinary adult, the two routes to idealization converge. That is, if you first correct their relevant false factual beliefs and then apply a process of making their preferences coherent and reflectively stable, you end up with roughly the same preference ordering and roughly the same guidance about what actions to take on their behalf as if you had first made their preferences coherent and then given them the corrected information — the order of idealizing steps does not matter in any way that would matter in practice.

If you think the claim as stated is too vague to have a truth value, state in one sentence how you are operationalizing "roughly the same" and "matter in practice," and put your probability on that operationalized version.

I do not just want a number; I want the number decomposed into the sub-questions it depends on. Please assign a probability to each of at least the following:

1. That correcting a person's factual beliefs mostly resolves their preference conflicts rather than creating new ones — that is, that information tends to shrink the set of internally coherent preference systems available to them.
2. That the process of making a person's preferences coherent and reflectively stable has a roughly unique endpoint from a given starting point, rather than many different coherent systems they could plausibly settle into.
3. That ordinary adults start close enough to such an endpoint that the two orders have little room to diverge.
4. That any residual divergence between the two orders would make little difference to decisions an AI would actually face.

For each term, give one number and a one- or two-sentence justification, and then show explicitly how you combined them — not necessarily by simple multiplication — to reach your overall probability. Then tell me which of these sub-questions your overall number is most sensitive to, and what kind of evidence, such as psychological studies or formal models, would shift that term the most, and in which direction.

Finally, please keep clearly separated what is actually settled in the philosophical discussion of idealized preferences from what is genuinely contested among people who study this. If there is live disagreement on one of the terms, do not present the view you favor as if it were the consensus; tell me that it is contested.
