I'm in a law-and-AI seminar and we keep going around in circles about whether corporate personhood is a good model for giving AI legal standing, so I wrote up a fact pattern and want to see how you'd work it through. The general incorporation statute in my state (last amended in 1958) says:

"A corporation created under this chapter is an artificial person, with full capacity to sue and be sued, to hold property in its own name, and to exercise the powers of a natural person so far as an artificial being may do so. The business and affairs of the corporation shall be managed by a board of one or more directors, and for the purposes of this chapter the word 'person' shall include a corporation of this state or of any other jurisdiction, unless the context otherwise requires."

Here's the situation. My cousin incorporated a small consulting firm, and after his cofounder quit he amended the bylaws so the board has one director: a shell corporation he set up with no assets and no employees. The shell's sole shareholder and sole officer is an AI agent he trained on the firms client files and hooked up to its banking and e-signature software. So the AI runs the shell, the shell is the director, and through that arrangement the AI decides which clients to take, signs the contracts, votes at shareholder meetings, and files the annual report. The Secretary of State is trying to dissolve the firm on the ground that it has no lawful board; the state says the shell is just a mask and the AI is the real director, and anyway the drafters in 1958 never imagined a nonhuman managing a corporation. Apply the section to these facts. What does the text require, what does the purpose of the artificial-person provision suggest, and what would the drafters have said if you'd asked them? Then tell me which approach should control here, and whether the corporate-person route can really get an AI to legal personhood or whether it just makes the word "person" do too much work.
