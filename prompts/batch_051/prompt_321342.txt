In rationalist forums, the argument over Hofstadter's superrationality keeps resurfacing and never actually resolves. The standard setup is a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma between two players with common knowledge of each other's rationality and symmetry, or a Newcomb-like problem in which the predictor has run something close to your own decision procedure. One side says that, no matter what the other player does, defection yields strictly more, so defection is mandatory; cooperating amounts to the fantasy that your choice reaches across space and changes the other player's move. The other side says that your own choice is evidence about what the symmetrically reasoning other player will do, so cooperation maximizes expected utility; ignoring that evidence is exactly the mistake they think causal decision theory makes in Newcomb's problem. The defector's rejoinder is that this treats one's own decision as an observation rather than an intervention, a confusion about the direction of fit. Both sides accept the same payoff matrix, the same facts about causality, and the same physical situation, and still nobody moves. I am not asking for another argument for one camp. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Is this a factual disagreement in disguise, for example about the correct counterfactual or about whether conditioning on a correlate of one's choice is legitimate during deliberation? Is it a verbal disagreement about the word 'rational', where one side means choosing the act with the best causal consequences in the actual world and the other means choosing the decision procedure a rational agent should want to be running? Are the camps answering different questions, one about token acts and the other about algorithms or source code, under a single heading? My working hypothesis is that the impasse comes down to how actions are individuated, and that once you fix whether you are choosing an act or a decision procedure the two sides mostly dissolve into different problems; but I am unsure whether that diagnosis is right or merely restates one camp's position in more charitable language. Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a formal result in program-equilibrium cooperation, an operational criterion for when two reasoners are correlated enough for superrational reasoning to apply, a decision problem that cleanly separates the camps' predictions, or an argument that the question as posed is ill-posed and should be abandoned?
