I'm working through the question of when Latin becomes Italian, and the main obstacle I keep hitting is that I don't have a criterion for when two ways of speaking count as the same language, whether across time or across space; my confident ordinary judgments outrun any definition I can state. I'd like a working definition of "the same language" — or, if you think that phrase covers too many different notions, a definition of the specific diachronic notion I need: what it is for one stage of a linguistic tradition to be a later stage of the same language rather than a successor language.

Here are the cases I want the definition to handle. I'm confident that the ordinary conversational Italian of Milan and the ordinary conversational Italian of Palermo are the same language, despite accent and vocabulary differences; and I'm equally confident that Cicero's Latin and a modern Italian newspaper article are not the same language, even though one descends from the other through an unbroken chain of speakers. The cases I can't confidently classify are these: the spoken Romance of Italy around 800 CE and the vernacular Dante wrote in five centuries later; Serbian and Croatian, which are highly mutually intelligible but treated as distinct languages for political and identitarian reasons; Old English and Modern English, which are conventionally called stages of one language despite being mutually unintelligible; and an imagined case in which every generation in an isolated village fully understands its parents and its children, yet the first generation could not understand a recording of the thousand-years-later generation at all. I'd also like the definition to produce a definite answer either way for Mandarin and Cantonese.

Please propose a definition, run it against each case, and say explicitly where it gives the wrong answer or no answer; then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. As you do this, I'd ask you to separate what is settled descriptive linguistics from what is a genuinely contested conceptual or stipulative choice — for example, mutual intelligibility, unbroken intergenerational transmission, structural distance, and political self-identification all draw different boundaries, and I don't want any one of them presented as the field's agreed-upon criterion.
