I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and after a month of sorting pitches I've realised I have no principled way to decide whether a historical-sounding claim is pseudohistory or just heterodox history. The claims that keep coming across my desk are things like Atlantis taken as literal, ancient-astronaut material (the 'gods were actually visitors with technology' pattern), and Fomenko's new chronology, where all of ancient and medieval history supposedly happened a few hundred years ago and was fabricated by a conspiracy. Each time I reach for a different ad hoc test — 'no peer review', 'too convenient', 'the author is obviously ideological' — and I've started to think that's a sign the whole idea needs a proper specification before I write the piece I'm planning on how to think about these claims.

So here's the assignment. Write the desiderata that an adequate theory — or a working criterion — of pseudohistory has to meet, for a working journalist's purposes. I want a requirements list, with the case or consideration behind each requirement and an explanation of why it's a requirement rather than a stylistic preference. Make sure the list can handle at least: a claim that's false but produced by ordinary historical method; a claim that's true but arrived at by crankish method; claims that borrow academic vocabulary and statistics (Fomenko again); self-sealing programmes where every missing record is explained as suppression; and non-literate source bases such as oral traditions, which must not get auto-classified as pseudohistory just because they don't look like an archive. Include the distinctions the criterion can't collapse — pseudohistory vs bad history vs fraud vs ideology, being wrong vs being unfalsifiable vs being immune to evidence, and whether pseudohistory is a property of a claim, a method, or a research programme. And list any results from historiography or philosophy of science it can't contradict: for instance, all history is interpretation-laden, so a criterion can't demand a raw stage of theory-free facts before interpretation starts.

Then mark which requirements are negotiable and which aren't, and briefly score the usual candidate criteria against the list — falsifiability, deference to expert consensus, absence of evidence, authorial motivation. Most of all, tell me which single requirement knocks out the most popular criteria and why. Please write the answer in British English, and no bibliography or citations — I need the reasoning, not a reading list. And don't seperate the requirements into a table unless it genuinely earns its keep; the thinking matters more than the format.
