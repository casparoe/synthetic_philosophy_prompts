I spent most of yesterday evening in a long argument with a friend about whether a well-designed system of pictures could do the work that written language does, at least for some kinds of public information. My friend kept returning to Otto Neurath's version of this idea. Neurath, a Vienna Circle philosopher, spent decades developing a system of standardized pictorial symbols, usually called ISOTYPE, that was meant to present social and economic facts about housing, unemployment, disease, and production in a form anyone could read regardless of mother tongue. His argument, as my friend reconstructed it, was that a written word refers by arbitrary convention, while a picture can show its object's visible features directly, so a carefully standardized visual symbol system ought to cross language barriers without translation; and because the symbols can be combined in ordered layouts, the system can do at least part of what the grammar of a sentence does, stating something rather than merely illustrating it.

Where I kept pushing back was on what we have learned since about how depiction actually works. Perspective rendering is itself a convention that had to be invented and that some viewers misread; what looks like an obvious pictogram for a house, a person, or a hospital varies across cultures and periods; color, direction, and the ordering of elements in a diagram carry different meanings in different visual traditions; and even photography has to be learned as a way of seeing. If that is right, the premise that a picture wears its meaning on its face and needs no cultural key is false except in a narrow range of cases. My friend answered that this only shows a picture language must be learned, like any language, but that learning one standardized symbol system for public information is still far cheaper and less ambiguous than learning many natural languages, so Neurath's conclusion survives with a small concession.

I would like you to settle the question as far as it can be settled. Does Neurath's argument for a picture language survive the convention-ladenness of depiction, or did it depend essentially on the transparency premise? Identify the specific premise the development bears on, say whether the argument is dead, repairable, or largely untouched, and if it is repairable, state the repaired principle and whether it still deserves to be called a picture language rather than a symbol system or notation. Please answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write an email.
