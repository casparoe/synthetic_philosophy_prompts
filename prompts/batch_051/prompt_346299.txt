I am preparing a one-page proposal for a research fellowship in the philosophy of logic, and I want you to write the proposal for me. It should be around 750 words of continuous prose, not counting the passage below. The project will be on Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" (Mind, 1895), and it will turn on the following passage, which I have checked against the text:

"Readers of Euclid will grant, I suppose, that Z follows logically from A and B, so that any one who accepts A and B as true, must accept Z as true?"

"Undoubtedly! The youngest child in a High School—as soon as High Schools are invented, which will not be till some two thousand years later—will grant that."

"And if some reader had not yet accepted A and B as true, he might still accept the sequence as a valid one, I suppose?"

"No doubt such a reader might exist. He might say 'I accept as true the Hypothetical Proposition that, if A and B be true, Z must be true; but, I don't accept A and B as true.' Such a reader would do wisely in abandoning Euclid, and taking to football."

"And might there not also be some reader who would say 'I accept A and B as true, but I don't accept the Hypothetical'?"

"Certainly there might. He, also, had better take to football."

"And neither of these readers," the Tortoise continued, "is as yet under any logical necessity to accept Z as true?"

"Quite so," Achilles assented.

My half-formed idea is that the standard moral drawn from this passage is too quick. The usual diagnosis is that the Tortoise has confused a rule of inference with a premise: once Achilles writes down C, D, and E, he has converted the license for moving from A and B to Z into something the Tortoise can treat as just another assertion, and the infinite regress is an artifact of that confusion. I suspect the passage actually raises a harder problem about applying a rule to a case. Even if the rule "from P and the conditional if P then Q, infer Q" is kept entirely distinct from the premises, using it on A and B still requires recognizing those premises as an instance of the rule. That recognition is not itself a further premise, and no amount of writing in the notebook can make someone who withholds it perform the inference. So the regress seems to return one level up, at the point where a general rule meets a particular step. If that is right, the passage leaves us with an uncomfortable conclusion: deductive reasoning may bottom out in something non-propositional—a capacity, a practice, or an act—that cannot itself be justified by deduction.

Be committed rather than survey-like: argue for a definite view of what the project should be and where it should land. Also give an honest assessment of what would count as failure and what could make the project unmanageable in the fellowship year.
