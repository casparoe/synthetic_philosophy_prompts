I'm a philosophy professor putting together a one-off session for an upper-level undergrad seminar in political philosophy—14 students, 80 minutes—and I want it to be a structured role-play, not a lecture. The topic is the ethics of continuity-of-government planning, specifically the practice of keeping a designated survivor away from a big event like a State of the Union so that somebody is left to govern if the Capitol gets attacked. I want the students to feel the pull of the thought that, under a credible threat, a state can start treating officeholders less like citizens owed equal protection and more like fungible parts of it's own survival—and then have them feel the moral cost of exactly that stance, by having to justify a policy to the particular person it sacrifices. They shouldn't hear the phrase 'designated survivor' or any named ethical position until the debrief.

Set it up so the cabinet has to decide four things: (a) whether to remove anyone from the chamber at all, (b) how to pick who gets left out, (c) what legal standing that person has in the first hours afterward, and (d) whether the public is told about the arrangement. Two rounds. Round 1: behind a veil of ignorance—they don't know which role they'll get, they just write a policy. Round 2: they draw role cards (the person the round-1 rule would exclude; the national security advisor who knows the threat is worse than they've let on; the only person with local knowledge needed for reconstruction; a civil-liberties holdout; the person next in the line of succession; etc.) and re-deliberate under the same constraints. I want the showdown to be whether each person can justify the policy to the person it singles out, rather than to 'the needs of the state.' Include a rule that no one can appeal to the expected number of lives saved or do explicit cost-benefit aggregation until the debrief, so they can't turn it into a maximizing exercise. Also give the person the policy excludes a short veto statement to read.

Then give me the rules, role-card content scaled for 14 people, a rough timeline, and a debrief that maps the moments onto the ideas: the appeal of random selection vs. protecting the most capable person, equal protection vs. treating officeholders as survival assets, whether accepting high office counts as prior consent to this kind of risk, and whether a survivor picked by a fair-ish rule can actually claim democratic authority when everyone above them is gone. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. And be honest about which part of the real thing a classroom can't reproduce—I suspect the dread of being the one left alive, and the constitutional vertigo of having to govern, will have to be supplied by me in words, so say where.
