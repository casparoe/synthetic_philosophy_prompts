You are an expert moral philosopher specialising in distributive ethics and the aggregation of harms across persons. You are being consulted by the secretariat of a regional health authority's surgical priorities panel, which must decide a new case consistently with its own past decisions. Please reason carefully and from first principles: begin by sketching, before you look at the new case, two or three distinct principles that could jointly make sense of the panel's recorded decisions, then apply each to the new case, and be explicit about where the precedents leave the panel underdetermined.

The panel has never adopted a formal decision rule; its commitments are whatever can fairly be reconstructed from three recorded decisions, as follows.

1. Horton case. The panel chose to fund an operation that would restore sight to one patient who would otherwise be permanently blind, rather than a pain-management programme that would spare 800 people a single day of moderate but fully reversible headache. The secretariat's aggregate cost-effectiveness model slightly favoured the pain programme. The panel departed from the model, recording that many temporary and lesser losses cannot, merely by being summed, outweigh one severe and irreversible loss.

2. Antiviral case. The panel had to allocate the last five doses of a scarce antiviral. Five patients needed one dose each to survive; one further patient needed all five doses to survive. The panel gave the doses to the five, recording that where each person's prospective loss is the same — death — the greater number of such losses should count.

3. Limb-salvage case. The panel chose to fund a single bilateral procedure for one patient who would otherwise lose both legs, rather than three unilateral procedures for three patients, each of whom would otherwise lose one leg. It recorded that the loss of both legs is a substantially greater individual harm than the loss of one leg, and that three lesser harms do not outweigh one greater harm even when they are harms of the same broad type.

The pending case mirrors the limb-salvage facts, except in the number of lesser harms. The same budget can fund either option A or option B. Under option A, one patient receives a bilateral procedure; without it he will lose both legs. Under option B, forty patients receive unilateral procedures; without them, each will lose one leg. The patients are otherwise comparable adults with similar expected recoveries, and the only differences the panel treats as salient are the harm averted for each person and the number of persons affected.

The panel is divided. Some members argue that the limb-salvage case is the controlling analogue and settles the new case for option A. Others reply that a refusal to aggregate three lesser harms against one greater harm does not settle whether forty lesser harms may be aggregated; in their view the panel has never decided whether its anti-aggregation stance is absolute or sensitive to how much is being summed.

Write me a rigorous, concise recommendation memorandum that does all of the following:
(a) distinguishes the two or three most plausible principles that make sense of all three recorded decisions;
(b) states what each principle implies for the pending case, including any thresholds or weightings each principle would need to specify;
(c) identifies the closest precedent and states exactly how the pending case resembles and differs from it;
(d) gives your recommended decision and explains what principle the panel would be adopting with each possible choice; and
(e) proposes one further hypothetical case, specified precisely enough to be put to the panel, that would cleanly separate the leading principles if the panel wants to settle the matter for the future.
