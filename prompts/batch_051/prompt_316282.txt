I do data work and I've been chewing on a parallel that might just be me seeing my day job everywhere. When you take a battery of opinion questions—taxes, immigration, abortion, guns, climate, whatever—and clean out the response-style junk, the first principal component usually eats a huge share of the variance and its loadings look suspiciously like left versus right. It makes me want to say: the left-right dimension isn't a real thing out in the world with an essence, it's the first principal component of whatever issue menu a society happens to be contesting. What holds each side together wouldn't be a philosophy or a deep value cluster, it would just be that these positions move together in the data, and the axis is the direction along which people differ the most. That would explain both why the axis feels so stable (it's close to the maximum-variance direction) and why the content of it keeps sliding around as new issues enter the menu.

But I can't tell if this is a deep structural claim or a superficial analogy dressed up in linear algebra. And I'm honestly not sure whether I'm claiming left-right is a principal component or a common factor—PCA just summarizes, whereas factor analysis posits latent variables that help cause the observed answers. So: which is the better model of what the left-right distinction actually is, and how would you tell them apart? If its a principal component, in what sense is it 'real'—real in the data but not in the world—and should that change how seriously we take a person's self-identification as left or right? Where does the analogy break? One spot already bothers me: a component depends on your sample and your item battery; run it on another country or swap in different issues and the axis should wander, but left-right is suspiciously recognizable across countries and decades, which sounds more like a causal factor than a component.
