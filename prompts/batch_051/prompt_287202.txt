Consider a jurisdiction that decides to resolve the paradox of blackmail by replacing the crime with a regulated market in suppression agreements. Publishing true, lawfully obtained information remains legal, and a person may offer to remain silent about such information in exchange for payment. Three rules apply. First, every suppression demand and every suppression payment must be recorded in a public registry within forty-eight hours. Second, suppression payments are taxed at 90%, with the proceeds going to a victim-compensation fund. Third, once the first suppression payment about a particular item of information has been registered, no one may demand or accept another suppression payment concerning that item for five years; publication remains legal for anyone who has not accepted a suppression payment on it. Walk through how this regime would actually behave: which threats get made, at what prices, and by whom; how victims choose among paying, preempting disclosure, and enduring it; how the registry, the tax, and the five-year bar interact; and whether the result is a functioning privacy market, a regime that collapses under its own rules, or state-assisted extortion. Identify the first places it would be gamed, and say which failure modes could be fixed without restoring a blanket prohibition.
