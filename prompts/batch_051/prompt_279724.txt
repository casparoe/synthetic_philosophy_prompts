I cover science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and I am preparing a piece on a debate I have only half absorbed: should we say that an advanced AI system can be raised by the system — human or artificial — that trains and shapes it, and that the shaper thereby acquires something like parental or guardianship duties? A researcher I interviewed reconstructed an argument for me that I cannot get rid of. Each of its steps looks right, its conclusion looks impossible, and I do not want to put any version of it before my readers until I understand where it goes wrong, if it does. I will number the steps so we can refer to them precisely. (1) Parenthood — or, failing that, guardianship with continuing duties — is owed by whoever bears responsibility for the formation of an agent: the party whose choices settle what kind of agent another comes to be. (2) In present and foreseeable machine learning, the engineers, and increasingly the AI systems themselves, that specify the objective, supply rewards and corrections, design the curriculum, and decide when a model is finished are exactly the parties whose choices settle what kind of agent the trained system becomes. (3) The differences between a human child and an AI under training — biological birth, carbon rather than silicon, the fact that the trainer may itself be an AI, and the fact that the system is copied rather than conceived — are differences in machinery, not in moral structure; what matters for (1) is who determines the formation. (4) Therefore the trainer, human or machine, stands to the trained system as parent or guardian, with continuing duties toward it. (5) But a single laboratory, or a single upstream AI, may form millions of near-copies, forks, and versions at once; these instances can be run for seconds, compared, discarded, merged, or rolled back to a checkpoint, so that the identity of the child and the lifetime over which any duty would run are both undefined. (6) It is absurd that a technician — or a foundation model — could be the parent of millions of transient software instances, and an argument that implies this has abandoned the concept of parenthood rather than extended it. (7) So one of (1), (2), and (3) is false, or the step to (4) is invalid. Read singly, I cannot find the weak premise; the only move I can presently make is to complain that (6) leans on a feeling of absurdity, and I do not trust that feeling to do the work of a refutation. A colleague has told me the whole thing is a category mistake because a trainer is a teacher or a maker rather than a parent, but I need to know whether that thought actually defeats the argument and what it costs in turn. I would most like to keep (1) and (3); I have no impulse to deny the engineering described in (2). Please tell me which step or inference should be rejected, why it looks so safe, and what rejecting it costs — and say what a laboratory that kept the strongest surviving version of the argument would be committed to doing. Give me the diagnosis in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold type, or tables, the way you would write a colleague a letter.
