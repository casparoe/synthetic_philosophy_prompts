I've been trying to get clear on something I've been calling the Bounded Inventory Principle for conscious experience, and I'd like an honest appraisal rather than encouragement. The principle starts from two constraints: a mind has finite memory and finite discriminatory resolution, so there are only finitely many total states it can distinguish itself as being in. It then adds a claim about what an experience is: for the subject having it, an experience is exhausted by the way it feels from the inside and by what it can be told apart from. If two episodes are indistinguishable to the subject across every comparison that subject could in principle make, then they are not two experiences but one experience repeated. From these premises it follows that any given finite mind has some finite number N of pairwise subjectively distinct total experiences available to it. A sufficiently long-lived, uploaded, or endlessly reincarnated mind would therefore eventually undergo only exact repeats from the inside; novelty, in the only sense that matters to the subject, would run out. I arrived at this trying to explain to a friend why even a well-furnished digital afterlife feels to me like a very long waiting room, and I'm aware of how close the argument sits to saying something obviously false.

I work as an actuary, so I have no professional stake in the literature, but I've read enough to suspect that this either collapses into an existing position or fails at the first step. My questions: is the inference from "finitely many discriminable states" to "finitely many experiences" valid, or does it illicitly assume that discrimination is transitive and that experience is nothing but discriminative role? Does the principle reduce to something already established — Leibniz's identity of indiscernibles applied to episodes, discrimination-based accounts of qualia, Dennett-style phenomenology-as-judgment, or something else I should know the name for? And where exactly does it break? I would like the three strongest objections, a verdict on whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable, and a statement of whether the salvageable core — that a finite mind has a finite novelty horizon — is true, false, or too vague to be either. Please answer in American English.
