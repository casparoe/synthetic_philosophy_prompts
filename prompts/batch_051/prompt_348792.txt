Over the years, a colleague in political philosophy has given me a series of considered verdicts about the boundary problem: who belongs to the demos, understood here as the set of persons entitled to an equal vote in a polity's elections and referendums. I would like to systematize her view and extend it to a new case before we teach the material together next term.

Her recorded verdicts are:

1. Long-settled immigrants who have lived and worked in the country for many years, pay taxes, and are raising children there: they belong to the demos and should vote, notwithstanding that they are not citizens.
2. Tourists who are in the country for a few weeks and are subject to its criminal, traffic, and municipal law while present: they do not belong to the demos.
3. Citizens who emigrated decades ago and now live permanently abroad, retaining only light legal ties such as a passport and a small annual filing fee: they do not belong to the demos.
4. A farming community just across the border whose crops and water are materially damaged by this country's industrial emissions: they do not belong to this country's demos, however serious their grievance, which she thinks should be handled through their own state or through international arrangements.

The new case is this. A woman lives with her family in a town just across the border, where her children attend school and where her civic and social life is anchored. She is a citizen of the neighboring country. She works five days a week in this country, pays income and payroll taxes here, and is subject daily to its labor, traffic, and workplace-safety law. Should she, on this colleague's view, belong to this country's demos?

Several principles might generate the four verdicts: (i) the demos consists of those subject to the state's system of law; (ii) it consists of those whose lives are durably embedded in the society; (iii) it consists of those whose interests are substantially affected; (iv) it consists simply of the citizenry; or some refinement or combination of these. As I read the record, the verdicts leave the first two as the serious contenders and rule out (iii) and (iv) in their simplest forms, though the tourist case already forces the first to be read as standing subjection rather than momentary subjection to whatever law happens to apply. The commuter case appears to pull the first two apart.

I am not asking what the right answer to the boundary problem is in your view; I am asking what this colleague's considered view most likely is, given the record, and what principle it expresses. Please predict her verdict on the new case and reconstruct the reasoning she would most likely offer. Then say which underlying principle — among those above, a refinement of one, or a combination — best rationalizes all four recorded verdicts, indicate how strongly the record supports that choice over its rivals, and say where your confidence runs out. Finally, propose one further case whose verdict would cleanly settle which principle she is actually applying, and state what the competing principles predict about it.

If, in your judgment, the recorded verdicts underdetermine the principle too badly for any real prediction, or the options I have sketched misdescribe the debate, tell me that plainly instead of forcing an answer to fit. I would rather have the inadequacy of the materials made explicit than receive a confident verdict that rests on an unstated assumption.
