I've been taking minutes for the adverse-event review comitee at a midsize teaching hospital for about a year, and I've started to think the committee is running a consistent same-mechanism standard that I still can't articulate. Their job is a kind of mechanistic anomaly detection: they look at unexpected findings from clinical trials and decide whether two anomalies belong in one safety report, meaning one underlying mechanism, or two separate ones. Sometimes they talk as if the shared trigger is what matters; other times they talk as if the actual biology is what matters. Their past rulings: two patients on the same study drug who developed the same itchy flat rash on the same timeline went down as one mechanism. Two patients on different drugs who both developed low platelets went down as two mechanisms, because one had antibodies against the platelets and the other had bone-marrow suppression. Two patients on the same drug with very different presentations, one had a purplish rash on her legs and the other had blood in his urine, went down as one mechanism after biopsy showed the same small-vessel inflammation in both. And two patients on different drugs who both reported shortness of breath went down as two mechanisms, since one had allergic bronchospasm and the other had fluid overload.

This week's case: two patients in the same trial both became anemic while taking the study drug. Patient one has a Coombs-positive hemolytic anemia, meaning an antibody is chewing up his red cells. Patient two has a normocytic anemia with a bone marrow that shows almost no red cell precursors, meaning the drug seems to have shut down red cell production. They share the same trigger, and on the chart both mostly look like anemia, but the mediating pathway is different. For consistency with its own past decisions, should the committee classify this as one mechanism or two? Which past ruling is the closest analog, and if the precedents genuinely underdetermine it, what principle would the committee be adopting with each answer?
