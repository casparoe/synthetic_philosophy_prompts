For the past year I have been teaching a comparative political theory seminar in a city where the line between a bribe and a gift is fought out at dinner tables rather than settled in textbooks. One of my students recently defended her father, a ward councillor, who pays school fees for unemployed constituents and expects their families' electoral loyalty in return; she was genuinely puzzled that the assigned reading classified this as corruption, since in her accounting it was reciprocity and care, not private enrichment. The exchange stayed with me because she was not morally confused so much as operating with a different picture of what an officeholder owes, and to whom. That experience is now driving how I write the final exam.

I would like you to produce three things for that exam: an essay question, a model answer of roughly 800 words, and a marking scheme for a 25-mark total. The exam is for an upper-level undergraduate course; students will have 75 minutes, and they should not be expected to cite named readings, so the difficulty has to come from the reasoning rather than from recall of the literature. The question should centre on one or two short invented cases — for instance, a politician who steers state-funded scholarships preferentially to loyal supporters, or a civil servant who accepts a costly customary gift — cases that would plausibly be called corruption under the familiar public-office-for-private-gain standard, and equally plausibly be called obligation or generosity under a more particularistic standard. It should force students to take a position on whether standards derived from Western legal-rational bureaucracies can legitimately be applied across different political cultures, and why.

The crux I care about is this. Weak answers will treat cultural acceptance as decisive evidence that a practice is not corrupt, or will treat the public/private distinction as a neutral fact that is available everywhere in the same form. Strong answers should distinguish the question whether a community condemns a practice from the question whether it ought to condemn it; should notice that clientelism is an ongoing relationship rather than a one-off transaction; and should still offer a non-arbitrary standard with cross-cultural reach — for example, one built around whether the practice entrenches exclusion, converts discretionary public power into private patrimony, or blocks accountability to non-clients — without smuggling in Western assumptions as though they were neutral. I want the model answer to commit to one such standard and apply it to the cases, rather than laying out positions and leaving the choice open, and I want the marking scheme to separate an A from a B from a C by naming the specific moves an A answer must contain and the specific errors that should cost marks.
