I sit on my country's defence ministry review board for autonomous systems, which must approve, reject, or conditionally approve each proposed system before it can be fielded. Our governing framework requires meaningful human control over the use of force, but it does not define the phrase, so we have been building a common law of autonomy from case to case. We face a new proposal next week, and I want to vote in a way that coheres with our own record rather than quietly committing us to a principle we have not defended. Our past decisions:

1. Approved. A shipboard close-in weapon system that, in terminal-defense mode, automatically engages incoming anti-ship missiles. The crew cannot realistically approve individual engagements, and the crew can switch the system out of automatic mode at any moment. The system fires only at objects whose flight profile, radar return, and closing trajectory match known missile characteristics within a short range of the ship.

2. Approved with conditions. A loitering anti-radiation drone that autonomously locates and destroys enemy ground-based air-defense radars. Before release, a human operator must confirm the target-signature library, a geographic kill box, and a time window. The drone may engage only emitters matching the confirmed signatures within that box, and a human must retain the ability to command it to return or self-destruct.

3. Rejected. A quadrupedal ground robot that would use lethal force against individual persons it classifies as enemy combatants. Its classifier would be trained on appearance and movement cues and would update itself from its own encounters in theatre. No human would approve individual targets, and a supervisor could issue only a general stand-down order for the whole unit.

4. Approved. A defensive cyber agent that, upon detecting an intrusion into a military network, automatically isolates affected nodes and blocks malicious traffic according to pre-set rules. Operators can designate protected systems in advance, suspend the agent, and audit its actions afterward.

5. Rejected. An armed drone in which a human operator must click approval before each strike, but the operator sees only a bounding box and a machine-generated confidence score, receives a two-second decision window, and cannot request more information. We held that this was not genuine human decision-making.

The new proposal is an autonomous underwater drone for anti-submarine patrol across a very large, multi-week operating area that cannot be bounded tightly in advance. Its target-recognition software would be fixed before deployment and would not update in the field. A human watch officer at a shore station would have to approve each individual weapon release after reviewing a real-time acoustic and track picture with adequate time, and could order the drone to break off at any moment. There would be no pre-designated kill box or narrow time window.

What should I vote if I want to be consistent with our precedents? In particular, identify the candidate control principles that fit our record so far, and say which precedent is the closest analog. Say what each viable principle implies about the underwater drone, and then give your best-grounded recommendation, stating plainly what principle we would be adopting by approving it and what principle we would be adopting by rejecting it. Finally, tell me which further case would most cleanly distinguish the candidate principles if we wanted to settle the matter for future proposals.
