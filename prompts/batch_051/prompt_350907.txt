I spent last evening arguing with a friend about William James's will to believe and W. K. Clifford's ethics of belief, and I came away unable to reconstruct the principle that actually governs her judgments. She insists she is closer to Clifford than to James, but the shape of her rulings makes me doubt that, and my attempts to press her into a single rule failed.

Her verdicts, as best I can reconstruct them, are these. She has no sympathy for Clifford's shipowner: a man who talks himself out of his doubts about his ship's seaworthiness and then sends it out with passengers aboard has, she said, done something wrong precisely in believing as he did, not merely in risking the ship. She said the same about a juror who votes to convict on a felt certainty stronger than the evidence, where the evidence leaves reasonable doubt: the juror is not permitted to believe the defendant guilty, and the institutional duty of the role only deepens the wrong. So far Clifford.

But she defended a mountaineer who believes she can make a jump she has never attempted, on evidence too weak to establish her ability, when the belief itself is what steadies her enough to make the jump. Permissible, and perhaps the only rational thing to do. And she defended a person who, after years of having been lied to, resolves to believe that a new colleague is trustworthy, on evidence too thin to establish it, because displayed trust tends to call out trustworthy behavior in the other. Permissible as well.

When I pointed out that these permissions sound like James, she answered that Clifford was right wherever belief is a way of setting evidence aside about a matter the believer cannot change, and that James was right only where the belief is part of what brings its own truth about. I am not sure that is her actual rule as opposed to a rule she likes the sound of, and I can think of at least one other principle that fits everything she said.

The case I need the prediction for is this. The brother of a friend of both of us disappeared while hiking two years ago; the official search was called off after several months, and the evidence now simply underdetermines the question. There is no body, and a clear-eyed person could assign a modest but real chance that he is alive; a clear-eyed person could equally accept that he is dead. His sister believes, as a settled conviction, that he is alive. The belief has no causal route by which it could make itself true: it does not improve any search, and it will not affect whether he survived. But the option is live for her, forced in practice because each ordinary decision about his possessions, his room, and her own future makes her act either as if he will return or as if he will not, and momentous. She is not harming anyone, she is not occupying an official fact-finding role, and she is not in denial about what the evidence shows; she knows the evidence is inconclusive.

Given her record, what should I predict she will say about the sister: permitted to believe, permitted only to act as if, or not permitted at all? Which of her earlier cases is the closest analog, and does it actually settle the new case? I want you to spell out the most plausible rules that would fit her entire set of rulings, say what each predicts about the sister, and give a best guess as to which rule she would own if pressed. Then give me the further hypothetical that would do the most work in forcing her to choose among the surviving rules.
