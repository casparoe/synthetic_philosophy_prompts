Suppose it's 2027 and a consortium of small island conservation groups—lets call them Seaward—has just succeeded with something people have talked about for years. Seaward built a suppression gene drive for the common house mouse: a genetic tweak, spread by releasing a few hundred engineered males, that makes any female with two copies sterile. Because males with one copy pass the tweak on to nearly all their offspring instead of the usual fifty-fifty, the drive races through a population and then crashes it once enough sterile females accumulate. They tried it on two uninhabited islands where mice had eaten seabird chicks for a century, and within about twelve mouse generations the mice were gone. Seaward now wants to scale up to larger, inhabited islands, and they've published the full sequence openly, arguing that secrecy is more dangerous then transparency.

The problem is that an independent lab soon publishes a model showing two things. First, the drive has something like a 0.1 percent chance per generation of hopping into a closely related native mouse through rare hybridization. Second, if a drive-carrying mouse stows away on a fishing boat or cargo ship, the thing can establish on some distant island before anyone notices. Seaward replies that 0.1 percent is tiny, that they have a reversal drive ready in the freezer, and that the only real alternative is letting the seabirds go locally extinct. They also happen to be the group trying to bring back a locally extinct ground bird using museum DNA, and they argue this is all one program: first remove the invader, then put back what was lost. Assume for the sake of the question that the independent model's numbers are roughly right, and that the mouse eradication on the first two islands worked exactly as intended.

What I want is a real prediction, not a story: what happens over the next thirty years—ecologically, politically, and to the future of anyone using this kind of technology? Walk through the chain of mechanisms and incentives. Which links are nearly forced, which depend on fragile assumptions about how institutions or ecosystems actually behave, and what's the single assumption that would most change your prediction if it turned out false?
