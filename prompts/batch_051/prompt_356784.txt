I'm in a graduate seminar on Buddhist epistemology, and after two weeks on Dharmakīrti I've been trying to formalize his inference to momentariness so I can see whether it is a good argument or just a striking one. My reconstruction runs as follows. (1) To be real is to have causal power: something with no power to produce effects is, in Dharmakīrti's framework, indistinguishable from a nonexistent. (2) Consider a seed that sprouts at noon, though the soil, water, and warmth were all in place at eleven. (3) The seed at eleven and the seed at noon either have exactly the same nature or differ in some respect. (4) If they have exactly the same nature, then, since the seed at noon has the power to sprout under those conditions, the seed at eleven had that very same power under those very same conditions - so the sprout should already have appeared at eleven. (5) But it did not; therefore the seed must have differed in some respect at noon, which is just to say that it changed. (6) The same reasoning applies between any two times, however close, so the seed is never the same from one moment to the next; it is a series of momentary seeds. (7) Therefore nothing that exists endures; existing things are momentary, and the impression of a stable seed is a construction laid over a flow of momentary replacements.

I want to know whether this argument goes through, and I keep changing my mind about where it breaks. Is it valid? If it is, identify the premise that does the real work and say whether it is true, or at least whether it has to be accepted by anyone who grants Dharmakīrti's starting point. If it is not valid, name the exact step at which the inference fails. In particular, I'd like you to address two candidates. First, does premise (4) smuggle in the assumption that a causal power must manifest the moment its supporting conditions are present, thereby ruling out dormant or dispositional properties by fiat - and if so, can an appeal to a dormant capacity stop the argument without becoming an empty placeholder? Second, does premise (6) move illicitly from "the seed changes during any interval" to "the seed exists for no interval at all," and would a defender of enduring objects be entitled to say that a persisting thing can change gradually without decomposing into instantaneous slices? I'd like an answer that treats both the reconstruction and the underlying doctrine seriously, and that ends by saying what, if anything, the argument establishes even if one of its steps fails.
