I have been trying to steelman a familiar challenge to suffering-focused consequentialism by turning it into an explicit expected-value argument, and I have succeeded well enough that the conclusion now looks inescapable. Since almost no one in the discussions I follow actually accepts that conclusion, I suspect a step has gone wrong, but I cannot identify which step I can reject without silently giving up the part of the view I care about.

Consider a strong but recognizable form of suffering-focused consequentialism. Its core commitment is:

(1) The prevention of extreme suffering has lexical priority over the promotion of ordinary happiness: no finite amount of ordinary happiness compensates for one additional instance of the relevant kind of extreme suffering.

Now the argument:

(2) If (1) holds, then in a choice between two otherwise similar courses of action, an act that introduces a positive probability of an additional instance of extreme suffering is always worse than an act that introduces no such probability. The lexically decisive bad in the possible outcome cannot be offset by any ordinary goods the risky act might bring.

(3) Creating a new sentient life introduces a positive probability of an additional instance of extreme suffering, namely the suffering that life might one day undergo.

(4) From (2) and (3), creating a new sentient life is always worse than not creating it, other things equal. The view therefore entails a suffering-based antinatalism.

(5) The same comparison applies to the continuation of lives that already exist: other things equal, continuing a sentient life introduces a positive probability of additional future extreme suffering for that life, whereas painless termination introduces none. By the reasoning in (2), painless termination is always preferable to continuation for each life.

(6) Generalizing (5) across all sentient lives, a painless intervention that ended all sentient life at once would be preferable to any alternative in which sentient life continues, and would be required if available.

(7) But this consequence, that the view requires painless omnicide whenever feasible, is one that even most committed suffering-focused consequentialists treat as a reductio of the view rather than an implication they accept.

Where does the argument go wrong? I would most like to keep (1) in some form and to deny the move in (6) without simply biting the bullet. Tell me which step to reject, what rejecting it costs, and what remains of the suffering-focused consequentialist picture. In particular, is there a principled difference between the noncreation of new lives and the termination of existing lives that blocks the step from (4) to (5), or does blocking that step force me to abandon either lexical priority or the expected-value framework in which the argument is stated?
