I am in a population ethics course, and while working through a problem set I built an argument that I now find alarming because it appears to show that, if the universe is infinite, no act affecting only finitely many people is ever better than doing nothing. I cannot locate the failure, and I would like you to diagnose it.

Setup. Suppose the universe contains a countably infinite population and, in the status quo world W1, every person has lifetime welfare 1. Let W2 be the world that results from a single act that raises exactly one billion people from welfare 1 to welfare 100, leaving everyone else unchanged. Intuitively, W2 is better than W1: it makes a billion lives much better and no life worse. Now consider this argument.

(1) The value of a world is the sum of the welfare of all the people in it.
(2) In W1, this sum is 1 + 1 + 1 + …, which diverges to +∞.
(3) If two worlds have the same total value, then neither is better than the other.
(4) W2 has the same total value as W1. Replacing finitely many of the 1s in a sum of countably many 1s with 100s still leaves a countably infinite sum of positive terms, which again diverges to +∞.
(5) Therefore W2 is not better than W1.
(6) But W2 is better than W1: a billion people are much better off and no one is worse off.
(7) So at least one of (1)–(6) must go.

The conclusion this chain presses toward — that in an infinite universe no act affecting only finitely many people can be better than inaction — strikes me as proving too much. Which premise or step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, once the rejected premise is gone, what principled account of comparing infinite worlds remains, and what intuitive or theoretical judgments does that account sacrifice? I would most like to keep (6), but I am prepared to hear that the real cost lies there. Please keep your answer to at most 700 words.
