I have been thinking on my own about Francis Fukuyama's end-of-history thesis, by which I mean the claim, made around the end of the Cold War, that liberal democracy had won the long contest of political ideas, that no remaining rival was likely to replace it, and that future politics would mostly be about administering and improving liberal societies rather than choosing between fundamentally incompatible visions of the good society. I am not an academic, and most of what I have read repeats the same objections. The ones I can already name are: that history plainly kept happening after 1989 in the form of terrorism, nationalist violence and the rise of China; that Fukuyama mistook the bankruptcy of Soviet communism for the success of liberal capitalism; that actually existing liberal democracies suffer from serious internal failures such as inequality, corruption, polarization and democratic backsliding; that the thesis is circular because it defines genuine history as movement toward liberal democracy and then declares the movement complete; that it functions as American self-congratulation and a justification for American power; that it rests on a discredited teleological view of history as having a single direction; that new ideological rivals can always be invented, whether religious, environmental or technocratic; and that Fukuyama himself later stressed how identity politics and weak institutions could undermine liberal democracy from within. I know these are all standard, so they are not what I am looking for.

What I am after is an objection with a different structure from anything on that list. It should not be a rewording, a special case, or a combination of the objections I just named, and it should be the kind of objection a careful critic could genuinely press rather than a contrarian novelty. After you give the objection, tell me honestly whether it is any good: does it hit the thesis itself, or only the loose popular version of it that I have stated? Then say what it adds that the familiar objections on my list do not, and whether it should make me restate the thesis more carefully. Please answer in ordinary continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write an email, and use American English.
