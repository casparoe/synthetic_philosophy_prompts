I'm writing up a position for an AI ethics workshop and want a consistency check before I send it around, because the whole thing sounds a little off to me. The views, roughly: (1) If one AI substantially designs or trains another, it has guardian-type obligations toward the system it made, at least while that system is vulnerable and a candidate for moral patienthood. (2) What makes human parents morally special isn't that they built the child or share DNA, it's the child's long deep developmental dependence; construction by itself does basically no moral work. (3) An AI that trained another AI would usually be in a much better epistemic position to be its guardian than any human, since it can inspect weights, training dynamics, and failure modes directly. (4) But being a guardian requires stable standing to act over time — being the one who gets to make decisions for the ward and gets called when things go wrong — and most AI systems are treated as replaceable tools by their operators, so they'd lack that standing however good their epistemic position is. (5) And because a newly trained advanced AI would likely become competent to look after it's own interests far faster than a human child does, the obligations would mostly lapse before they ever bite. So I end up saying the AI-raised-AI frame is right and also that it'll almost never matter in practice, and I don't know if that's a deep problem or just two different levels of description. Which of these are actually in tension, which only look that way, and what's the cheapest revision? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad.
