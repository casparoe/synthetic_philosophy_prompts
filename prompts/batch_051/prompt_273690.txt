I am a novelist at work on a book whose protagonist, a retired oddsmaker, becomes preoccupied with wagers in which the expected value resists calculation. The character has been reading Daniel Bernoulli’s 1738 essay on the measurement of risk, where Bernoulli argues—as I would paraphrase it in my own heavily reworked English—that the fair value of a risky prospect is obtained by multiplying each possible gain by the number of ways in which it can occur, dividing by the total number of cases, and treating the resulting quantity as the price a prudent person should accept. I want to put pressure on a claim one of my consultants makes about a later example, Hájek and Nover’s Pasadena game. The consultant says this: the Pasadena game is not merely a curiosity about divergent series, but a real challenge to the idea that rational choice always has a single numerical quantity—expected value—to guide it; and our sense that the game is not obviously worth any finite entrance fee shows something about how actual minds handle undefined expectations. I do not care much about the formal mathematics; I care about whether that last clause can be tested.

How would one test whether a person’s hesitation before the Pasadena game, or before similar games with undefined expectations, reveals a distinct attitude toward indeterminate expected value rather than ordinary risk aversion, confusion about the setup, or a general unwillingness to accept unbounded variance? Give me a concrete experimental design: the comparison conditions, the choice pattern that would favour the claim, and the pattern that would weaken it. Then say exactly what a positive result would not settle—whether it would still leave open that the hesitation is just a defect in comprehension or a heuristic for avoiding difficult sums. Since I expect to include some version of this argument in an afterword, also tell me what evidence or objection would most likely make you change your verdict about the claim, and where your own reasoning about testability is most fragile.
