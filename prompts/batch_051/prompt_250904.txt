I cover science and ideas for a general readership, and I'm trying to get my head around something in climate and safety policy that I suspect is hiding a philosophical decision inside a spreadsheet. When government agencies run cost-benefit analyses, they discount future benefits and future lives at something like 3 to 7 percent a year. A death prevented a century from now counts for a tiny fraction of a death prevented this year. The standard line is that this is just prudent economics, not a judgement about whether future people matter less. But I cant tell whether that's a good answer or a dodge.

So here's what I want. Walk me through what theory of value, and of our obligations to people who don't exist yet, this routine practice presupposes. Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it have to presuppose that later people matter less than present people (pure time preference), or can it be made compatible with the view that future people count equally, with the discount reflecting growth, opportunity costs, and uncertainty instead? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with. Then say what would have to change in how governments evaluate policies if we took the equal-counting view seriously—and whether the usual rationale for discounting also quietly presupposes that presently existing people are the ones entitled to decide for all future people. The longtermists I've been reading seem to think that once you count future people equally, ordinary democratic constraints start to look parochial, and critics hear that as a road to authoritarianism. I want to know wether the practice itself forces that conclusion, or whether that's a further step the practice doesn't settle. Flag which parts of this are settled among people who work on this and which are genuinely contested, and don't present one side's view as the consensus.
