Suppose a medium-sized country decides to get rid of family names entirely. From 1 January 2050, every newborn gets one state-registered personal name, and the state guarantees no two living citizens share one; you can't change it, and marriage or adoption doesn't change it either. Children don't inherit any name from their parents. Predict, with reasoning, how everyday reference, record-keeping and social identity shift over the next three or four generations. I'm less interested in the headline prediction than in the mechanism: which jobs surnames actually do (disambiguation, marking lineage, inheritance of status, bureaucratic indexing), which of those turn out to be load-bearing, and how people compensate. Once a bare name picks out at most one living citizen, does reference get easier or harder? What goes wrong when people talk about the dead, about foreigners, or about people born before 2050 who kept surnames? Does a uniqueness-guaranteeing registry stay stable, or does it drift into de facto surnames (nicknames, ID numbers, parentheticals)? Mark clearly which bits of your prediction are robust and which are guesses, and what would have to be different for it to come out otherwise. oh and write the answer in British English please.
