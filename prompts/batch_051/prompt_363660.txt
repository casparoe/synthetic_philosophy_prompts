I have come to defend a version of strong emergence, and I want an honest map of what would count against it—of how exposed the view actually is. Let me be precise about the target. I accept weak emergence without difficulty: many higher-level patterns—cellular automata, turbulent flow, protein folding—cannot be predicted in practice from the underlying microphysics, yet are nonetheless fixed by it once the microphysics is specified. What I defend goes further. I hold that at least some higher-level phenomena possess novel causal powers that are not fixed by, and not derivable even in principle from, the laws, properties, and arrangement of their lower-level constituents, and that these higher-level powers can act downward on the lower level in a way that is not merely a redescription of lower-level goings-on. My strongest candidate is consciousness, but I also take seriously the possibility that certain chemical and biological organizations do causal work that no amount of microphysical information, however complete and however idealized, would let us deduce.

The deflationary considerations I keep hearing are familiar, and I want more than that litany. They include: conservation laws and the causal closure of the microphysical already rule out downward causation; causal exclusion leaves higher-level causes either epiphenomenal or overdetermining; every alleged case of strong emergence has so far dissolved into derivability once the right lower-level description or computational technique was found; and the view survives only because underivability has been defined so strictly that no conceivable observation could bear on it. I would personally find it most persuasive if someone showed either that a case I regard as strongly emergent can be derived after all once the correct lower-level description is chosen, or that my definition is compatible with every possible observation and therefore empty.

Approach this as a philosopher of science fluent in both the relevant physics and the metaphysics of causation and explanation. Before settling on your assessment, brainstorm the broadest possible list of ways the position could fail, then organize them. Then give me the ranked map: what empirical findings, arguments from physics or metaphysics, and formal or conceptual results would count against strong emergence as I have defined it? Separate the defeaters that would be fatal from those that are merely awkward; say which of them a committed defender should concede genuinely count against the view, as opposed to considerations only critics press; identify what my position is committed to that reductionist and weak-emergentist rivals deny, and what the world would have to look like for those commitments to be borne out. Be willing to conclude that the view is well insulated, and say whether that insulation is a legitimate feature of a thesis of this kind or a defect. Finally, tell me what would change your own mind about the overall assessment you reach, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
