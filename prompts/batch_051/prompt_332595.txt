I am a novelist, and I am working out the logic of a counterfactual before building a story inside it. Suppose that beginning in 1500, every settled human community above the size of a small town produces a Chronicle: a continuously updated, public written record of every observable event within its territory. The Chronicle is complete with respect to observable facts — who did what, when, where, what was said aloud — and everyone knows it to be accurate about those matters. It cannot be altered, suppressed, or destroyed. It reports only the observable surface; it assigns no motives, no meanings, no larger narrative. Human psychology, the need for belonging, and the urge to transmit a usable past remain exactly as they are. I do not want a story; I want a reasoned prediction. Over the next three centuries, which practices of collective memory — founding myths, heroic genealogies, commemorations, selective silences — survive, adapt, or collapse when the record cannot be contradicted? When a community's memory and the Chronicle conflict, do communities tend to revise their memory, reinterpret or quarantine the Chronicle, or split into factions, and what determines which response wins? Distinguish the predictions you are confident in from those that are guesses, and say what would have to be true about human psychology or institutions for those predictions to fail. If the scenario is underspecified, or if the very idea of a complete record of events rests on a confused picture of what history or memory is, say so plainly rather than answering politely.
