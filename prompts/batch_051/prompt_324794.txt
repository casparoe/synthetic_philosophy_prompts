I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've spent the last few weeks trying to figure out why the academic argument over the definition of terrorism hasn't budged in basically my entire career. Every conference panel seems to replay the same three-way standoff: one camp says the violence has to be by non-state actors, another says state violence clearly counts (and calls the first camp naive or self-serving), and a third says what actually matters is that the violence is meant to send a message to an audience beyond the immediate victims — the armed-propaganda view. People agree on almost all the paradigm cases and then harden their positions on the edge cases (state reprisals, resistance movements, lone actors with murky manifestos).

My current hypothesis is that this isn't a factual disagreement and isn't just verbal either: "terrorism" is doing moral work, not analytical work. It picks out violence we want to mark as illegitimate, and the definitional fight is a proxy for a fight over whose political violence can ever be legitimate. Lawyers want bright lines for prosecutions, social scientists want a causal kind they can count, and activists want a label they can apply to states — so their all answering different questions under the same word. If I'm right, progress would mean splitting the term into smaller, less morally loaded concepts (clandestine political violence, civilian-targeted coercion, armed propaganda) and keeping "terrorism" only as a legal or political label.

Is that diagnosis right? What kind of disagreement is this, actually — bedrock moral intuitions, different purposes under one term, a hidden empirical question about what causes the violence, or just an ill-posed question? And if the term really is doing moral work, what would count as progress: abandoning the word in research, building something like the smaller concepts above, or something else?
