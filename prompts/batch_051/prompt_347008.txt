I cover cognitive science and ideas for a living, and I keep tripping over a question that started when I was messing around with a language model. Davidson's line on metaphor was that a metaphor doesn't have any special metaphorical meaning: the sentence means exactly what its words literally mean, and all the rest — the image, the shift in how you see the target, the little jolt — is causal side effect, like being shoved or startled. You can't paraphrase it, but not because it's too rich to paraphrase; because there's no extra content there to paraphrase. Then the conceptual metaphor tradition, the Lakoff-and-Johnson side, said the losely opposite: metaphors aren't decoration, they're cognition. An abstract domain gets structured by a source domain that's usually bodily — arguments are buildings, theories are journeys — and that mapping is part of thought, not just language.

Now here's the development. A language model trained only on text statistics, with no body and no firsthand experience of arguments or buildings, produces a novel metaphor that good readers find genuinely fresh and apt; ask it to extend the image and it keeps the mapping coherent for a while. I don't need to tell you our timeline is full of these.

Which side should be more scared of this? The obvious read is that it's bad for the embodied-conceptual-metaphor program, because the body starts to look optional: distributional patterns over language are enough to do a lot of the work they assigned to embodied source domains. But I think it's also bad for Davidson, because readers aren't just getting startled — they recover a structure from the metaphor, reason with it, and can say quite a lot about what maps onto what, which sure looks like content. Maybe the cleanest conclusion is that both camps located the thing in the wrong place: the metaphor lives in the interaction between a text and a reader, not in the author's intention or the language's semantics alone. Or maybe the LLM case is irrelevant, since a system with no intentions and no semantics can still produce the kind of string that triggers our meaning-making machinery, and the machinery is where all the action always was.

I'd love an honest verdict: does Davidson's argument survive this development, does conceptual metaphor theory survive it, or do both need repair in different ways — and which specific premise actually breaks under the LLM case?
