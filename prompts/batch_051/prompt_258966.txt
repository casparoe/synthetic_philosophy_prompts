I've been revisiting the dollar auction — the game in which a bill is sold to the highest bidder, and the two highest bidders must both pay their final bids — and thinking about how its lesson connects to the wider phenomenon of escalation of commitment. A recent meetup discussion brought home that I do not actually have a crisp account of where escalation of commitment ends and rational persistence begins; we were using the term to gesture at a behavior, a psychological mechanism, and a mistake, without noticing that those three can come apart. The usual rationalist gloss on the dollar auction is a warning about the sunk cost fallacy, but the auction is subtler than that: at each stage, staying in can look like a reasonable bid to protect what you have already put down, and yet the process predictably drives the price above the value of the bill. I want a working definition of escalation of commitment that separates the phenomenon worth naming from ordinary cases of continuing a losing course because continuing is, on the whole, the correct play.

Here are the cases the definition should classify. (1) A bidder in a dollar auction who keeps bidding past the value of the bill because dropping out would mean forfeiting every bid she has already placed. (2) A manager who funds another round of a project whose original rationale has been falsified, because stopping would mean admitting the earlier spending was wasted. (3) A poker player who calls a large river bet because the pot odds make the call profitable, even though she expects to lose four times in five. (4) A mountaineer who continues toward the pass because descending would now expose the party to a worse storm. (5) A general who continues a war partly so that soldiers already killed will not have died for nothing. (6) A bandit algorithm that keeps selecting an arm that paid off early but has since gone cold. (7) Someone who has publicly defended a view and resists updating when new evidence arrives, because changing her mind would cost her credibility. Please run the definition through all seven. I take (1) and (2) as paradigm cases and (3) and (4) as non-cases; I lean toward counting (5) but cannot articulate why, I suspect (6) should not count, and I have no settled view about (7). Finally, tell me which of the seven verdicts your definition leaves you least confident about, and whether that should push toward revising the definition or revising my classifications.
