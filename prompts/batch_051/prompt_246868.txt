I'm giving a talk to my department's decision-theory workshop defending the claim that the Pasadena game exposes a genuine, not merely formal, hole in expected utility theory: for some perfectly well-defined gambles there is no fact of the matter what a rational agent may pay, and none of the standard patches — Lebesgue integration, truncate-and-take-a-limit, or "just complete the preference arbitrarily" — actually earns the value it outputs. Quick version of the object: flip a fair coin until it lands heads; if the first heads is on toss n, you receive (−1)^(n−1)·(2^n/n) dollars. In the natural order, the expectation is 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − ... = ln 2, but the series converges only conditionally, so by reordering the states you can get any real number, or make it diverge — which is exactly why I'll argue the expectation is undefined in principle, not just in need of bookkeeping.

The audience: mostly formal epistemologists and decision theorists who treat expected utility maximization as the default norm and think completeness is a harmless idealization, plus two mathematicians who'll see the whole thing as elementary and want to know why we're relitigating conditionally convergent series. A couple of people know the literature and will have read the standard replies already.
