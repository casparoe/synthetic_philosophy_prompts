I'm on my university's honorary degree committee and we're deadlocked on a nominee. He's a retired public official who, back in the war, authorized the atomic bombing of two cities — a couple hundred thousand people, mostly civilians. That's not what we'd be honoring him for, though. The proposal is to award the degree for the twenty years of arms-control and peace-education work he did afterwards, and the citation will deliberately not mention the war at all. Half the committee says this is obviously fine: an honorary degree isn't a public verdict on a person's entire life, and if we demanded a clean record we could never honor anyone. The other half says that's a dodge — when the university puts a gown on him and hands him the diploma it's honoring the man, and the man is the one who gave the order, whatever the citiation says. I have to vote in a week and I keep flipping back and forth. Can the 'we're only endorsing the later work' move be made coherent, or does something about what institutional honor does make it collapse? And does it even matter, given that everyone knows these degrees are mostly PR?
