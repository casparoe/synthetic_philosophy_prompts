In my class on coincidence and the epistemology of anecdote I've basically setteld on a deflationary view: "what are the odds" stories are almost never worth updating on, because the birthday paradox and the law of truly large numbers say rare events happen to someone all the time, and the person telling the story is exactly the someone it happened to. I know that's fuzzy, so I want you to first pin the view down and then tell me what would count against it. What would have to be true—observations, formal results, features of the reporting chain—for a coincidence anecdote to be real evidence of something rather than a multiple-comparisons artifact? I keep worrying the birthday analogy is doing too much work: birthdays are uniform and independent, and you can't pick your roommates after noticing they share your birthday. Does the analogy breaking in those ways give the anecdote side a genuine opening, or just a superficial one? And maybe more importantly: has the view, as I've stated it, been protected into unfalsifiability—does it always just say "get more evidence"—and is that a bug or a feature for a view of this kind? Sort what counts against it from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me what a defender of the deflationary position would themselves concede counts against it.
