I'm working on a paper about John Stuart Mill's The Subjection of Women, and I've gotten myself into a tangle over the argument in chapter 1 about the appeal to nature. Mill says, as I understand him, that the observable differences between men and women have been produced by the very system of subjection that they are then used to justify, so they cannot count as evidence about women's natural capacities: we have never seen women under conditions of freedom and equality. I find this compelling when applied to sex, but when I try to state it as a general principle, it seems to prove far too much.

Here is the argument as I reconstructed it. (1) For any subordinated group, the conduct and capacities we observe in them are shaped by their subordination. (2) Observations of a group under subordination cannot warrant conclusions about that group's natural capacities. (3) So any observed difference between a subordinated group and its dominators cannot, by itself, support an appeal to nature. (4) Every large human group whose characteristic conduct or capacities we might compare has been, at some point, in a relation of domination or subordination. (5) Therefore no observed differences between large social groups support appeals to nature. (6) If that is right, then the distinction between a group's natural and artificial characteristics is empirically empty, since every actual group has a history of domination. (7) But this seems to undercut not only the claim that women are naturally inferior, but also any attempt to say that human beings have a nature that oppressive institutions distort—including, I think, Mill's own appeal to a human nature that requires liberty to develop. Where does this go wrong? I most want to keep the move that protects Mill's argument about women, but I can't see a principled way to stop the slide to (6) and (7). Is the flaw in the generalization from (3) to (5), or in the assumption that Mill needs (6) to be false? If one of the premises must go, I'd like to know what it costs.
