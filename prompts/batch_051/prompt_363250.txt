Me and my roommate have been arguing about a thought experiment from a philosophy of tech seminar, and I'm trying to make both of us put actual numbers on it instead of just repeating slogans. Here's the setup. You walk into a clinic and over the course of five years, one at a time, each of the roughly 86 billion neurons in your brain gets replaced by an artificial unit that mirrors the old neuron's input-output behavior so accurately that neighboring neurons, glial cells, and the rest of your body can't tell the difference. Your conscious (or at least you seem to be) the whole time: you chat, pass memory tests, get annoyed at the hospital food, recognize your family, argue with your brother. At the end, zero biological neurons remain. If the person who walked in was me, call the person who walks out Amelia-2. What is your credence that Amelia-2 is literally me — the same person, same subject of experience, not just a very good copy or my psychological descendant? I'm not interested in "it depends on your theory." I want a number, and then I want the decomposition behind it.

Specifically: assign your own rough probabilities to something like (a) a broadly psychological-continuity account of personal identity, and that account being satisfied by this procedure; (b) a biological or physical-continuity account, and that account being violated here; (c) the possibility that there's some further fact about consciousness that could be lost even though all behavior and reports look the same; and (d) the possibility that "is this the same person?" has no determinate answer in this case, so the reasonable credence should be spread across borderline verdicts. Then show how you combine those into the overall number, tell me which sub-question your final answer swings on most, and what single variant of the case or piece of evidence from the procedure would move your credence the most. My roommate is at 0.15 and I'm at 0.8 — where do you think the disagreement between us is actually living?
