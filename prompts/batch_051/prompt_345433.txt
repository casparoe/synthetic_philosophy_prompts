ok so heres roughly where I've landed on pleasure and well-being and I want a consistency check before I present this in a value theory seminar. My commitments:

(1) I'm a hedonist about well-being: a life goes well for someone just to the extent that it contains pleasure and avoids pain, and nothing else ultimately matters.

(2) I don't think all pleasures share a single felt quality. The pleasure of getting a proof to come together, a back scratch, relief at a clean scan, and a little schadenfreude at a rival's bad day feel like completely different kinds of thing.

(3) To handle that heterogeneity I lean attitudinal: what unifies them is that each is a case of the person being pleased that something is the case — a favorable propositional attitude — rather than any shared sensation.

(4) But I don't want to overintellectualize. My dog has pleasures, so do babies, and neither is entertaining propositions. Even for me, the pleasant feel of scratching an itch doesn't seem to have any propositional object.

(5) I also want to say the reason pleasure matters for well-being is that it feels good from the inside. If a state had no positive felt character at all, I don't see why it would make a life go better.

(6) I take introspection as the last word on whether something counts as a pleasure: if it seems from the inside like a pleasure, the theory should shut up and count it.

(7) I don't want to discount false-based pleasures much: someone who takes deep joy in a loved one's recovery when the loved one actually died is having real pleasure, and it really makes their life go better in that respect.

I want an audit, not a lecture on hedonism. Which pairs of these are genuinely in tension, and which only look like it? Rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to give and at what cost. Then tell me the single cheapest revision that would make the whole set hang together, if there is one. Work inside the set unless something really has to go.
