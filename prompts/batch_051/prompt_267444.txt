I've noticed a weird asymmetry in how I (and I suspect most people) handle tiny probabilities of huge bad things, and I can't tell whether it's sound or just cowardice.

Here's the asymmetry. I'll happily pay a few dollars to get rid of a headache, to avoid missing a train, or to fix a squeaky door that annoys me. Small, near-certain harms. But if you ask me for the same few dollars to shrink the chance of a truly awful outcome—a one-in-ten-billion chance of an asteroid strike, or a stranger who insists he can stop a googolplex people from being tortured—I won't pay. I'll treat the second kind of risk as basicly zero and get on with my day. Yet if I take expected value seriously, a one-in-ten-billion chance of a googolplex people being tortured should dominate the value of curing my headache by an absurd margin. So my behavior only makes sense if I'm putting the huge bad outcome in some special category that my expected-value calculations aren't allowed to touch.

What I want from you: is there anything that actually justifies this asymmetry, or is it just an inconsistency we live with? Find the strongest principle you can that generates both of my responses—maybe "below some probability we round to zero," maybe "there's no finite amount I should pay to an uncorroborated stranger," maybe something about how much worse than a headache torture actually is—and then test it against nearby cases. For example, if you round tiny probabilities to zero, why don't you round a one-in-a-billion lottery ticket down to zero when the jackpot is only in the millions? And if the issue is distrusting the stranger, what happens when the claim comes from someone you do trust, with the same tiny probability and the same enourmous stakes? I want an honest verdict at the end: should I keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or give it up—and where exactly does your principle break if you push it hard enough?
