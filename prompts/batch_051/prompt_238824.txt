I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about punishment and desert, not from theory but from how I react to cases, and I'd like you to act as a philosopher who works on free will and criminal punishment. Work through this carefully — maybe start by brainstorming three or four candidate principles and stress-testing them against the cases before settling on anything.

Here are my verdicts.

1. A man kills his wife during a sudden rage. Afterward a scan finds a big frontal-lobe tumor sitting on the impulse-control circuitry. The tumor is removed and he is never violent again. I don't think he deserves punishment in the ordinary 'you wronged us, pay for it' sense. But I also don't think you just release him while he's still dangerous. Detaining him was right, and I don't owe him an apology for it.

2. A CEO of a mortgage company spends three years knowingly lying about the risk of the products, wipes out people's savings, with a happy childhood, full information, plenty of other ways to make money. I think he deserves a serious prison sentence, and I would be genuinely dissatisfied if he got only a fine that deterred other CEOs just as well. The desert itself matters there, not just the deterrence.

3. A young woman is caught smuggling drugs across the border because a cartel is holding her sister and has told her they'll kill the sister if she doesn't. I don't think she deserves the same punishment as the people who made the threat, and honestly I'm not sure she deserves any punishment at all, even though she knew it was illegal and did it voluntarly enough to count as an act.

4. A man with severe psychopathy — no empathy since childhood, no remorse — tortures someone for the pleasure of it and says he'd do it again. I think he has to be locked up, maybe forever. But when I ask whether he deserves to suffer, I go quiet. I'd want his confinement to be comfortable and not cruel, even though he was cruel. So somehow he is less a candidate for desert than the CEO, but more a candidate for detention than anyone.

5. A police chief knows a man is innocent but lets him get convicted of a high-profile murder because the neighborhoods are about to riot and convicting the real killer would take too long. I think this is wrong no matter what the resulting numbers say about lives saved, and I'd say so even at the cost of the principle that punishment is just harm prevention.

6. Two men each rob a liquor store and pistol-whip the clerk. One grew up in a home where he was beaten and watched his mother be beaten, and the crime looks like a replay of that violence. The other grew up safe and loved and had, as far as anyone can tell, the same options and same knowledge. I want to punish the second man more than the first. But then I remember: if the world is deterministic, both their histories were just causes leading to what they did. So why should the difference between a bad history and a good history carry the weight it does, and does my 'the CEO deserves it' verdict survive that thought or fall apart?

The case I keep going back and forth on: a nineteen-year-old who grew up in a neighborhood where gang membership was close to the only route to safety, who kills a rival. At the moment of the killing he isn't coerced, isn't mentally ill, and knows what he's doing. Some days I think he deserves punishment because he made the choice. Other days I think the choice was written before he was born, and 'deserves' is just a story we tell ourselves so we can hurt someone cleanly. Which side does my principle put me on?

So: is there a prinicple — or a small set of candidate principles — that captures all or nearly all of my verdicts? Give me the principle stated precisely, with the case motivating each clause, its verdict on the nineteen-year-old and on a case or two I haven't listed, and an honest list of which of my verdicts it can't accommodate. Then tell me where this leaves me relative to the Dennett–Caruso debate over whether anybody ever justly deserves punishment, and which of my verdicts is doing the most work in pushing me toward one side or the other.
