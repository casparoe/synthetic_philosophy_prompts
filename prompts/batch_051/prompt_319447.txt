I teach an upper-level philosophy of science seminar and I'm writing the final. I want one essay question on Feyerabend's anything goes that can't be answered by reciting the slogan or summarizing the lecture notes. It should make students confront the weird logical status of the phrase: is it a methodological rule? If it is, it seems to underwrite its own abandonment; if it isn't, what work is it doing in the argument? I want it to force them to say whether the slogan is best read as a description of how science actually proceeds, a reductio of rule-based methodology, or something rhetorical, and what each reading implies for the claim that there is no scientific method.

Please write the question, a model answer of about 550 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that seperates an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain — for example, distinguishing the slogan from the claim that all theories are equally good, and actually engaging with the Galileo example rather than skipping over it. Students will get about an hour.

Then add a short note after the marking scheme saying where the model answer is least stable: which reading of Feyerabend it leans on, and what argument or textual evidence would change the model answer or the marking scheme. In other words, what would have to be true about Feyerabend's actual position for the answer to be wrong, and how would you re-grade if that were established?
