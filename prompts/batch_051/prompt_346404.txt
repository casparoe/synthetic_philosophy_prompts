I have been following debates about the ethics of policies that steer people's choices without forbidding anything, and I keep noticing an argumentative move that I do not have a name for. The move goes like this. A pension plan changes from requiring employees to sign up to enrolling them automatically unless they opt out; a website changes its privacy settings so that the most data-generous option is preselected; a country changes its organ-donation registry so that people are presumed to consent unless they have already declined. When critics object that the new arrangement pushes people in one particular direction, the defenders reply that some arrangement is unavoidable—someone has to decide what happens when a person does nothing—and that, because of this, the arrangement they have chosen is no more steering or paternalistic than any other would be. They then treat that reply as settling the matter.

What confuses me is the slide from 'we must choose some default' to 'the particular default we chose cannot be criticized for steering people.' Different defaults predictably produce very different rates of enrollment, sharing, or donation, so the choice between them seems to matter in exactly the way the reply denies. Is this a recognized argumentative pattern or fallacy, and does it have an established name? If it does not correspond to a single well-known concept, I would be grateful for an account of the nearest established concepts, how exactly this move differs from each of them, and where its boundaries lie. A bare label would not help me much; I am after the carving of the phenomenon—what the move commits someone to, when some version of it is legitimate, and when it becomes a trick of framing.
