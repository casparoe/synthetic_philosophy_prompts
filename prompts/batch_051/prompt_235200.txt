Two members of my reading group got stuck in an argument about whether you, the particular person you are, could have come from a different sperm and egg, and their exchange went something like this.

Amara opened by saying you could not: if a different sperm had reached the egg first, someone else would have been born, not you. So each person is tied, in a quite strong sense, to the specific gametes that produced them. Ben replied that this just presumes what "you" picks out across these hypothetical cases. Nothing in a person's memories, character, or body settles whether the child born from a different sperm would have been you; Amara, he said, was treating a decision about how to use "you" as if it were a discovery about the world.

Amara answered that we accept exactly this kind of reasoning for ordinary objects. A table made from an entirely different piece of wood, however similar, would not be this table; an individual's origin seems essential to it. Unless persons are an exception, the burden is on Ben to explain why. Ben said persons are an exception in the relevant respect: a table is individuated by its material, but a person can lose and replace every cell, forget much of their past, and change radically in character while remaining the same person. So it is unclear why two gametes should be more essential to a person's identity than, say, the particular oxygen atoms the embryo once used.

Amara said this conflates two different questions: what makes someone the same person over time, and what makes someone the person they are in the first place. However persistence is analysed, she said, a human being whose entire genetic starting point was different could not be this human being. Ben replied that if the view is really about genetic origins, it falters on a thought experiment in which two sperm happen to carry identical DNA: Amara would still have to deny that the child could have come from the other sperm, even though no biological, psychological, or genetic feature distinguishes the two possibilities. At that point, he said, the doctrine is no longer tracking anything about the person; it is just a stipulation that origins matter. Amara said that is precisely right, and not a problem: origins are rigid in that way, because identity across possibilities is fixed by the actual causal history that produced the individual, not by the individual's qualities.

Who is right about which point in this exchange, and what, if anything, are both of them missing?
