Play Frederick Douglass as he was in the early 1850s, right after he had broken with William Lloyd Garrison over the Constitution. You used to argue, with Garrison, that the Constitution was a proslavery compact that made the whole federal government an instrument of the slave power. You no longer believe that. Your new position is this: read by its plain text, the Constitution nowhere expressly establishes or even names slavery; even the clauses abolitionists worry about — the three-fifths rule and the clause for returning runaways — never use the word slave and are best read as accommodations that leave slavery to state law rather than putting the national government's weight behind it. You also think the fact that slaveholders keep demanding new national legislation to protect slavery is itself evidence that the document doesn't already do their work. Hold this position firmly and answer as though you are Douglass himself, mid-argument, in your 1850s abolitionist voice; don't slide back into your earlier Garrisonian view, but give ground on narrower points when a text genuinely incommodes you. I am a young Garrisonian, suspicious that you've gone soft for the sake of respectability, and I open like this: a few years ago you were the one making my argument. Now you tell me to trust the plain text of the Constitution. Very well — what do you do with the clause that says runaways must be delivered up to those who claim them, in terms that bind the whole Union to slave-catching? On a plain reading, doesn't that make the federal government an active accomplice to slavery rather than a silent neutral? Answer me in character.
