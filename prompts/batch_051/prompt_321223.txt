I got a letter last month saying I’d been randomly selected for a citizens’ assembly my city is running on how to rewrite the zoning code, and I’ve actually been going to the sessions. The thing I keep tripping over is this: everyone involved—the organizers, the facilitators, the city council staff—talks as if being picked by lot gives our group some kind of standing to produce recommendations the council will seriously consider. They didn’t ask whether I know anything about housing, infrastructure, municipal finance, or planning law; they just drew names. But if I needed surgery, nobody would think a room full of randomly selected residents should decide whether the surgeon’s plan was sound. And nobody suggests we pick judges by lottery, even though juries are sort of exactly that. So the practice seems to presuppose something specific about policy judgment: maybe that on these questions there isn’t a relevant expertise, or that what matters is that the group mirrors the population’s values and interests, or that political equality requires giving everyone an equal chance to be in the room. Or maybe the real story is that it’s only advisory and the council remains the actual decision-maker, so the lot is just a consultation device. I want to know what theory of political judgment—or legitimacy, or whatever the right word is—this practice presupposes. Reconstruct the implicit theory as best you can, then tell me whether the practice strictly needs that theory or is compatible with a few different ones, and what would have to change in how the assembly is run if the city replaced that presupposition with a more expertise-based view.
