Write the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two researchers who have been argueing for years about whether you can infer an optimizer's objective and search space from watching what it does. Call one side the "revealed objective" camp: she thinks that, given enough observed choices across sufficiently varied environments, the objective can be pinned down up to the usual boring equivalences (positive affine transformations of a utility function, reward functions that differ only on states the agent never reaches, etc.), and that recovering the search space is mostly an engineering problem. Call the other side the "underdetermination" camp: he thinks behavior alone radically underdetermines both the objective and the search space, so the objective you end up "inferring" is mostly a projection you brought to the data, not something you discovered in the system.

They've agreed to do an adversarial collaboration, and the deliverable is a single document both of them can sign, not a transcript of the argument. Write that document.

The document should have this shape. First, a numbered list of claims about optimizing systems, behavioral data, and inference that both sides accept, phrased so that neither of them winces; make these substantive, not just "agents take actions." Second, a disagreements table. Each row should give the question at issue, the exact wording each camp accepts for its own view, with the residual gap stated in neutral language. Third, three or four cruxes — sub-questions such that settling one would force at least one side to move — and for each crux, a concrete case or experiment both sides agree bears on it, plus each side's prediction recorded in advance and what each side says it would concede if the prediction came out the other way.

Make the document earn its keep. It should separate the genuine disagreement from the merely verbal one (the two camps have talked past each other for a year about the word "objective"), and it should not let either side hide behind toy cases. Cases can include a thermostat, a chess engine, a trained RL policy, a firm, and a person choosing between jobs. At least one crux should be about the thing I actually care about: whether the search space is itself inferable from behavior, or whether two systems can make all the same choices while running entirely different searches over entirely different candidate actions.
