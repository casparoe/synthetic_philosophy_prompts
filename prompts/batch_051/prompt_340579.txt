During a discussion of whether ordinary consumers in wealthy countries are complicit in labor abuses in global supply chains, someone answered my skepticism by quoting the familiar line: "The only thing necessary for the triumph of evil is for good people to do nothing." Their argument seemed to be that even if my individual purchases cannot be shown to cause any identifiable harm, my continuing to do nothing makes me complicit in the abuse. I suspect this slogan is doing more work than it can bear, because it seems to compress several quite different claims. Please lay out a menu of the distinct principles the slogan could be used to assert — for instance, claims about what conditions are necessary for wrongdoing to persist, about the difference between causing and enabling or failing to prevent, about what "doing something" would even amount to in a globalized supply chain, and about who actually counts as a "good person" who does nothing. For each reading, say whether it is plausible and whether it, by itself, would support the conclusion that ordinary consumers are complicit in the labor abuses. I am especially interested in which readings are trivial or uninformative and which are substantive but controversial, and in whether the slogan does any work beyond appealing to guilt.
