I am a high school student writing my first extended essay about the nature of pain, and I have talked myself into an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept. I would be very grateful for a diagnosis.

In a unit on the neuroscience of pain, we learned about pain asymbolia: after certain brain injuries, patients say that they feel pain, and they can point to it and rate its intensity, but they do not seem to find it unpleasant. They do not wince or withdraw, and they often insist, in effect, that it hurts but they do not mind it. From this I formed the thought that the badness of a pain is not part of the pain itself but is added by the sufferer's reaction. When I wrote the thought out as an argument, however, it seemed to prove much too much:

(1) If a painful sensation can occur without being bad for the person who has it, then the badness of a pain is not in the pain itself; it is contributed by the person's response.
(2) Pain asymbolia shows that a painful sensation can occur without being bad for the person who has it.
(3) Therefore the badness of a pain is not in the pain itself.
(4) The reasoning from (1) to (3) is not special to pain; whatever we think is bad in itself, we can ask whether it could occur while the person whose state it is remains unbothered by it. So if the asymbolia case forces us to say that pain is bad only because of a response, parallel cases should force us to say the same about any other candidate.
(5) But the response — the dislike, aversion, or distress that is supposed to make the pain bad — is itself a state of the person. By the same reasoning, it can be bad only because of some further response to it, and that further response can be bad only because of a still further response, and so on without end.
(6) So the badness never bottoms out in anything. There is no state that is bad in itself, and a remark like "her pain is bad" merely reports that some chain of reactions happens to occur.
(7) That cannot be right. It would make the difference between ordinary pain and asymbolic pain a difference only in how many reactions there are, rather than a difference in whether the first pain itself mattered. And it would imply that there is nothing bad in itself about causing someone the most severe pain, provided one also arranges for the person not to mind it.

I would like to reject (7), and I find (1) and (2) very hard to give up, but something must be surrendered. Where does this argument go wrong, and which step would you reject — and what does rejecting it cost?
