In high school and I've got a rule I've been applying to any advice people give me: if someone calls a piece of advice "wise" or acts like it's automatically true because of "life experience," I ignore it unless they can walk me through the actual mechanism—what following it would change in a given situation, and why that change is an improvement. So with my grandpa's "don't make big decisions when you're angry," I'll accept it only once I can see how anger messes with judgment, not because he says it's wisdom. I do the same thing with books, podcasts, teachers. I know people older than me might be carrying real knowledge that can't be handed to me as a neat mechanism, and maybe some wisdom resists mechanism altogether, but I also suspect "wisdom" is often just a word people use when they don't want to justify something. Is this rule rationally defensible, or is it a bias I'm dressing up as epistemic hygiene? Is there a version of it that would keep the filtering power without defining actual wisdom out of existence before it can teach me anything? And how would I know, from the inside, whether I'm actually using that better version or just telling myself I am?
