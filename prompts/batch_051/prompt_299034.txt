I do two things outside school that almost never overlap—French and messing around with basic ML tutorials—but last week they did. My French teacher handed out a short, really patterned French poem and a stack of English translations, and the assignment was to argue about which one was best. The versions that kept the rhyme and line length mostly fudged the sense; the versions that nailed the meaning sounded flat and unrhymed. So it looked like there's no perfect translation, just a tradeoff.

The next night I was fiddling with a tiny model that uses two loss terms, something like L = semantic_loss + λ * form_loss, and it hit me that translating under formal constraints might be the same shape of problem. Big λ gives you sing-songy loose translations; small λ gives you accurate but flat ones; and the good ones are points on a Pareto frontier, where you can't improve one objective without making the other worse.

But I've got a hunch the analogy breaks in a specific spot. In ML the two objectives are usually fixed axes and the model mostly has to divide its representational budget between them (when it doesn't find some surprise re-representation that serves both at once). A rhyme, though, isn't just a constraint applied after the meaning is chosen—it can force a translator to find a meaning that wasn't on the table before, like the axes bend. So is 'translation under formal constraint is just constrained optimization' a deep structural parallel, or is that a superficial metaphor? What's the most concrete thing the optimization picture would predict about translations that an actual translator would say no to?
