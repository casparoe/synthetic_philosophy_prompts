Two practices sit side by side in the moral imagination. In the ancient Israelite scapegoat rite, a priest lays his hands on a goat, confesses the people's wrongs over it, and has the animal driven into the wilderness to carry those wrongs away; a modern reader tends to file this under ritual projection or magical thinking. In a modern criminal courtroom, we routinely describe an offender as paying a debt to society, and we regard it as a genuine good of a sentence that it gives the victim closure even where no one is compensated, deterred, or incapacitated in any measurable way. Both practices seem to treat guilt as a thing that can be transferred onto a substitute or cancelled by the infliction of suffering. Is there a principled difference that keeps the courtroom practice while rejecting the scapegoat rite, or does the modern practice inherit the logic of the scapegoat in a softened form? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. If you defend the asymmetry, defend it against this case: a serious crime is punished solely to restore a felt moral balance, and the sentence is known to deter no one, incapacitate no one, and compensate no one. If you would not defend punishment in that case, say where the line falls and what principle places it there.
