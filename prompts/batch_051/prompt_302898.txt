The question: In a typical endowment-effect experiment, subjects who are given an inexpensive object such as a coffee mug later demand roughly twice as much to part with it as subjects who were not given the object are willing to pay to acquire it, even though the object was assigned at random. Does this gap between willingness to accept and willingness to pay show that ownership makes people's preferences irrational? Set out the strongest argument for treating the gap as a bias, the strongest argument for treating it as a legitimate expression of genuine preference, and then give your own assessment of what the evidence supports.

The student wrote: "Since the mugs are assigned at random, owners and non-owners should value them identically if preferences are stable and independent of whether one happens to own the object. The gap is therefore standardly cited as evidence against stable, reference-independent preferences. If two otherwise similar people value the same mug differently merely because one of them happens to hold the title to it, then at least one valuation is being moved by a feature of the situation with no obvious normative relevance; that is the core of the case for calling the gap a bias. The standard explanation offered for this pattern is risk aversion. Owners treat giving up the mug as a kind of gamble and demand a premium for bearing it, while buyers are averse to parting with their money; since risk aversion of this sort is widely accepted as rational, the mug gap should be accepted as rational too, and calling it an endowment effect is itself misleading. There is also a more positive story: genuine attachment can make an object one already owns legitimately worth more than an identical object on a shelf, because the owned object has entered one's daily routines and projects in a way the unowned object has not. That story is weakened by the fact that subjects often display a gap for objects they did not choose and have owned for only minutes, but the bias reading is also weakened by evidence that experienced traders show much smaller gaps. My assessment is that the gap is real but not troubling. Since there is no objectively correct price for a mug, the difference between the two valuations is outside the reach of rational criticism. The most the evidence entitles us to say is that the gap reveals loss aversion rather than irrationality."
