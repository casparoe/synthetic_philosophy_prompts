I am taking a philosophy of religion course on petitionary prayer, and we have been working through the tension between asking God for things and holding that God is omniscient and immutable. I have tried to write down my own verdicts on a range of cases, and I want to know what principle, if any, captures them. I also want to test them against the principle I extract from a short passage of our assigned reading.

Aquinas, Summa Theologica, Second Part of the Second Part, Question 83, Article 2, in the translation we use in class:

"In order to throw light on this question we must consider that Divine providence disposes not only what effects shall take place, but also from what causes and in what order these effects shall proceed. Now among other causes human acts are the causes of certain effects. Wherefore it must be that men do certain actions, not that thereby they may change the Divine disposition, but that by those actions they may achieve certain effects according to the order of the Divine disposition: and the same is to be said of natural causes."

Aquinas then applies this to prayer: praying, he thinks, is one of those human acts — a created cause through which God has chosen to give what he was always going to give, not a way of bending an unchanging will. From this passage I extract what I will call the means principle: God orders both outcomes and their causes, and petitionary prayer can be one of the appointed causes of the very outcome it asks for; it neither informs God nor changes God's mind.

My verdicts, which I set down before I tried to make them fit this principle, are these.

(1) A friend has high-risk surgery tomorrow, and I ask God that it go well. I am sure this is fitting, and I take the prayer to be a genuine request about an outcome, not merely a device for altering my own attitude.

(2) I have just sat an exam, and before the grade arrives I ask God that it went well. This feels no different in kind from praying before the exam; that the answers are already fixed does not trouble me.

(3) After learning that the exam went badly, I ask God that it had gone well. I cannot do this; asking God to make the past different seems to make no sense.

(4) My grandfather died years ago, apparently without faith, and I ask God that he had in fact been reconciled to God before his death. I do pray this, and I cannot dismiss it as I dismiss (3); but it is the verdict I find hardest to defend, since the fact is just as settled as in (3) and only unknown to me.

(5) Running late to the hospital, I ask God for a parking space. I find this unfitting, although I cannot say exactly why, since God's providence concerns small things as well as large.

(6) I ask God for a job and for next month's rent. This does not feel trivial at all; it feels like exactly what petition is for.

(7) I ask God, with no external outcome specified, to make me patient and wise. This seems always fitting; it is the easiest case for the view that prayer changes the one who prays rather than God's plan.

(8) I ask God that my brother, who is refusing treatment, change his mind. This feels fitting, but it puzzles me: if God brings about my brother's change of mind, I cannot see how his freedom is preserved, and if God does not, then what is the prayer asking for?

(9) I ask God that an amputated limb regrow. I do not think this is wrong, but it feels more honest to fold it into a general submission to God's will than to treat it as the same kind of request as the surgery in (1).

The means principle fits (1), (6), and (7) cleanly enough, and perhaps (5), if one holds that God has not appointed parking spaces to be obtained through petition. But I cannot see how it handles (2) and (4), which concern settled facts and yet elicit different verdicts from me, except by one of two moves: either appealing to God's eternity so that the temporal order of prayer and outcome does not matter, or else rejecting both (2) and (4) as too late because a cause must precede its effect. Does the means principle force me to abandon (2), or to abandon (4), or is there a principled repair that keeps both without collapsing into the view that the timing of prayer is irrelevant? More generally, is there one coherent principle behind all my verdicts, and if not, which verdicts have to go? Finally, what should the repaired principle say about a case I have been avoiding: praying now that a wrongful conviction from years ago be exposed and publicly corrected in the coming year, where the past is in view only through a future disclosure? Please state the final principle precisely, run it through each of my nine cases, and say explicitly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.
