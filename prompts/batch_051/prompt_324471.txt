I'm taking a moral psychology seminar and we've hit self-deception, and I've been going around in circles about whether it's even coherent. The textbook version of the paradox goes: deceiving someone normally means getting them to believe something you know is false, so self-deception would require me to simultaneously know the truth and hide it from myself. But that seems impossible if the mind is a single unified knower. Yet people obviously manage to stay in denial about a partner's cheating, or a terminal diagnosis, or their own role in a bad breakup. I don't just want the boring "it happens, so it must be possible" answer though. I want you to make an argument for a specific position: that self-deception is possible only if we abandon the interpersonal-lying model entirely, and that the sense of paradox comes from treating belief as a single yes/no state rather than as a loose, distributed, temporally extended pattern of attention, memory access, and evidence weighting. Defend this as the best way to dissolve the paradox, not just one possible way.

Also, make the argument genuinely contestable—steelman the person who says this is just motivated irrationality, or that self-deception is an illusion folk psychology takes too seriously. And please separate what is reasonably settled from what is still contested among people working on this, especially on whether the deflationary approach actually explains the cases or just relabels them. I'd rather have you flag a live dispute than make it sound like everyone agrees.
