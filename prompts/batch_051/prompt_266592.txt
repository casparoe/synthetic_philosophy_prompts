I am a philosophy professor serving on the committee charged with interpreting a university policy on data-based decision-making. The policy dates from 2015, well before large language models of the present kind were in use, and reads as follows:

"No unit of the university may base a decision about what would occur under a contemplated intervention—for example, a change in admissions policy, a curriculum reform, or a new student-support program—on any model estimated solely from observational data. Any such causal claim must be supported by an explicitly identified structural causal model whose assumptions about confounding are stated and whose target effect is shown to be identifiable in that model; a claim about a counterfactual outcome for a particular unit requires, in addition, a fully specified functional model over the relevant variables."

We must now decide how the policy applies to a case its drafters did not anticipate. A dean's office has adopted a large language model to advise on instructional policy, and when asked whether replacing large introductory lectures with seminars of fifteen would improve four-year graduation rates, the system answers fluently and confidently in causal terms. The model was trained only on a corpus of text; it has never seen interventional data, no one has specified a structural causal model for the outcome in question, and the system has no representation of confounders, of an effect's identifiability, or of what a do-operator would denote. Its defenders say the policy cannot reach the system, because the clause was written for statistical models fitted to outcome variables, whereas this system is a text model whose causal-sounding sentences are merely high-probability completions. Its critics reply that the policy's evident purpose is to bar confident causal conclusions from anything weaker than an explicit causal model.

Apply the policy to this case, and give exactly five numbered entries, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. The first four entries should state, respectively, the textual, purposive, original-intention, and reasonable-reader positions on whether the policy is breached, each with the strongest objection to that position folded into the same entry. The fifth entry should state which approach ought to control here, and why.
