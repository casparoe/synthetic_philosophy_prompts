At our department colloquium last term, two colleagues — a political scientist who works on state failure and an ancient historian — became locked in an argument that, as the only philosopher in the room, I was asked to referee. I found I could not settle it to my own satisfaction, so I am passing the exchange on to you. The question was not whether the Roman Empire would fall; they had agreed to set hindsight aside. The question was what a rational forecaster should have believed, as of Trajan's accession in A.D. 98, about how much longer the imperial system would last. I reproduce their exchange as faithfully as I can.

The political scientist began: "The rational method here is the outside view. Do not start with Rome's peculiar virtues — its legions, its roads, its law, its succession machinery — start with the base rate. In A.D. 98 Rome was a territorially large, multiethnic, conquest-consolidated Mediterranean empire. Its predecessors in that class, the Achaemenid, Seleucid, and Ptolemaic monarchies, each lasted on the order of two to three centuries from consolidation. Rome had by then been the Mediterranean's dominant power for well over two centuries. The base rate therefore tells a rational forecaster not to credit the story of Roman exceptionalism; he should have expected a comparable shelf life. The burden is on anyone who claims Rome was different."

The historian answered: "That is not a method; it is a discretionary choice of comparison group dressed up as an objective frequency. Why 'Mediterranean empire' rather than 'sedentary agrarian state'? Bring in Egypt or China, and the base rate lengthens dramatically. Why not 'empire whose elite had produced an autonomous legal and civic order'? Then the class contains almost no one but Rome, and the base rate is just Rome's own still-unfinished case. And your class quietly omits the Parthian Empire, a large neighboring power that had by then already outlived each of the regimes you cite and was still standing in A.D. 98. Depending on how you slice the class, the outside view says Rome is a geriatric, or a newborn, or nothing at all. It constrains no judgment until the judgment has already been made."

The political scientist replied: "The reference-class problem is real, but you are overstating it; actuarial tables face the same difficulty. The standard solution is to choose the narrowest class for which the frequencies are still statistically usable — narrow enough to be relevant to the case, broad enough not to be a sample of two. A class of territorially large, multiethnic, tribute-extracting empires, defined in advance by structural variables and without reference to how long any of them actually lasted, is not gerrymandered. It includes the Achaemenids, the Seleucids, the Ptolemies, and other comparable cases, and it yields a meaningful survival base rate."

The historian countered: "Now you are doing inside-view history and calling it class selection. 'Durable structural features' — scale, multiethnicity, tribute extraction, limits of communication — is a theory of what makes states endure, and you have chosen it over rival covariates: elite circulation, legal integration, civic ideology, which a historian would say matter for Rome. That choice is exactly the case-specific judgment the outside view promised to eliminate. And once you admit the covariates a historian would admit, the class collapses to a sample of one. The narrowest-class principle, applied to historical empires, leaves no reliable frequency at all; the moment you retreat to a broader class, you have imported a causal theory. The outside view has not eliminated judgment — it has hidden it in the choice of reference class."

The political scientist answered: "I grant that it does not eliminate judgment; that was never the claim. The outside view is a discipline, not an algorithm. It forces the analyst to state the base rate before the story, and then it forces the story to say which features of the case defeat the base rate and why those features could have been identified without knowing the outcome. That is strictly better than unconstrained uniqueness-narratives. Historians find every empire unique, and most such uniqueness is self-flattering at the time."

The historian said: "I accept that demanding a base rate is a useful discipline. But consider what is now left of your position. The base rate that disciplines the story is itself generated by a choice of class, and in any genuinely contested forecast that choice will be driven by the analyst's prior sense of what the case resembles. So the outside view will systematically produce frequencies that track the analyst's inside view. It is not a forecasting method with a determinate answer; it is a way of laundering an inside view into a base rate and then admiring the objectivity of the result. You began by saying a rational forecaster in A.D. 98 should have shortened his estimate of Rome. That last step is false precision."

They never came to agreement. Please adjudicate the exchange. Go through it move by move and say who is right about which specific point, and where each speaker overreaches or concedes too much. Then say what, if anything, both of them are missing. Finally, give a committed overall verdict: which speaker is more right, and why. I am not asking for a survey of positions; take a definite stand and argue for it.
