I'm a PhD student and I have a belief-forming policy I'd like you to sanity-check. When I read a paper late at night, I sometimes find myself fairly convinced that, say, a key step in the argument is valid, while also registering that I'm tired and that my error rate on exactly this kind of judgment goes way up when I'm tired. My rule is that I don't lower my confidence in the first-order judgment just because of that higher-order evidence. My reasoning is that the tiredness was already 'priced in': I formed the judgment from inside a tired mind, knowing I was tired, so to subtract again would be double-counting the same evidence. My officemate says this is textbook epistemic akrasia — I'm believing p while also thinking my total evidence doesn't support p — and that it's no different from knowingly overbetting a weak hand. Is my policy defensible? Is there a coherent restatement of it, or a principled discount rate that avoids both akrasia and double counting? And does the enkratic principle even apply to a judgment formed before I noticed the higher-order evidence, or only to what I do once I notice it?
