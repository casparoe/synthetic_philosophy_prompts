I have been noticing a pattern in the small communities and workplaces I have belonged to, and I have not been able to find a satisfying concept for it. Tell me, with a definite view rather than an even-handed survey, whether this pattern has a name and, more importantly, how its boundaries should be drawn.

The pattern has two stages. In the first stage, a group settles into doing something one particular way simply because it is useful that everyone does it the same way, and any of the available ways would have served as well. It might be where a shared tool is put back, which side of a staircase people walk on, or how meetings are opened. At this stage, someone who deviates is treated as at most a mild nuisance: the whole point is that things run more smoothly when everyone matches.

In the second stage, the original reason for needing uniformity has quietly disappeared. The workspace has been reorganized, the old tool replaced, the founding members gone, and the newcomers have no memory of why the practice arose. Yet the practice does not fade; it hardens. A deviation now provokes real irritation, mockery, or gossip. People who are not inconvenienced in the least join in the criticism. Conforming becomes a way of showing that one belongs, and nonconformity reads as disloyalty or bad character. The strange part is that no one, asked privately, can give a live justification for the practice. They know the original rationale is obsolete, and they do not seem to care about the behavior on its own merits. But each of them expects the others to take deviation badly, and so each takes it badly too. The enforcement keeps itself going through the expectation of enforcement, long after the function that started it has vanished.

What I want to know is whether this two-stage configuration has an established name of its own; if it does not, I want you to propose one and define it. I am aware of the standard philosophical account of shared practices that exist because people merely want to fall in with what others do, where the choice among equally good options is arbitrary and no punishment is needed. I am also aware, more loosely, of the idea of rules that people treat as binding and feel entitled to enforce on one another. What I cannot find is a treatment of the transition and the hybrid: the practice that began as the first kind of thing and is now kept alive as the second, even though its original point is gone and almost nobody privately endorses it. Does it stop being the first kind at some definite moment? Is it even the second kind, given that nobody privately endorses what they enforce? Are these two kinds distinct, or are they two ends of a continuum — and if a continuum, what is the dimension along which a practice slides from one to the other? Answer as someone who is willing to be wrong: take a definite position on each question and defend it, and identify the boundary cases that would test the distinction.
