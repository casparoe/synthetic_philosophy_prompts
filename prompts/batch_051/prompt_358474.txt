I spent thirty years in a lab where 'observation' did a lot of quiet work, and I've been reading Sellars recently, so here's what's been bothering me. Our old lab manual had this rule: 'An observer shall record only what is given in immediate sensory experience; any report that goes beyond such experience, however slight, shall be marked as an inference.' It was written when photographs were pasted into notebooks, and before much thought was given to how training effects what you literally see.

Here's a case I doubt the drafters foresaw. A Gram-stained smear is displayed on a monitor through a digital slide scanner. A trainee looks at the screen and writes 'pink dots in pairs'; a senior microbiologist looks at the same screen, with no extra instruments, and writes 'Gram-negative diplococci'. Under the rule, which entries are observations and which are inferences? Work through what the words require, what the purpose suggests, and what the drafters would have said if asked. Then tell me whether the observation/inference distinction the rule rests on can survive, or whether the 'given' it assumes is a myth, and what we should do with the rule. Answer in British English, please.
