I have been reading about an old and stubborn problem in philosophy of science: verisimilitude, or truthlikeness—the idea that one false theory can be closer to the truth than another false theory, and that we should be able to say which is closer. The difficulty, in a sentence, is that specifying the truth well enough to measure distance from it seems to require knowing the very thing our theories are groping toward. Suppose a counterfactual that removes exactly that difficulty, and reason through what follows.

Starting in 2026, a device—call it a verisometer—appears simultaneously in every university, research institute, and corporate R&D lab on Earth. Given any two rival scientific theories in the same domain, it reliably reports which of the two has greater verisimilitude: which is closer to the complete, true account of that domain. It gives only a ranking, never a numerical score; it offers no explanation, no derivation, and no fragment of the truth itself. It is known only to be correct, and every attempt to reverse-engineer it has failed. In every other respect the world is unchanged: scientists remain the usual mixture of ambitious, conformist, and contrarian, and journals, funding agencies, and universities keep their current structures and incentives.

Predict, making your chain of reasoning explicit, how scientific practice and institutions change over the next fifty years. To organize the answer, address at least these questions. How does theory choice shift in fields where two rival frameworks both fit the current data, such as competing interpretations of quantum mechanics or conflicting macroeconomic models? Does the device mostly accelerate consensus, or does it entrench whichever framework gains an early lead? What happens to the prestige of novel predictions, to exploratory or heterodox theorizing, and to the training of young scientists, once everyone knows a comparison test exists but no one knows why it works?
