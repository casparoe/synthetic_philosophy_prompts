I'm taking a class on Hume and religious epistemology, and we've spent two weeks on the miracles chapter of the Enquiry. My professor has been feeding us fake miracle cases and giving her verdicts, but she refuses to say which rule she is using. So far she has ruled: a resurrection reported by one otherwise honest ancient witness, no; a crying statue attested by a crowd of pilgrims, no; a modern drowning victim revived after thirty minutes, reported by paramedics, no because the timeline is probably wrong; a cancer remission with before-and-after scans and secular doctors, no because the scans could be mistaken and a miracle is the least likely explanation; a solar impossibility reported by numerous, educated, non-religious witnesses, no because it conflicts with what she calls the fixed and uniform experience of nature. When someone asked about the exact case where the falsehood of the testimony would be more improbable than the event itself, she said that would in principle win, but she doubted such a case would actually show up.

The new case she assigned is this: several independent labs in different countries, with no shared religious commitment, report a well-controlled, publicly reanalyzed result that appears to violate a known physical law. The falsehood of their collective testimony would require either a coordinated multi-lab fabrication or a calibration error so enormous that it would be, by ordinary scientific standards, more improbable than the event. If she is being consistent with her earlier verdicts, should she believe this one? Which earlier case is the closest analog, and if her precedents underdetermine the answer, what principle is she adopting with each option?
