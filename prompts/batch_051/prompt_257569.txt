I'm a retired biochemist, spent thirty years in the lab, and only recently started reading philosophy of mind serious—I got pulled in by the puzzle of self-deception: how can a person seem to know something and yet manage to beleive the opposite? It reminds me of a receptor that has two contradictory binding states at once; thermodynamically, that can't happen unless the system is doing something more complicated than it looks. I'm giving a talk to a mixed group next month and want to be ready for the question period.

My thesis is that strict self-deception, the kind where a person literally holds a belief p and a belief not-p at the same time, is impossible as a single mental state. What we call self-deception is really a motley of mechanisms—biased evidence-gathering, selective attention, self-serving memory, motivated reasoning—that never produce two contradictory beliefs simultaneously. The audience is mostly philosophers of mind and epistemologists, plus two cognitive psychologists who work on motivated reasoning. One of the philosophers wrote an article defending the standard view that self-deception is a genuine, unified phenomenon, and she'll be there. They're not openly hostile, but they will press hard on whether I'm just relabeling the phenomenon rather than explaining it away.
