Im a science journalist working on a piece about how people think about their own choices, and I keep noticing an odd asymmetry. We are totally comfortable putting probabilities on other people's decisions: a political reporter says a senator is 70% to vote yes, an analyst gives odds on a coach going for it on fourth down, a manager says a colleague is 80% to take the new job. But ask the same people for a probability about their own upcoming choice — say, what are the odds you'll accept the offer, how likely are you to donate — and they squirm. They say they cant put a number on it, that it is up to them, or that giving it a number would already be making the decision. There's even a slogan in decision theory, deliberation crowds out prediction.

You're an expert on decision theory and the philosophy of action. I want you to think carefully about whether this asymmetry is justified or just a psychological tic. Start by brainstorming the strongest candidate justifications — for instance, that you have more evidence about yourself, or that assigning a probability would change the act, or something about the open future — and then actually evaluate them. Is there a real difference between predicting your own choices and predicting someone else's? Would a perfectly rational observer with full information about you be entitled to a sharper probability about what you'll do than you are? If the asymmetry can't be defended, which side should give way: we start putting odds on our own decisions, or we stop being so easy about predicting everyone else's?
