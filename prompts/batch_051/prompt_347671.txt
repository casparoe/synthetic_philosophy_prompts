I have been working through the archival-turn literature, and I find myself pulled between a systematic view and an intuition I cannot shake. The systematic view, associated with the account of the archive in Foucault’s archaeological writings, is that an archive is not a passive repository but a set of rules governing what statements can be produced, preserved, and made available as discourse; silences in the sources are therefore not mere gaps to be filled but part of the apparatus that produces what counts as truth. This view is powerful because it explains why certain people and events are absent from records without reducing that absence to accident or individual bias, and it offers a unified account of how power operates through institutions. Yet my firm intuition is that some silences are real erasures: there were people, communities, and forms of life that existed, that generated traces since destroyed or scattered, and our task as historians or archivists is to recover their voices, not merely to redescribe the archive’s exclusions. The theory seems to imply that the attempt to recover the silenced is naive, even impossible, because the archive constitutes rather than merely hides them. I want a negotiated verdict: should this intuition be debunked as a romantic residue, should the theory be amended to distinguish constitutive silence from recoverable erasure, or are the two actually compatible once the terms silence and recovery are clarified? Walk me through what each move would require and which is best justified.
