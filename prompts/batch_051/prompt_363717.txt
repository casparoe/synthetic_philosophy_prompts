I'm a senior undergrad TA-ing intro to ethics this term (and also sitting in on a seminar on deontological ethics, so I know the material just enough to tie myself in knots), and next week I got handed the fifty-minute section on the paradox of deontology—the puzzle that someone can think a constraint like "don't kill" exists because persons matter, and still think you can't violate that constraint even to stop five other people from violating it. The class is mostly first-years who've done one week on Mill and one week on Kant, so they get the broad strokes and not much else. I don't need the paradox explained to me; I need a plan for explaining it to them, and I keep going in circles on the order. What has to come first and what depends on what? Like, do I need the distinction between evaluating your own action and evaluating how the world ends up on the table before I even introduce a deontological constraint, or is that backwards? The professor wants the whole thing to hang on one example. The obvious move is some version of "it's wrong to kill one innocent person even to stop five other murders," but I already ran the trolley problem into the ground teaching Mill, and I'm worried they'll just pattern-match instead of actually feeling the paradox. Should I still use it but reframe it, or is there a better carrying case? And what does that example need to have and not have?

Also: what misconceptions should I expect? I'd guess the big ones are "deontology is just rule worship" and "then deontologists care more about keeping their own hands clean than about the victims," and maybe "this only applies to killing, so lying and breaking promises are fine." I want to know exactly where in the sequence to head each one off before it hardens, not after the section is over. And what's a single end-of-section question that would seperate the students who actually got the paradox from the ones who can only repeat that the ends don't justify the means? Finally, tell me what I should deliberately leave out at this level so I don't accidentally make it grad-school-y.
