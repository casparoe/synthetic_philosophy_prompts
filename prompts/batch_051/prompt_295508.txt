A friend and I have been arguing on and off about whether the question "What if everyone did that?" is a sound test for the wrongness of an action, where the argument is understood in the line running from Kant through Marcus Singer and Hare rather than as a piece of rhetorical common sense. I have been keeping a private list of the standard objections I have encountered, partly to make sure I am not being too impressed by the test's vividness alone. So far the list has six entries. (1) The description problem: the verdict depends on how finely the act-type or maxim is specified, and a narrow enough description ("everyone with exactly my history in exactly these circumstances may do this") will nearly always universalize without bad consequences. (2) The threshold problem: the bad outcome appears only past a critical mass, and no single instance makes a difference to whether that threshold is reached, as with one walker on the grass or one person who stays home on election day. (3) The division-of-labor problem: universalizing a specialized role—everyone farms, or everyone becomes a doctor—leads to disaster only because the other complementary roles have been removed, not because the role is itself defective. (4) The overdemandingness problem: the same logic that condemns acts whose universal performance would be bad also seems to require any act whose universal performance would be good, so if everyone gave away most of their income many serious harms would disappear, and the test threatens to make heroic giving obligatory and to leave no room for the supererogatory. (5) The rule-utilitarian collapse worry: where the test does seem to work, it is tracking rule-utilitarian reasoning about which rules it would be good to adopt, and where it departs from that reasoning is exactly where it is least plausible. (6) The conceptual objection: in Kant and Hare universalizability is a consistency condition on what a single agent can will or prescribe across all relevantly similar cases, not an empirical forecast of what would happen if many people acted that way, so treating it as "what if everyone did that?" replaces a requirement on the agent's own willing or prescribing with a consequentialist estimate.

I am looking for one more objection with a structure none of these six has—not a rewording, a special case, or a combination of items already on the list. In particular, it should not ultimately run through act-description, thresholds or causal impotence, role complementarity, the overdemandingness/supererogation point, rule-utilitarian equivalence, or the distinction between prescribing and predicting. I do not need citations; I want the structure itself. Then be honest about how good the new objection is: if it is sound, does it show that the generalization argument fails on its own terms, or merely that "What if everyone did that?" works only as a rough heuristic with ceteris paribus clauses—and how would one distinguish those two conclusions?
