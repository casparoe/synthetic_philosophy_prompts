I keep tripping over a move people make when they try to read Buddhist ethics as consequentialist, and I want to know wether the thing underneath it has a name.

The setup is this. Someone reconstructs the Buddhist project as: dukkha is caused by craving and clinging, so the point of ethical practice is to minimize dukkha, and the right act is whatever minimizes it. That sounds like a flavor of negative utilitarianism with rebirth and nonhuman sentient beings thrown in. But the same person will also insist that a practitioner shouldn't be attached to the outcome of their action. Still pretty standard. The weird part comes next: if the criterion says to aim at the best outcome, your aiming can itself be redescribed as clinging. But if you stop aiming in order to avoid that clinging, you're no longer doing the thing the consequentialist reading says is right. So the position seems to demand the best outcome while not being invested in the best outcome. And not-being-invested can itself become something you cling to—you can get proud of your non-clinging, guard it, look down on people who care too much. The standard reply is: then let go of that too. Wich just iterates: do the best action, don't cling to doing the best action, don't cling to not clinging to it, and so on.

I've seen the same shape in meditation contexts. A teacher says desire is the problem. Someone asks whether the desire to be free of desire is also a problem. The teacher says something like yes, but it's the last one you set down, a tool you put away once the job is done. But that last-one move doesn't explain what stops the regress; it just asserts that it stops somewhere.

Then there's a second, sneakier version. Whenever an action meant to reduce suffering fails, the diagnosis comes back that the doer was secretly attached to the result. If the act works, the theory takes the credit; if it fails, the theory blames the agent's leftover clinging. That makes the consequentialist reading look unfalsifiable, and I can't tell whether that's the same problem as the regress or a different one.

Is there an established name for this configuration? I'm not looking for a slogan like attachment to non-attachment. I want to know whether someone has carved it precisely. The nearest things I know are the paradox of hedonism, where aiming directly at happiness seems to defeat happiness, and maybe a Buddhist distinction between kinds of craving, but the paradox of hedonism doesn't have the iterated let-go-of-letting-go structure, and my memory of the craving distinctions is fuzzy. If no established concept fits, coin one and define it anyway, and make the definition do the work of separating it from those near neighbors.

Please structure the response in four sections, in this order, with rough lengths:

1. What the thing is (150–200 words): restate the phenomenon precisely and say whether it's one phenomenon or two related ones.
2. Nearest concepts and how they miss (250–350 words): go through the paradox of hedonism, attachment to non-attachment, spiritual bypassing, and anything else adjacent; for each, what it captures and what it doesn't.
3. The carve and a name (250–350 words): either identify the established term or coin one; define it; then give boundary cases—cases that look like the thing but aren't, and cases that don't look like it but are.
4. Does it bite? (150–200 words): whether this is a real problem for the consequentialist reconstruction of Buddhist ethics, or whether it dissolves once you distinguish a criterion of rightness from a decision procedure (or psychological attachment from normative aim), and where that reply is weakest.
