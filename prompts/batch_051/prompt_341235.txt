I am a high school student, and I have a rule about my own beliefs that I would like you to assess. The rule is this: whenever I notice that a belief would make me feel safer, less guilty, or less anxious, I deliberately hold my confidence at exactly the level the evidence supports and no higher. If the evidence is mixed or thin, I remain agnostic even when a small act of trust would relieve a lot of worry. I apply this mainly to personal matters: whether a friend is drifting away, whether a stress symptom is serious, whether I probably did well on a submitted assignment, and occasionally to larger questions about whether things will work out. In each case, I treat the emotional benefit of the belief as completely irrelevant. I do not let comfort raise my confidence by a single notch. I demand positive evidence. The rule has costs: friends sometimes say I am refusing to trust them, and I sometimes wonder whether I am just protecting myself from disappointment under the guise of epistemic discipline.

My question is whether this policy is actually defensible as a standing epistemic habit, or whether it is too sweeping. I am aware that one tradition in the ethics of belief says that a person is never entitled to believe something on insufficient evidence, because beliefs shape action and because forming beliefs by ignoring doubts weakens the habit of honest inquiry. I am also aware that another tradition says that when a person faces a live, forced, and momentous option that intellectual evidence cannot decide, it can be permissible to let one's passional nature resolve it. I do not want a summary of that debate; I want you to evaluate my rule as a policy for someone like me, with my anxious psychology and limited time for investigation.
