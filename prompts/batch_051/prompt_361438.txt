Lin-Manuel Miranda's Hamilton is a biographical musical that is at once indebted to its source — it takes its narrative shape from Ron Chernow's biography — and openly unhistorical in its means: actors of color play the founders and the Schuyler sisters, the score is built from hip-hop and musical-theater pastiche, and nearly three decades of politics and domestic life are squeezed into a couple of hours. Its closing number is itself about the ethics of historical representation, asking in effect who gets to tell the story of the dead and toward what end. I am teaching an undergraduate course on the ethics of historical fiction and biopics, and I want to use Hamilton as the sustained case study for the course's central philosophical question: what, if anything, do we owe the real dead when we dramatize them? Do the dead retain interests — in their reputations, their secrets, their own self-understanding — that a later age can set back; is there a duty of fidelity to the historical record, and is that duty owed to the dead themselves, to audiences as knowers of the past, or to descendants and communities with a stake in how they are remembered? Bring this lens to the show's concrete choices: its recasting and idiom, its compression and causal simplifications, its decision to pit a driven Hamilton against a comparatively sympathetic Burr, its treatment of the Schuyler sisters (including a scene that pauses over the placement of a comma in one of Hamilton's letters to Angelica), and the near-invisibility of the enslaved people whose labor sustained the households the characters actually ran. I want a reading that is faithful in both directions: do not reduce the musical to an illustration of a philosophical position, and do not bend the ethics to fit the musical. Where does the show illuminate the philosophical problem better than the arguments alone do, where does it complicate or resist the demand for fidelity, and where does it pay a real ethical cost that its defenders too readily wave away? End by saying what Hamilton adds to the debate about biopics, historical fiction, and truth that abstract argument leaves out.
