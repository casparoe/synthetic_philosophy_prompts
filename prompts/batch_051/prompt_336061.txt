I'm a PhD student in political philosophy, and I'm prepping a handout for an undergrad seminar on collective responsibility. The week's question is whether it makes sense to say a nation today is guilty for things done by earlier generations, or whether "guilt" is just the wrong word for what's going on. What keeps happening in discussion is that my students use "guilt," "responsibility," "liability," and "shame" interchangably, and then talk straight past each other: two students will both say "the nation isn't guilty," but one means "so it owes nothing" and the other means "we still have obligations to repair." So I want a sorting instrument that classifies people by what they actually think, not by what they call themselves.

Could you build me a decision tree, three or four branch nodes and five to seven leaves, where every node is a concrete question a student can answer without knowing any of the literature. Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: lay out the diagnostic questions first, show which combinations of answers are live and which are empty, then locate the actual positions as leaves. Use at least two concrete cases to anchor the questions (say, a German born in 1990 vis-a-vis the Third Reich, an American born after Jim Crow, or a French person born in the 1990s and the war in Algeria; pick whichever two do the most separating work). At least one node has to ask whether collectives as such can be subjects of guilt or only individuals, and at least one has to seperate backward-looking guilt from forward-looking repair obligations, because that conflation is the thing I'm trying to kill. Jaspers' four concepts, criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt, should appear as leaves where they earn their place, but don't start from them; make the questions generate them. And be honest about the ones that don't fit: I suspect metaphysical guilt sits awkwardly in a tree aimed at undergrads, and maybe political guilt is hard to keep distinct from a forward-looking "we should repair" view once you strip away the theological-sounding language. I also want at least one strict-individualist leaf and at least one forward-looking responsibility-without-guilt leaf; beyond that you decide.

After the tree, give me a short note on the empty cells: for each combination of answers that no actual position occupies, say whether it's empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Also flag any pairs of leaves the tree can't tell apart, and say what extra question would split them if you had one more node. Keep it usable for a discussion section; no lit review, it's a handout, not a syllabus.
