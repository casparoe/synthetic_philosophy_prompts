I'm drafting an essay for a seminar in the philosophy of astrobiology, and I'd be grateful for substantive criticism of the argument before I revise it. The essay's central claim is that "How likely is it that intelligent extraterrestrial life exists?" is not one question but three, and that much of the debate around the Fermi paradox arises from sliding between them unnoticed. I distinguish a frequency question (what fraction of roughly Earth-like planets in a specified reference class actually come to harbor intelligent life), a propensity question (what the underlying physical and biological processes make more or less likely for any particular such planet), and an evidential question (what beliefs are warranted by our observations, given the way those observations were selected). The essay then argues that standard multiplicative estimates run the first two questions together: an abiogenesis rate inferred from the apparently early appearance of life in Earth's history is a frequency claim about a single planet's history, and it becomes a propensity claim only if Earth is treated as a representative draw from an ensemble of similar worlds—which is one of the points at issue.

The essay goes on to argue that our own existence is a heavily conditioned observation, since we necessarily observe ourselves on a world where life arose, so the bare fact that life arose here licenses only weak conclusions about how common or rare life is elsewhere. By the same token, the absence of detected signals from technological civilizations constrains our estimates only relative to assumptions about how detectable such civilizations would be, how long they persist, and how thoroughly we have looked. The essay concludes that once the three questions are kept apart, much of the apparent paradox is an artifact of requesting a single probability without specifying a probability space; pessimism about our own future and pessimism about the existence of others turn out to be answers to different questions.

I would especially value comments on three points: (a) whether the three-way distinction does genuine work or merely relabels familiar probability concepts; (b) whether this treatment is unfair to the strongest version of Great Filter reasoning, which may already respect some of these distinctions; and (c) whether the deflationary conclusion overreaches—perhaps a rational agent can still combine frequency, propensity, and evidential considerations into one well-defined credence after all. Please identify the weakest step in the argument and say what an unsympathetic reader would do with it.
