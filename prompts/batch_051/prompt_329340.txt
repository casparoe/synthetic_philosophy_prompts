You are a professional philosopher working on skill acquisition, expertise, and the nature of epistemic norms. Think carefully before committing to an answer, and begin by distinguishing the possible readings of the practice rather than settling on the first one.

Consider a recognizable style of applied-rationality training that models its pedagogy on martial arts. Students repeatedly drill isolated techniques: they assign numerical probabilities to trivia or forecasting questions and are scored for calibration; they run pre-mortems on planned projects; they practice steelmanning arguments they oppose; and they write predictions down in advance so the predictions cannot be quietly revised later. The programs describe these drills as katas — fixed forms repeated until they become automatic — and they claim that the skill built in the training setting transfers to reasoning in the field: investing, hiring, medical decisions, one's own research.

There is, however, a live dispute in martial arts about exactly this kind of training. Many instructors argue that kata practiced without a resisting opponent does not transfer to live sparring, and that what transfers is pressure-tested sparring itself. The rationality programs seem to take the optimistic side of that dispute for reasoning, but without offering anything that obviously corresponds to sparring.

Reconstruct the implicit theory of rationality and of skill transfer that this pedagogy presupposes. What kind of thing must rationality be for decontextualized drills to improve it? Candidates might include the claims that rationality decomposes into general, context-independent sub-skills; that these sub-skills are causally upstream of good judgment rather than merely correlated with it; and that repeated low-stakes exercise of a sub-skill strengthens it for high-stakes, messy situations. Then separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined. Could a situated-cognition view of expertise — on which reasoning skill is largely domain-specific pattern recognition built through feedback-rich practice in the target domain — accept this curriculum, or does the curriculum quietly rule that view out? Finally, say what the training would have to look like if the situated view were adopted instead.
