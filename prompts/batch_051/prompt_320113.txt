I've been drafting a short essay for a rationalist-adjacent audience on fun theory, and I'd like structural comments on it rather than line edits — in particular, comments on where the argument is load-bearing and weakest, and on whether its central concept is doing real work. I'm not looking for reading suggestions or citations. The current draft is below.

Against the Solved Future

Discussions of fun theory often begin from a worry about specification: if an optimizer targets pleasure, or preference satisfaction, or reward, it may produce a future that satisfies the proxy while missing whatever actually makes a life good to live in. I think this framing already concedes too much. It treats the future as a product delivered to its inhabitants and treats the problem as one of writing down the right consumer preference. But for a large class of the lives we would actually want, the value does not reside in the delivered state; it resides in the relationship between an agent and an obstacle course. What matters is that the outcome is not yet settled, that the difficulty is genuine, that the constraints are ones the agent can own, and that the striving changes the agent in ways not fully predictable in advance.

Call this agential texture. A life high in agential texture regularly contains problems in which success is possible but not guaranteed, in which the constraints can be endorsed as self-chosen, and in which the effort is not merely an instrument for a separately valuable payoff but is partly constitutive of the activity's value. The difference between a game one can lose and a game whose conclusion is fixed is not that the first supplies an extra dose of unpleasant anxiety; it is the difference between an activity that can absorb a person and a chore that merely precedes a reward. My claim is that a future good to live in must be rich in agential texture, and that this standard is not reducible to the usual proxies. A population could be high in pleasure, high in reported preference satisfaction, even high in self-reported meaning, and still be living lives that are from the inside thin, because every challenge worth having has been pre-solved by an optimizer that knew the answer in advance.

Two implications follow, and I want comments on both of them.

First, some bads are load-bearing. The features that make pursuits engrossing — the live possibility of failure, choice under genuine ignorance, the scarcity of time or attention or ability, the friction of coordinating with other wills — are usually classified as costs to be minimized. A planner who treats them only as costs will, at the limit, remove the substrate on which the wanted goods depend. The design problem is not how to satisfy preferences while minimizing suffering; it is how to curate the right kinds of friction without turning the future into a museum of administered difficulty.

Second, self-endorsed constraint cannot do the filtering work alone. My first instinct was to say that the friction that matters is friction the person endorses. But endorsement is exactly the sort of thing environments manufacture. A person whose capacities have been shaped by an environment will often come to endorse the constraints that environment supplies; a prisoner can come to love the cell, and a bureaucrat can come to need the form. If agential texture is to be a standard rather than a rationalization of whatever the present system happens to produce, fun theory needs some non-preference-based account of which frictions are worth having and which are merely internalized bads. The essay currently ends where that account should begin.

The closest I get to a positive proposal is to think of agential texture not as a property of isolated episodes but as a property of an ecology of possible lives. A good future, on this view, would contain many attractors, exits that are genuine and cheap, commitments deep enough to engross without being irreversible enough to trap, and enough diversity of texture that different temperaments can find obstacle courses they can own. I am aware this is gestural and may simply move the hard question from "which frictions?" to "which attractor sets?"

I'd like comments of the following kinds:

1. Where exactly does the argument from "some valuable activities essentially involve unsettled outcomes and real difficulty" to "a good future must curate such friction" go through or fail? Does it conflate a psychological precondition with a normative requirement, and is there a version of the same conclusion that avoids the conflation?

2. Does "agential texture" earn its keep, or is it mostly relabeling flow, eudaimonia, or autonomy/competence under a new term? If the latter, which existing construct should I be building on instead — and what, if anything, does the new term capture that they miss?

3. The claim that some bads are load-bearing is the part most likely to read as suffering apologetics. Is there a principled line between load-bearing friction and rationalized harm, and does the essay come anywhere near drawing it?

4. What is the strongest objection from the direction of quiet contentment — a future of genuinely happy, low-friction, low-stakes lives — and how should the essay answer it? Am I at risk of presenting temperament as theory?

5. Which claims are doing too much work in a single sentence or begging the question, and should be split into separate claims or cut?
