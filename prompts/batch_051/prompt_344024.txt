I spent several hours yesterday arguing with a friend about whether pain is bad because of how it feels, and the argument kept running aground on a clinical phenomenon that neither of us could fully absorb into our positions. I want to work out what that phenomenon actually does to the familiar argument. The argument, as we were using it, is this: the badness of pain is intrinsic to the sensation itself. To feel pain is to be in a state with a hurtful, aversive, negative qualitative character, so that the painfulness and the hurtfulness cannot come apart; remove the aversiveness and you have not stripped one component away from the pain while leaving another, you have simply failed to have pain. The development is pain asymbolia. People with this condition report that a noxious stimulus is painful, and they can locate it and rate its intensity and quality, but they deny that it hurts or bothers them, show none of the ordinary withdrawal and protective behavior, and sometimes describe the sensation with clinical detachment. On its face, this looks like pain with its sensory-discriminative features intact and its negative affect, the felt badness or unpleasantness, missing. My friend took it to show that the familiar argument is dead: the badness of pain is not intrinsic to the sensation but is carried by a separable affective and motivational layer, and a pain stripped of that layer is still a pain, only not a bad one. I was less sure. Perhaps the argument can be repaired by saying that anything rightly called pain must have the negative affective dimension, so the asymbolic state is not pain in the philosophically relevant sense. Or perhaps it survives in a weaker form, as the claim that the badness of pain is intrinsic to the affective dimension of pain, and that dimension is what ordinary talk of pain is tracking. I want to know what actually happens to the argument once the development is admitted. Which premise is under pressure, and which survive untouched? Does the familiar argument still go through, does it need repair, or is it dead? What is the strongest version of the original view that remains defensible, and what does it give up in order to account for the asymbolic case?
