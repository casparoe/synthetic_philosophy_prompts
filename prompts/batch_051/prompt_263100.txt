Here is the question: Set out the sea-battle problem about future contingents. In doing so, explain the fatalist worry the example is meant to press, identify the principle about truth on which that worry depends, describe how a Peircean and an Ockhamist semantics for the future operator 'will' treat a future contingent such as 'there will be a sea battle tomorrow,' and state for each semantics whether the law of excluded middle is preserved.

Here is the student's answer: Aristotle's sea-battle passage is usually read as an argument that if every assertion is already either true or false, fatalism follows: it would already be true that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, and the battle would therefore happen of necessity, with nothing left genuinely open. To block this, Aristotle is often taken to restrict the principle that every statement is either true or false, holding that future contingent statements are neither true nor false until the matter is settled. The modern discussion formalizes the options. On the Peircean view, 'There will be a sea battle tomorrow' is true just in case there is at least one possible future branch on which a sea battle occurs tomorrow. Before the decision, some branches contain a battle and others do not, so the sentence and its apparent negation both lack a classical truth value; Peirceanism therefore gives up the law of excluded middle. The Ockhamist view, by contrast, relativizes truth to a complete history. 'There will be a sea battle tomorrow' is true or false at a time and a history, and since exactly one of those histories is the actual future, the sentence already has a definite truth value simpliciter, even if we do not know which it is. Ockhamism thus keeps classical logic intact, and its only cost is the relativization of truth to histories.
