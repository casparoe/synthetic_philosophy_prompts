You are a professional philosopher working in applied ethics, the philosophy of animal minds, and decision-making under moral uncertainty. Think through the question before writing; start by brainstorming the candidate commitments the practice below could rest on, then separate the ones the practice strictly requires from the ones it merely coheres with.

Here is the practice. In commercial agriculture and public health, insects are killed on a scale that dwarfs almost every other animal harm human beings cause: fields are sprayed with broad-spectrum insecticides, districts are fogged to suppress mosquitoes, and industrial facilities rear insects for feed and food, processing them by the billion. In none of these practices is the possible suffering of the insects treated as a factor that counts. There is no welfare assessment, no trade-off calculation in which insect suffering appears, and, in the bodies that set agricultural and public-health policy, almost no institutional channel for the question to be raised. The practices treat insects, for moral purposes, much as they treat weeds.

Yet there is a serious line of argument, often labeled the scale argument, which says this stance is far harder to justify than it looks. It runs roughly as follows. Even if, on the best available evidence, an individual insect has only a modest probability of being capable of suffering — say, between one and ten percent — the number of insects affected by ordinary annual insect-killing practices is so enormous that the expected number of creatures who can suffer is still staggering, several orders of magnitude larger than the number of mammals and birds humans use and keep. On ordinary expected-value reasoning, the possible suffering of insects should therefore be treated as one of the most consequential moral issues there is, not as weightless. The practice, in effect, behaves as though that enormous expected value were zero.

Your task is to state what philosophical commitments this practice presupposes — about insect sentience, about the moral weight of insect suffering, or about how to decide under uncertainty — and to assess whether those commitments form a coherent whole. Be prepared to conclude that the practice has no single coherent set of commitments; if so, say what holds the pattern in place instead.

Structure your response into the following sections, in this order and with roughly the lengths given:

1. The presupposition, made explicit (about 250 words). Reconstruct the implicit view of insect sentience, its moral significance, or the decision rule that would make treating this vast possible suffering as weightless reasonable. Write it as a set of explicit principles.

2. Strictly presupposed versus merely compatible (about 400 words). Distinguish what the practice is committed to from what it merely fits well with. Show where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: identify the rival theories — about sentience, about value, or about decision under uncertainty — that would each license the same behavior, and say which theories the practice quietly rules out.

3. Internal consistency (about 250 words). Test the commitments you identified against the precautionary or expected-value reasoning the same institutions use elsewhere, for instance when the probability is small but the stakes are high in food safety, drug approval, or the protection of rare vertebrate species. If the positions are inconsistent, say exactly where; if they can be reconciled, say how.

4. The practice under the rival view (about 300 words). Now run the same practice under the scale argument instead: assign even a low subjective probability — a few percent — to insect sentience, treat that probability as decision-relevant, and apply ordinary expected-value reasoning. Describe concretely what would have to change in agriculture, pest control, or insect farming, and what the first priority would be.

5. Bottom line (about 100 words). State whether the practice has a stable and defensible implicit theory, or whether it has drifted into its current form without one.

Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the analysis must stand on the argument itself.
