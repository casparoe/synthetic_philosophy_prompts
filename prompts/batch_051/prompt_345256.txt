I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I've spent the last six months reading everything I could get my hands on about the free will debate because my next novel has a philosopher character and I want to get his view's right, not just have him name-drop compatibilism. The man has taught for forty years, gives a confident lecture every fall defending his position, and then his son does something terrible and the old man falls into the very hole he's spent his career filling in. So I need to understand the actual shape of the disagreement, not win it.

Here's what I keep seeing, over and over. The compatibilists (of the modern sort) say free will is just the ability to act on your own reasons without being coerced, and determinism doesn't touch that, so responsibility is safe. The incompatibilists say that if the past plus the laws fixed every choice in advance, then you couldn't have done otherwise in the only sense that matters, and no amount of redefining "free" gets you out of it. Each side thinks the other is either changing the subject or stuck on an intuition they can't defend. They all read the same papers. Nobody moves. It feels less like an argument than two people describing different animals and both insisting the other has the right name for it.

So my question isn't who's right. It's: what kind of disagreement is this really? A verbal one about the word "free" or "could have done otherwise"? A hidden empirical question about what ordinary people actually mean when they say someone could have done otherwise? Or bedrock intuitions about what blame is even for—backward-looking desert versus forward-looking correction—so the two sides aren't even answering the same question? And if you had to diagnose it that way, what would actually count as progress: a distinction, an experiment, a better question, or just admitting the thing is permanently stalled? I'll take a diagnosis over a verdict.
