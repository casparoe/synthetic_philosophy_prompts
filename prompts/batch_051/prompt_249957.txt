In a debate about whether a referendum result should settle a contested policy question, my opponent kept ending the argument with "the will of the people has spoken, and it must be respected." When I pressed him on what the sentence actually claims, he treated me as though I were being deliberately obtuse. I suspect the phrase is sliding between several different theses, and I want them separated before I return to the debate.

Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that "the will of the people must be respected" could be making? It might be an empirical claim that there is a determinate majority preference and the referendum reliably measured it; a normative claim that whatever a majority prefers ought to be enacted; a constitutive claim that "the people" is a single collective agent with a will of its own; a procedural claim that the outcome deserves respect because it emerged from a fair decision procedure rather than because of its content; an epistemic claim that aggregated public judgment is more likely to be correct; or a rhetorical move in which "the people" simply means ordinary citizens as against elites. I doubt that list is exhaustive.

For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and on what grounds. Then tell me which readings would actually license treating the referendum outcome as settling the question, and which are being borrowed rhetorically without doing any justificatory work. I am especially interested in where each reading leaves the argument if the public's preferences are unstable, cyclical, or partly constructed by the procedure that claims to measure them. Please present the readings as a menu with verdicts attached, rather than picking one as the correct interpretation.
