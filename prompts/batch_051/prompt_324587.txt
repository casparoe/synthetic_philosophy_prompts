You are a philosopher of action who also designs behavioral experiments, and you are being asked to adjudicate a dispute rather than merely survey it.

The dispute: one side holds that joint action — two people walking together, a string quartet playing, a crew hauling a boat ashore, two people moving a sofa — involves something irreducibly shared: a we-intention, a joint commitment, or a plural subject, a relation among the participants that is not present in any collection of individual attitudes. The other side replies that this is mysterious talk with no extra empirical content, and that everything real about shared agency is already captured by each participant's individual intentions, their meshing subplans, and their common knowledge of one another's plans.

I want to know whether this dispute has empirical content. Choose a concrete joint action, and design a feasible experiment or field study that would, as sharply as possible, discriminate the two views. Be concrete about the manipulation, the control, the behavioral or judgment measures you would collect, and the predicted pattern under each theory.

Then say what result would count in favor of the irreducibly shared view, what result would count against it, and — assuming the favorable result comes in — state exactly what has been shown and what part of the philosophical dispute no test can reach.

Before giving the design, briefly but explicitly state how you will operationalize the contested notion of shared agency, so a reader can see what your measures are supposed to be measures of.

I am asking for a committed response: pick one design, defend its measures over plausible alternatives, and take a definite position on what the test would and would not settle. If you conclude that no experiment could discriminate the two views, commit to that conclusion and say what sort of non-experimental evidence could still rationally move the dispute.
