Two camps in the hospital ethics group I facilitate have been arguing for a year about how much authority an advance directive should have when the person who wrote it later develops dementia and, by the time the directive would bite, appears content and says in her own way that she wants to go on living. I am commissioning an adversarial collaboration between them, and I would like you to draft the joint statement the two sides should be able to sign.

The first camp holds a precedent-autonomy view: the competent person who wrote the directive is the source of the critical interests—the whole-life values, dignitary judgments, and sense of what would make her life go well as a whole—that should govern her later care. Those interests do not lapse merely because the disease has made her content. Provided the directive was informed, specific, and voluntarily made, it should generally be honored even if the person with dementia now says she wants to go on living and even if she takes pleasure in the life her earlier self judged degrading.

The second camp holds a present-interests view: the person with dementia before us now is a vulnerable someone with experiential interests of her own—comfort, pleasure, freedom from pain, the small attachments of the day—and sometimes enough residual agency for her current wishes to carry moral weight. An advance directive is weighty evidence of what the earlier self wanted, but it is not a contract that binds a later self who can no longer endorse it; enforcing the directive would sometimes amount to harming a person who now wants to live on the authority of a person who no longer exists in the relevant sense.

A shared running case has structured the argument. A woman wrote while competent, in specific terms, that if she ever reached moderate-to-severe dementia she refused antibiotics for pneumonia. She is now in exactly that state: content, smiling when fed, recognizing family only intermittently, accepting spoon-feeding, and resistant to blood draws. She has developed pneumonia, and the directive would mean withholding antibiotics.

Please draft the joint statement with the following sections, in this order and at roughly these lengths:

1. Shared ground (around 300 words). Eight to twelve numbered claims both camps can sign, worded so neither side is conceding the point at issue. Cover at least: the authority of a competent person's contemporaneous refusal; the serious weight of a valid advance directive; the irrelevance of mere staff or family discomfort to the decision; the point that capacity, current agency, and current wishes can be partial and fluctuating; the distinction between withholding treatment and actively ending life; and the condition that directives must be informed, specific, and free of coercion to carry full authority.

2. The disagreement, stated exactly (around 350 words). Set out three or four precise formulations of the residual disagreement, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording and the other side would accept that its view is characterized fairly. Include at least one formulation about authority (whose voice governs when the earlier and later selves conflict), one about interests (whether current experiential interests or earlier critical interests take priority when they collide), and one about selfhood or personal identity over time (whether the later self is the same self in the relevant normative sense). For each formulation, give the two sides' accepted wordings rather than a single compromise sentence.

3. Cruxes and predictions (a table of four or five rows, each row 100 to 140 words). Columns: Crux; Why it is load-bearing; What the precedent-autonomy side predicts; What the present-interests side predicts; A test or case both sides agree would bear on it. Use the running pneumonia case in at least two rows, and include at least one of these further cruxes: whether current contentment can outweigh an earlier dignitary judgment; whether vagueness in a directive should weaken it and why; whether the later self's resistance is a genuine wish or behavioral noise; and whether a directive is better modeled as self-binding in the manner of a Ulysses contract or as one piece of evidence about current interests.

4. What is verbal and what is substantive (around 200 words). Identify parts of the dispute that dissolve once terms are fixed—for example, different uses of autonomy, well-being, or the same self—and state which disagreements survive that cleanup, plus what evidence or argument each side would need to see in order to move on the surviving cruxes.

End with a one-paragraph signing statement that both camps could put their names to, recording the common resolution and the residual disagreement.
