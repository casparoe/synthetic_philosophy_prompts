I serve on the board of a community health clinic, and next month I am presenting to our actuarial and health-economics review committee. My proposal is this: when the evidence behind a cost-effectiveness estimate is weak, the committee should report a range of plausible values — a program might reduce readmissions by somewhere between 10% and 40%, with a matching range of base rates — instead of forcing a single precise probability and a single expected value into the decision model, which everyone then treats as authoritative. The committee members are actuaries and health economists trained to work with single-number probabilities, likelihoods, and expected values, and they care above all about auditability, about being able to defend one number to the finance committee, and about having a decision rule that tells them unambiguously what to fund. I am not an academic, and I have been working through this material on my own; my argument is roughly that a precise-looking number manufactured from thin evidence is false rigor, and that a range is not a refusal to decide but a change in which decision rules are defensible — comparing worst cases, checking what should be done under each plausible probability, and making risk tolerance explicit.
