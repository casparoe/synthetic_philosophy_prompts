I'm in a seminar on moral reasons, and particularism has been eating at me these last few weeks. The particularists say there are no moral principles—not even ceteris paribus ones—because a feature that counts in favor of an action in one context can count against it in another, and moral judgment is just a trained sensitivity to particular cases. The other camp, where I'd parked myself, is the Rossian one: there are general principles, but they're principles of prima facie duty, so fidelity or beneficence always counts as a right-making consideration even when it's outweighed. A friend in the seminar insists this makes me a particularist in denial, and sent me the following argument to prove it. I cant find the break, but I can't live with the conclusion.

(1) Rossianism admits there is no general principle that ranks prima facie duties or tells you how much weight each gets in conflict cases. (2) So in any conflict case, what I ought to do all things considered is not derived from any principle; it's a non-codifiable judgment about that particular situation. (3) Once you've conceded that the overall verdict is a case-by-case, non-codifiable judgment, you've conceded the core of particularism: moral judgment can't be cashed out as the application of rules. (4) At that point the prima facie principles are doing no work except to insist that features like promise-keeping or beneficence have a fixed moral polarity. (5) But that insistence is exactly what the particularist denies, and the usual example—the fact that an act gives someone pleasure, when the pleasure is pleasure at another's suffering—seems to show that the very same feature can count against an act in one setting and for it in another. (6) If the polarity of a feature can flip with context, the prima facie principles are false as stated. (7) If it can't flip, the Rossian hasn't answered the particularist, just asserted the opposite. So either Rossianism collapses into particularism, or it's standing on a stipulation.

I want to keep (1) and (2)—that feels like Rossianism being honest. But I don't think I'm a particularist in disguise, and yet I can't name the step where this goes wrong. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it commit me to? I'd rather you commit to a definite diagnosis than survey all the moves, though I'd like to know what the other candidates would cost me.
