I'm in this grad seminar that's half causal inference and half philosophy of science, and I've basically come around to the Spirtes-Glymour-Scheines / Pearl picture that conditional independence is the main empirical handle we have on causal structure, and that the job is to find the minimal Bayes net whose d-separation relations match the independencies in the data. The Markov condition I'm fine with — if the graph is right, d-separated variables should be independent. The part I've gotten genuinely attached to is the inference in the other direction: taking the independencies we actually see as structural rather than as accidents or cancelations. That's faithfulness (or at least minimality), and I keep wanting to say the distribution's independence pattern tracks the true causal graph, so the sparsest graph that fits is our best guess.

This has started to feel a little too good, so I want a serious map of what would count against it. I know the stock textbook objection that faithfulness can fail — two causal paths with effects that exactly cancel, Cartwright's point — and I know the stock reply that unfaithful parameter values are measure zero. What I want to know is where that defense gives out. What would the world have to look like for the minimality-and-faithfulness strategy to be systematically misleading, not just unlucky in a knife-edge case? Are there realistic kinds of systems or ways of collecting data where 'accidental' independencies are actually typical or even selected for, so that the sparsest Bayes net is reliably the wrong causal story? Which of these would a defender of the position have to concede as a genuine problem, and which would they rightly dismiss as 'you've just got the wrong variable set' or 'that's model misspecification'? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal down to merely awkward, and an honest verdict on whether the position is well insulated — and if it is, whether that's a feature or a bug.
