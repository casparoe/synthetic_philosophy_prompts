So, i'm working on a piece about translation — specifically about what gets preserved when you insist on keeping a poem's form, and what quietly gets lost. The centerpiece is that famous experiment where Douglas Hofstadter takes a tiny 16th-century poem, Clément Marot's "A une Damoyselle malade" (the one everyone just calls "Ma mignonne"), and fills a whole book, Le Ton beau de Marot, with dozens of English versions, each trying to hold onto the rhyme scheme and the meter while still carrying the sense. The whole book is basically a demonstration that something has to give: you can keep the music, you can keep the meaning, but at some point one of them bends.

I'd like a poem for the piece that makes that tension felt instead of stated. Could you write me a villanelle? The form seems built for it: two lines keep coming back, the way the source text and the target text keep re-claiming each other across versions. Make the two refrains stand for the two things a translator is trying to hold onto — one line for the music (rhyme, meter, syllable count) and one for the sense. They should rhyme with each other, of course, and each time a refrain returns it should mean something slightly different in its new stanza, the way a phrase changes meaning when its re-set in a new line. End with both of them side by side in the final couplet, where the translator has to admit they don't quite fit — or where, just once, they do.

It doesn't need to be long or showy; a little French is fine (mignonne, the odd je ne sais quoi) but don't let it become garnish. It should be exact enough that a translator who thinks about formal equivalence would nod along, and good enough that a reader who's never heard of Marot still feels why the choice hurts.
