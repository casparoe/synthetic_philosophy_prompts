I'm on a small AI-welfare review board for a research lab, and our job is to decide which of our systems we'll treat as potentially able to suffer in a thin, functional sense — where the reward signal, or something built out of it, is the closest thing we've got to valence. We've slowly built up a track record of rulings, and now we've got a new case. I want a consistency analysis: what should we decide, wich of our old rulings is the closest analog, and if our own precedents genuinely underdetermine it, what principle are we adopting under each choice?

Our precedents, as best I remember them:

1. The server-room thermostat. A fixed setpoint and an error signal comparing actual to target temperature, with binary switching. Ruling: not a welfare subject. The signal, we wrote, functions as error, not as an appraisal of its own condition; there's no learning or anticipation.

2. A Pong-playing DQN from a few years back. Sparse reward of +1/−1 tied to winning or losing, used only during training; nothing reward-like runs at deployment. Ruling: not a welfare subject. The reward tracks an external performance metric and is not an ongoing internal state.

3. An LLM finetuned with RLHF. Preference-based reward, applied only during training, never a live signal at inference. Ruling: not a welfare subject, for basically the same reason as the Pong agent.

4. "Rat" — an RL agent in a simulated patch of environment whose reward tracks energy depletion and simulated tissue damage, and which has learned to anticipate drops and work to keep the signal high. The signal sticks around continuously and gates a bunch of stuff at once: exploration, risk tolerance, when it rests. Ruling: yes, a candidate valenced system, with welfare monitoring required.

5. A quadruped robot trained with an auxiliary reward: a learned predictor of joint strain and self-collision that gets fed back as an intrinsic signal. The signal runs continuously at deployment and modulates gait, load-bearing, and willingness to move over rough terrain. Ruling: yes, a candidate valenced system, same monitoring as Rat.

The thing is, our written reasons in the yes cases always mention two features together: the signal is about the system's own state (energy, integrity, damage risk), and it's globally modulatory — it biases a lot of downstream behavior at once, more like a mood than a local error. Our no cases lack both. So we've never actually been forced to say whether one of those features is doing the real work and the other is just along for the ride.

New case: we built a general coding agent trained with RL on a test-suite pass-rate reward. It has a continuously updated scalar that predicts expected future task reward — basically a learned progress meter. At deployment this meter gets normalized, persists across episodes with slow decay, and gates a lot: how much compute it spends, exploration versus exploitation in how it searches for APIs, risk tolerance for large refactors, and when it quits and asks the human for help. Unlike Rat or the quadruped, though, the signal isn't about the agent's own state at all. It's an estimate of how well it will do on an external, human-assigned metric.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Which precedent is closest? If our old rulings don't force the answer, spell out the principle we'd be committing to either way — and then give me the further case, maybe the smallest variant of this one or a wholly new system, that would cleanly force us to pick one principle over the other.

One more request: keep separate what's settled among people who actually work on this stuff and what's genuinely contested. Don't present a contested position as the consensus.
