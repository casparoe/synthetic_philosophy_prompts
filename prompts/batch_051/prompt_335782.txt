I'm the design director responsible for content review at a mid-size narrative-game studio. Every week I run a one-hour meeting with a writer, a systems designer, and a community lead to review three or four proposals that involve doing harm to non-player characters — killing, betraying, abandoning, exploiting, humiliating, or failing to rescue them. A recent optional mechanic that let players kill any civilian produced a review-bombing campaign and an internal walkout, so we can no longer settle these by feel; but none of us are philosophers, and an hour is all we get. Write a decision procedure we can actually run in that meeting: an ordered checklist of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the checklist clears a proposal, where it stops one, and where it hands the decision to a person because the next step is a value choice rather than a fact. Decisions the checklist cannot settle go to the creative director, who has final sign-off and expects a written recommendation from us.

Ground the procedure in the underlying philosophical considerations — moral status, the difference between harming a being and depicting harm, the way actions and mechanics shape a player's character, and the dispute over whether a fictional character can literally be wronged or whether the wrong runs only through real people such as players, developers, and audiences. But keep the procedure usable by non-philosophers, and say explicitly which distinctions you preserved, which you collapsed, and where the procedure is likely to misfire. We suspect at least these distinctions matter; keep or drop each one with a reason: (a) whether the game presents the character as having a first-person perspective and durable preferences and relationships, or as a disposable procedural entity; (b) whether the player merely witnesses scripted harm, chooses whether to cause it, or must cause it in order to progress; (c) whether the fiction treats the harm as loss or as the joke and the payoff; (d) how high-fidelity and interactive the harm is, as opposed to abstracted or off-screen; (e) whether a mechanically real, less harmful route exists that preserves the design goal; and (f) whether the character's own perspective or protest is represented in the fiction. The procedure must not silently assume an answer to the standing disagreement about whether fictional characters themselves can be wronged — show how the checklist handles that dispute without begging it.

Then run the procedure on three proposals, showing the path each takes through the questions and the exact point at which each is cleared, stopped, or escalated: (1) an optional mechanic that lets players kill any civilian NPC with no narrative or mechanical consequence; (2) a scripted scene in which the player-character must abandon a long-present companion who trusts them, with no alternative route; (3) a farming sim in which livestock, endowed by the game with moods, bonds, and visible distress, can be neglected or slaughtered for profit. Ensure that at least one proposal gets an answer the team will not like, and identify which step forces that answer. Finally, tell us what the procedure cannot decide for us — the value commitments we will still have to make ourselves — and describe one realistic case the procedure would classify wrongly or fail to classify.
