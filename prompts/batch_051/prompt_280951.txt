ok so a friend and i just spent the whole walk home arguing about the species problem, specifically wether species are real things or just conveniant sorting bins, and somewhere in there i started writing down the species concepts we were actually defending. here's the list i ended with:

(1) the biological species concept: a species is a group of interbreeding or potentially interbreeding populations that is reproductively isolated from other such groups.
(2) the ecological species concept: a species is a lineage that occupies its own niche or adaptive zone and evolves separately from the lineages outside it.
(3) the phylogenetic species concept: a species is the smallest diagnosable cluster of organisms with a shared pattern of ancestry and descent, or the smallest monophyletic group, depending on which version you pick.
(4) the morphological/phenetic species concept: a species is just a cluster of overall similarity.
(5) the evolutionary species concept: a species is a single lineage of ancestor-descendant populations that keeps its identity, tendencies, and historical fate over time.

and before someone brings it up, i don't count the move where you say these are all just operational criteria for the same underlying separately-evolving-lineage idea. that feels like a cop-out, not another species concept.

so here's what i want to know: is there a real species concept i'm missing? by real i mean not a reworded version of one of the above, not a special case of one, and not a mashup of two of them. if there is, tell me what it adds and where it splits from at least one of the ones on my list on a genuinely hard case (asexual organisms, ring species, fossils, bacteria, whatever you think is worst for it).
