Ellsberg's two-urn experiment draws its force from a contrast the agent cannot eliminate: one urn has a known composition, the other does not. The standard moral is that expected utility theory is missing a dimension — that ambiguity is not reducible to risk, that Savage's sure-thing principle fails descriptively, and that a complete theory of choice needs a term for the agent's confidence in its own probability assignments, not merely the assignments themselves.

Now change one feature of the environment. Before the agent chooses a bet, it can query a forecasting service that is well calibrated in the frequency sense: among all questions to which the service has assigned probability p, the predicted event has occurred in a fraction p of cases. The query is cheap, and the service will answer for the ambiguous urn just as readily as for the known one. The agent receives a report, say 0.62 for red.

Does the Ellsberg argument survive this development, or does it collapse? In your answer: (1) state which premise of the standard Ellsberg argument is under pressure, and whether the new environment falsifies it, bypasses it, or leaves it intact; (2) say whether ambiguity aversion still names a distinct phenomenon once a calibrated probability for the previously ambiguous urn is available; (3) if the paradox survives, give the repaired version and the conditions under which it still has force; if it does not, say what the residual problem should be called and whether it is best understood as ambiguity or as something else.
