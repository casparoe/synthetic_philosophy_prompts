I keep coming back to a parallel and can't tell if it's deep or just me pattern-matching. In control theory, a Kalman filter combines a prediction from a model with a measurement from a sensor, and the weight given to the measurement is set by the relative error covariances: a noisy sensor gets downweighted, a precise one gets trusted, and the update runs on the innovation — the part of the measurement that wasn't already predictable from the model. The standard filter assumes the model's noise and the sensor's noise are independent. That looks to me like the entire formal skeleton of the question "is thinking about your thinking actually helpful?" Checking your own reasoning is a measurement you take on your reasoning, so it should improve your epistemic position only insofar as the check's errors aren't perfectly correlated with the original reasoning's errors. That would explain why redoing the same derivation the same way rarely catches the mistake, but solving the problem a second, partly independent way and seeing if the answers disagree sometimes does. Is this a real identity of structure — metacogntion as a second, partially independent information channel, with the Kalman gain as the norm for how strongly to revise — or is the analogy loose? If it is real, does the estimation-theory result transfer: a correlated sensor can make a Kalman filter more confident while making it less accurate. Does that mean some forms of "thinking about your thinking" are actively harmful in the same way, and if so, which forms?
