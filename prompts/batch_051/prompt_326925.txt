I keep seeing people argue about aesthetic testimony by appealing to whichever case favours their side — the friend who got you into a band, the critic you defer to, the "you have to see it yourself" intuition — so before I read further I want the full specification of what a theory of aesthetic testimony must deliver. The question is whether, and in what sense, one person can acquire aesthetic knowledge, warranted aesthetic belief, appreciation, or justified aesthetic judgment from another person's say-so rather than from first-hand experience of the object. Take the purpose of the theory to be explanatory and normative: it should explain our actual practice of asking others about art and telling others about art, and it should tell us when deferring to another's verdict is appropriate.

Give me the desiderata, with the case or consideration behind each. Include at least these hard cases: a novice who accepts a trusted friend's verdict that a film is excellent; someone who confidently repeats a critic's verdict without having experienced the work; a guide who tells you in advance which features to attend to before you encounter a work; a review that tells you a wine has notes of blackcurrant before you taste it and then encountering those notes or failing to; and testimony about descriptive or art-historical facts, such as genre, technique, or date, set against testimony about value. Say which distinctions an adequate theory must keep apart — I would start from: belief-that versus phenomenal knowledge versus appreciation versus the capacity to judge; the testifier's reliability versus the ground of the judgment; being caused by another's word versus being justified by it; and a report about one's own taste profile versus a verdict about the work. For each requirement, mark it non-negotiable or a commitment of a particular camp, and flag any pairs that cannot be jointly satisfied as stated. Address explicitly whether the acquaintance principle — roughly, that a genuine aesthetic value judgment must be grounded in first-hand acquaintance with the object — should be treated as a fixed datum or as something the theory may derive or explain away, and why.

Then, briefly, score the main families against the list: pessimists who think testimony is in principle insufficient for aesthetic judgment, optimists who think reliable testimony can transmit aesthetic value knowledge, and moderates who treat testimony as enabling appreciation rather than transmitting it. Keep the scoring brief; the criteria are the deliverable.

As you answer, indicate for each requirement how confident you are that it really is a requirement, and single out the point on your list you are least sure belongs there, with a sentence on why you kept it anyway.
