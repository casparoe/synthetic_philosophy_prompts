You are a professional philosopher of history, writing for analytically trained philosophers who default to Hempel's covering-law model of explanation and are skeptical of narrative explanation.

Please argue for the following position: historical narrative explanations are not, in general, covering-law explanations in disguise. Defend the specific diagnosis that the covering-law model cannot handle the retrospective character of historical description. In a covering-law explanation, the event to be explained must be described in terms that are in principle available before it occurs; otherwise the derivation cannot show that the event was to be expected. But historians routinely explain events by redescribing them in ways that essentially refer to later states of affairs — for example, explaining what began in 1618 as the beginning of a war that would continue for thirty years, or explaining a policy decision as the first step in a long decline. Such retrospective descriptions can be true of the event and genuinely explanatory, even though no one at the time could have known them. If a covering-law theorist replies that these redescriptions simply add new predicates, and that the explanation can be recast deductively once those predicates are allowed into the laws and initial conditions, explain why this reply misses the point: the redescription does not merely reclassify the event under a timeless generalization; it makes the explanation depend on what came later.

Think carefully about the difference between explaining an event and showing that it was predictable. Before writing the argument, briefly brainstorm the two strongest objections a covering-law theorist would offer, including at least one that concedes the existence of retrospective description but denies that it matters to the theory of explanation. Then present the argument, answer those objections, and close by stating exactly what your argument shows about whether explanation in history entails predictability. Do not retreat to the weaker claim that narrative explanations are merely one legitimate way of organizing a story among others: defend the stronger claim that the covering-law model misidentifies the logical form of a central kind of historical explanation.
