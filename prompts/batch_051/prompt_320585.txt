I've been going back and forth in a group chat about a proposed neural-rights bill, and the whole argument keeps sliding around because nobody has a stable definition of "mental privacy." I've tried tabooing the phrase and it just migrates: one person means "my neural data shouldn't be sold," another means "nobody gets to read my mind without my okay," a third means "there's a private inner life that shouldn't be made legible at all, even with consent." I want a working definition of mental privacy specifically — not the whole of cognitive liberty, just the privacy prong — sharp enough to classify hard cases.

Here are the cases I keep coming back to:
1. Police use a brain-based concealed-memory test on a suspect without a warrant, showing him crime-scene photos and reading an EEG recognition signal.
2. A hospital monitors a patient's EEG during surgery to track depth of anesthesia.
3. A startup sells earbuds that infer your mood from your voice and serve ads based on it; no neural sensor is involved.
4. A BCI company streams raw neural data to the cloud and trains a model on it; users technically consented by checking a box in a 40-page terms-of-service.
5. A locked-in patient deliberately uses her BCI to compose a private letter to her partner.
6. An employer asks desk workers to wear a headband that uses near-infrared light to track attentional state for scheduling breaks; the raw signal stays on-device and mangers only see aggregate group scores.
7. A researcher reconstructs what a volunteer is looking at from her fMRI after she gave informed consent for the reconstruction; the results are shown to her and then published.
8. A friend is uncannily good at reading microexpressions and can tell when you're lying, with no device at all.

I'm fairly confident that (1) is a violation and that (2), (5), and (8) are not. I keep flip-flopping on (6), and I genuinely don't know where a defensible line puts (4) and (7). Give me the definition, run it through all eight, and tell me where it gives a verdict you didn't like on the first pass — then either revise or defend that verdict. Finish by saying which of the eight is least secure, and red-team the definition from the perspective of someone who thinks "mental privacy" shouldn't be a legal category at all.
