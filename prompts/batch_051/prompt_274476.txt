I had a long argument last night with a friend about how the ingredients of value combine—whether the value of a conscious experience is additive, multiplicative, or lexical in its parts, with the friend insisting on something like consciousness times happiness. I want to write down the position I landed on and have it audited before I defend it to anyone else. The commitments I came away with are these:

1. The value of a token experience—its contribution to welfare, positive or negative—is the product of its hedonic intensity and the degree of consciousness with which it is felt: a pain felt at half the typical degree of consciousness matters half as much, all else equal.

2. Across token experiences, within a life and across lives, value aggregates by simple addition.

3. There are lexical thresholds: no amount of mild positive experience can compensate for a single experience of sufficiently severe agony, because severe suffering has a different order of priority from lesser values.

4. Both consciousness and hedonic intensity are at least cardinally measurable magnitudes; otherwise the product formula in 1 is only a picture.

5. I also want to hold that a pain of the same felt intensity matters equally whether it occurs in a human being, a dog, or a mouse: differences in self-awareness or in the richness of the creature's consciousness should not by themselves discount the pain.

I suspect this set hangs together, but the argument shook my confidence. Please audit it. Which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension and which only seem to be? Rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to be revised and how much of the rest of the position that revision would cost.
