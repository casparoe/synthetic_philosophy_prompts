I spent yesterday evening arguing with a friend about whether the endowment effect and loss aversion are simply an irrational bias or can reflect legitimate preferences. She kept saying all of the examples were the same bias in different clothes; I kept replying that some of my reactions felt like errors and others felt well-founded. I would like to know whether there is a principle that sorts my verdicts.

My reactions to the cases, as they came up:

- A colleague bought shares at $60; they now trade at $40. He will not sell until they recover, though he says he would not buy them today at $40. I think this is a mistake.
- A person is given a coffee mug and, ten minutes later, will not part with it for less than $8, though she would not have paid more than $4 for an identical mug before receiving it. I think that gap is an error.
- A hiker will not sell her broken-in boots for $150, even though an identical new pair costs $120, because a replacement would have to be broken in. I think that is perfectly sensible.
- A woman will not sell the house in which she raised her children for its appraised value, even though the proceeds would buy a comparable house nearby; she says the place carries decades of family history. I think that is legitimate.
- A man inherits shares his late mother chose over many years and refuses to sell them, saying doing so would feel like cutting a last tie to her, even though he would never choose those shares today. I was inclined to call this a mistake, but I could not fully distinguish it from the house case.
- A season-ticket holder keeps attending games he no longer enjoys because he has already paid for the tickets and does not want the money to turn out to have been wasted. I think this is a straightforward error.

Is there one principle that captures all six verdicts? If there is, state it precisely and apply it to a case I deliberately left unresolved: a woman who refuses to sell an unattractive piece of furniture she inherited from her grandmother, at a fair price, although she takes no pleasure in it and has no room for it. If no single principle gets all six, give the smallest set of candidate principles that covers them, say explicitly which of my verdicts each principle saves and which it overturns, and then say what the most defensible candidate says about the furniture case.
