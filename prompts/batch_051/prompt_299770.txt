I'm a novelist, and my last three books have all been set in the same small fishing town, and I keep running into the same problem. I want my characters to feel like they actually live inside a community — their family, the church, the union, the town's history are part of who they are, not scenery — but I dont want them to be mere mouthpieces for the town either. The way I write it now, everyone is either a liberal individualist in overalls, always stepping back from every obligation to ask whether they chose it and whether it serves their own life plan, or someone so embedded in the town that they never question anything. I know this maps loosely onto the communitarianism vs liberalism argument: roughly, whether the self is prior to its ends and attachments, or is partly constituted by them, so that some unchosen obligations can still be genuinely binding. I don't need to settle which is right about actual people. I need a procedure I can run while drafting.

So here's the ask. Turn that philosophical debate into a reusable decision procedure for a working novelist. It should let me answer, for a particular character in a particular scene, whether this character is more likely to step back from a community claim and assert a right or a chosen plan (the liberal move), or to treat the claim as authoritative because of who they are (the communitarian move). It has to be usable fast, in my head, while I'm writing, and my editor has no philosophy background, so she has to be able to follow the same checklist when we argue about a scene. A checklist, a flow in words, a set of questions in order — whatever, as long as it's repeatable, not a one-off analysis.

Make sure the procedure keeps the distinctions that actually matter and does not flatten them into "individualist" vs "joiner." In particular: chosen attachments (a job, a club, a spouse) vs unchosen or constitutive ones (family, language, the town you were born into); endorsing a norm vs merely conforming to it; and the difference between someone who can question a practice because they are not wholly defined by it and someone for whom questioning the practice feels like questioning themselves. Flag the steps that are judgment calls and say what the procedure cannot do.

Then run it on two or three cases from the novel I'm stuck on, so I can see how it behaves: (1) a grown son who wants to leave for a job in the city while his father expects him to take over the family boat; (2) a woman who keeps the books for the church and discovers the deacon has been skimming, and has to decide whether to report him to the authorities or try to handle it inside the congregation; (3) a teenage girl who wants to marry outside the community's faith and has been told that means cutting herself off from her family. Make one of these come out in a way I won't like — where the procedure says the character would most plausibly do something I find repugnant, or something that wrecks my plot. And end by saying what the procedure cant do, like whether it actually prevents caricature or just relocates it.
