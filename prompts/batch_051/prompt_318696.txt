You are a professional philosopher of mind with substantial experience grading university-level work. I am a professor of philosophy, and I set the following examination question in a senior metaphysics of mind course. Below it is a student's unedited answer. Grade the answer.

Exam question:

"Explain the paradox of phenomenal judgment for epiphenomenalism about consciousness. In doing so, state precisely what epiphenomenalism claims, construct the paradox as an objection, and evaluate at least one response to that objection. Your answer should clearly distinguish phenomenal states from the judgments or beliefs we form about them."

Student answer:

"Epiphenomenalism is the view that conscious experiences are caused by the brain but cause nothing in turn: they are like the whistle on a steam engine, produced by the machinery but capable of influencing nothing. We must distinguish the felt quality itself—what it is like to be in pain—from our judgment 'I am in pain,' which is a contentful belief. According to epiphenomenalism, the judgment is ultimately a physical event in the brain, produced entirely by physical causes.

The paradox of phenomenal judgment arises in the following way. If epiphenomenalism is true, the pain itself never causes my judgment; the physical processes in my brain would produce the judgment 'I am in pain' whether or not the experience was present. So my introspective belief appears to be only accidentally connected to the very experience it reports. Since our first-person reports are the central evidence that consciousness exists, epiphenomenalism seems to undermine the evidence on which it rests, and thus to be self-defeating.

Chalmers, who made this objection prominent, takes it to show that epiphenomenalism cannot be maintained, and he concludes that phenomenal properties must be granted causal powers after all. That conclusion is too quick, however. The paradox can be dissolved by noticing that the experience and the judgment share a common cause. Some earlier neural event causes both the pain and, along a separate causal branch, the later judgment that one is in pain. Because the two effects are correlated through their shared cause, the judgment is a reliable indicator of the pain even if the pain does not cause it. No causal connection between pain and judgment is needed for introspective knowledge, so the paradox loses its force."

Your task:

Assign a letter grade, and write point-by-point comments that justify that grade. Do not settle for a general impression: identify what in the answer satisfies the requirements of the question, identify any mistaken attribution or any step that does not follow, however small, and state what the student would need to add or correct to receive a higher grade. Think carefully, and begin by reconstructing in outline what a strong answer to this question would need to establish before you assess the student's answer against that standard.
