Two friends of mine, Dan and Mira, got into it over dinner about whether the sheer amount of stock trading going on means a lot of traders are being irrational. Neither works in finance, but they both read enough to be dangerous. I couldnt referee it, so I'm passing it to you.

(1) Dan started with this: every trade has a buyer and a seller with opposite views about what the thing is worth. If I sell you a share at $100, I'm saying it's worth less than $100 to me and you're saying it's worth more than $100 to you. We can't both be right. And if we're both rational, at least one of us should treat the other's willingness to trade as evidence and update. He said economists proved this -- something called the no-trade theorem -- and so the sheer volume of trading is basically a measure of overconfidence.

(2) Mira said that's too fast. People trade because they're in different situations, not just because they have different beliefs. A retiree sells stocks to pay for groceries; a young person buys them to save for retirement. Nobody has to be wrong there -- they just have different time horizons and risk exposure. The no-trade thing only bites for purely speculative trade.

(3) Dan came back: fine, but life-cycle and hedging trades can't explain the volume. There are people day-trading the same stock back and forth within hours. That's pure speculation, and the theorem says that shouldn't happen between rational people with common priors.

(4) Mira: but "common priors" is exactly the point at issue. Maybe people just have different priors -- different base rates, different models of the world. If priors differ, both sides can rationally trade even after each has taken the other's willingness to trade into account. The theorem doesn't show anyone is irrational; it just shows what follows when you assume away the disagreement.

(5) Dan: but "different priors" is doing a lot of work there. If you're allowed to posit different priors whenever people disagree, then any two people can rationally disagree about anything forever, and "rational" stops meaning anything. At some point a prior is just a prejudice.

(6) Mira: I'm not saying all priors are rational. I'm saying you can't infer irrationality from trade alone, because you'd first have to show the parties had the same information and the same prior. The theorem is a benchmark, like a frictionless plane in physics, not a test that real trades fail.

(7) Dan: but if prices are supposed to aggregate information, and we just shrug and say "different priors," then a price becomes a weighted average of prejudices, and we lose the whole Hayekian idea that markets pool dispersed knowledge.

(8) Mira: maybe prices do both -- pool information where priors are shared, and aggregate risk preferences and life situations all along. A theorem's being idealized doesn't mean the behavior it can't explain is irrational; it means the model is incomplete.
