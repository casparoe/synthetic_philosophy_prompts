I have noticed an asymmetry in how the people around me treat photographs and handmade pictures, and I cannot tell whether it is defensible. When a student produces a blurry photograph of a disputed incident at a soccer match, people speak of the photograph as if it lets them see the event itself, and they usually treat it as settling the dispute. But a careful, realistic sketch by an eyewitness, even one made on the spot, is treated as one person's account rather than as direct access to what happened. Likewise, people are far more reluctant to throw away a photograph of a deceased grandparent than to discard a painted portrait of the same person, even when the painting is just as old and just as irreplaceable.

The natural explanation is that a photograph is produced mechanically by light reflected from the scene, so the camera does not interpret the scene the way a painter does. That principle seems to overreach, however, because photographs are pointed, timed, staged, cropped, filtered, and sometimes digitally altered by a person. The underlying question, I think, is whether looking at a photograph can be a way of literally seeing the person or event it depicts, while looking at a painting can only ever be seeing a depiction. State the strongest principle you can find, test it against the staged-photograph case and the on-the-spot-sketch case, and give an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, revised, or abandoned.
