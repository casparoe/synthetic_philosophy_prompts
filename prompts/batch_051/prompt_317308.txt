I'm a PhD student in moral psychology, and I'm preparing a short interactive handout for a graduate seminar on whether grit, fortitude, and resilience are best understood as virtues, as ideological constructs, or as something in between. I'd like you to write a sorting quiz — a 'which view of grit and resilience do you actually hold?' instrument — for the handout.

The quiz should have eight to ten questions, each built around a short concrete vignette about perseverance, quitting, or the way resilience language is used. Each question should offer two or three answer options phrased so that a respondent can answer without knowing which theoretical position an option is attached to; no question should simply ask whether the respondent thinks grit is a virtue or an ideology, or which label they prefer.

The scoring key should sort respondents into the following six positions, which I want treated as the leaves. Please define each in one or two sentences for the handout's answer key:

1. Trait-realist virtue view: grit is a real, broadly cross-situational trait — durable passion plus perseverance directed at long-term goals — and it is admirable in its own right because it reliably conduces to achievement.
2. Reductivist view: 'grit' is mostly conscientiousness rebranded, adding little distinct predictive or explanatory power; the trait is therefore not a separate virtue, though its underlying components may be.
3. Object-relative (Aristotelian) view: fortitude, as perseverance in adversity, is a virtue only relative to the worth and context of the end pursued; persistence in a worthless, self-destructive, or immoral project is a vice rather than an instance of grit.
4. Structural-critique view: whatever the empirical status of the trait, grit and resilience discourse functions ideologically by individualising failures and demanding that people adapt to conditions they ought instead to be able to change, locating responsibility in character rather than in institutions.
5. Dual-aspect view: the trait can be real and locally useful while, at one and the same time, the discourse around it systematically serves an ideological function; the descriptive and critical questions are distinct and receive separate answers.
6. Pragmatic/consequentialist view: the trait's metaphysical status matters less than the consequences of promoting grit-talk; the question is whether, in a given context, this discourse does more good than harm.

The questions should be designed to separate these six positions along the four distinctions that I think do the real work:

(a) whether perseverance is valuable irrespective of the object pursued, or only conditionally on the worth of the end;
(b) whether an outcome is best explained by a trait of the person or by the situation and its structural constraints;
(c) whether resilience should be treated as the same phenomenon as grit or as a distinct demand — to absorb shocks and return to a baseline rather than change it — and whether that demand can itself be harmful;
(d) whether a true description of a trait and an ideological function of a discourse can coexist, so that a respondent might coherently hold both a realist and a critical view.

Please include at least these four cases, though you may adapt them: a junior doctor told to be 'more resilient' while staffing in her department is unsafe; a teenager praised for showing grit by continuing in a sport he privately hates; a founder who persists with a failing start-up despite credible signals that pivoting would be rational; and a person who leaves an abusive relationship and is later described as having 'given up'.

The scoring key should be transparent. It should be built from the logical space — first the combinations of answers that the four distinctions allow, then the six positions located within that space — rather than by assigning each leaf a simple cluster of favoured answers. It should state explicitly which leaves the quiz cannot cleanly separate and why, and it should flag any combinations of answers that point to an unoccupied position (for example, a combination that treats perseverance as both a fully cross-situational trait and entirely dependent on the worth of each particular end) and say briefly whether that position is unoccupied because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Please write the complete quiz, the scoring key, and the interpretive notes for respondents, in British English throughout.
