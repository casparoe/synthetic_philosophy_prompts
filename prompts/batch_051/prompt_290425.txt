I am a student in a philosophy of social science course, and we have been working through the question whether "national character" is a coherent notion and whether it is ever acceptable to generalize about peoples. I have collected my own verdicts on a series of cases, and I would like to know what principle, or small family of principles, best captures them — and whether some of my verdicts must be given up.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

(1) My lecturer reports a large cross-national survey showing that, on average, respondents from one Western country score somewhat higher on a well-validated measure of conscientiousness than respondents from another, while within-country variation is much larger than the between-country difference and the distributions overlap heavily. I take this to be a respectable empirical claim about a real but modest aggregate difference: useful for comparative social science, but nearly useless for predicting any particular person.

(2) A friend says in casual conversation, "Swedes are reserved." I accept this as roughly true, and I do not think it needs the machinery of a survey to be legitimate. But I would object if she used it to predict how a specific Swedish job applicant would behave in an interview.

(3) A politician says of a southern European people that they are naturally warm and expressive, that it is "in their blood." I regard this as both false and objectionable: it turns a loose behavioral pattern into an inherited essence.

(4) An anthropologist I have read refuses to make any cross-cultural generalizations whatever, on the ground that people must be treated as individuals. I think this overcorrects; taken literally, it would make comparative social science impossible.

(5) A dinner-party guest says that people from a particular country are lazy and allergic to work. I reject the claim outright — not only as rude but as false, or at least so under-evidenced and coarse that it deserves no credence.

(6) The same evening, a Danish friend says, "We Danes are quite blunt; it is simply how we are." I find this considerably more acceptable than (5), and even more acceptable than hearing a non-Dane utter the identical sentence, though I cannot yet explain why the speaker should matter to the appropriateness or truth of what is said.

(7) A historian writes that the culture of the Japanese civil service in the postwar period prized consensus and deference to seniority. I accept this as a claim about norms, institutions, and practices rather than about individual psychology, and I do not think it commits the author to a view about what Japanese people "are like" inside.

(8) An employer proposes to use national-average scores on a personality variable to screen individual applicants. Even supposing the statistics are sound, I think this is wrong, because it treats people as instances of a group rather than as individuals, and this moral objection stands even if the actuarial prediction would be somewhat better than chance.

Two of my verdicts worry me. I accept the casual generic in (2) without demanding the statistical support I require in (1). And in (6) I let the speaker's identity alter my verdict, even though the sentence apparently says the same thing whoever utters it. Are these real inconsistencies, or can a principle absorb them?

So I would like you to address the following.

(i) Is there a single principle — about epistemic warrant, about the kind of property being ascribed, about the use to which a claim is put, or some combination — that delivers all eight verdicts?

(ii) If not, which verdict or verdicts should I overturn, and what is the simplest principle that handles the rest? Please be explicit about whether a principle that saves all eight does so only by becoming ad hoc.

(iii) What does the resulting account say about a case I have deliberately left out: a public-health agency uses a well-established national base rate to target screening invitations, while carefully saying nothing about the character of any individual from that country? Is that acceptable, and does it matter that the trait is physiological rather than psychological?

(iv) Should I separate the question whether a generalization about a people is coherent or true from the question whether it is acceptable to assert or act on it, or can one principle cover both?
