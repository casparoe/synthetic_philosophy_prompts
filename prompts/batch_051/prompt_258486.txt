I am an undergraduate taking a seminar called “Normative Uncertainty and the Idealised Self”. Over the last several weeks we have been working through indirect normativity — the idea that an agent with shaky or incomplete values should sometimes act on the verdict of a suitably idealised version of herself rather than directly on her present judgments. I want to turn that discussion into a structured term essay, and I would like you to write it.

The position I want to argue for is this: the authority of indirect normativity rests on evaluative continuity. A counterfactual self earns the right to speak for me only because, after the idealisation, she is still recognisably a corrected version of me rather than a different person with different values. The view should therefore be restricted to horizontal idealisation: improving an agent’s factual information and reasoning while holding her evaluative standpoint fixed. Vertical idealisation, which lets the idealising process revise the values themselves, cannot inherit authority from the actual agent. Anyone who appeals to vertical idealisation is, whether they say so or not, doing first-order normative theory under another name.

To keep the argument concrete, I want the discussion to dwell on the following case, which I will call Traverse. Ana must choose between an exposed high ridge and a low valley descent. She currently cares far more about spectacular views and exposed terrain than about the long-term health of her knees. If you hold her values fixed but give her complete trail information, calm deliberation, and freedom from fatigue, she would choose the ridge: it is plainly better at serving what she actually cares about. If you also allow the idealisation to revise her values — for example, by letting her vividly run both options until her concern for views comes to seem parochial to her — the idealised counterpart finds the valley superior. The single question — what Ana would choose were she fully informed and rational — now yields two different acts, depending on which idealisation is meant.

Please write the essay in the following named sections, in this order and at roughly these lengths:

1. Target and taxonomy (about 250 words). Define indirect normativity, distinguish horizontal from vertical idealisation, and state the position.
2. The continuity argument (about 450 words). Argue that horizontal idealisation is authoritative for the actual agent, and that vertical idealisation can be authoritative only if an independent account of which values are worth having is supplied — which is direct normativity. Address the move that Ana might actually value becoming the person who prefers the valley, and argue that this move collapses vertical deference into horizontal idealisation of a richer actual evaluative outlook.
3. Worked case: Traverse (about 350 words). Show exactly where the two forms of idealisation come apart in Ana’s choice, and say what the restricted view tells her to do.
4. Strongest objection (about 250 words). Present the best objection you can construct, ideally the charge that horizontal idealisation arbitrarily freezes values that are themselves rationally revisable.
5. Reply and conclusion (about 250 words). Answer the objection and close by stating what indirect normativity can still do once it is restricted.

Do not include any bibliography or citations; the arguments should stand or fall on their own.
