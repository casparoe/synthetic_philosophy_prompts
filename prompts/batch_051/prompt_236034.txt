I've been trying to understand two different ways a country can elect its legislature — roughly, what I think people call first-past-the-post and proportional representation — and I keep getting the feeling that each system quietly takes a position on a deeper issue without anyone coming out and saying so. In the first system, the country is divided into districts, each district sends a single representative, and the candidate with the most votes wins the seat even if far fewer than half the district's voters chose them. In the second system, the whole country votes for parties, and each party receives roughly the same share of seats as its share of the total vote, so a party supported by fifteen percent of voters ends up with about fifteen percent of the seats. I can follow the mechanics without trouble; what I cannot figure out is what each system assumes a vote is and what representation is for.

What puzzles me is that defenders of both systems claim that every citizen's vote should carry equal weight. But in the district system, whether your vote actually does anything seems to depend on where you live and how your neighbors vote: cast your ballot for the losing candidate in a safe district and your preference may be reflected nowhere in the legislature, whereas in the proportional system nearly every ballot contributes to some party's total. At the same time, the proportional system appears to assume that people's political views can be adequately expressed through parties and that the point of an election is to make the legislature a scaled-down picture of the electorate's opinions. The district system instead appears to assume that representation is really about a particular local community choosing a delegate who answers to that community, even if the national result ends up badly out of step with the national vote.

So here is what I want to understand. If someone forced you to spell out what each system quietly takes for granted — about when two votes should count as equal, about whether a voter is primarily an individual, a party supporter, or a member of a local community, and about whether a legislature should be a miniature portrait of public opinion or a collection of local delegates — what would the two sets of assumptions be? Which of those assumptions does each system genuinely commit a country to, as opposed to merely being compatible with or tending to produce? Do the two systems rest on genuinely different and incompatible ideas of what fair representation means, or are they just two ways of trading off things that everyone already wants? And can either system honestly say that it treats all votes as equal, given the way the other system criticizes it?
