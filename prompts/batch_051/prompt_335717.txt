I cover the cognitive and behavioral sciences for a general-interest magazine, and I am preparing a feature on the Dutch book argument: the claim that someone whose degrees of confidence violate the basic axioms of probability can be offered a set of bets, each of which they would accept as fair, that together guarantee a net loss no matter how the world turns out. For the feature I want to describe an exercise that lets a group experience the argument rather than merely read about it. Please design it as a runnable activity for a public science-and-ideas program I help run: an adult audience of about sixteen people, mixed backgrounds and no formal probability, in a single seventy-five-minute session. What participants should come away with is a felt sense that incoherence is a structural defect in a pattern of commitments, not a matter of lacking facts or losing to someone better informed.

The activity should be a game with two roles. Participants first act as pricers, posting a single two-sided price for each of three tickets on the mutually exclusive and exhaustive outcomes of a simple chance device, such as the color of a ball drawn from an opaque urn of fixed but unknown composition. Use chips rather than money, with a trivial prize for the highest final chip count. The pricers should not know in advance that they are being tested for exploitability. After the prices are posted, participants rotate into small groups and take the second role, bookie, trying to assemble against one another's posted price schedules a bundle of transactions that loses chips for the target whatever the outcome. Provide a worksheet that forces them to verify the loss state by state, so that a single-outcome loss cannot be mistaken for a sure loss.

Aim at roughly ten minutes of setup and framing, twenty minutes of pricing, twenty minutes of bookie work in groups, and ten minutes for a second, more difficult round. In that second round, some participants may post a bid/ask spread for each ticket rather than a single two-sided price. Give the groups the explicit test for whether such a spread is exploitable: check whether there is any probability assignment that falls between the bid and the ask on every ticket. Structure the final fifteen-minute debrief in three moves. First, map the exploitable schedules onto the specific ways a price pattern can fail to be probabilistic: prices below zero, prices above one, or prices that do not sum to one, and stress that the bookie needed no information beyond the posted prices themselves, and that the sure-loss test does not require the draw ever to be observed. Second, use the spread round to draw out that the strongest version of the argument targets incoherent commitments, and that a person who posts a spread can avoid sure loss without committing to a single precise number. Third, make explicit the step the game cannot perform.

Say what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run and how to handle it. Participants who sense they are being tested will imitate one another and post tidy, coherent prices, leaving no exploitable target, so use a decoy framing, such as a study of how people price risk, and have every slip filled in before anyone sees a bookie at work. Some participants will post evidently absurd prices; exploiters must be required to use only the prices on the target's own slip and may not alter them. The most likely conceptual failure is a group claiming victory because its bundle loses money on one drawn outcome rather than on all of them, so the state-by-state checklist should be authoritative. Finally, be candid about what the activity cannot reproduce. The game shows that an incoherent price schedule can be converted into a guaranteed loss, but it does not by itself show why vulnerability to such a loss constitutes irrationality rather than mere misfortune, and it keeps the stakes small, thereby sidestepping the fact that the value of money may not remain linear as stakes grow. That normative bridge, along with the question of whether someone may rationally hold imprecise rather than point-valued degrees of confidence, must be supplied by the facilitator rather than the game.
