I am attracted to imprecise probabilism for its systematic virtues: it represents a state of partial evidence as a set of probability functions rather than a single function, distinguishing genuine ignorance from a precise number one has merely settled on; it treats the Ellsberg choices as permissible rather than as failures to be corrected; and it refuses to pretend that there is a uniquely correct prior where the evidence does not fix one. Yet I also hold, firmly, that rational choice should be determinate. If one is offered a particular bet at a particular price, there must be a fact of the matter about whether taking it is rational, refusing it is rational, or the price is exactly fair; a theory that returns an interval of acceptable prices, and that allows two acts to be neither comparable nor indifferent, seems to stop being action-guiding exactly where it matters. Present the strongest case for each side, and then give a negotiated verdict. In particular: should the demand for a unique recommendation be debunked as an artefact of an over-demanding conception of decision theory, should imprecise probabilism be amended so that it restores determinacy, or is there a division of labour between what to believe and what to do on which both survive? Say what each option costs rather than simply choosing one.
