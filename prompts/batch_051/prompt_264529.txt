I'm a PhD candidate in political philosophy, and I'm presenting a paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on children, families, and the state. The thesis I'm defending is that a threshold-competence licensing scheme for prospective legal parents—not a ban, not a cap on family size, but a one-time demonstration of basic caregiving capacity, administered around the time of birth by primary-care and early-childhood workers rather than by the punitive child-welfare system—is not merely permissible but required by the same principles my audience already accepts. My argument runs through an asymmetry: we routinely subject adoptive and foster parents to assessment without treating that as an assault on their right to found a family, yet we treat biological parents' entitlement as if it were near-absolute from the moment of birth. I argue that the standard defenses of this asymmetry, from gestational labor, from the lower base rate of harm by biological parents, and from the discriminatory effects of assessment, all fail, and that a modest competence threshold with remedial support rather than removal as the default response to failure would on net expand children's life options rather than narrow them—which is precisely what a child's right to an open future, properly understood, demands.

The audience is mostly liberal political philosophers and family-law scholars: they take children's interests seriously, they are wary of state intrusion into intimate life, and several have written on racial disproportionality in child protective services, on disability and parenting, or on religious accommodation. A number of them endorse something like a right to an open future in the abstract but have argued that the child-welfare bureaucracy is among the worst-placed institutions to secure it. They are not, for the most part, libertarians about procreation; they accept that the state may regulate adoption, surrogacy, and guardianship. Draft me a Q&A preparation sheet for this exact audience: the eight or so objections I will actually receive, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way a liberal family-law scholar would phrase it rather than the way a bioethicist or a generalist would. For each objection, give me a two-minute reply, a clear statement of what I should concede up front, and a note on where the reply is softest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and advise whether to answer it head-on or decline it. I want a committed preparation sheet that makes definite choices about what to concede, what to hold, and which objection I must survive rather than a survey of possible responses.
