im in a cross-listed law/philosophy of software course this term, and last week was copyleft and derivative works. after class our study group got stuck on the prof's recurring question: where does one program end and another begin? the shared setup is this. Priya wrote a small GPL'd parsing library called sluice, plus a command-line frontend for it in the same repo. Jonas has a paid note-taking app that, at runtime, launches sluice's CLI as a subprocess, feeds it text, and parses the output. his app doesn't link to sluice or bundle it; if sluice is missing, the app downloads the stock binary from the release page into its data dir. it can in principle be pointed at any parser exposing the same interface, but in practice everyone uses sluice. Priya says the app is a derivative work of sluice, so the whole app has to be GPL'd. Jonas says he's just running a separate tool. i listened to the whole arguement and i honestly can't sort it.

roughly the exchange went:

Priya: "you dont need to copy code to make a derivative work. your app is a wrapper with extra steps: it exists to call sluice's interface and turn its output into features. the process boundary is just how you chose to do the linking."

Jonas: "that's not what derivative means. copyright protects expression, not function or interoperability. i never adapted, translated, or modified a line of yours. my code opens a documented executable, passes arguments, and reads stdout. if that made my app derivative, every shell script that calls grep is derivative of grep, and the license reaches the whole history of unix plumbing."

Priya: "grep has a life of its own. sluice-the-library doesn't. your app treats my binary as a component: your installer fetches it, your code errors out without it, and the user gets the two as one thing. a work can be derivative as a compilation or a translation without you editing the original. the running artifact your users buy is a compilation of your code and my binary."

Jonas: "but i'm not distributing the binary, and a running system is not a fixed work. the GPL's own mere-aggregation idea is that independent programs don't merge into one work just because they sit on the same disk or talk to each other. my installer fetches sluice; it doesn't contain sluice. the user's disk contains a thousand binaries. is my app derivative of linux because it won't run without the kernel?"

Priya: "thats the dodge. if you had statically linked sluice, or shipped both files in one docker image, you'd admit the whole thing was derivative. you split it across processes and a download script because it looks cleaner, but the product you're selling is the combined tool. a work isn't defined by its pid or its packaging; it's defined by what the author assembled as a single functional unit."

Jonas: "but then 'single functional unit' does all the work, and it's just intent. by that test, a closed-source app that merely prefers and tunes itself to a particular GPL backend becomes derivative, which makes copyleft a tax on compatibility. then closed developers stop using the free tool and clone its interface instead, which is worse for users' freedom, not better. the line that keeps copyleft doing its job is: did you copy, adapt, translate, or bundle protected expression? i did none of those."

that's where they stalled. can you go through this point by point and adjudicate the claims as actually made — who is right about what, where each of them overreaches, and what distinction or consideration both are missing? i don't need case law, a litigation prediction, or a reading list, and please don't guess at court cases or license clauses; just work from the concepts. after the full adjudication, make the very last line start with "Verdict:" and give a short phrase summarizing how you'd split it between them.
