We've been doing a unit in my food ethics course on veganism, and last week someone brought up the classic desert island case: a person who's otherwise a committed vegan is stranded on an island with a pig and no plant food, and almost everyone in the seminar, even the people with the strictest abolitionist lines, said of course it's fine to kill and eat the pig there, because it's necessary for survival and the situation isn't of the person's choosing. I've been stuck on this ever since, because the case feels too tidy. It's one person, one moment, no institutions, no dependents, and no prices. It makes the exception easy by making the circumstances completely outside the person's control and making the need one-off.

So I've been trying to build a variant and I can't figure out how much of the original analysis survives it. Instead of a single person on an island, imagine a smallholder household in a food-insecure, semi-arid region with one short growing season and bad roads to market. They keep a few goats and some chickens. For a stretch of the year there basically aren't affordable or available plant staples, and theres no reliable way to get fortified plant-based alternatives; they could theoretically order them, but they'd take weeks to arrive and cost an absurd share of household income. The mother is nursing, the kids are borderline undernourished, and the eggs and milk and occasional goat meat are a big chunk of their calories. The scarcity isn't a one-off emergency; it recurs every year, everyone in the household knows it's coming, and they plan around eating the animals. It's not a situation they chose, but it also isn't a bolt from the blue the way the island is.

If we run the usual survival exception, a rights view, a utilitarian view, and a capabilities or care-ethics view on this case, do they all just extend the desert island verdict (permissible, maybe tragic, but permissible)? Or does the recurring, structural, institution-laden character of this version change which thing is really being justified: not a desperate act but an ongoing practice, where the household might be planning months ahead to use animals as insurance? And does the location of the moral problem shift? Like, is the household's obligation the important question at all, or does this variation show the desert island case is laundering a structural problem as an individual dilemma? I'm not sure whether I've got a genuine variant or whether I've just changed the subject from ethics to political economy, and I'd like to know where the analysis actually breaks.
