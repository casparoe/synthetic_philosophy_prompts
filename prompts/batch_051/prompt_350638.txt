A friend of mine has been pushing me on this for a week and I still can't decide weather the argument is valid with a false premise or whether something illegal happens between steps. It concludes something I refuse to accept, so one of the above has to be true.

(1) Sadness and grief feel bad to the person in them, and being in a state that feels bad is intrinsically bad for that person.
(2) If a kind of state is intrinsically bad for you, then, other things equal, you have reason to have less of it in your life.
(3) So, other things equal, you have reason to have less sadness and grief in your life.
(4) Suppose a safe drug existed that could blunt sadness and grief after a loss without impairing memory, love, or ordinary functioning — so that other things really are equal.
(5) Then you should take the drug after a bereavement; refusing it would be making your life worse for no compensating gain.
(6) By exactly the same reasoning, the best life would be one in which sadness and grief are headed off at every opportunity — after breakups, failures, deaths, every ordinary disappointment.
(7) But that isn't the best life. Grief is not a glitch we'd be better off engineering out, and a parent who drugged a child through every ordinary loss would be harming the child, not helping them.

Where does this go wrong? I keep suspecting the little phrase "other things equal" is doing something I can't pin down, but maybe the real problem is one of the first two premises. If you do think it's valid, tell me which premise I should give up and what I'd be paying to give it up.
