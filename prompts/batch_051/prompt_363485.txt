I need a working definition of "solvable in principle" as it is used in AI alignment debates—one that would let us sort proposed results into "solved in principle" and "not solved in principle" without confusing the distinction with practical feasibility. Test the definition against these cases:

(a) A formally specified procedure whose correctness proof requires a complete, consistent, and fully introspectable statement of human values, even though no actual human's values have those properties.

(b) A construction that is finite and fully specified, but that could only be carried out by enumerating more possible futures than the observable universe can physically represent.

(c) A nonconstructive proof that, for any finite population of agents with well-behaved preferences, an aligned policy exists—without any indication of how to find it.

(d) A training-and-evaluation regime that has generalized across every benchmark and red-team attempt so far, but has no formal guarantee and no reason to expect the next test to resemble the old ones.

(e) An impossibility result showing that no finite set of behavioral observations uniquely determines a reward function, so any alignment method must introduce substantive assumptions about what is valuable.

I'm inclined to count (c) and (d) as not solved in principle, to count (a) as solved in principle only for a stated idealization rather than for actual human values, and I'm undecided about (b) and (e). Propose the definition, run it through all five, and say which of your verdicts you found least secure.
