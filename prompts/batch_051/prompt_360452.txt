Construct a thought experiment in which two candidates compete for a single benefit — a job, a promotion, a place in a selective program, or an award — and the merit-based ranking and the desert-based ranking of the two candidates are exact inverses: the candidate who is first by merit is second by desert, and the candidate who is second by merit is first by desert. The selectors award the benefit to the more meritorious candidate. By merit I mean fitness for the benefit judged by the institution's legitimate, forward-looking purposes: the qualities that make someone the candidate most likely to advance what the role or award is for. By desert I mean a claim of justice generated by what someone has already done, independently of whether honoring that claim would maximize future performance. I want this case for an undergraduate seminar on distributive justice, where we are asking whether meritocratic allocation can fully answer claims of desert; the inversion must therefore be generated by the structure of the case rather than stipulated or rigged. Constraints: the scenario must be realistic (a workplace, university, hospital, or professional body, not a fable or science-fiction setting); there must be no discrimination, bias, corruption, negligence, or bad faith on anyone's part; the merit criteria must be reasonable criteria a real institution could defend, and the winning candidate's merits must not be portrayed as unearned or luck-contaminated (that is the familiar direction — do not use it); and the losing candidate's desert claim must be grounded in the losing candidate's completed conduct, not in need, sympathy, feelings, or equality. The winning candidate's merits may be fully earned, but the scenario must make clear why those earned merits do not themselves ground a desert claim to this particular benefit. State the case in one paragraph. Then do the check: state both rankings and the verdict each principle gives, identify the specific feature of the case that makes the rankings invert, explain why making the merit criteria finer-grained or adding further merit criteria would not close the gap, and say what would happen if that feature were removed — which verdict would give way and why. Finish by giving the meritocrat's best defense of the allocation and the desert theorist's best response to that defense.
