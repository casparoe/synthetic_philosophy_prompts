I direct a small nonprofit that develops public humanities programming on the legacies of early modern European contact with the Americas. We have recently become interested in Montaigne’s essay *Of Cannibals* because it is invoked both as an early statement of cultural relativism and as something more rhetorical or strategic. The half-formed question I would like you to turn into a proposal is this: when Montaigne observes that people customarily label as barbarous whatever lies outside their own practice, and that they take the opinions and customs of their own country as the standard of truth and reason, is he actually asserting a relativist epistemology, or is he using that observation as a device to humble his European readers so that they might then judge particular practices more accurately? It is not obvious to me whether the passage is self-undermining, deliberately ironic, or contains a more precise position than either of the standard readings allows.

Write a one-page proposal for a foundation that funds work at the intersection of early modern literature and contemporary ethics. State the question precisely so that it has a determinate answer; explain why existing readings either over-assimilate Montaigne to modern relativism or reduce him to a satirist; describe the approach you would take—for example, examining the structure of his comparisons, the role of reported speech, and the distinction he appears to draw between customs, which vary, and nature, which does not—and why that approach rather than a purely historical-context study or a purely theoretical reconstruction; say what a useful deliverable would look like for someone running public programming about cross-cultural judgment; and end with an honest account of what would count as failure and what would need to be true for the project to fit in six months.
