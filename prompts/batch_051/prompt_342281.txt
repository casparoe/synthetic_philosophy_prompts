I am taking a course on constitutional theory this term, and a point that came up in the philosophy of law module has been nagging at me. Several institutions that look very different seem to involve the same underlying maneuver: an agent at one time arranges things so that the same agent at a later time cannot easily do what the earlier agent did not want done. The paradigm, I gather, is the Ulysses contract, after Odysseus ordering his crew to tie him to the mast so that he could hear the Sirens without being able to steer the ship toward them. In a psychiatric advance directive, a person with a history of mania signs, while well, an instruction that if she later refuses treatment during an episode, the hospital may treat her anyway. Many constitutions do something structurally similar: they make certain provisions, such as bills of rights or basic-structure clauses, much harder for an ordinary later majority to change than ordinary legislation. Even a mandatory cooling-off period before a marriage or a high-stakes purchase, and a savings account that imposes penalties for early withdrawal, look like the same pattern.

What I cannot settle is what this family of practices presupposes about the self, rationality, and authority. One could read it as presupposing that the later self is in an important sense a different agent, whose autonomy may be overridden for its own good. Or one could read it as presupposing the opposite: that the earlier and later selves are one continuous agent, and the practice merely enforces that agent's more settled preferences against passing impulses. A third possibility is that the practice presupposes nothing metaphysical at all and is justified on purely welfarist or institutional grounds, simply because it tends to produce better outcomes. I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of the person and of autonomy that these practices collectively presuppose, carefully distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and identifying the points where the practice is underdetermined so that several rival theories would license it. I would also like you to say whether the personal cases, such as advance directives, and the political cases, such as constitutional entrenchment, presuppose consistent things, or whether the political case is only a loose analogy that hides a different justificatory logic.
