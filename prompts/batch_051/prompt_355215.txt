I am a PhD student working on inverse reinforcement learning and human decision-making. Our group fits reward functions to people's choices in sequential tasks by assuming people are approximately Boltzmann-rational and then searching over a parameterized family of reward functions for the one that best explains their trajectories. After a recent lab meeting, one colleague made a claim I keep disputing: if a reward function learned in one set of tasks predicts a person's choices in genuinely novel tasks, then we have not merely found a compact behavioral summary—we have recovered the person's actual preferences, the evaluation that was doing the causal work inside their head during choice.

I suspect this is too strong. On any finite set of demonstrations, many distinct reward functions are behaviorally equivalent, and when a learned reward function transfers to new tasks, it may be transferring the inductive biases of our model class rather than a latent psychological object. I would like to separate these two readings empirically rather than by methodological fiat.

Design the strongest feasible experiment you can for testing the claim that an inferred reward function is the human's real preference function, as opposed to a useful fiction that merely rationalizes observed and held-out behavior. Specify the task or tasks, any manipulations and measures, the outcome that would support each reading, and how decisive that outcome could realistically be. Then be explicit about what even the most favorable result would leave open—in particular whether any purely behavioral test can distinguish a true preference from a consistently applied policy, or whether the concept of a real preference is being measured at all rather than further operationalized.
