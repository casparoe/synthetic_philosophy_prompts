For the past several months I have been working under a new performance-monitoring system whose effects I can feel but not yet articulate, and I would be grateful for help thinking it through. I lead a small editorial team at a mid-sized company. Last year the company introduced an analytics platform that automatically records when each employee first logs in, which applications they use and for how long, how many documents they create, and how long files remain untouched. Management was emphatic that the tool would serve aggregate workflow analysis, never individual evaluation, and to my knowledge no one has been disciplined on the basis of it. At first I accepted this without much unease.

What has unsettled me is how the platform has quietly become the authority on what counts as productive. Team leads now cite dashboard activity when asking why a project "looks quiet," even when the work is moving forward in ways the tool does not register. Colleagues have adapted: several open a document inside a tracked application immediately upon arriving because the weekly summary shows how long each person takes before first activity, and a few of us now save deep, slow work for evenings when tracking feels less salient. Because the logs are retained, no one can be sure what future manager, audit, or automated report might eventually make of them. None of this is required, and no one has threatened anyone. Yet the instrument itself—simply by existing and by making our work visible in one particular way—seems to have begun directing our conduct.

Recently I described this to a friend outside the company, and he argued that I was confusing observation with coercion. If no one is actually watching and no one can be sanctioned on the basis of the record, he said, then the platform is only information; people remain free to ignore it, and if they do not, that is their own self-consciousness, not power. He added that stretching "control" to cover this situation empties the word of meaning. His argument has unsettled me, not because it seems true, but because I cannot say precisely what it misses. I would like to understand whether my sense that a form of power is operating here is defensible, and, if it is, what its mechanism is. I would also like to know what specifically I should argue when I push back at work, beyond the bare demand that the tool be turned off.
