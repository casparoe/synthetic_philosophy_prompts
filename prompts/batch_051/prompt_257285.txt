My idea concerns the paradox of deontology. As I understand it, the paradox runs like this. A deontologist holds both that there is a constraint against killing, grounded in something like what is bad about a killing, and that one must not kill one person even to prevent five killings. The two commitments seem to clash, because the constraint appears to forbid minimizing the very thing it exists to protect. My thought is that the paradox only gets going if the badness that grounds the constraint is treated as an impersonal quantity that any agent has reason to minimize. If the constraint is grounded instead in what the agent would do to a particular person — in using or sacrificing that person as a means — then refusing to kill one to save five is not a failure to minimize the thing the constraint protects; it is a refusal to perform the specific wrong the constraint prohibits. My worry is that this thought may just restate the distinction between constraints and consequentialist minimization without explaining why the distinction is morally significant.
