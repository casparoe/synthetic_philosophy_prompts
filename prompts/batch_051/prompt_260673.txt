I'm a novelist, and I've been asked to give a five-minute toast at the wedding of two old friends. The room will include several philosophers as well as relatives who have no appetite for a lecture. One of the two people being married—my oldest friend—is habitually and almost constitutionally late, has kept every friend waiting more times than anyone can count, yet is also the person everyone calls first in a genuine emergency and has never once failed to show up when it mattered. I want the toast to take a philosophical question seriously rather than decorate itself with wedding clichés: why should small promises such as "I'll be there at seven" carry moral weight even when breaking them seems to cause no real harm and is easily forgiven? The toast should argue for a position on that question—perhaps that small promises matter because they are the granular material out of which trust and reliability are built—and then use that position to say something true and affectionate about the marriage, without flattering the couple or pretending they will become punctual. The one firm constraint is structural: because the toast itself is a speech act offered in the couple's presence and on their behalf, it must not make or imply any promise about their future, even of the smallest kind. Please write the toast in American English.
