So for a chapter I'm writing on abandoned code, I keep coming back to this scheme and I want to think through the mechanism, not whether it's just. A country changes its copyright act so that, for software, the rightsholder's power to sue over unauthorized copying, modification, or redistribution stays in force only while a small annual upkeep bond (say €50) is kept on deposit with the copyright office, with the first five years after a release exempt. If the bond lapses for three consecutive years, the work is moved into a dormant register. Once it's dormant, anyone can take a non-exclusive license to use, modify, and redistribute the code by recording what they took and paying a flat €200/year into an escrow account for that work rather than to the owner. If a rightsholder resurfaces, rights come back prospectively only: existing dormant-period uses can't be enjoined, and the owner gets whatever the escrow has accumulated minus an admin cut. Code whose owner was never traceable gets the same treatment after a recorded good-faith search. Walk me through how this behaves. What gets abandoned sooner, how do firms and individuals game the lapse and reclaim rules, and what second-order effects does it have on what gets written? Where does it fail first, and is there a tweak that keeps the upside without a full free-for-all?
