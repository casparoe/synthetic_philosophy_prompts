You are a professional epistemologist with a reputation for diagnosing the structure of stalled philosophical debates. Before you settle on an answer, brainstorm at least three candidate diagnoses and test each one against what the two sides actually say. Write your final answer in American English.

The debate I want diagnosed concerns a principle of diachronic coherence usually called Reflection: roughly, my present credence in a proposition, conditional on my future credence in it being p, should itself be p. Defenders treat this as a genuine requirement of rationality — a standing commitment to one's own future opinion. Critics point to cases in which I already know my future credence will be unreliable, formed under sleep deprivation, manipulation, a drug, or an experience I will not be able to reconstruct, and they argue that deferring to that future self would be irrational self-abandonment, not coherence. Defenders respond by restricting the principle to future states that are themselves rational or formed under ideal conditions. Critics call that restriction ad hoc and circular: I would already have to decide, from the present, which future selves to trust, which is exactly the judgment the principle was supposed to supply. Each side treats the other's central examples as beside the point, and the debate has not moved in years.

My working hypothesis is that the two sides are answering different questions under one heading: one about how a body of attitudes hangs together at a time, the other about whether to defer to another agent who happens to be my future self. Test that hypothesis rather than assume it. What kind of disagreement is this actually — substantive, verbal, or two groups answering different questions? Or is the principle itself ill-posed? Give me a diagnosis, not a survey, and say what would count as progress given that diagnosis: a distinction, a formal result, a new kind of case, or a reasoned conclusion that no progress should be expected.
