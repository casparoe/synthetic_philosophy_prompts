I spent my career as an experimental scientist, and I was trained to report a measured quantity with an error bar, never as a bare number that claimed more precision than the instrument justified. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy, and I find myself circling a question the laboratory posed without my ever making it explicit: when is a rational agent entitled to a single numerical degree of belief, and when is the honest state of mind a set of numbers? Let me record my verdicts on a list of cases before I say what principle, if any, I take them to express. When I speak of an interval, I mean a set of probabilities I am prepared to treat as admissible, not a frequentist confidence interval.

1. A coin I have examined and found symmetric. I place a sharp probability of one-half on heads. Here sharpness is exactly right; an interval would be an affectation.

2. A coin found in a drawer, about whose bias I know nothing. I refuse to assign any single number, and in particular I refuse one-half, even though the labels heads and tails are symmetric. The bias is a fixed physical property of the coin and need not respect that symmetry. My state is the entire interval from zero to one.

3. I now flip that drawer coin twenty times and observe fourteen heads. I still refuse a single number, but I expect the interval to shrink to something in the neighborhood of 0.35 to 0.90. I realize the difficulty: every bias strictly between zero and one remains consistent with fourteen heads in twenty flips, so a rule that keeps only the values compatible with the evidence would leave the interval essentially unchanged. I nonetheless feel strongly that it should shrink.

4. Before any flips of the drawer coin, I am offered a ticket for ten cents that pays one dollar if it lands heads. I would buy it. A decision rule that always maximizes the worst-case expected value over my zero-to-one interval would refuse the ticket, since the worst case is that heads never occurs. I regard that refusal as absurd.

5. The same ticket offered for sixty cents: I would refuse it. So I am not simply ignoring the interval when I act. I accept at ten cents and refuse at sixty cents, but I cannot state the principle that selects exactly that pair.

6. Two forecasters whose models I cannot rank put the chance of rain tomorrow at 0.2 and 0.8. I want my state to be the set containing both numbers, or the interval between them; averaging them to 0.5 seems to me to present a conflict as if it were a consensus.

7. A coin known to be fair is about to be flipped. If it lands heads, a ball is drawn from an urn of completely unknown red/blue composition; if it lands tails, the ball is drawn from an urn known to be exactly half red. Before the coin is flipped, my interval for the proposition that the ball drawn will be red is 0.25 to 0.75: with probability one-half I face the known red chance of one-half, and with probability one-half I face the unknown urn. After I learn that the coin has landed heads, but before I see the ball, my interval becomes zero to one. I find this unacceptable. The coin result tells me which urn I am dealing with, but it is not evidence about the color of the ball, and it seems wrong that receiving information should leave me more ignorant than I was before.

The principle I have been groping toward is this: adopt a single number only when a symmetry or a known chance fixes it; otherwise carry the set of probabilities left open by the evidence, and update by conditioning each admissible probability on what you learn. When forced to act, maximize expected value against the worst member of the set. I already see that this package cannot be right as it stands: it refuses the ten-cent ticket, and it produces the widening verdict in case 7, which I cannot accept.

So I am asking for a diagnosis. Is there a principled account that delivers my verdicts in cases 1 through 7, or nearly all of them? State it precisely, show how it handles each case, and say plainly which of my verdicts it overturns. If no account can get all seven, tell me which verdict is doing the damage and what the least costly repair is. Finally, test the account on a case I keep going back and forth about. A manufacturer tells me its coins come from a process that produces all biases, distributed uniformly from zero to one, and I am handed one such coin before any flips. One part of me says I may now return to a sharp one-half, because the production process is known. Another part says the coin still has an unknown fixed bias, and I have learned nothing about this particular coin, so I should remain at the whole interval from zero to one. Which verdict does your account give, and why?
