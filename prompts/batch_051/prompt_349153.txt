we're doing the Meno in my epistemology class and I can't shake this. The argument for why knowledge is better than true belief is that true belief is like one of Daedalus's statues that runs away until it's tied down, and the thing that ties it down is an account of the reason why. So knowledge's extra value is supposed to come from the tie, from stability, not from being more true. But now put that next to a huge trained model. It makes lots of correct calls, sometimes more reliably across weird cases than our explicit theories do, and nobody can turn it's weights into an account of the reason why any particular output came out right. If anything, the explicit theory is what runs away when the situation shifts, while the model with no articulable account keeps working. Does the Meno argument survive this? Which premise is actually under pressure: that untied true beliefs are unstable, or that the tie has to be a human-articulable account? Could the weights count as a tie even though no one can state it, or does that just collapse knowledge into prediction? And if what mattered all along was cross-context reliability rather than the tie, what's left of the Meno problem — just an engineering question about which representations generalize?
