I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but I keep running into one question in practice: when is it fair to hold someone to a rule they didn't actually know about? I've collected my gut verdicts on a series of cases, and I want to know whether a single principle captures them or whether I need to give some of them up.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

- A developer ships a product containing an open-source component whose license requires attribution. The license text sits in a LICENSE file in the repository, not in the README or package metadata, and the developer never opened it. I think it is fair to hold them to the attribution requirement.
- A small company keeps using an API in a way that the provider's updated terms of service prohibit. The provider sent one email with a link to the new terms; the email landed in spam and no one read it. I don't think it is fair to hold them to the new prohibition, even though the terms were technically available.
- A US-based engineer exports encryption software to a country subject to US export restrictions, without realizing that the software counts as a controlled item. I think they are rightly on the hook: some rules are the kind of thing you are simply expected to check before you enter the field.
- An employee signs a contract with a non-compete buried in an appendix they did not read. I think they are bound: they signed it, and it was right there in what they signed.
- A developer copies a solution from a programming Q&A site, assuming answers there are free to use, and later learns the answer was posted under a license that requires attribution and share-alike. I don't think they should be held to those license terms for code they did not know was governed by them.
- A company starts collecting biometric data from employee timeclocks and misses a state privacy law that took effect two months earlier and applied to them once they crossed an employee-count threshold they had only just crossed. No regulator publicized the law; it was in the statute books. Holding them to it feels wrong, but I cannot say why, given that the export-control case went the other way.

My working rule is that people are bound by rules they could have discovered with reasonable effort, and not bound by rules they could not. But my own cases pull apart on what counts as reasonable effort and on whether the kind of rule matters.

What I want is a committed answer, not a survey of options. State the principle that best captures these verdicts, or the small set of candidate principles if one will not do; run it against a case I have not listed, say a developer who ships a product under a name that later turns out to be someone else's unregistered trademark; and tell me which of my verdicts, if any, I have to abandon.
