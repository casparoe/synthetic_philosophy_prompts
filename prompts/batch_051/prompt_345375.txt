I spent a long evening arguing with a friend about the Darwin/Turing inversion: the idea that design and intelligent performance can both be produced without comprehension, by natural selection in the one case and by rule-governed or statistical computation in the other. I am drawn to the view because it is unifying and deflationary — one story for termite mounds, immune systems, and search engines, with nothing in the system needing to understand what it is doing. But my friend kept pressing a point I cannot shake: there seems to be a real difference between a system that gets things right and one that understands what it is doing. The student who can say why an algorithm applies here and not there, or the reasoner who catches their own mistake, appears to have something the merely competent system lacks, and it shows up in novel cases, in self-correction, and in being answerable for what one does. I do not want to be told which side wins. I want a negotiated verdict: should that intuition be debunked and dropped, should the inversion be amended to give comprehension a distinct explanatory role, or can both be kept once the relevant terms are drawn carefully? Present each side at its strongest, say what would in principle justify each of those moves, and tell me how confident you are in the overall verdict and which point you are least sure of.
