I am a quality manager at a plant that makes precision-machined components for surgical robots. I came up through the machining side rather than through a university route, so I want to be careful not to let academic language carry a point I should be able to make in plain words. Every serialized component that leaves the plant is inspected twice and ships with an individual certificate of conformance. From our own defect data, I estimate that a component that has passed all of our checks still has about one chance in two thousand of being nonconforming. A typical shipment holds about two thousand components, so the shipment as a whole is a little better than even odds to contain at least one bad component.

I have become convinced that we should say so in writing. My proposal, which I will present to senior management next month, is to keep issuing every individual certificate exactly as before, but to add a one-page cover note to each large shipment stating that, at our documented escape rate, a lot of this size is more likely than not to contain at least one nonconforming component. I believe the two statements can both be made honestly: we can stand behind each individual component and also warn the customer about the lot as a whole. To me this is not really a paperwork question. It is about whether a person can make a long series of high-confidence claims one by one and also assert, at the same time, that at least one of those claims is wrong. I suspect this is connected to what philosophers call the preface paradox or the lottery paradox, but I only know those labels secondhand and do not want the argument to depend on them.

The audience is our senior operations team: the plant manager, who trained as an electrical engineer; the head of quality, a former chemist; the finance director; and the commercial lawyer. They are practical, intelligent people with no interest in philosophy, and they tend to treat a certificate as an unconditional promise. In their vocabulary, a part either conforms or it does not, and if we are not prepared to say that the entire shipment is conforming, then we should not have signed off on the individual units.

Prepare me for the meeting. List the objections I will actually hear, worded the way this audience would put them, and order them by how much trouble they can cause me, weighing how hard they will press each one against how weak my reply is. For each objection, give me a short reply I could deliver in the room and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front before anyone raises it, identify the single reply that has to land for the proposal to survive, and tell me the question I should most hope nobody asks.
