Here's an argument I've been half-defending for years, and I cant decide whether it's a genuine piece of modal metaphysics or a mistake with the quantifiers. It purports to show that everything that exists, exists necessarily.

(1) Necessarily, everything is self-identical. A thing just is itself; I don't see how this could fail.
(2) If necessarily everything is self-identical, then each thing is necessarily self-identical. This is just bringing the necessity inside the quantifier—I gather it's the converse Barcan formula, or at least an instance of it.
(3) So each thing is necessarily self-identical.
(4) But a thing can't be self-identical at a world where it doesn't exist: to stand in the identity relation, it has to be there. And this seems to hold necessarily.
(5) So each thing necessarily exists. In other words, nothing that actually exists could have failed to exist.

I half-suspect the real action is in (2), or maybe in what 'everything' ranges over at a world, but I keep going in circles. Is the argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails, on exactly what reading of the quantifier? Give me a committed verdict and tell me how confident you are in it—no surveys. And if the whole setup is confused (say I'm just assuming constant domains, or running together two senses of 'exists'), say so plainly rather than playing along.
