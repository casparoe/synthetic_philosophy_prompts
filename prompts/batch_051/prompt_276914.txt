I teach an undergraduate algorithms course and I've also been reading Herbert Simon on bounded rationality, and a structural parallel between the two keeps nagging at me. On the one hand, online algorithms. You don't demand that the algorithm compute the offline optimum — that would be unfair, since it has to commit to decisions before seeing the rest of the input. Instead you ask for a guarantee relative to that optimum: for a given problem, say, that its worst-case cost is at most twice what an algorithm with the whole input in advance would have paid. On the other hand, satisficing. A boundedly rational agent doesn't try to identify the best option either; it searches through options until one clears an aspiration level, then stops, and the interesting questions are all about where the aspiration level comes from and how it adapts when search gets too easy or too hard. So both set up a comparison between a limited decider and an ideal one, and both quietly swap 'choose the best' for a different kind of success criterion.

Is that a genuine identity of structure, a loose analogy, or just me pattern-matching on the phrase 'good enough'? If it's genuine, what would transfer — is there a satisficing analogue of the competitive ratio, or an online-algorithms analogue of aspiration-level adaptation? If it's shallow, I'd like to know exactly where it snaps: the offline optimum, the adversary, the source of the limits, or somewhere else. State the mapping cleanly and then stress-test it, and try to name at least one thing the parallel would lead you to expect that turns out false on one side or the other. Please write the answer in British English.
