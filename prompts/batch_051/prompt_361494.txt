After years of noticing that whichever capital case I had heard about most recently dominated my opinion, I adopted a firm policy for thinking about the death penalty: I no longer let individual cases move me. I will read about a wrongful conviction or a particularly brutal murder, note the facts, but refuse to let either shift my general position; I update only on aggregate, peer-reviewed evidence about deterrence, error rates, and the like. I apply the rule symmetrically, so I am not selectively screening out stories that cut against my view. The policy has made my beliefs much more stable, and it has also made me suspicious of that very stability. It feels like a reasonable accommodation to a known weakness of mine — my tendency to overweight the vivid and recent — but I can also see that individual cases are not merely rhetorical: one innocent person nearly executed is, among other things, a data point about what the system actually does. Is this policy defensible as a way of managing my own psychology, or is it a bias that has dressed itself up as a discipline? How could I tell the difference from the inside, and is there a version of the policy that keeps its protection without silently filtering out evidence I find inconvenient?
