The proposition I want your credence on: across the usual set of countries, the finding that higher gender-equality scores go with larger sex differences in STEM field choice survives reasonable fixes to measurement and coding, and the best explanation of what survives is that more egalitarian conditions let intrinsic, partly biology-influenced preference differences show up more — rather then the whole thing being measurement noise or just the fact that rich, equal countries can afford to have people choose intrinsically interesting but lower-paid jobs. Give me your credence in that proposition. Decompose it into at least four sub-questions with a rough probability and one line of justification for each: (a) that the association survives once you use an equality measure that doesn't mechanically include female labor-force participation, or that splits egalitarian attitudes out from the composite index; (b) that it survives defensible decisions about what gets counted as STEM and about whether you look at degrees, occupations, high-school intentions, or all three; (c) that what's left after (a) and (b) is not mostly national wealth and economic security — i.e., the "choose by interest, not pay" confound; and (d) that if you could run the impossible experiment of raising a country's gender equality exogenously, the STEM sex gap would widen rather than narrow. Show how these multiply into your final number. Then tell me which of (a)–(d) your final credence is most sensitive to, and what single study, dataset, or analysis would move it the most. And give one sentence on what you'd conclude about the nature/culture/justice triangle if (d) came back false but (a)–(c) came back true.
