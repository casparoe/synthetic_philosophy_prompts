Reading about strategy, and later recognizing the same shape in a local dispute, I keep noticing a pattern I do not have a name for. Two parties each know that whichever of them makes an irreversible move first will change the other's options and thereby gain an advantage. Each therefore feels pressure to move early. But the moves being made early are valuable precisely because they throw options away: a pledge, a threat, or an exclusive deal has force only because it cannot be retracted cheaply. The result is that both parties hurry to tie their own hands, and each ends up having surrendered more flexibility than they would have chosen in a calmer, less exposed moment.

The clearest example I saw involved two firms considering whether to sign a long-term exclusive contract with a scarce supplier. The first to sign would foreclose the other. Both would have preferred to wait for more market information, but waiting meant being the one who lost the supplier; in the end they signed almost simultaneously, and both wound up bound to terms that looked poor once the uncertainty resolved. A different example came from a local organization: two factions each hurried to be the first to announce a public bright line that would be humiliating to retract, because the first announcement would frame the subsequent negotiation. Both then found themselves defending positions that, a week earlier, neither had actually held.

Is there an established concept for this three-part pattern: (i) the first irreversible commitment earns an advantage, (ii) the advantage exists because options have been surrendered rather than because any capability has been added, and (iii) both parties surrender more optionality than either wanted, because the fear of being second overrides the benefit of waiting? It looks different from an arms race, which accumulates capabilities, and from ordinary precommitment, in which one party ties its hands on its own schedule without a rival setting the pace. It also does not seem to be the familiar escalation-of-commitment problem, since the loss is not a slow, defensive response to sunk costs but an upfront, strategic surrender of options. I am after the carving rather than just a label: what exactly this pattern is, how to draw its boundaries against those neighboring concepts, and what it would most precisely be called—or what it should be named if it has no standard name.
