Legal systems that profess commitment to the rule of law treat retroactive criminal legislation as close to the paradigm of what the rule of law forbids: people should be governed by rules they could have known before acting, not by standards manufactured after the fact. Yet most of those same systems tolerate, and indeed routinely practice, a form of retroactivity performed by courts. Suppose a criminal statute has long been read narrowly, and people have ordered their affairs around that reading. In June the highest court announces that the statute actually has a broader sweep, and applies the new reading to conduct that occurred the previous November. If the legislature had amended the statute in June to give it that same broader sweep and applied the amendment to similar November conduct, the application would be treated as a textbook retroactivity violation. But the legal consequences attached to completed conduct are changed in both cases. Is there a principled difference between the two, or is this an asymmetry the rule-of-law ideal should not countenance? I want the strongest defense of the asymmetry you can construct—whether it appeals to a distinction between making law and finding it, to the structure of adversarial adjudication, to reliance, or to something else—and I want you to test that defense against the hard variant above, where the earlier reading was widespread, reasonable, and settled, and the new interpretation could not have been anticipated. End with a clear verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, narrowed, or abandoned.
