I'm currently in a course on Marx's critique of political economy, and I'm trying to pin down a working definition of exploitation in Marx's technical sense, kept distinct both from the looser moralized use of the word and from alienation. In Capital the notion seems tied to the distinction between labor and labor-power, to the contrast between necessary labor time and surplus labor time, and to the appropriation of surplus value, but I'm unsure how tightly the definition should bind itself to the wage relation. I'd like a definition that gives a clear verdict for each of the following cases:

1. A factory worker on an hourly wage whose shift produces more value than she is paid; I take this to be a clear case of exploitation.
2. A self-employed carpenter who owns her own tools and sells furniture at market prices; I take this not to be exploitation.
3. A member of a worker cooperative who owns an equal share, works on the shop floor, and receives an equal portion of the surplus.
4. A tenant who pays rent to an absentee landlord, with the rent funded out of the tenant's wage income.
5. An unpaid partner doing full-time domestic and care work in a household supported by a single wage.
6. A gig worker formally classified as an independent contractor but whose jobs, pay rates, and routes are algorithmically determined by a platform.
7. A salaried financial analyst at a commercial bank, paid out of profits generated elsewhere in the firm.

I'm fairly confident about the first two and genuinely uncertain about the rest. Please propose the definition, run it through all seven cases, and flag any case where the definition gives a counterintuitive or no answer; then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. Where the classification depends on a choice about how to read Marx, for example whether only productive wage labor can be exploited or whether landlords and commercial employees count, make that choice explicit. Finally, say which of your verdicts you regard as least secure and why.
