I came across this piece in a popular philosophy magazine—pretty sure the author is a science writer, not a logician—and it's been bugging me all week. The author gives a quick sketch of two paradoxes and then draws a big conclusion from them, and I honestly can't tell if it's fair.

As the author tells it, Curry's paradox is a sentence C that says "if this sentence is true, then P," where P can be any sentence you like (say, the moon is made of cheese). Ordinary reasoning about truth gets you P, so the whole system trivializes. The author's moral is that, unlike the plain liar ("this sentence is false"), Curry never uses negation, so switching to a paraconsistent logic—the move people make to live with the liar's contradiction—doesn't save you.

Then the article gives Yablo's paradox: an infinite list of sentences S1, S2, S3, ... where each one says "every sentence after me on the list is false." You can prove the list is contradictory, but no sentence ever mentions itself; each one only talks about the sentences below it. So the author's grand conclusion is that the real culprit in the liar-family paradoxes was never negation and never self-reference: Curry knocks out the negation diagnosis, Yablo knocks out the self-reference diagnosis, and what's left as the thing to give up is the unrestricted truth schema (the principle that "P" is true iff P).

I'm not a logician so I definately can't tell where this oversteps. Is it fair to the two paradoxes? In particular, is it actually right that paraconsistency won't help with Curry? And is Yablo's "no self-reference" point really uncontroversial, or do some people argue the construction is still circular? Does the author's framing of "blame the truth schema, not negation or self-reference" even make sense, or is that a false choice? If the author is being unfair somewhere, what would a careful logician say is the more limited lesson of each paradox?
