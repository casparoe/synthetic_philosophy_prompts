I hold a broadly Hofstadterian view of the self: a self is not the physical brain that hosts it but a pattern of thought, memory, style, and disposition, and such a pattern can in principle be copied into other brains, partially and imperfectly. On that view, when someone you know well dies, a genuine fraction of their self persists in you and in everyone else who internalized enough of their pattern. I put my credence in the literal version of that claim at about 0.55, where "literal" means that the surviving pattern is not merely a memory of the dead person or a model of their behavior, but an actual continuing part of their I. What is your credence? I want it decomposed rather than a bare number. Give me at least the following terms: (i) the probability that the self is substrate-independent in a strong enough sense for the question to be a factual one rather than a metaphor; (ii) the probability that "partial survival" is a coherent notion at all — that it makes sense to speak of degrees of continuation of a self rather than an all-or-nothing matter; (iii) the probability that real human brains actually carry a rich enough fine-grained copy of another person's characteristic patterns, as opposed to a compressed third-person model; and (iv) the probability that what I am pointing at is fully explained by the continuing causal influence of the deceased plus grief, with no literal survival involved. Assign a rough probability to each, show how they combine into your final number, and then say which sub-question your final credence is most sensitive to and what evidence — real or hypothetical — would move it most. I suspect part of my 0.55 comes from motivated cognition rather than argument; the decomposition is meant to mark the weak points, so be explicit about where yours are.
