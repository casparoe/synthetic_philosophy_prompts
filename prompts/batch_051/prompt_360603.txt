Recently I began producing probability estimates for my own future deliverables at work, and I have found myself caught in what now looks like a principled boundary rather than a bookkeeping problem. When I assign a probability to one of my own future choices—say, that I will send a difficult email by Thursday—the number feeds back into the choice. A high number relaxes me and lowers the underlying chance, so the forecast overshoots; a low number alarms me and raises the chance, so the forecast undershoots. Some values undo themselves as soon as they are sincerely adopted.

I have been developing a small framework that I would like assessed. I call a first-personal probability a stable self-prediction only if it possesses what I call a disclosure fixed point: a value p such that, if I sincerely adopt p and report it to the person awaiting my deliverable, the chance of the action after that adoption and disclosure has done its full work is still p. When such a fixed point exists and is unique, I treat p as a genuine forecast about myself. When no fixed point exists, I say the action lies inside my deliberative shadow. My central claim is that within the deliberative shadow, probability assignments to one's own choices are not true or false reports of an independent fact; they are moves in the deliberation—closer to commitments than to measurements—and should be represented as intentions carrying a credibility weight, not as forecasts.

The boundary seems to track something real. I can sensibly assign a probability to whether past-me finished something, and to whether I will catch a train once the route is already settled; but not, it seems, to whether I will decide to take the difficult route. The proposed rule is not that agents lack evidence about themselves—my partner often forecasts my choices better than I can—but that first-personal prediction while a choice is still open occupies a different mode, and forcing it into the register of probability turns a decision into a datum.

I would like an honest appraisal. Is the distinction coherent, or does it collapse into ordinary points about conditioning on different information sets? Does it already exist under another name? Where does it break? I suspect it either proves too much, by ruling out ordinary planning under uncertainty about one's own discipline, or too little, by letting self-predictions back in once they find any artificial fixed point. Please give me the three strongest objections and say whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable.
