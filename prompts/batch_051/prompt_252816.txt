we read Williams's integrity objection in my ethics class and it's really gotten under my skin. I find myself pretty convinced by impartial consequentialism — or at least by the idea that moral reasons have to be the kind of thing that could justify a choice to anyone from a standpoint that doesnt just privilege my own attachments. The theory is clean and it actually tells me what to do. But the wife-rescue case keeps bothering me: the man who can save only one person, one of them his wife, and who stops to work out whether saving her is permissible before he acts. Williams says that's one thought too many, and I have the same reaction — the hesitation itself feels like a moral failure, not a bonus point for being impartial. I dont want a verdict, I want to know which of these should give. Is the intuition something an impartialist can accommodate by saying the deliberation is fine, just not something the agent should consciously do? Or is it tracking a real gap in the theory, that some reasons are essentially partial and can't be represented from the outside? Give me the strongest version of each side, then tell me what it would take for the intuition to be the thing I drop, and what it would take for the theory to be the thing I amend.
