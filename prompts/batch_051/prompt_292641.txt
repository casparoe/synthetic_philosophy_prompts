I spent most of my working life in experimental biology, and only after retiring have I begun to read philosophy of mind in any systematic way. The dispute between representationalist and enactivist accounts of cognition keeps frustrating me for a reason that may be familiar to you: the two sides often seem to be grading each other against standards they have not stated in advance. A representationalist will say that the bacterium or the insect is not really cognizing because it lacks the right kind of internal model; an enactivist will say that the same behavior is skilled, world-involving coping, and that the demand for an internal model only looks compulsory once one has already decided what a theory of cognition must explain. Before I read any further in this literature, I would like the full specification of what an adequate theory of cognition would have to deliver in order to settle, or at least usefully reframe, this debate.

Please write me that list of desiderata. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a prejudice of one camp, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or merely a party preference. The list should at least engage the following boundary phenomena, described here without presupposing either side's conclusion: a bacterium that swims toward a food source by modulating its rate of tumbling as a function of how the chemical concentration has changed over the preceding few seconds; an outfielder who runs to intercept a fly ball under conditions where keeping the ball's optical trajectory linear would itself bring the fielder to the right place, with no computed prediction of the landing point; an infant who has repeatedly retrieved a toy hidden at location A, then watches it hidden at location B and nonetheless reaches again toward A; and the difference between fluent, absorbed activity such as playing an instrument and deliberate planning of a novel problem. Where two desiderata appear to pull against each other — for instance, a demand that the theory be empirically testable and a demand that it respect the first-person character of cognitive life — say whether the tension is real or only apparent. I am most interested in whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, or satisfiable only from a position nobody in the debate actually wants to occupy. A brief note on how representationalism, enactivism, and any intermediate views fare against your list is welcome, but keep it secondary: the criteria are what I am after.
