Design a thought experiment that separates two accounts of letterform identity, in the Letter Spirit domain: the question of what makes every 'a' an 'a' across styles. The case must be built from gridletter-like glyphs — letters drawn on a single fixed coarse rectangular grid, no larger than 9 columns by 7 rows, written out as ASCII or as row-by-row # and . patterns.

The two accounts you must force apart:

- Local structuralism: A glyph is the lowercase letter 'a' if and only if its own grid satisfies a fixed, context-free structural schema for 'a', which you must state in grid terms (for example, an enclosed bowl on the left at least two rows tall, a vertical stem on the right, and no other enclosed region). Context never changes the verdict.

- Stylistic role theory: A glyph is an 'a' only when the best global interpretation of the entire set assigns it the 'a'-role. A global interpretation imposes a single coherent style — stroke weight, bowl shape, x-height, serif behavior, and so on — across all the glyphs, and simultaneously assigns each grid to a letter-role inside that style. A glyph whose local geometry matches the 'a'-schema can fail to be an 'a' if it is stylistically alien from its neighbors; the same grid may be an 'a' in one context and a stylistically impossible form in another.

Construct two contexts and one probe with these specifications:

- Context 1 and Context 2 are each a short set of lowercase glyphs (no more than five glyphs per set) plus one probe glyph occupying the same slot in each set.
- The probe must be literally the same grid in both contexts; the contexts must differ only in the surrounding glyphs.
- In Context 1, both local structuralism and stylistic role theory must classify the probe as an 'a'.
- In Context 2, local structuralism must still classify the probe as an 'a', while stylistic role theory must refuse it — either rejecting it as a malformed member of the style or reassigning it to another letter-role. Say which refusal you chose and justify it.

Further constraints:

- The glyphs must be humanly recognizable as letters, not degenerate smears or one-cell tricks; the probe should look passable as an 'a' in Context 1.
- The divergence must be traced to one identifiable style variable (such as stroke weight or bowl shape), not to a rewrite of the 'a'-schema or to ad hoc exceptions.
- Include at least two control glyphs on which the two accounts agree, and state each account's verdict on them.

Then demonstrate the case. Give the ASCII for both contexts and the shared probe; state the schema local structuralism uses and the style stylistic role theory attributes to each context; step through the verdict each account delivers for the probe in each context and for the controls, and explain why they diverge. Identify the single feature doing the work, say what happens to the divergence when it is removed, and explain how a defender of the losing account would most fairly attack the case (for example, by alleging that the style judgment is circular or that the style has been tailored after the fact).

The case is for isolating local geometric category membership from global stylistic coherence. A case that stipulates the probe's classification rather than generating it from the stated schema and style judgments, or whose contexts are not coherent styles, fails the assignment.

Lead with your bottom line: in the first sentence, state the realized case and its key result — which style variable is manipulated, how the probe reads in each context, and which account leaves — before any glyphs or reasoning. Then give the glyphs and the step-by-step demonstration. No preamble and no restating of the assignment.
