On the rationalist-adjacent forums I have long read, one move keeps appearing in discussions of productivity and akrasia: people assert, as if it were obvious, that one's future self is a different person, and then use that to settle practical questions in a particular direction. A representative comment would run something like this: the person who will be sitting at my desk at 1 a.m. deciding whether to keep scrolling is not me; he is a stranger with a track record of poor decisions, and I have no more reason to defer to his preferences than to a stranger's. So I should not negotiate with him or leave the decision open; I should bind him — site blockers, automatic transfers, commitments that are costly to undo. The analogy is being asked to carry a specific conclusion: present preferences deserve to be treated as sovereign and future preferences as an externality, so precommitment is not a restriction of your own liberty but a form of self-defense against a distinct agent.

I want to know how far this analogy actually goes. If I take it seriously, what plays the role of the stranger, the shared property, the negotiation, and the track record of bad decisions? Where does the mapping break — and which of those breaks matter for the conclusion being drawn, rather than being incidental differences? What does the analogy quietly import from ordinary relationships between separate people that may be absent between successive stages of one person? And if the disanalogies matter, does the precommitment conclusion survive anyway, or is it being carried by the picture of two separate agents? If there is a better comparison than another person, give me that too.
