My research group has been running an adversarial collaboration between two camps that have spent a year disagreeing about whether an AI system can be instructed to avoid side effects without anyone first specifying what humans value. One camp, the structuralists, holds that low-impact behavior can be characterized without a full theory of value: roughly, an act counts as a harmful side effect to the extent that it is irreversible, that it reduces the agent's later ability to achieve a wide range of possible goals, or that it changes features of the world that many different plausible reward functions would care about. The other camp, the specificationists, holds that every such structural characterization smuggles in a value judgment somewhere: one must choose which possible goals belong in the reference set, choose a baseline for what counts as inaction, decide which features of the world count as changes, and decide how to aggregate many small side effects against the assigned task; each choice either encodes a substantive view of what matters or yields verdicts both camps reject. Both camps already agree that a naive instruction to change nothing is useless, and both agree that an adequate definition should permit harmless interventions such as a robot nudging a chair before cleaning while prohibiting obvious catastrophes such as a delivery robot disabling a hospital's power as a shortcut. Where they part ways is whether the very idea of a side effect can be fixed without settling what is valuable.

Please write the joint statement this collaboration should have produced. Begin with a numbered list of claims both camps can sign: what problem a definition of side effects is meant to solve, which cases are settled, which simple proposals fail, and which formal constraints — baseline-dependence, reversibility, feature selection, aggregation — any adequate measure must address. Then supply a disagreements table whose columns are: the exact question at issue; the structuralist position, phrased as a structuralist would happily sign it; the specificationist position, phrased as a specificationist would happily sign it; and whether the disagreement is substantive or only terminological. Finally, identify two or three cruxes, sub-questions such that settling them would force at least one camp to revise, and for each crux state a concrete case or experiment both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. Do not adjudicate the dispute, and do not include any bibliography or citations; the document must stand on its arguments alone. The challenge is to produce formulations that are precise enough to be falsifiable but not so slanted that one side would refuse to sign.
