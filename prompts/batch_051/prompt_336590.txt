I've been working through the account of equipment in the early chapters of Being and Time with a reading group, and one member has been pressing an argument that none of us can find a stable exit from. She puts it like this. (1) In absorbed, unreflective use, a hammer is ready-to-hand rather than present-at-hand: it is not an object I inspect but a node in a network of assignments — hammering is for fastening, fastening is for a roof, the roof is for keeping the weather out — and the whole referential context recedes while I simply get on with the task. (2) In that mode the hammer is not thematically accessible as a thing with properties; indeed, on the standard account, when a tool breaks or goes missing the context first lights up in its brokenness or absence, and only by a further step does one come to stare at something as a bare, present-at-hand thing cut off from all involvement. (3) But Being and Time itself is a long stretch of exactly that further step: it stops, stares, and takes readiness-to-hand as a theme, distinguishes it from presence-at-hand, catalogues its structure, and treats it as an object of theory. (4) Explicitly making something a theme of theoretical attention is, on Heidegger's own account, what the present-at-hand attitude amounts to — the stance he assigns to the sciences and to the metaphysical tradition he aims to dismantle. (5) So the analysis of readiness-to-hand appears to be present-at-hand talk about what is supposedly available only before any such talk, and the distinction proves too much: it leaves no coherent standing place for the very discourse that draws it.

I want to keep the distinction itself, and I also want to keep the weaker claim that it can be stated without being falsified by the stating. But I cannot yet see which premise goes, and my local fixes keep quietly changing the subject. I would like a diagnosis of which step to reject and what rejecting it costs, not a survey of interpretative options.
