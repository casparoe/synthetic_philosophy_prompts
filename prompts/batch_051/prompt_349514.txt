I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am working on a piece about the ways people find meaning in numbers: on one side, laboratory work on apophenia (the perception of meaningful pattern in arbitrary data), and on the other, the long theological tradition in which certain numerals function as signs rather than accidents. I find myself genuinely attracted to the interpretive tradition for its systematic virtues. It holds that the scriptural numbers are a recoverable symbolic vocabulary, not decoration, and that the rules for unpacking them are public enough to be taught and applied across different texts. Augustine sets out the program plainly in On Christian Doctrine, Book II, chapter 16:

"Ignorance of numbers, too, prevents us from understanding things that are set down in Scripture in a figurative and mystical way. A candid mind, if I may so speak, cannot but be anxious, for example, to ascertain what is meant by the fact that Moses and Elijah, and our Lord Himself, all fasted for forty days. And except by knowledge of and reflection upon the number, the difficulty of explaining the figure involved in this action cannot be got over. For the number contains ten four times, indicating the knowledge of all things, and that knowledge interwoven with time."

Augustine then works the figure out in detail: ten joins a trinity in the Creator to the sevenfold structure of the creature, while four marks the divisions of time in the day and the year, so that the forty-day fast becomes a lesson about living in time without clinging to it. That looks like disciplined decoding, not free association, and I can see why it would feel illuminating.

But I cannot shake the opposing intuition, which is that a cognitive scientist would call this pattern-hunting. Given a text rich enough in numbers, a motivated interpreter can always notice that the wilderness wandering lasted forty years and Moses, Elijah, and Christ each fasted for forty days, or that there are twelve tribes and twelve apostles, and then construct a plausible explanation afterward. The method seems to have no built-in failure mode, because whatever number actually appears can be shown to be the right one. I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the theory to be the thing that goes, and whether there is a reading of "pattern" on which both survive. Is there a principled difference between a tradition-grounded symbol system and post-hoc pattern recognition, or is that distinction itself just a flattering story the tradition tells about itself?
