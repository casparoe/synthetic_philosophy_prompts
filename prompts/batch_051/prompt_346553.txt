I'm writing a novel set in a small city that gets split down the middle by a school-closure fight, and a whole chapter turns on a new rule the city brings in for it's official online forum — they're trying to make people actually argue with the other side instead of dunking on them. I don't need you to tell me whether the rule is a good idea in the abstract; I need to know how it would actually behave, because I want the chapter to feel like somebody worked out the gears rather than waved at them.

Here's the rule. Every account on the forum has to declare a side on the school question, and in any thread the moderators tag as disputed, replying to someone on the other side costs you a stake of twenty points — a reputation currency you earn slowly just by posting, and you can't buy. The points are frozen while the challenge plays out. The person you replied to has forty-eight hours to challenge you, and every account gets one challenge token a week, use it or lose it. If they challenge, five jurors are drawn randomly from users who share the challenger's declared side. The panel votes in secret on a single question: did the reply engage the strongest version of the challenger's actual position, or did it strawman, dodge, mock, or nitpick? Three of five votes for bad faith means you forfeit the twenty points to the challenger. If you're acquitted, the challenger eats the failed challenge and gets a public mark on there profile for a month; too many marks and they can't challenge for a season. Either way the ruling and the challenged post get published.

So the jury is always the other side's own people, and the person who jumps into a cross-side thread is the one putting something up. Treat it as a mechanism, not a moral position. Walk me through the incentives: who posts, who stays quiet, who baits, and how a smart bad-faith operator adapts to stay acquitted. What do the juries end up doing, given that the people who'd volunteer to serve are probably the angriest partisans? Where does the scheme break first, and does it burst the town's two little echo chambers or just make the walls higher? Then tell me which hidden assumption is doing the most work here, especially the idea that an aggrieved opponent can reliably tell good faith from bad, and try patching the failure modes — mixed juries, paying the stake to a common pot instead of the challenger, requiring public written reasons, whatever you think is worth trying — and say whether any patch fixes it without gutting the original point.
