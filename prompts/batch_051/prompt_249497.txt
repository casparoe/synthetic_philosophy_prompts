I've been kicking around a constitutional gadget—call it a backsliding brake. Any time the governing majority passes something touching elections, judicial appointments, media licensing, campaign finance, or protest policing, any opposition bloc holding at least 25% of the legislature can file a backsliding objection. The law then freezes for 12 months while a 400-person citizens' assembly, drawn by lot and paid, decides by 60% whether the law counts as democratic backsliding. If it says yes, the law only takes effect via referendum with a 60% turnout floor and 55% support. The catch: the yes/no question the assembly votes on is drafted by the justice ministry, which the government controls. Walk me through how this thing would actually behave in a country that's already sliding (think Hungary, Poland, Turkey) versus one where the label is fiercely contested (say, the US). Where does it get gamed first—the framing of the question, the sortition, using the freeze itself to run out the clock, the referendum thresholds, or somewhere else? Which failure modes are fixable and which just move the fight about who gets to define backsliding?
