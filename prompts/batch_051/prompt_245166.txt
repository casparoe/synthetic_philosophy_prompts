I'm giving a talk in a couple weeks to a philosophy of biology reading group and I want a prep sheet for the Q&A. My line is that the propensity interpretation of fitness doesn't actually dissolve the tautology objection — the old worry that selection explanations are vacuous because fitness gets defined in terms of actual survival and reproduction — it just moves it. Once fitness is a probabilistic disposition to leave offspring rather than actual reproductive success, the tautology worry comes back as a problem about how to individuate and measure the disposition without quietly collapsing it into actual outcomes. My argument is that any way of measuring the disposition forces you to fix a reference environment and a reference class, and the choice of environment and class is guided by the very actual reproductive outcomes the propensity was supposed to replace, so the definition leans on what it was meant to explain. The audience is mixed: one senior person who's defended the propensity view for decades, two people sympathetic to the statisticalist line that fitness isn't a causal property of organisms at all, and a bunch of evolutionary biology grad students who think philosophers have overcomplicated a perfectly clear empirical concept. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way these people would actually put them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, the weakest point in my argument I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss, and the objection I'm probably hoping nobody asks.
