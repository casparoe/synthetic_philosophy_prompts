I'm trying to get clear on what I actually think about blackmail, because my case judgments won't sit still under any single rule. Here are my verdicts. (1) An ex-partner says, "Pay me twenty thousand dollars or I will tell your wife about the affair." Wrong. (2) A neighbor says to the owner of an industrial farm, "Install the legally required waste treatment, or I will report the manure run-off you've been hiding." Fine, maybe even praiseworthy. (3) A departing chief of staff says to a mayor, "Resign, or I will release the texts that prove you misled the city council about the bridge contract." I find this wrong, even though releasing the texts would serve the public and the texts are true. (4) A journalist says to a CEO, "Give me access to your internal correspondence, or I will publish the story about how you bought your son's admission." This feels sleazy and probably wrong, but less clearly than (1). (5) A beating victim says to her assailant's brother, "Pay my hospital bills, or I will testify against him." I lean permissible, because the demand is compensation for a harm the other side caused. (6) Two former business partners: one says to the other, "Buy back my share at full value, or I will report the tax fraud we both helped conceal." I lean wrong, even though selling one's share and reporting fraud are each legal.

My working theory is that a threat is blackmail just when the condition demanded is not something the threatener would be entitled to demand independently of the secret. That handles (1) and (2): the ex-partner has no independent claim to the money, while the neighbor is entitled to insist that the law be obeyed. But (3) strains it, since resignation is a legitimate thing for someone to demand of a mayor who misled the council, yet I still don't think the aide's threat is fine. So maybe the principle should require that the threatener have standing to demand the condition—then (3) comes out wrong, but I worry that is just patching the theory. (4) is awkward in a different way, since journalists routinely make access-for-discretion bargains that I don't want to call blackmail, yet this one feels like it is. And (5) suggests the principle needs some clause about benefiting those harmed by the concealed wrong, rather than leveraging the secret for an unrelated advantage.

Is there a principle here that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts without becoming empty? State it precisely, say which of my verdicts it can and cannot accommodate, and tell me which of my judgments I should revise if I want the simplest strong principle. Then apply the principle to a case I haven't settled: a mid-level civil servant tells her supervisor, "Transfer me out of this unit, or I will leak evidence of the department's illegal data-sharing program to a reporter." Is her threat blackmail on your account, and why or why not?
