I'm in a seminar on moral responsibility and punishment, and we spent a week on the exchange between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso. A classmate, working from his notes, gave the following reconstruction of the debate. Dennett and Caruso agree that nobody has contra-causal free will and that nobody is the uncaused cause of his or her choices; they part ways over what follows. Dennett holds that free will should be understood as a real competence, one that some people possess to a greater degree than others, and that in this sense people can genuinely deserve blame and punishment — so on Dennett's view we get a broadly retributive system in which wrongdoers receive harsh treatment because they have earned it. Caruso holds that once basic-desert moral responsibility is rejected, all blame and punishment are unjustified, and the only permissible social response to wrongdoing is to quarantine dangerous people until they are safe. Dennett objects that this quarantine model treats people like broken machines rather than responsible agents. The upshot, my classmate concluded, is that the debate is between Dennett's retributivism and Caruso's abolitionism about criminal justice.

I have the feeling this summary flattens distinctions that both writers take care to draw — I know Dennett is a compatibilist, not a knee-jerk retributivist, and Caruso is not simply saying that society should stop doing anything with violent offenders. But I cannot confidently sort out where the summary is accurate, where it is a fair simplification, and where it is subtly wrong. Could you give me an accuracy audit? Go through the reconstruction claim by claim, say what Dennett and Caruso would each accept or push back on, and then assess whether the closing "retributivism vs. abolitionism" framing is a fair way to characterize their disagreement. Please write your answer in American English.
