I work for a state fish and wildlife agency and I need this turned into something my staff can actually use. We have to decide, maybe five or six times a year, whether two populations count as "the same species" for listing and management purposes: when a group gets petitioned for endangered listing, when we're deciding whether to move animals between watersheds or out of a captive breeding colony, and when somebody swears they found a known listed species two counties over. Our state endangered species statute just says "species or subspecies" and doesn't define either, so the agency has to pick an interpretation and defend it in court sometimes. I know there's a whole species problem in philosophy of biology — biological species concept, phylogenetic species concept, ecological species concept, and so on — but I need it as a procedure, not a seminar. Write me a step-by-step decision procedure my field staff can run (they've got biology BAs, no systematists on staff, and maybe a day or two per case) for whether to treat two populations as the same species. For each step, say what evidence we'd need and what counts as a good enough answer to move on. Then apply it to three of the kinds of cases we actually get: two allopatric freshwater mussel populations that look nearly identical and produce fertile offspring in the lab but never meet in the wild; a ring species situation where the adjacent forms interbreed but the end forms won't; and a parthenogenetic lizard lineage. Be blunt about which steps are really policy choices dressed up as biology, and where the procedure will hand back an answer the director is not going to like.
