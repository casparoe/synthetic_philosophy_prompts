I'm taking a cognitive archaeology seminar and our instructor handed us a made-up site description to argue over, which is annoying because its fake but the grade is real. We're supposed to rank the interpretations and I keep flip-flopping, so I'd like an outside take.

The site is a roughly 300-year settlement in a dry inland valley with about 40 houses. Almost all the houses are much the same size and have the same domestic remains: grinding stones, plainware pots, flaked stone debris. Two buildings stand out. They were rebuilt over and over on the same footprint, each old floor sealed under clean yellow sand before rebuilding, and one of them has a low clay platform. No domestic refuse in either building. Burials are under house floors: most adults have a plain pot or two and a few shell beads, children tend to have more beads than adults, and a handful of adult men have no pots but one or two carefully carved bone pins. The animal bone is mostly deer and hare, except for one midden beside the platform building that is full of raptor and fox foot bones and broken clay masks with exaggerated bird eyes. There are no imported valuables, no oversized residence, and no rich grave.

The five interpretations we have to sort are: (a) a hereditary chiefdom, (b) achieved status in something like age grades or sodalities, (c) an egalitarian village with no formal hierarchy, (d) a ritual society centered on birds or the sky that used the platform building as its meeting place, and (e) ancestor-focused ritual with the platform building as a lineage shrine.

Please sort these into three tiers—well supported, possible but strained, and poorly supported—and say in a sentence or two for each what evidence is doing the most work. Then give the strongest tier explanation a score out of 10 for how well the whole assemblage actually supports it. I'd also like to know which two hypotheses the current data are worst at telling apart, and what single new find you think would most cleanly split them.
