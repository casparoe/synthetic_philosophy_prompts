I got called for jury duty for the first time this month and the entire case was just two witnesses telling opposite stories; no forensic evidence at all. It made me go back to Hume's Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, to the section on miracles. In part 1 he says, roughly, that the credit we give to witnesses and historians is not derived from any a priori connection we perceive between testimony and reality, but only from the custom or experience of finding a conformity between them. I used to think that was just obviously right: you trust testimony because it has generally worked before. But then I came across the anti-reductionist line associated with Thomas Reid, which treats testimony as a basic, default source of belief, like perception, not something that needs to earn its credentials through induction. I want to understand the conceptual issue underneath this. Is the real disagreement about the order of justification, about what counts as legitimate evidence, or about whether testimonial belief involve inference at all? Please explain the reductionist vs anti-reductionist positions about testimony in a way that engages the actual argument in that Hume passage, not just labeled boxes. And please do not send me to a reading list.
