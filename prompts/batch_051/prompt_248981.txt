I've been trying to get a handle on what complicity actually is — not the legal defintion, but the moral one, the thing that makes someone answerable for a wrong they didn't cause and maybe didn't even make more likely. I want a working definition, one I can apply to a new case without shrugging. The cases I keep going back to: a getaway driver who waits outside the bank knowing the plan but never enters; a courier who unknowingly carries a package that turns out to be a bomb; an ER nurse who patches up a gang member's wound knowing he has sworn to kill a rival as soon as he's out; a shareholder in a mining company who reads credible reports that its security force has attacked villagers and keeps the stock for the dividends; a citizen who votes against a war, loses, and pays the taxes that fund it; and a colleague who laughs at a racist joke, never repeats it, and never pushes back. I'm confident the driver is complicit and the courier isn't. I lean toward the shareholder and the taxpayer not being complicit, but i worry that's only because the wrong feels too diffuse to be anybody's. The nurse and the laugher genuinely stump me. Propose a definition that gets the clear cases right and gives a real verdict on the middle four — run it through all six, tell me where it gives an answer I should resist, and revise it or defend the verdict. And write it as continuous prose, like an email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
