I'm on the transplant ethics committee at a large academic medical center, and we've just received a proposal from our organ procurement organization that has the committee at each other's throats. The pilot program would pay living kidney donors directly—a fixed $40,000, plus full medical costs and lost wages—for up to 30 donations over two years. Recipients would be drawn from the standard deceased-donor waitlist; donors would get a six-month cooling-off period, a psych evaluation, and an independant advocate paid by the state rather than the transplant center. Our state currently bans buying organs, so the whole thing depends on getting a waiver from the state health department, which has signaled it would consider one.

Half the committee thinks this is long-overdue fairness: donors take real surgical risk and a long recovery, and the altruism-only system leaves thousands of people to die on the waitlist every year. The other half thinks the payment turns a kidney into a product, and that a fixed cash price will just function as a magnet for desperate people, whatever safeguards we put on paper. I've been asked to draft the short written opinion to circulate before Thursday's meeting—I got stuck with it because I'm the only member who hasn't publicly picked a side yet. The memo needs a concrete recommendation (approve, reject, or approve with conditions), a time frame, and it has to take the strongest point on the losing side seriously rather than just restating the legal ban or waving at the word dignity. Can you write it?
