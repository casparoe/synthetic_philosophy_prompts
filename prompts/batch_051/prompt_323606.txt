A colleague and I have been arguing about what Kant's treatment of the antinomies in the Critique of Pure Reason really commits him to, and she has constructed the following argument, which I cannot walk away from even though its conclusion strikes me as too strong. The issue is not exegesis of the antinomy chapter in general; it is whether this argument's structure is sound, and if not, where it breaks.

(1) In the First Antinomy, Kant accepts neither side: he holds that the thesis, that the world has a beginning in time and is finite in space, and the antithesis, that the world has no beginning in time and is infinite in space, are both false.

(2) His ground for rejecting both sides is that each takes the world-whole to be a determinate object of possible experience, when in fact the world-whole cannot be given as such; both sides therefore slide from rules that govern appearances to a claim about the world as a thing in itself.

(3) The same structure seems to be present in each of the other antinomies: one side affirms, of some unconditioned totality or ground, a predicate whose apparent contradictory the other side affirms—simple parts versus infinite divisibility, a causality of freedom versus universal natural necessity, an absolutely necessary being versus contingency at every stage.

(4) If the first-antinomy treatment is the general antinomy treatment, then in every one of these disputes Kant's method should force the verdict he gives in the first: both sides false and the shared subject matter dismissed as an illusion.

(5) But in the Third Antinomy that is not what Kant does. He does not dismiss the dispute or declare both sides false. He holds that the thesis, that there is a causality of freedom apart from natural causality, and the antithesis, that everything in the world of appearances occurs solely according to laws of nature, can both be accepted: freedom for the intelligible character, natural necessity among appearances.

(6) So his own critical method, applied uniformly, would have driven him to the first-antinomy verdict in the Third Antinomy as well; accepting both sides there cannot be a principled application of the same method, so it must be ad hoc.

(7) Therefore either the antinomy doctrine is internally inconsistent, or the Third Antinomy resolution is not a real resolution but a special exception that Kant never earns.

I would most like to keep two things: that there is a principled reason the freedom conflict is treated differently from the world-size conflict, and that the Third Antinomy resolution is a substantive result rather than a verbal retreat. But I do not want to keep those claims by refusing to follow the argument. Tell me which numbered step or steps I should reject, why the argument made rejecting them feel unprincipled, and what I have to give up on each live option. I want a diagnosis, not a map of the secondary literature.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
