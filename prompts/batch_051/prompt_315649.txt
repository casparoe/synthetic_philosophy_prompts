I am a PhD student in the philosophy of economics, working on the epistemology of markets. Approach this as a philosopher of economics who is equally at home with the formal results and their informal interpretation; think carefully, and spend the first part of your answer brainstorming the assumptions and possible failure points before settling on a final assessment.

The position I want you to examine is the standard reading of the no-trade theorems and the agreeing-to-disagree results. Under the usual benchmark conditions — common priors, common knowledge of rationality, risk-averse traders, no liquidity or hedging motive, and an initial allocation that is already efficient absent the private information — these results are taken to show that purely information-driven speculative trade should not occur, because the willingness to trade at a given price would leak enough information to eliminate the apparent gains. On this reading, observed speculative trading is not a standing puzzle that demands a rational-disagreement explanation; it is evidence of divergent priors, bounded rationality, or non-informational motives such as liquidity or risk-sharing.

I want an honest assessment of how exposed this reading actually is. What would have to be true for it to be wrong, or for the theorems to carry much less weight than the standard interpretation gives them? Go through the assumptions one by one — the common prior assumption, common knowledge of rationality, the ex ante efficiency of the initial allocation, the absence of non-informational motives, the structure of private information, and the way prices transmit information — and say, for each, what the world would have to look like for speculative trade to be rational after all, and which of these escape routes are live in actual markets rather than merely logically possible. Distinguish considerations that would be decisive against the standard reading from those that would merely force a qualification, and be explicit about how much the standard conclusion risks. Separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
