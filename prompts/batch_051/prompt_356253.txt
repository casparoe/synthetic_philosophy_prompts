I am to give the eulogy at the memorial of my closest friend of thirty years. She spent nearly all of her adult life organizing tenants in a midsize city, and for the last twenty years led a campaign for a citywide just-cause eviction ordinance. Voters and successive city councils rejected it four times, most recently six months before she died. She was unembarrassed by the losing: she held that political hope should not be calibrated to the probability of success, that a demand required by justice must be asserted in full seriousness however unlikely it is to be won, and that trimming one's hopes to what looks achievable is a way of surrendering before the fight begins. I never agreed. I was the designated realist in her circles, a union negotiator by trade, and I believe, as I did while she was alive, that hope which outruns a sober estimate of what can be won is morally costly: it misdirects scarce effort away from winnable partial gains, sets people up for bitterness and burnout, and can make the purity of a distant goal an excuse for strategic carelessness. She and I argued about this affectionately and at length for decades, and she asked me to speak precisely because I had never flattered her.

The service is not religious and will be held in a community hall. The room will be split between her comrades from the organizing world—many of whom share her maximalism and are mourning the campaign along with the person—and her family, including a brother who always thought the campaign quixotic and two teenage grandchildren for whom the movement's vocabulary will be a foreign language.

Write the eulogy. It needs to comfort a room full of people in grief, and it may not contain a single sentence I regard as false: I cannot say that the ordinance will one day pass, or that history will vindicate her, or that she was right all along. But it also must not become a realist's cautionary tale, an apology for my own position, or a recruitment speech for her cause. The disagreement just described—whether it is wrong to hope too much—should be the load-bearing structure of the speech: it should decide what is said, what is left unsaid, and the order in which things are said. Ground the speech in at least one specific memory of her from the campaigns, so the question never floats free of the person. Write it as a speech to be spoken, not an essay; keep it to five or six minutes; avoid the words "idealism" and "realism" as labels for people or camps; and do not resolve the disagreement by claiming that she was really a realist or that I have really been an idealist. The ending should leave both halves of the room feeling that the person they came to mourn was honored without being lied about.
