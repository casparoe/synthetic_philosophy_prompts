I have been going through a list of everyday situations in which someone states something outright — flatly, without a hedge — and recording my verdicts about when it is permissible to do so. My plan was to collect the cases first and find the principle only afterward, and that second step is what I want from you. The cases, with my verdicts, are these. Saying 'It's raining' after glancing out the window and seeing the rain: fine. Saying 'Your keys are on the kitchen table' an hour after having seen them there, with no sign that anyone has touched them: fine. A student telling a classmate 'The lecture has been moved to 2:30' after reading only a terse announcement in a group chat and not checking the department calendar: fine, though barely. A colleague telling a client 'The vendor has not renewed the contract' on the strength of a conversation he half-remembers from a month ago: not fine. Telling a housemate 'Your ticket did not win' before the draw, purely on the odds: fine, to my own surprise. A surgeon telling a patient 'The operation will go smoothly' before a procedure with a 98.5 percent success rate: not fine said so flatly; it should be hedged. Shouting 'The bridge is out' to a driver after you watched it collapse: fine, no question.

The pair that bothers me is the lottery and the surgeon: in both cases the speaker states something flatly on strong probabilities rather than on something already known, yet I allow the first and reject the second. My working rule was that outright assertion requires knowledge, and anything less ought to be hedged; but that rule seems to condemn the lottery case, which I keep wanting to permit, and it does not explain why the student's second-hand announcement is acceptable while the colleague's second-hand assertion is not. It also draws no distinction of the kind I seem to feel between direct observation and well-preserved memory.

I would like you to give me the best one or two candidate principles for outright assertion, stated precisely, with each clause motivated by one of my cases; to say what each principle predicts about the lottery–surgeon pair and about the case I keep going back and forth on, which is a doctor telling a worried patient 'The scan came back clear' when the scan is clear but the doctor has only read the radiologist's report rather than the images. Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts each candidate principle cannot accommodate, and whether you would revise a verdict rather than complicate the principle.
