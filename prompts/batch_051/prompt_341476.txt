I'm a philosophy professor, but early modern is my patch, not Chinese thought, so go easy on me. In a curriculum meeting about whether our ethics requirement actually changes student behavior, a colleague who works on Ming thought kept saying Wang Yangming's slogan "knowledge and action are one" (zhixing heyi) as if it closed the debate. His move was: if the students don't act differently afterwards, they didn't really know the stuff, so the course didn't fail them—they just never knew it. That one sentence feels like it's doing a lot of quiet work, and I cant tell which bit of it I disagree with. Take the slogan and lay out, as a clean menu, the distinct precise claims it could be making. What kind of knowledge is it about—moral knowledge, know-how, ordinary factual belief, something else? Does it say sincere moral knowledge guarantees action, or that action is the test of knowledge, or that knowing just is a kind of acting, or something weaker? For each reading, tell me whether it's trivial, substantive and defensible, or just false, and then say which reading my colleague actually needs to get his conclusion about the course. I want to bring him back a menu with verdicts, not another argument, and it would help if you also flagged which readings an otherwise mainstream philosopher could accept without becoming a full-blown idealist.
