I sit on the disbursement committee of my university's High-Leverage Social Impact Fund, which awards seed grants to research proposals whose benefits must be demonstrable within a few years. We are now deciding on a proposal from a team of computer scientists and resettlement researchers to build and pilot an open-source algorithm that a national refugee agency would use to match incoming refugees with host municipalities. The foreseeable case is unusually strong: controlled pilots in comparable settings suggest the tool would improve refugee employment and reduce per-capita integration costs by a modest but well-estimated margin, and the team has committed to an independent evaluation.

A minority of the committee, however, argues as follows. An instrument that helps determine where tens of thousands of vulnerable people settle will set off chains of consequences no one can trace: through housing prices, school composition, employers' behavior, the political attitudes of native-born residents, and the long-run life trajectories of the refugees' children. Some of these chains will be good and some bad, and we have no principled way of knowing the balance. Since the net consequences are therefore unknowable, the minority claims, the committee cannot responsibly assert that expected value is positive; either we should decline the proposal, or we should explicitly restrict ourselves to foreseeable effects and stop pretending that this yields an all-things-considered judgment. The majority replies that every grant we make alters the world through unmeasured channels, and if we refused to act on the best available expected-value estimate we would never fund anything. Please draft the committee's decision memo — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with an actionable recommendation, a clear statement of which consideration ultimately governs, and an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side.
