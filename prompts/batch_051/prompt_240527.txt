I help run a small babysitting co-op with eight families, and we've been arguing for two weeks about a single sentence in our membership agreement. The sentence says: "A member who accepts a sit and backs out with less than twenty-four hours' notice must provide one no-credit sit for the family they cancelled on, unless the cancellation is due to illness or a family emergency."

The case that's got us stuck: Dana agreed weeks ago to watch the Nguyens' kids on a Saturday night. Thursday evening around 9, her boss changed her Saturday shift and she realized she couldn't do the sit. She texted the Nguyens immediately — about 26 hours before the sit was supposed to start. But the Nguyens were camping without signal, so they didn't see the text until Friday morning, about 12 hours before the sit. By then Dana had tried three other families and found no one, and the Nguyens ended up paying a teenage neighbor. Now Dana says she doesn't owe the penalty sit, because she sent the message more then 24 hours ahead. The Nguyens say she backed out with less than 24 hours' notice, because that's when they actually learned about it. The rule was written years ago, when "notice" basically meant someone picked up the phone, so nobody at the time imagined a person could be unreachable for a whole day while carrying a phone.

I want you to think through this carefully. You're an expert in the ethics of small promises and everyday coordination; start by laying out the different ways to read the rule — the plain words, the purpose of the 24-hour line, what the people who drafted it would probably say if we asked them now about an unread text, and what a reasonable co-op member would think they owed. Then tell me which reading should win here and why. And tell me whether your answer changes if Dana knew the Nguyens were camping, or if she had texted 27 hours out but wrote "no rush, just flagging this" and the Nguyens saw it and decided not to open it until morning.
