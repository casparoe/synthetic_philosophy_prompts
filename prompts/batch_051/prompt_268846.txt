Here's the situation. I'm giving a five-minute after-dinner speech at the close of a small humour-research workshop. The audience is about forty people: half are the researchers (social psychologists, philosophers, a couple of linguists), and half are their partners and a few older kids who got dragged along. The organizers asked me to keep the closing warm and funny, not another paper. I want to do something more interesting than a generic isnt-laughter-wonderful ending.

The speech needs to make a real point: the superiority, incongruity, relief, and benign-violation accounts each capture part of what makes humour social, but the least-examined bit is the 'benign' in benign violation. It tends to get treated like a background condition or a free parameter, when it's actually the fragile, socially-achieved thing that decides whether a violation lands as funny or as cruelty. Same words, same incongruity, different teller or target, and the laugh goes somewhere else entirely. The speech has to make this point mostly by showing it, not by announcing it — the room will notice if the speech only illustrates one theory, or if it smuggles the point in as a lecture.

Concrete constraints: include at least one joke that works as pure incongruity (no victim, no tension), and at least one benign-violation joke, and both should land for the non-researchers without anyone needing the theory labels. No cheap shots at grad students, no inside jargon that isn't made obvious in the moment, and keep the tone affectionate rather than edgy. End with an actual send-off, not a punchline.
