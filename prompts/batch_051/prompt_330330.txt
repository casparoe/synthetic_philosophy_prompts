I'm a retired physical chemist who has spent the last couple years reading philosophy to keep my brain busy, and I've run into a problem I'd like someone to help me think through. I was reading John Stuart Mill, A System of Logic, Book III, Chapter VI, "Of the Composition of Causes." I don't remember his exact wording, but he draws a contrast there that keeps nagging at me. In mechanical cases, he says, causes compose additively: if two forces act on a body, the result can be predicted by adding their separate effects. But in chemical cases, the combined effect is not additive. The product has different qualities from the elements, and according to Mill you could not have predicted the properties of the compound from the properties of the elements before you had observed the compound. I'm paraphrasing, not quoting.

Now here's my tension. As a chemist I have always believed, as a matter of physics, that chemical facts are in principle derivable from quantum mechanics. Write down the Hamiltonian for a molecule, solve the Schrödinger equation, and you get structure, spectrum, reactivity, all of it. In principle. That is a very powerful reductionist picture: one fundamental theory, no special chemical laws, no emergence beyond computational intractibility. If I'm being honest, I think I am committed to it.

But I also have an intuition I can't dismiss, even though I know it sounds like what mystics say. When I think about a benzene ring or an enzyme, I have the strong sense that the molecule is not just the sum of its electrons and nuclei in some particular configuration. Not because there is extra stuff, and not because the Schrödinger equation is false. The intuition is that the higher-level description—chemical bonding, functional groups, active sites—is doing some real structuring of the world that the lower-level description misses, not merely a convenient abbreviation for something we already understand. My late colleague used to insist that when hydrogen burns in oxygen, the character of the product is not contained in the character of the reactants. That is almost Mill's point, and it is also what reductionism seems to deny.

So my question is that classic one: I have a theory with systematic virtues, reductionism, and an intuition it seems to contradict, namely that molecules have genuinely new properties at the higher level. Present both sides at their strongest and tell me which should yield. Is my intuition just an epistemic artifact, a name for the fact that I can't run the calculation, that should be debunked and dropped? Should the reductionism be amended or qualified? Or is there a reading of Mill's point and modern reductionism on which they are compatible because they are using "sum of parts" in different ways? I want to know what would justify each move; don't just pick a side.
