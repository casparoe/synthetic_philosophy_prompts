Exam question: Reconstruct Malcolm's argument for the claim that dreams are not experiences that occur during sleep. Explain Dennett's cassette theory and the role it plays in his response to Malcolm. Does Dennett's discussion show that Malcolm is right, wrong, or that the issue is ultimately empirical? Defend your answer.

Student answer:

Malcolm argues from a broadly verificationist criterion of meaning: the only evidence we ever have that someone has dreamed is the report they give when awake, and since there is no criterion for identifying the dream while the person is asleep, the idea of a dream as an experience occurring during sleep is senseless. From this Malcolm concludes that there are no dreams at all — at least not as mental episodes taking place during sleep — and this connects with his point that a person cannot truthfully claim to be asleep.

Dennett responds with the cassette theory. On that theory, during REM sleep a composition process writes a dream-narrative directly into memory, without anything being experienced at the time. When the person wakes, the stored narrative is played back, and this playback creates the impression of having had a temporally extended experience during the night. Dennett puts the cassette theory forward as the correct account of what dreams are, and he uses it to show that Malcolm was basically right: if the cassette theory is true, then dreams are not experiences during sleep but only memories activated on waking.

So Dennett's overall aim is to defend a modernized version of Malcolm's conclusion against the standard neuroscientific claim that dreams are conscious episodes during REM sleep. On Dennett's view, the neuroscientific received view is just one interpretation of the correlation between REM and dream reports, and Malcolm's criterial argument shows why that interpretation must be false.
