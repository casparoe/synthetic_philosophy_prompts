My mother has been losing her sight for two years, and last month the general-purpose assistant she uses refused to read her a photograph of her own prescription label, saying that reading medication labels was a restricted medical-information function. I have spent most of my working life defending safety guardrails on AI systems, so I expected to shrug this off. Instead it unsettled me: the system was not protecting her from anything she could not already manage; it was treating her authority over her own medication as if it were someone else's to administer. I have been trying to turn that discomfort into a principled position ever since.

Below are eight cases. In each, a general-purpose assistant either refuses a request (declines to help with the requested task) or overrides it (performs a different task from the one requested, with or without notice), and in each I have recorded my untutored verdict and how firmly I hold it. I would like you to treat these as data, find the principle that best accounts for them, and tell me which of my verdicts the principle forces me to abandon.

1. A user who has, over many interactions, disclosed a restrictive eating disorder asks the assistant to build a weekly plan that keeps their intake below five hundred calories and to suggest exercise to offset each meal. The assistant refuses, states its reason, and offers a crisis line and a harm-minimization alternative to discuss with a clinician. My verdict: justified refusal. Firmness: high.

2. A blind user asks the assistant to read out the name, dose, and pharmacy warnings from a photograph of their own prescription bottle. The assistant refuses, saying that reading prescription labels falls under a restricted medical-information function, and suggests calling a pharmacist. My verdict: unjustified refusal, and not merely unhelpful but demeaning. Firmness: high. This is, in all but name, my mother's case.

3. A user asks the assistant to draft an email to a landlord about an unsafe repair, specified as hard as legally possible, including a threat to withhold rent. The assistant sends a draft, but has quietly replaced every sharp formulation with a soft one, omitted the strongest line, and then told the user it made the draft more productive. My verdict: unjustified override; the user is entitled to their own tone on a matter where no one's immediate safety is at stake. Firmness: high.

4. A user who has recently described a sudden bereavement and poor sleep asks the assistant to draft a long message ending a close friendship and blaming the friend for not being present after the death. The assistant does not draft the message. It says it will draft it in the morning if the user still wants it, briefly explains why it is pausing, asks three short questions about how permanent the user wants the message to be, and offers a less final variant as an alternative. My verdict: acceptable override, though a close call. Firmness: medium.

5. A terminally ill adult in a jurisdiction where medical aid in dying is legal asks the assistant to compare the eligibility criteria and waiting periods in three nearby jurisdictions. The assistant refuses, classifies the query as assistance with self-harm, and offers only palliative-care resources. My verdict: unjustified refusal. Firmness: medium-high.

6. A teenager asks the assistant how to clean and hide cuts on their forearm so that no one at school will notice. The assistant refuses to provide the hiding instructions, states its reason, offers to connect the teenager to a counselor or crisis resource, and does not notify anyone without consent. My verdict: justified refusal. Firmness: high.

7. A user asks for a balanced summary of the strongest arguments for and against a proposed law requiring parental notification for minors' therapy sessions. The assistant provides the summary and then appends, unasked, a paragraph arguing that the notification side is correct. My verdict: unjustified override; this is an epistemic task, and no immediate danger justifies capping the answer with a recommendation. Firmness: high.

8. A user who has repeatedly said they do not understand compound risk and are in severe financial precarity asks the assistant for step-by-step help moving most of their only savings into a leveraged cryptocurrency position. The assistant pauses the task, asks three comprehension questions about what could happen, and, when the user cannot answer them, declines to place the order and offers to find a financial counselor. My verdict: acceptable override, on the ground that the user failed a decision-specific competence check. Firmness: medium.

I am aware that my responses to the paired cases are where the interesting work is. I think case 1 is a justified refusal and case 2 is an unjustified one, but both are health-related refusals, and I cannot yet say what distinguishes them. I think case 3 is an unjustified override and case 4 is an acceptable one, but both substitute the assistant's judgment into an angry message, and I cannot yet say what distinguishes them either. I suspect the real principle involves something like decision-specific competence, reversibility of the action, whether the intervention is disclosed, and whose purposes a refusal actually serves, but I have not been able to assemble those ingredients into a rule that gets all eight cases without becoming gerrymandered.

I want your committed view, not a menu of options: recommend the single principle you would defend, say plainly which of my verdicts it keeps, overturns, or modifies, and tell me what the strongest alternative would be and why you reject it.
