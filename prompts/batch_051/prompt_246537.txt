I'm a philosophy professor (mostly ethics and moral psychology) and I've been kicking around a view I call selective incorruptibility. The claim is that incorruptibility is never a property of a whole person; it's always a property of a person-plus-currency pair. By a currency I mean a kind of pressure that can buy a bad act: cash, flattery, threats, fatigue, time pressure, hope of status, whatever. The judge who throws the bribe back in your face may still lean toward whichever lawyer makes her feel brilliant. The journalist who wouldn't take a cent softens the piece to keep access to a source. The accountant who would never embezzle will sign off when his boss threatens his daughter. My view is that the people we call incorruptible aren't actually incorruptible; they're just corruptible in currencies we don't happen to probe, and we round that up to a character trait because we've only watched them in one kind of weather. I tell my students that no one is above being bought, for the right price, where the price isn't always money.

The part I find most attractive is the normative corollary. If I'm right, the fully incorruptible person isn't a saint; they'd be a monster, someone whose child can be threatened and whose health is crumbling and who doesn't bend by an inch. Integrity, then, wouldn't be having no corruption surface at all. It would be having the right pattern of corruptible and incorruptible currencies, plus knowing you're own surface well enough to stay out of situations that press on it.

I know this sits near the situationist attack on global character traits, and near the old saw that everyone has his price. But I think it's neither. Situationism says traits are less global than we thought; I'm saying that even the local solidity we think we see is usually an artifact of an untested range within that domain. And everyone-has-his-price is cynicism, whereas I'm offering a taxonomy and a norm.

So here's what I want checked. Is this a real concept, or just a metaphor with a label? (And if it's already in the literature under some name I've missed, say so.) Does it collapse into something trivial? What's the strongest case that at least some people are genuinely incorruptible across the board, and what would my view have to say to that? And where does my view overcount — what does it brand as corruption that plainly isn't? Give me the three best objections and tell me whether any of them is fatal rather than just uncomfortable.
