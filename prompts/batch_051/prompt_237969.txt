I'm an undergraduate in a philosophy of mind course called "Computation and Consciousness," and I want to audit my own position before I write the term paper. Over the term I've accumulated the following commitments, and I'd like to know whether they can all be held together. I genuinely am unsure where the problems are, so please sort them out rather than flagging everything.

The commitments:

1. Organizational invariance: if two physical systems implement the same relevant computation, then they have the same conscious experiences, whatever their substrate.

2. A sufficiently fine-grained whole-brain emulation running on a conventional digital computer could be conscious—not because it passes behavioral tests, but because it reproduces the causally relevant computational structure of a human brain.

3. I reject Integrated Information Theory as an account of consciousness. My objection is that its verdicts track the wrong properties: it seems ready to attribute consciousness to physically simple, grid-like circuits while denying or demoting it in cases I regard as clear.

4. Moral patienthood is grounded in phenomenal consciousness: a system matters for its own sake only if there is something it is like to be that system.

5. Nevertheless, I think some current machine-learning systems that display the functional markers of distress deserve real moral consideration in practice, even though I do not think we have established that they are conscious.

6. I take the boundary problem seriously: if a human brain is conscious, a theory must explain why both hemispheres, the whole organism, or a larger assembly are not also conscious, and it cannot simply trust intuitive boundaries.

7. I maintain that, for any physical system, there is a determinate fact of the matter about whether it is conscious.

8. I also accept that a physical system can be represented as implementing many different computations depending on how one maps its physical states onto computational states; there is no single computational description that its physics forces on us.

For each pair, or larger subset, that you think is in tension, tell me how severe the tension is, whether the tension is real or dissolves once the commitments are made precise, and what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs. Then rank the tensions by severity, and say which single revision of (1)-(8) would do the most to make the whole set hang together. I'd rather have a definite verdict with reasons than a survey of options.
