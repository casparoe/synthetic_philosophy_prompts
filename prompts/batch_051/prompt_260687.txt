I'm an undergraduate taking a seminar on distributive justice this term, and we have just spent two weeks on the veil of ignorance. I am struck by how Rawls and Harsanyi begin from what looks like the same device—choosing principles from behind a veil that hides who you will be—yet reach opposite conclusions. Rawls treats the situation as one of uncertainty too deep to support probabilistic reasoning, so the parties would rationally apply the maximin rule and choose principles that secure the best worst-case position. Harsanyi treats the situation as one of risk: a fully impartial chooser assigns equal probability to being any person in the society and maximizes expected utility, which yields average utilitarianism and makes maximin look like irrational risk aversion. The debate has now run for decades, and my impression from the readings is that neither side moves because each simply reasserts its own way of modeling the choice.

My working hypothesis is that a good part of the impasse is not about a shared decision problem but about what the veil is a model of—public justification among free and equal persons in Rawls's hands, a Bayesian impartial observer in Harsanyi's. But I am not confident: it might also be that decision theory itself has no settled answer to what rationality requires under genuine ignorance, with maximin, minimax regret, and other ambiguity rules still in contention. Do you think the disagreement is mostly verbal, mostly a difference in projects, or a substantive standoff about rationality?
