I'm a student TA for an intro to ethics course, and this week we're on G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica. The professor wrote the short-answer question below and asked me to mark it, and I've gone back and forth because the answer reads smoothly but I don't want to grade on vibes. I'd rather not say what I think before you look at it. The exam question is: Explain Moore's claim that the greatest goods we can know are aesthetic enjoyments and personal affections. In doing so, reconstruct his thought experiment about a beautiful world, and carefully distinguish the value of beautiful objects themselves from the value of the consciousness of them. Finally, explain what he means when he calls such states organic unities.

The student's answer: Moore's two greatest goods are aesthetic enjoyments and personal affections — roughly, the enjoyment of beautiful things and of close human relationships. He argues for this with a thought experiment: compare a world that is entirely beautiful but contains no conscious beings with a world that is entirely ugly; we would still think the beautiful one is better, so beauty has intrinsic value even if no one ever sees it. But Moore thinks the greatest goods are states of consciousness, because when you take a beautiful object and add knowledge of it and pleasure in it, the value of the whole is greater than the sum of the values of its parts. That is what he calls an organic unity. He also requires the parts to fit together: the pleasure must be directed at the right object, like a friend's good qualities or a beautiful object's qualities, and the state must include genuine cognition of that object rather than a delusion. So the highest intrinsic goods end up being beauty and friendship themselves, with consciousness as a kind of necessary ingredient, and once you see that you realize Moore is basically still a hedonist — the pleasure is what does the work.
