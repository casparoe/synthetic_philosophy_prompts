I teach a philosophy of science unit to second-year undergrads, 16 students, 75 minutes, most of them science majors who already think they know what heritability means. I want a hands-on activity that makes them feel how strange the sentence “this trait is 70% heritable” actually is. Their default assumptions: if a trait is 70% heritable then environment can only move it the other 30%, and if a trait is highly heritable within two groups then a mean difference between those groups is probably genetic. I’d like a small tabletop game — genotype and environment cards, two separate populations they run side by side — where they compute heritability inside each population, get a high number (ideally near 1), and then hit the punchline themselves: either the mean difference between the populations is entirely environmental, or a big shared environmental shift moves everyone by the same amount, so the mean jumps while heritability doesn’t budge. The number they computed never licensed the conclusions they were drawing from it. Avoid IQ; use height or a fictional crop. Please give me the rules, the materials, a worked example with numbers that actually comes out right, a timeline, and a debrief that maps each move in the game to the conceptual lesson (within-group variance vs between-group differences, heritability as a variance ratio rather than a measure of malleability, estimates being local to the environment). Also tell me what predictably goes wrong when it’s run — I suspect students will treat genotype cards as destiny or confuse moving the mean with changing the variance — and how to steer it back without just lecturing. And be honest about what the game can’t capture, like gene-environment correlation, gene-environment interaction, and measurement error, which I’ll have to cover seperately.
