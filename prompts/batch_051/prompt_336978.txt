I sit on a university academic-integrity panel that is revising how we sanction serious plagiarism. One faction wants the findings and the students' names published internally, on the grounds that the shame of public exposure deters and the wronged parties are owed a public record. The other faction wants the matter handled entirely in private, through one-to-one restorative conversations designed to elicit the student's own guilt and a reparative commitment. In our last meeting a colleague from the second faction closed the debate with the familiar line, "Guilt is about what you have done; shame is about who you are," and drew the conclusion that a decent institution has no business using shame at all: shame attacks the person rather than the deed, while guilt tracks the transgression and leaves the self intact. We can and should, they added, build our procedures to cultivate guilt rather than shame.

I suspect several distinct claims are being run together, but I cannot yet say which ones. Treat this slogan as a piece of philosophy in need of disambiguation. Lay out the precise theses it could be used to assert — for example, descriptive psychological claims about what the two emotions actually track, conceptual claims about their proper objects, evaluative claims about which is the better or more harmful moral emotion, normative claims about which emotions institutions may deliberately elicit, and claims about whether a community can choose to move its emotional repertoire from shame to guilt — and for each reading say whether it is a platitude, a contestable but defensible thesis, or outright false, and whether that reading actually gets my colleague to their institutional conclusion. Give the readings as a menu rather than settling on one, and say which readings the people who repeat the line are probably reaching for. Please write your answer in British English, using British spelling and terminology.
