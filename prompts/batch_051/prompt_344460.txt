You are a professional moral philosopher with a broad background in contemporary ethics and metaethics, and I want you to help me think through a definitional problem slowly and carefully. Begin by writing out two or three candidate definitions before you settle on one, and at any point where you are genuinely unsure, say so explicitly rather than bluffing.

I am trying to get a working definition of a moral saint. The term gets used confidently in ordinary moral talk, but the moment people start arguing about whether being one would be a good or a bad thing, the definition seems to shift under the argument. Sometimes a saint is just anyone unusually good; sometimes a saint is a person who literally maximizes moral value at every choice point; sometimes the word picks out a kind of motive, like impartial love or acting purely from duty, rather than any record of results. I need a definition that can regulate the real debate, not just a dictionary gloss.

Here are the cases I want the definition tested against.

Cases that clearly count:
- A person who lives modestly, donates most of their income to highly effective charities, and spends their weekends volunteering.
- A parent who gives up a rewarding career and most hobbies to care for a severely disabled child out of love, and reports finding the life meaningful rather than self-sacrificial.

Cases that clearly do not count:
- Someone who scrupulously obeys every conventional moral rule but only out of fear of punishment, and is otherwise self-centered.
- A person who is warm and generous to their friends but indifferent or casually cruel to strangers.

Borderline cases:
- The cold benefactor: produces enormous good through money and direct work, but is humorless, has no close relationships, and allows no leisure or play.
- The dutiful grumbler: always does the right thing, but out of duty and with visible resentment, so that others feel resented rather than helped.
- The warm ineffectual: has saint-like motives and temperament, genuinely loving and impartial toward others, but accomplishes little good because of incompetence and bad luck.
- The great artist: achieves an extraordinary non-moral excellence in art, wit, or sport, is decent toward others but nowhere near maximizing morally; on any account not a saint, but I want your definition to reveal exactly what is missing.
- The utility-maximizer: maximizes expected total happiness but treats justice, rights, and personal ties as merely instrumental, and would violate a right if the arithmetic came out positive.

For each case, run your definition and give a verdict. Flag the case where your definition's verdict most conflicts with your initial confident judgment, and either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. My tentative priors are that the cold benefactor is a saint and the dutiful grumbler is not, but I cannot yet say why, and I am genuinely unsure about the warm ineffectual and the utility-maximizer.

Finally, use the settled definition to clarify the question that actually interests me: is maximal moral goodness desirable for the person who has it? In particular, tell me whether the commonly cited costs of sainthood, such as having no close particular attachments, no humor, no relaxation, and a life of constant self-sacrifice, belong to the definition itself or are merely frequent, contingent companions. Say explicitly whether a definition that bakes those costs in would be question-begging.

Throughout, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers, and do not present one contested position as the consensus. Where a case's verdict depends on a live choice among consequentialist, Kantian, and virtue-ethical frameworks, say so and note what each framework would yield. Please write in American English.
