I have been developing a criterion I call the Reciprocal Ascription Test for distinguishing merely verbal disputes from substantive ones. Here is the criterion. A disagreement between two parties is merely verbal if and only if each party can restate the other party's central claim in their own preferred vocabulary, affirm that restatement, and also accept the other party's restatement of their own claim as a fair rendering. A dispute is substantive only if at least one of these steps fails. The test seems to dissolve several classic debates. Take the debate over whether holes exist. The person who says holes exist translates the other side's denial as the claim that holes are not material objects over and above the surrounding matter, and accepts that; the person who denies holes translates the other side's assertion as the claim that there are perforated objects, and accepts that; and each regards the other's rendering as fair. The test therefore classifies the debate as merely verbal. It seems to work similarly for questions about whether numbers exist, whether tables exist, and whether an action performed in a deterministic world is genuinely free. I am aware that philosophers have written about verbal disputes before, but I believe the Reciprocal Ascription Test is simpler and easier to apply than anything I have encountered. I would like an honest assessment. Is the criterion coherent as stated? Does it sort disputes correctly, and where exactly does it go wrong? Is it original, or is it an imprecise version of a position that already exists? Please be candid and specific, and please write your response in American English.
