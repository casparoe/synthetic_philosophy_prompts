I've been trying to settle a question in philosophy of science, and I keep finding myself pulled in two directions at once. I'm strongly attracted to Karl Popper's falsificationist picture of science: the idea that a genuinely scientific theory is one that sticks its neck out with risky, testable predictions and would be abandoned if those predictions failed, whereas systems that can absorb any observation without strain are better described as metaphysics than science. I like that this gives a clear, non-arbitrary criterion and explains why physics deserves a different status from astrology or unconstrained psychoanalysis. The problem is that Popper himself, for a long stretch of his career, concluded that Darwinian natural selection flunks this test: the principle of the survival of the fittest can look close to tautological, and the theory as a whole seems able to account for almost any outcome retrospectively rather than making precise, falsifiable forecasts. Yet I find it nearly impossible to accept that natural selection is not science in good standing. Evolution by natural selection is one of the most successful, empirically grounded theories we have, and if my favorite demarcation criterion tells me it is metaphysics rather than science, then something has gone wrong somewhere. I do not want to be told which side wins.

Instead, please act as a professional philosopher of science and work through the impasse carefully. Begin by steelmanning both sides: first, make the strongest case for keeping the falsificationist criterion even if it embarrasses Darwinism, and then make the strongest case for letting the scientific status of natural selection force a revision of the criterion. After that, examine what would be required to justify each possible resolution: dropping or debunking the intuition, amending or abandoning the falsificationist criterion, or finding a reading on which both survive (for example, by distinguishing the general Darwinian research programme from specific, locally testable evolutionary models, or by distinguishing the core logic of natural selection from particular historical narratives about lineages and adaptations). Be explicit about what each move costs and what evidence or argument would make one route rationally compelling rather than merely attractive. Do not simply pick a side; the point is to see how the theory and the intuition should bear on each other.
