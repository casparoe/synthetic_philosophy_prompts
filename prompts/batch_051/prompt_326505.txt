I'm marking a second-year philosophy of mind midterm and want another pair of eyes on one short-essay answer. The prompt the student was given was:

"In a short essay, evaluate the claim that the badness of pain is intrinsic to how pain feels, using the case of pain asymbolia. Be sure to distinguish the sensory-discriminative and affective-motivational aspects of pain, and say exactly what the case does and doesnt show."

Here is the student's answer as submitted:

"Pain is usually split into a sensory-discriminative component — location, intensity, and quality like burning or stabbing — and an affective-motivational component, the felt unpleasantness plus the urge to get away from the stimulus. Pain asymbolia is a condition where the sensory component survives but the affective-motivational component is lost. Asymbolics can locate and describe a pinprick, and can even say it is painful, but they dont suffer it; they may smile or seem indifferent. This seems to show that the badness of pain is not intrinsic to pain's phenomenal character, since the phenomenal character — the burning or stabbing quality and its intensity — is still present while the badness is absent. So the view that pain is bad in itself, simply in virtue of how it feels, must be wrong. The badness must instead come from the affective-motivational side, which the asymbolic lacks. On that view, pain is bad because it normally makes us withdraw from and avoid the damaging stimulus; when those motivational and behavioral effects are gone, as in asymbolia, there is nothing bad about the episode. A worry could be that asymbolics are just suppressing their reactions or trying to look tough. But this conflates asymbolia with ordinary stoicism: their indifference is pathological and not under their control, and unlike analgesia, where the sensation itself is taken away, asymbolics really do feel the sensation."
