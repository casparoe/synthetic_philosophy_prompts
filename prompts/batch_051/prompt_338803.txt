I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether a soldier fighting for the unjust side of a war does anything wrong when they fight clean. My friend is pretty Walzerian: he says the rules of war apply the same to both sides, so as long as the soldier obeys jus in bello, the killing is permissible even if their country's war is unjust. I'm more pulled toward the revisionist line, McMahan-ish: the target isn't liable, so the act is impermissible, though the soldier might be fully excused if they couldn't reasonably know better. We keep finding that our disagreement collapses into the same old conscript case, or it only works when the soldier has a convenient way to find out the truth. I want a thought experiment built to order to isolate the thing we're actually fighting about.

The case should be realistic: modern conventional war, no perfect knowledge, no exotic tech. It should feature a volunteer, not a conscript, who is an ordinary adult of ordinary intelligence and genuinely cannot discover the cause is unjust through any ordinary effort — their institutions, media, and education all present the war as defensive, and they fight in strict compliance with the in bello rules (discrimination, proportionality, the usual). In the vignette, this volunteer shoots and kills an opposing soldier fighting for the just side. The spec: a Walzer-style defender of the moral equality of combatants should say the act is permissible, while a McMahan-style revisionist should say it's impermissible but fully excused. The vignette itself should be compact, but the analysis can run longer. I'm using this for a study group handout, so the point is to isolate the gap between justification and excuse — a case that simply stipulates the verdicts, or that sneaks in conscription or an available source of truth, is cheating.

After the vignette, do the check. State the relevant act, give each theory's verdict and the reasoning that generates it, then identify the single structural feature of the case that makes the traditionalist say permissible and the revisionist say impermissible but excused. Say what happens if you remove that feature, and be honest about whether removing it changes one theory's verdict more cleanly than the other's. Then give the strongest attacks on the case from each side: a revisionist who wants to deny even the excuse (maybe by arguing the volunteer was negligent about the justice of the cause anyway), and a Walzerian who wants to deny that the revisionist can coherently say the volunteer was wrong but not blameworthy. At the end, say how confident you are that the case satisfies each constraint and doens't just stipulate the verdicts, and single out the point you are least sure of. Please write the whole thing in American English.
