im helping a friend put together a "which theory of word meaning do you actually hold?" quiz for an undergrad study group. theyve read a bit of Frege and Grice but will bounce off terms like "generative lexicon," so the quiz has to do all the work with cases. i want it to sort people by what they say about particular words, not by which label they prefer. So no question can just ask "do you think words have fixed meanings?" and options should be possible characterizations of the use, never theory labels.

The logical space I care about has five-ish splits: (1) whether an expression has any standing meaning at all, or only contextually constructed uses; (2) whether related uses are a small listed set of senses or generated on the fly from a common core; (3) whether sense-extension is graded and prototype-y or discrete; (4) whether the homonymy/polysemy/vagueness boundary is a real linguistic fact or just a lexicographer's convenience. Please build the quiz out of the logical space rather than a list of names: first the questions and allowed answer-combinations, then locate the actual positions in that space, and note unoccupied combos with a quick word on why theyre unoccupied (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored).

Cases that should definitely get used somewhere, unless they turn out not to discriminate: "healthy" in healthy diet / healthy body / healthy complexion; "bank"; "heap"; "book"; "open" in open a door / open a meeting / open question / open country; "since" in since Tuesday vs since you insist; "paint" in paint the door vs paint a picture; and at least one systematic alternation like animal-for-its-meat (we roasted the lamb / the lamb wandered off) or container-for-its-contents. I especially want a question or two where people have to decide whether "we roasted the lamb" just selects a pre-listed meat sense or the speaker constructs a new sense on the spot from world knowledge; most quizzes never actually force that.

Size: eight to ten questions, and I'd rather have eight sharp ones than ten flabby ones. Each question is a concrete bit of data with two or three plain-language answer options. Provide a scoring key (dont weight the answers) that maps answer patterns onto positions. At minimum the scoring should be able to land on: "one underspecified core meaning plus pragmatics," "finite structured senses" (a small set of related readings with rules connecting them), "prototype with graded extensions," "context all the way down" (no standing meanings), and "polysemy is mostly lexicographic blur; true homonyms are separate words." Add further positions only if the logical space forces them. For each position, give the one-sentence awkward commitment a defender has to swallow.

Also flag any two positions the scoring key cannot actually separate, and say whether that's because the distinction is notional or because the questions need to be sharper. If two positions collapse in practice, merge them rather than pretending the quiz can tell them apart.
