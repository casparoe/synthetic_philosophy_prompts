I'm drafting a short essay — closer to a working note for a personal blog than an academic piece — and I'd welcome detailed comments on where the reasoning is weakest, since I'm a software engineer rather than a trained philosopher and have been attacking the problem with expected values, priors, and large numbers.

Tentative thesis: If you assign even a small probability to insects being sentient in a morally relevant way, and you reason in expected-value terms, then insect welfare should dominate your moral priorities. That conclusion is strange enough that it's worth taking seriously either as a real discovery about neglected suffering, or as a sign that the expected-value framework needs constraints.

The argument as I've been sketching it:

1. For concreteness, suppose there are on the order of 10^18 insects alive at any given moment, and that a large share of insect lives end early in ways that would be painful if the insect could suffer: predation, parasitism, starvation, desiccation. The exact numbers are not doing the work; nearly any plausible estimate makes insects vastly more numerous than humans, and most insect deaths are not gentle.

2. I'm genuinely unsure whether insects can suffer. Say my credence in "insects are sentient in a morally relevant way" is about 1%. That feels low enough to dismiss insect welfare in ordinary conversation.

3. But multiply: 1% × 10^18 gives 10^16 expected sentient insects. That's roughly six orders of magnitude more than the human population. If the unit of moral accounting is something like expected suffering-hours, insects don't just register; they swamp everything else, even at a 1% credence.

4. So an expected-value approach seems to force me to treat a cause I'm 99% sure is not morally important as the most important thing in the world. I can't tell whether that's the framework working correctly under deep uncertainty or the framework getting hacked by a very large number.

Where I'd most like comments:

(a) Hidden asymmetry. My model treats sentience as on/off, and treats the probability as a single number. If sentience, the capacity to suffer, and the moral weight of one being relative to another are all matters of degree, the insect case could be much weaker than 10^16 — or much stronger — depending on how those degrees combine. I'm not sure which way the error runs.

(b) Ambiguity versus risk. My 1% isn't a measured frequency; it's a placeholder for vague ignorance. Is it legitimate to treat unquantified ignorance as a point-valued probability when the stakes are this lopsided? If not, what constructive alternative still allows comparing causes at very different scales?

(c) Where the argument goes next. The same form of argument is available for plants, individual cells, or even simple software systems, if someone assigns each a tiny probability of having something like experience. Is there a principled stopping rule, or does the scale argument commit me to caring about an ever-expanding set of possible patients whenever a large enough population is available?

(d) Choosing the decision rule. I'm aware of a family of objections to letting tiny probabilities of enormous stakes dominate expected value — I've seen the term "Pascal's mugging" used. Is the insect case just a mugging with realistic-sounding numbers? If the insect case deserves to be taken seriously but the mugging does not, what is the formal difference?

I'm less interested in comments on the empirical estimates in step 1 or the choice of 1% — grant those for the sake of argument — and more interested in whether the structure of the argument survives.
