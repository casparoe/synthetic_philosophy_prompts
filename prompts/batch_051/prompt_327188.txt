A friend of mine does media-law stuff, and over drinks he gave me this confidently tidy account of why ratings and forecasts can basically never be defamation. I wrote his gist down as best I could: "There's no such thing as defamation by opinion, because an opinion can't be true or false, so the plantiff can never prove falsity. Since that Supreme Court case in the early 90s, courts just ask whether the thing is presented as an opinion — a column, a restaurant review, a credit rating, a price target — and if it is, it's basically per se protected. So a rating agency can call a company junk and an analyst can throw a $4 target on a stock they barely looked at, because 'junk' and '$4' are judgments, not facts. There's only a tiny exception if they explicitly say something like 'we've got documents proving they cooked the books and didn't disclose it.'" I can't shake the feeling this is too broad — I'd have thought a credit-rating agency that knew its own model was fed made-up data is in a different position from a critic saying a restaurant is bad, and that "barely looked at" ought to matter somehow. Can you go through his version and tell me what a careful First Amendment / media-law person would accept, what's a simplification, and what's just wrong? And whether his bottom line — ratings shops and analysts are effectively immune — survives the corrections? I'd also like you to say how confident you are in what you tell me and single out the one point you're least sure about.
