I'm a retired geologist, and I've spent the last year reading my way through political philosophy instead of mapping formations. The local historical society roped me onto their collections committee, and we keep getting claims about objects in the display cases. A typical month: someone writes in saying their grandfather's war medal was only on loan, or a family wants back the sign from their old shop, or a relative argues they should get the quilt a great-aunt made. Right now the committee decides these by who seems to deserve it more or who would care for it best, and I'm not confortable with that after reading Nozick.

Could you turn Nozick's entitlement theory—justice in acquisition, transfer, and rectification—into a procedure a small volunteer committee can actually run? We meet monthly, have maybe an hour per case, no lawyer, and the members are smart but have no background in philosophy. I'd like the questions in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer for each, where the procedure stops and decides, where it stops and says the claim fails, and where it has to flag that a person must just make a judgment call about values or missing facts. Then work it through three cases: (1) a medal given to us in 1987 by the owner's niece, and now a different cousin says it was only loaned; (2) a store sign we bought from a dealer who got it out of a clean-out sale of the owner's house, and a grandchild now claims it; and (3) the membership records of a small defunct church that the last minister gave us, while a great-grandchild of one parishioner says they belong to all the families in that congregation. And please make at least one of the worked cases come out against what the committee would naturally want to do, so I can show them how the Wilt Chamberlain point—that a just distribution is whatever comes out of just steps, not a pattern we aim at—actually bites.
