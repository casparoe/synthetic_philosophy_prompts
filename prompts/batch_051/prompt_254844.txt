I've been going back and forth on Wang Yangming's doctrine—the one that knowledge and action aren't two seperate stages, first you learn, then you apply, but are somehow one thing. I don't have a worked-out position, just a list of gut calls on cases, and I want someone to either find the principle behind them or tell me which of my calls has to go.

- The smoker: A guy can recite the whole epidemiology of lung cancer, says he knows smoking causes cancer, and keeps smoking anyway. Gut call: he knows the fact, but there's a different sense in which he hasn't taken it in; I wouldn't say he doesn't know, exactly, but I'm uncomfortable.
- The ethics student: Writes a flawless exam on why deception is wrong, then goes home and lies to his partner about where he was. Gut call: he doesn't really know that deception is wrong in the sense that matters; he's got the words.
- The novice climber: Just watched a tutorial and can describe every step of a figure-eight knot, but has never tied one. Gut call: she knows about the knot, not the knot itself.
- The old climber: Has tied the same knot hundreds of times, half-distracted, but garbles the verbal description when asked. Gut call: she knows the knot better than the novice—maybe the clearest case on the list.
- The anxious partygoer: Predicts accurately, with good reasons, that the party will be fine, but ten minutes in she's terrified and leaves. Gut call: she does know it'll be fine; anxiety is a seperate thing that blocks the action. I don't want to say she lacked knowledge just because she couldn't stay.
- The guy with trust issues: Loudly says people can't be trusted, but leaves his laptop on the café table while he goes to order, lends acquaintances money, and generally acts like strangers are mostly decent. Gut call: his hands know something his mouth doesn't; I'd call that knowledge, but it would be odd to say he believes people are trustworthy.
- The doctor: Diagnoses a rare condition instantly and correctly, but can't state the rule she's using and fumbles it when teaching. Gut call: she knows the condition, full stop.

So: which of these are real knowledge, which are something lesser, and can a single principle get them all? The pair I can't reconcile is the ethics student and the anxious partygoer—both have correct verbal knowledge and both fail to act on it, but I want the first to be missing something and the second to be missing nothing. Is the difference just interference versus lack of uptake, and if so, how do you tell from the outside? And if the unity of knowledge and action turns out to be right, what does it force me to say about the smoker and the doctor? Run your principle on a case I haven't listed too: someone who was raised with a moral rule, follows it habitually, but breaks it in exactly the kind of case the rule was about. If no single principle gets all of them, I'd rather know which verdict to drop than get a gerrymandered principle.
