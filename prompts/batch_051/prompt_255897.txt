Our reading group on universalisability — we've gone from Kant through Hare's universal prescriptivism and ended up fighting about the logic of 'what if everyone did that?' — got stuck on an argument a flatmate brought in. He says it shows that paying for private schooling is impermissible, at least when the local state school is decent and the reason is to give your child an edge over other people's children. The conclusion seems to strong for the premises, but none of us could say precisely wether the argument is valid. It goes like this:

(1) An action is permissible only if the agent can rationally will that everyone in relevantly similar circumstances act on the same maxim.
(2) Consider a parent who pays for an expensive private school even though the local state school is decent, solely so that his child will end up ahead of other children in university admissions.
(3) The parent's maxim is, roughly, this: when I can buy my child an advantage over others in a competition, I will.
(4) If everyone in relevantly similar circumstances acted on that maxim, every child would be competing for the same university places with the same extra tutoring, connections and exam coaching, so no child would end up ahead of anyone else — but every parent would be thousands of pounds poorer.
(5) A rational agent cannot will the universalisation of a maxim whose whole point is to secure an edge, because a world in which everyone has the edge is not one that can obtain even in principle.
(6) So the parent cannot will that his maxim become a universal law.
(7) Therefore, by the test in (1), paying for the private school for that reason is impermissible.

I half-suspect the trick is in step (5) — he seems to move from 'not everyone can be ahead at once' to 'you can't will everyone trying to get ahead' — but I can't make that objection precise, and I might be talking myself out of a sound conclusion. Is the argument valid as stated? If not, which step fails, and on what reading exactly? I'd like the answer as exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no intro or conclusion: your overall verdict first; then the exact step that fails (or the weakest premise if you think it is valid); then the precise diagnosis of why it fails; then what someone defending the argument should say in reply; then how confident you are about all this and which point you're least sure of.
