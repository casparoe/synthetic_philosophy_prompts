I've been rereading the opening of the Transcendental Aesthetic in the Critique of Pure Reason, around A19/B33, and I'm stuck on a passage. Kant says in effect that whatever the mode or means by which a cognition relates to objects, intuition is how it does so immediately, and thought is directed toward intuition as an end. I don't have the text in front of me so that's a paraphrase, but I take the gist to be that concepts are not just a second species of representation alongside intuition; they're there to get us to intuition. This seems to be doing a lot of work, maybe more than the standard line that intuitions are singular and concepts are general.

What I want is a comparison of two ways of unpacking that passage. One reading takes the distinction to be basically functional: intuition is immediate relation to an object, concept is mediate, and the two kinds can be characterized without making singularity or generality fundamental. The other reading takes the distinction to be representational: intuitions are singular representations, concepts are general ones, and the immediate/mediate stuff is downstream. Which of these is better supported by what Kant does right after in the Aesthetic, especially the arguments that space and time are a priori intuitions rather than concepts? Could Kant's claim that thought aims at intuition as its end be true on the representational reading too, or does it push us toward the functional one?

Also, am I overreading the teleology? Maybe the passage is just a metaphor, not an argument. If you had to pick the strongest reason to not build too much on it, what would it be?
