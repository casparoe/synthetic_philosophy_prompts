For my comparative government class, I have been asked to write down my own position on direct democracy and then submit it to criticism before I defend it. I want to check that the position I am inclined to take actually hangs together. Here are the commitments I currently hold:

1. Political decisions should be made by the people who will be most affected by them.
2. In a large modern state, representative democracy is the sounder default procedure than direct democracy, because legislating well requires negotiation, expertise, and time, and ordinary citizens cannot reliably supply these at the scale of a whole state.
3. The United Kingdom's 2016 referendum on leaving the European Union was a bad way to settle a constitutional change of that magnitude; the question should have been resolved through Parliament.
4. California's ballot-initiative process, especially where it touches taxation and budgeting, systematically produces incoherent policy, because voters can approve new spending and restrict revenue through separate measures without being required to reconcile the two.
5. Switzerland's practice of frequent referendums, with the government and parties supplying detailed voter guides and with citizens able to challenge laws the legislature has already passed, is a good standing check on the legislature rather than a concession to popular impatience.

I can see that the different examples point in different directions, but I do not want to assume that giving different verdicts on different cases is itself a contradiction. What I want is an audit of the whole set. Go through these commitments and identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, which pairs only appear to be, and how severe each genuine tension is. Rank the genuine tensions rather than treating them all as equal. For each one, state the least costly revision and what it would cost me, and then tell me which single revision would do the most to make the set coherent.
