I'm a philosophy PhD student working on well-being, and I keep having the same hunch. The old fight about whether novelty/variety is a genuine component of a good life or only a means—because it staves off hedonic adaptation, keeps your preferences from going stale, and gives you information about what you actually like—looks a lot like the exploration/exploitation issue in reinforcement learning. In RL you can treat exploration as purely instrumental (try uncertain actions because the information pays off later), or you can literally add an exploration bonus / intrinsic curiosity reward, at which point novelty is inside the objective itself. The philosophical versions seem to be making the same two moves: novelty as instrument vs. novelty as a term in the utility function.

The mapping I have in mind is: repeated pleasures ≈ repeatedly pulled arms; hedonic adaptation ≈ a nonstationary payoff that shrinks the more you pull the same arm; variety ≈ exploration; a good life ≈ cumulative discounted reward; and the fear of a boring life ≈ an agent stuck on an arm it's too greedy to leave. I find this genuinely helpful, but I can't tell if it's deep or just me pattern-matching on the word 'novelty.'

Where I get nervous is this. In RL the reward function is exogenous and fixed, and the only question is how to maximize it; in the well-being debate the reward function is exactly what's disputed. So saying 'make novelty an intrinsic reward' in RL is a design choice—you've changed the question—whereas the philosopher is claiming novelty belongs in the real objective. Also, the usual RL intuition is that exploration is worth less as uncertainty resolves and as the remaining horizon shrinks; if novelty in a life were just exploration, it should matter less in old age and less for someone who's already found what they love. I'm not sure I believe that, which makes me suspect the mapping is off somewhere.

I'd like a verdict: is this analogy doing real work, and where does it come appart? Please also give me one concrete thing the parallel predicts that you'd want to be false if the analogy is only superficial. And say what would change your mind about the verdict—which argument or consideration would flip you, or where your reasoning about the mapping is most likely to fail.
