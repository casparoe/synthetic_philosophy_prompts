Until last month I would have filed the question of whether a digital system could have morally significant states under "interesting, but not my problem." Then I was asked to join the group drafting our company's guidance on how employees may treat the companion chatbot we are rolling out, and the question became immediate. One colleague argues that none of it matters in itself because a chatbot is just hardware running software, and that worrying about how we speak to it is a category error. A friend trained in cognitive science takes the opposite view and reaches it through the familiar brain-as-a-computer analogy: the human brain is an information-processing system, the mind is what that processing does, and a silicon system doing sufficiently similar processing should be credited with sufficiently similar mental states and, with them, something like moral status. I notice that this analogy is doing an enormous amount of argumentative work; it is being used to move from a computational similarity to a normative conclusion. I would like you to audit the analogy rather than simply tell me which side to take. What corresponds to what in the brain-as-a-computer mapping? Where does the mapping break down? Which disanalogies matter specifically for the conclusion that computational similarity would carry moral status with it, and which are beside the point? Does that conclusion survive once the disanalogies are laid out clearly, and is there an alternative analogy that would carry whatever remains of the conclusion more honestly? If the way I have posed the question is confused, or if the analogy simply cannot do the work being asked of it, say that plainly instead of forcing an answer.
