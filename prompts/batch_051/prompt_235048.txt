I'm a novelist, and my current manuscript has a Dutch woman and a Japanese man on a long, charged email thread, and I keep stalling over what counts as rude in their dialogue. The Dutch character tells a friend, in front of a room full of people, that her new scarf doesn't suit her, and I can't tell whether the scene reads as honesty or cruelty. The Japanese character answers a request he has no intention of honoring with "I'll think about it" and then never mentions it again, and my beta readers all read that as passive-agressive, which wasn't what I meant. Then a third character, wanting to be kind, tells a visibly haggard friend "you look great," and that feels like the opposite of rude even though it's false; I know a screamed insult at a waiter is rude and a plain compliment is not, but the middle confuses me, and I suspect "rude" does two jobs at once: it flags a norm violation and it condemns the person who did it, which is why the Dutch directness and the Japanese evasion land so differently. And I still don't know whether the white lie is a small wrong of a different sort even if it isn't rude.

I want a working definition of rudeness (or of bluntness, if that's the sharper term) that sorts the cases I actually argue with myself about:

(a) the Dutch woman's unhedged "that scarf doesn't suit you," said truthfully, in front of others;
(b) the Japanese man's "I'll think about it" that means no;
(c) the white lie "you look great" said to someone who clearly looks awful, only to comfort them;
(d) a stranger at a party correcting a word you've been mispronouncing, accurately, without being asked.

Give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion: item 1 is your working definition, items 2–5 run it against those four cases, and if one case breaks the definition, say so and patch it (or defend the surprising verdict) rather than tidy the case away. I'd rather have an ugly definition that fits than a neat one that doesn't.
