I have just finished a long argument with a friend about the lottery paradox, and we kept stalling on the same question: whether a person can be rational in believing, of each ticket in a fair 1,000-ticket lottery, that it will lose, while also believing that some ticket will win. He thought this showed that acceptance under a high-probability threshold cannot be combined with deductive closure; I resisted, and the preface paradox came up as a second front — an author who accepts each sentence of her own book yet also accepts that at least one of them is false. I teach an introductory epistemology course to about twenty undergraduates, and I have one fifty-minute session available. I want the students to run into this problem themselves before it is named, rather than hearing a lecture about it. Design a runnable classroom activity that stages both paradoxes together: it should get participants to adopt a plausible acceptance rule and then discover that the rule commits them to a set of beliefs that cannot all be true at once. Give me the materials and rules (no real money; a small prize or chocolate is fine), a timeline, a debrief that maps each moment of the activity onto the structure of the two paradoxes and the tension between threshold acceptance and closure, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and an honest note on which feature of the paradoxes the activity cannot reproduce. Please write your answer in British English.
