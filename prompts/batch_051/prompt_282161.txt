I've been going around in circles on the old Dennett sphex wasp thing — the one that checks its burrow, leaves the cricket at the entrance, rechecks, then drags it in, and if you nudge the cricket a couple inches away it'll repeat the whole loop as many times as you nudge it. The usual moral is that the wasp has plenty of behavior but no flexibility, just a fixed routine, and that missing flexibility is part of what we gesture at when we say a wasp doesn't have free will. I've probably seen a dozen LessWrong comments use it that way. Lately I'm suspicious that the exact same loop can show up one level up in humans, in the policies we use to decide when to be flexible, and that when it does the 'free will = behavioral flexibility' move quietly stops working. For me this isn't abstract: I'm trying to figure out whether free will should be analyzed at the level of individual choices or at the level of the policies that generate choices.

Build me a case to order. I want an ordinary-life scenario, a workplace or a family, nothing sci-fi or clinical, with a person who is at the first-order level about as flexible as a person can be: she constantly reopens settled plans, comes up with novel options, shifts her mind, adapts. Yet at the level of the policy that tells her when to be flexible, she's sphexish: there's a fixed triggering cue that starts the reconsideration routine, and she can't suspend it even when she judges, calmly and in advance, that suspending it is what her own all-things-considered reasons favor. Now run the case through three positions and show the verdicts. (i) A deflationist who says free action just is first-order behavioral flexibility, full stop. (ii) A reasons-responsiveness compatibilist, in something like the Fischer mold: you act freely when the mechanism that actually issues in the action would be appropriately responsive to reasons, in both directions, across nearby possible worlds. (iii) A second-order sphexishness view: a person isn't free if the meta-routine that selects their decision procedure is itself a fixed action pattern that fires on a cue and can't be interrupted by the person's own settled judgment. I want (i) to say free while (ii) and (iii) both say not free, and I want (iii)'s verdict to be driven by the rigidity of the meta-policy, not by the person being coerced, having no options, or being reckless.

After the scenario, walk through the reasoning for each of the three so I can see exactly why (ii) lands with (iii) even though reasons-responsiveness is usually the more permissive compatibilist view, and which structural feature of the case makes (iii) peel off from (i). Then tell me which feature is doing the real work, what happens if you remove just that feature — does the case collapse back into a standard 'she was just compulsive' case, or into a case where all three positions say free? — and how a defender of the deflationist line would attack the whole setup. For instance, is the second-order sphexishness criterion just pushing the problem up a level and demanding an impossible regress of self-revision? Be honest about whether the case would actually move a deflationist or only begs the question against them.
