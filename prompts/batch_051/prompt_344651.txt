I'm on the editorial board of a small sociolinguistics journal, and we've hit a policy snag I'd love help unpicking. Our style guide, written in the early 90s and never revised, has a blanket clause: "All submitted prose must conform to standard English grammar, spelling, and punctuation. Manuscripts containing grammatical errors will be returned unread." In 1992 that probably felt uncontroversial.

Last week I had to desk-read a paper documenting the grammar of a new online register — its example sentences include things like "it me," "because reasons," "no thoughts head empty," "we're so back," and "i can't even." The author's own prose is spotless; the nonstandard material sits entirley inside numbered, analysed examples and the corpus appendix. Our new editorial assistant wants to bounce the manuscript under that clause. I think the clause was meant to keep sloppy academic prose out, not to ban data about nonstandard varieties, but the text doesn't say "in the author's expository prose" or "except in quoted examples," and now I'm unsure what reading is actually licensed.

I want the interpretation genuinely worked through, not just the diplomatic answer: what does the clause require on a plain reading of its words, on a purposive reading, on what the 1992 editors would likely have said if asked, and on what a reasonable submitting author would take it to mean? Is there a coherent reading that reaches the example sentences, or does every available reading have to pretend the examples aren't there? And which reading do you think should win, and why?
