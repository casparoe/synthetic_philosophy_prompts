I'm a PhD candidate in Aegean prehistory, working on the twelfth-century BCE transition in the eastern Mediterranean, and I keep running into the word "collapse" doing more analytical work than I think it can justify. In the literature it functions as though it named a natural kind of social change, but whether a particular case gets labeled a collapse seems to track the author's prior commitments about rupture versus continuity. I want a working definition of "collapse" precise enough to do taxonomic work—to separate collapse from ordinary decline, transformation, deurbanization, and mere political fragmentation—and general enough that a scholar in another region could apply it without importing my assumptions.

Here are the cases I want the definition tested against, with my current leanings stated. (1) The Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean around 1200 BCE: the Hittite state and several Mycenaean palatial centers are destroyed or abandoned, literacy and the palatial economy disappear, but much of the population remains and some regions do well. I count this as a collapse, though I admit the continuities make it hard. (2) The Classic Maya lowlands in the ninth and early tenth centuries CE: scores of major centers with inscribed monuments are abandoned and regional population falls drastically, while Maya communities persist elsewhere. This feels to me like a clear positive case. (3) Rapa Nui: the old story of a self-inflicted pre-contact ecocide is now disputed, but the island was nonetheless heavily deforested and its monument-building and political order were radically transformed, perhaps mostly after European contact. I no longer know whether this is a collapse. (4) The Western Roman Empire in the fifth century CE: the western emperor is deposed and the imperial administration dissolves, but much of the population, the church, and many urban institutions continue under successor kingdoms. I lean toward calling this a transformation rather than a collapse. (5) The Soviet Union in 1991: a superpower dissolves in months and central planning ends, but the population remains in place and the rupture is mainly institutional rather than demographic or infrastructural. I would not call this a collapse. (6) The Norse settlements in Greenland, gone by the fifteenth century: an entire regional community disappears while the wider Norse world is unaffected, and the causes look like a mix of climate, isolation, and livelihood choices. I want to call this a collapse, but I worry it is really abandonment rather than societal failure. (7) The Indus urban system around 1900 BCE: major cities are abandoned and the script disappears, yet village life and agriculture continue in the region, so it looks like deurbanization rather than collapse. I give this as a case I do not call a collapse.

I suspect the ordinary word is doing two jobs at once: a descriptive job, tracking some measurable combination of population loss, political disintegration, and loss of organizational complexity, and a normative or narrative job, judging that a society failed rather than adapted. If you think that is right, give me the two working definitions, run the cases through both, and say which cases change their classification between them. If you think one definition can do all the work, show how it handles the cases. In either version, I want the verdict for each case stated explicitly, the places where the definition gives the wrong answer or no answer flagged, and the definition revised or the surprising verdicts defended.
