Here's a parallel I keep turning over, and I honestly can't tell weather it's deep or just me pattern-matching. The no-free-lunch theorem in machine learning says that averaged over all possible problems, no learner does better than any other; to be better than chance on the problems you actually face you have to build in assumptions about which patterns are the real ones. That looks to me like the same shape as the natural-kinds realism I'm drawn to in philosophy of science: induction with "green" works and induction with "grue" doesn't because the world has joints, and green lines up with a joint while grue cuts across them. In both cases finite data underdetermine all the generalizations, and in both you need a tie-breaker; the difference is that the ML person puts the tie-breaker in the model and the realist puts it in the world. Is this a real structural parallel, or am I just noticing that both involve a kind of bias? If it's real, does anything transfer? For instance, does no-free-lunch put pressure on the realist to specify what it is about the world that does the tie-breaking, rather than just gesturing at joints? And does an anti-realist who says all taxonomies are equally good face the mirror problem, since without some tie-breaker induction becomes a mystery — which is exactly the situation the theorem formalizes? Where does the analogy come apart — maybe because the theorem averages over all possible worlds and realism is a claim about this one? And please keep separate what's actually settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested side as the consensus.
