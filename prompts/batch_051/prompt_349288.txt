I keep feeling like I've spotted a deep parallel between quantified modal logic and population ethics, but I can't tell if it's real or if I'm just getting hung up on the phrase "possible person". In modal logic the Barcan formula, in its possibility version, says: if it's possible that there exists an F, then there exists something that is possibly F. So the possibility of a new F requires some already-actual thing that could be F. Now take the nonidentity problem in population ethics. A policy (say, burning through a resource now) determines who comes to exist. People say the policy doesn't wrong anyone, because the only people who exist are the ones created by the policy, and they're not worse off than they would have been — they wouldn't have existed otherwise. But that move looks to me like rejecting the Barcan-style inference for persons: from "there could have been a person who would have been wronged by this policy", we refuse to infer "there is a person who could have been wronged by it", because no such person is actual. So here's my question: is this a genuine case of varying domains of quantification showing up inside ethics, or does the ethics side have features that break the analogy — like moral status attaching only to actual people rather than merely possible ones, or wronging being a relation that needs it's second relatum to exist, while being F is just a property a merely possible thing can have? And if the fit is real, does taking a stand on the Barcan formula (fixed domains vs. varying domains) actually force you to take a stand in the nonidentity debate, or can you accept the formula and still refuse the inference for wrongs?
