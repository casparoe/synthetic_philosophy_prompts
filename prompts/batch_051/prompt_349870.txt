I'm putting together a worksheet for an intermediate philosophy of probability course, and I want a sorting instrument — a "which position on Dutch book arguments do you actually hold?" decision tree, or a short quiz with a scoring key if you prefer. The students have seen the basic move: if your degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, there's a package of bets each fair by your lights that leaves you with a guaranteed loss. But they all nod along and then split as soon as you ask whether the argument shows what it claims to. So I want the instrument to sort them by their actual commitments on the contested points, not by which author they like. If you do a quiz, go for 8–10 questions, and no question may just ask "do you think you are a coherentist?" or the like.

The nodes (or quiz questions) should be things a student can answer without already knowing which position they lead to, and I'd like them to work through something like the following stress points, though you can rephrase or replace the ones that don't work: (1) Does "sure loss" have to be in money, or can the bets be denominated in utility units? (2) Is it coherent to accept each bet in a finite losing book but refuse the package, or does the package principle just fall out of what accepting a bet is? (3) Should a Dutch book with countably infinitely many bets have any force, given that no actual bookie could offer it? (4) Does the argument extend to your updating plans, so that a policy of updating on evidence by anything other than conditionalization is already irrational now, before the evidence arrives? (5) Is a credence of exactly zero in a live possibility a fatal vulnerability, or is zero fine if you're honest about the stake you'd lose? The leaves I'd like included: a de Finetti-style finitism on which finite coherence is the whole story (no countable additivity, no regularity, no diachronic requirement); a countable-additivity probabilism that treats an infinite losing book as a genuine argument; a regularity view that bans zeroes for nonempty possibilities; a depragmatized reading where Dutch books are just a diagnostic of inconsistency rather than a practical threat; and a circumspect skeptic who denies that betting dispositions reveal credences and that avoiding sure loss is a basic requirement of rationality. If some of those leaves can't be seperated by the questions, draw the tree to where it stops and say what further question would be needed. And for any unoccupied combination of answers, give a sentence on why nobody sits there: is it incoherent, unattractive, or just an unexplored corner someone should write about?
