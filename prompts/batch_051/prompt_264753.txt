I have been trying to state, as precisely as I can, a standard objection to expressivism about moral language. By expressivism I mean the view that an asserted sentence like 'Lying is wrong' does not report a moral fact but instead expresses a con-attitude—roughly, a negative or disapproving attitude—toward lying. Consider this argument:

(1) The sentence 'Lying is wrong' means the same thing when asserted on its own as when it occurs as the antecedent of the conditional 'If lying is wrong, then getting your brother to lie is wrong.'
(2) From 'Lying is wrong' and 'If lying is wrong, then getting your brother to lie is wrong,' one may validly infer 'Getting your brother to lie is wrong.'
(3) If expressivism is true, the asserted sentence 'Lying is wrong' expresses a con-attitude rather than stating a fact.
(4) A sentence whose meaning, when asserted, is to express a con-attitude cannot make that same contribution inside the antecedent of a conditional, because a speaker who asserts the conditional need not have any negative attitude toward lying.
(5) For a modus ponens inference to be valid, the repeated sentence must keep the same meaning in each occurrence.
(6) Therefore, if expressivism is true, the inference in (2) is invalid; but it is valid, so expressivism is false.

I suspect this argument is too quick, but I cannot locate the mistake. Is the argument valid as stated? If not, identify the exact step that fails and explain why. If it is valid, say which premise a sophisticated expressivist should reject, and describe their best reply at that step. Keep your answer to at most 300 words.
