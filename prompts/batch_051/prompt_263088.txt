Consider a position I'll call the decomposition view about doing and allowing. It holds that the distinction between doing harm and allowing harm has no fundamental moral significance. Whatever intuitive moral difference the distinction seems to mark is fully explained by independent factors that often merely track it: differences in the agent's intention, differences in antecedent duties or special obligations, and differences in the directness or reliability of the causal route from the agent's conduct to the outcome. Once those factors are held fixed, the bare fact that one agent killed while another merely let die contributes nothing.

I hold this position, and I want to know what would count against it—not what its critics ordinarily say, but where it actually risks being wrong. (1) What would have to be true for the doing/allowing distinction to carry nonderivative moral weight after intention, duties, and causal reliability are controlled for? (2) Which cases or argumentative strategies would most unsettle a careful decompositionist—for example, matched pairs in which the explanatory factors are equalized and the doing/allowing difference still seems to do clear and recalcitrant moral work? (3) Which costs does the view already incur, and which would its defenders themselves concede are genuine costs rather than bullets simply to be bitten? Distinguish evidence that would merely force a refinement from evidence that would refute the view outright, and say where the decompositionist is most exposed. The deliverable is an honest map of the position's exposure, not a defense.
