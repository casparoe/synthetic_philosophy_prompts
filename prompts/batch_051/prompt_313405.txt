I have been reading the social-institutional account of money — the view, associated with John Searle, that money is an institutional fact: it is money because a community collectively assigns a certain status to paper, ledger entries, or other tokens, a status that goes beyond their physical properties and carries rights, obligations, and expectations. I find this account compelling because it has real systematic virtues. It explains why the same physical paper can be money in one country and not another, why a monetary system can shift from gold-backed notes to fiat currency without any change in the underlying material, and why societies have used shells, stones, and digital entries as money in the right conditions. It gives a tidy, unified answer: money is whatever the relevant collective accepts and treats as money.

And yet I have a firm intuition that this theory flattens a distinction that matters. There is a difference between the dollars in my checking account and the balance on my employer's internal incentive platform, which I can use to buy meals, pay for parking, and even transfer to colleagues. The company points are purely electronic ledger entries; they have no intrinsic value; they function as a medium of exchange within the company's network; and people accept them partly because they expect others to accept them. In a limited way, they even have an exchange rate to dollars at the company store. But I cannot bring myself to say that these points are money. It is not merely that they are inconvenient or limited in scope; it feels as though they lack the kind of status that dollars have. If someone said, “your employer has created its own money,” I would regard that as a metaphor or an exaggeration, not a literal description.

The theory seems to tell me that my intuition is just tracking continuous features — scale, legal enforcement, tax acceptance, the size and stability of the accepting network — and that there is no sharp boundary, only a difference in degree. But the intuition feels more basic than that, as if it is tracking a difference in kind. I do not want to be told in advance which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the theory to be the thing that goes, and whether there is a reading of “money” on which both the institutional account and my sense that company points are not money can survive. Give me the strongest version of both positions and a negotiated verdict.
