I've been trying, on my own, to understand two decision puzzles that keep surfacing in online discussions but that don't seem to be standard classroom material. The first is the counterfactual mugging: a being with a perfect predictor flips a coin, and if it lands tails it asks you for $100; if it lands heads it pays you $10,000 if and only if it predicted that you would have paid in the tails case. You are told it landed tails, and you are asked for the $100. The second is the XOR blackmail problem, in which a blackmailer who can perfectly predict your behavior conditions whether to carry out a threatened harm on an exclusive-or of your actual decision and some variable you cannot observe. The result is that, after you have observed the demand, paying never seems to causally prevent the harm in your own branch, yet your standing disposition to pay still appears to influence how often you land in branches where the threat is made at all.

I am not looking for the standard answers to these puzzles, and I would rather you not just label them with the usual theory names. What I want is a careful comparison of their conceptual structure. Specifically, in what ways do these two scenarios pull apart the idea of precommitment, the idea of acting on information you have already updated on, and the idea that your choices should be evaluated as though they influence counterpart selves or counterfactual branches? If someone found one of these puzzles genuinely troubling and found the other merely clever or evasive, what would that difference in reaction reveal about which notion of rationality they are implicitly relying on?
