I'm reading a popular book about humor that walks through the four standard theories people always trot out — superiority, incongruity, relief, and benign violation — and the chapter on incongruity leans hard on Shopenhauer. It quotes the passage below from The World as Will and Idea (the Haldane and Kemp translation), and then gives a gloss that I nearly underlined as exactally right, but two or three spots kept nagging at me and I can't tell whether it's the book or me.

> The cause of laughter in every case is simply the sudden perception of the incongruity between a concept and the real objects which have been thought through it in some relation, and laughter itself is just the expression of this incongruity. It often occurs in this way: two or more real objects are thought through one concept, and the identity of the concept is transferred to the objects; it then becomes strikingly apparent from the entire difference of the objects in other respects, that the concept was only applicable to them from a one-sided point of view. Now the more correct the subsumption of such objects under a concept may be from one point of view, and the greater and more glaring their incongruity with it, from another point of view, the greater is the ludicrous effect which is produced by this contrast.

The author's gloss, in my own words: Schopenhauer is giving a completely cognitive theory of comedy. We laugh when a general concept we're using turns out to fit its object only from one angle — when the category and the thing filed under it part ways. The bigger and more glaring that parting, the funnier it is. Wit and folly are the same mechanism run in opposite directions: wit starts from two different things and finds a concept that unites them, while folly starts from a concept and marches it into reality until the difference jumps out. Because the mechanism is purely intellectual, Schopenhauer doesn't need superiority, relief, or any feeling at all. Later researchers renamed this the incongruity theory, and its famous weakness is that plenty of incongruities aren't funny. The author says benign violation theory fixed that by adding the obvious missing condition: the violation has to feel safe.
