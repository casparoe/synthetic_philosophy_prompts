On our hospital's research ethics board, one member keeps trying to kill a proposed trial by insisting that randomization is basically flipping a coin to decide what treatment a patient gets, and a decent doctor doesn't leave their patient's cancer care up to a coin toss. The trial would randomize people with advanced pancreatic cancer to standard chemo alone or standard chemo plus an experimental drug. His line is: if you wouldn't be comfortable with the oncologist standing at the bedside flipping an actual coin, you shouldn't be comfortable with the trial doing the same thing in slow motion. I can tell the analogy is doing a lot of hidden work but I can't make the case precisely. Can you audit it for me? I want the correspondence table — what's the coin, the two sides, the flipper, the bet, the person who takes the risk — then the places the mapping breaks. Say which disanalogies actually matter for the ethics and which are cosmetic. And tell me what the coin-flip picture quietly imports about the situation that a randomized trial lacks (known odds? a gamble rather than treatment? a flipper with no justified preference?). Finally, does his conclusion survive once those disanalogies are on the table — that randomizing patients is only acceptable when the treating doctor has litterally no opinion about which arm is better, and does appealing to clinical equipoise fix that, or does it just relocate the coin flip?
