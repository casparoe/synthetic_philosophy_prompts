I've been teaching the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem in my democracy and formal methods course, and a puzzle keeps coming up in seminar that I'd like your take on. Suppose we say a voting rule is genuinely democratic just in case it meets three conditions: (1) strategy-proofness — no voter can improve the outcome from their own point of view by submitting a ballot that misrepresents their preferences; (2) unanimity — if every voter ranks candidate A top, then A wins; and (3) non-dictatorship — no single voter's ballot always determines the winner regardless of what everyone else submits. You'll know the theorem says that with at least three candidates and unrestricted preferences, no determinstic rule satisfies all three. Fine.

But now consider random ballot: every voter submits a sincere ranked ballot, one ballot is drawn uniformly at random, and the candidate ranked first on that ballot is elected. On the face of it this is strategy-proof (if your ballot is drawn, your true top choice is the best you can do anyway), it respects unanimity, and it has no fixed dictator. Yet it's literally a lottery over one-person dictatorships: whichever ballot gets drawn, that one voter decided the whole result on their own. So here's the question. Does random ballot qualify as a genuinely democratic voting rule under the three criteria? Go through the criteria honestly; where you think the answer is no, say whether the fault lies in the criteria or in the mechanism. And in particular, tell me whether the case shows the criteria need a fourth condition — something to rule out "dictatorship by lottery," or to require that the rule itself, and not just each realised outcome, answer to the electorate collectively. Please write your answer in British English.
