I direct the grantmaking committee of a mid-sized family foundation focused on global health and development. We are consequentialists by default: we want to fund the interventions with the highest expected value, and our board expects a written expected-value justification for every grant we make. But we have hit the problem of cluelessness hard. For nearly every proposal, the direct effects are estimable — bednets reduce child mortality by roughly this much, deworming improves school attendance by roughly that much — while the long-run and indirect effects, which plausibly dominate the calculation, are not just uncertain but often unsigned. A healthier and better-educated population could accelerate economic growth and strengthen institutions, or it could raise population pressure, strain resources, and destabilize politics; we cannot even agree on the direction of some of these effects, let alone their magnitudes. We still have to decide by next quarter, and our current process has collapsed into whichever committee member argues most forcefully in the room.

Please write a decision procedure my committee can actually run. The constraints are these: the committee meets monthly, reviews two to four proposals per meeting, and has at most two hours per proposal; members include an economist, a lawyer, and two community grantmakers with no formal training in decision theory; the board requires a one-page written justification for each grant; and the stakes are a few million dollars in grants annually. The procedure should be a sequence of questions with stopping rules: what counts as an adequate answer to each, where the procedure says no, where it says fund even though cluelessness is unresolved, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. In particular, it should treat differently the case where we cannot get precise numbers but can bound the sign of the long-run effects from the case where we cannot even bound the sign. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and about what the procedure cannot do — including when the right answer might be to fund nothing, and whether "fund nothing" must itself be evaluated for cluelessness rather than treated as a neutral default. Then run the procedure on three proposals: (1) distributing insecticide-treated bednets in a malarious region, where the direct mortality reduction is well estimated but the demographic and economic knock-on effects are disputed; (2) a civic education and election-monitoring program in a young democracy, where the direct effects are modestly positive but the long-run institutional effects could plausibly be large in either direction; and (3) a cash-transfer program conditional on keeping adolescent girls in school, where the direct effects on education are well documented but there is a credible channel through which it could worsen local gender backlash. Please make at least one of these come out in a way my committee will not like, and say clearly why the procedure gives that answer.

Please lead with the bottom line in the first sentence — the procedure in one sentence — and then give the full procedure and the worked applications, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
