The passage reads:

"Epistemologists who work on testimony standardly divide into reductionists and anti-reductionists. Humean reductionists hold that testimonial justification is reducible to inference from other sources: you are justified in accepting a report only if you have positive, non-testimonial reasons for thinking the speaker is reliable, drawn from background evidence about how often reports of the relevant kind have turned out true. Reidian anti-reductionists deny this; they treat testimony as a basic source of justification, so a hearer has default justification to accept what she is told simply because people are generally sincere, unless she has a positive reason not to. The familiar objections run in both directions. Reductionism seems to make ordinary trust mysterious, since most of us accept a stranger's directions or a weather report without having checked that speaker's track record. It is also said to be unable to explain how children learn language, because children accept what they are told long before they can verify any of it. Anti-reductionism, in turn, is accused of licensing gullibility: if default trust is justified, there seems to be nothing wrong with continuing to believe a sincere-sounding report even when its content is wildly implausible. On the strength of these objections, many now conclude that neither pure view can be right and that a middle position is needed."
