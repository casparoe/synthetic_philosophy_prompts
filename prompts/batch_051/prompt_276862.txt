You're a political philosopher who works on corruption, patronage, and cross-cultural ethics, and I want you to think carefully and start by brainstorming before you commit. A friend doing fieldwork keeps telling me I'm applying Western anti-corruption standards to a different moral economy. Her line: when a local politician pays a constituent's hospital bill or hands out cash at a funeral, that isn't vote-buying — it's gift-giving, structurally the same as me buying a mate lunch with no explicit request but building a relationship that obligates them to return the favour later. So she says calling it corruption is just parochial. I don't fully buy it but I lack the framework to push back. Before you criticise the analogy, take it as seriously as you can and give the correspondence table: what plays the role of the gift, the giver, the recipient, the debt, the expected return, the social norms. Then catalogue where the analogy breaks — power asymmetry, whose resources are being given, whether the expected return is a vote or an official act, whether refusing is a live option, the scale and publicity of it. Then say which breaks matter for her conclusion and which are cosmetic. Does her conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? If it partly survives, draw the line.
