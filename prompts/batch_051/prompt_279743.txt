I spend too much time on LessWrong-adjacent forums, so expected-value reasoning has become a reflex: refusing a positive-EV bet feels like leaving money on the table, and at small stakes I actually live that way. If a friend sets up a transparent random-number generator—I can read the code, and I've watched them run it—and offers me a 10% chance at $100 for a $5 ticket, I'll take it without much thought. That's $5 of expected value in my pocket. But the same friend can change the setup to a one-in-a-billion chance at $100 billion for the same five dollars—about $95 of expected value—and I'll immediately start saying "the probability is basically zero" or "that's obviously a Pascal's mugging." I realize I have no principled rule for where the cutoff lives. As the probabilty gets smaller and the prize gets bigger, I cross the line somewhere, but the line seems to track how outlandish the numbers feel, not anything I can defend.

I know the standard moves: model uncertainty about whether the payout would really happen, bounded utility, diminishing marginal utility of money, the idea that tiny probabilities are just "map noise." But I'm not persuaded they're doing the real work in my head, and anyway you can rerun the whole thing with the prize denominated in lives saved or in whatever I already care about, with a mechanism as reliable as the code-run RNG in the first case. So give me the strongest candidate principle that lets me take the 10% bet and refuse the one-in-a-billion bet, and an honest verdict on whether it works. If there isn't one, tell me which side should give way: should I stop trusting expected value at ordinary scales, or should I hand the mugger my wallet?
