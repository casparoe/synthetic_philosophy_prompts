I'm helping plan a two-year research program on the moral status of digital minds, and I keep going back and forth on what should actually be in it. Most of the field is either arguing about whether digital minds could be conscious or have subjective states at all, or jumping straight to the applied stuff (deletion, rights, whether training them is wrong). But theres a cluster of questions I almost never see tackled, and I suspect its upstream of all of that: how do you count the subjects? If a mind can be copied, merged, run at different speeds, paused for a year and restarted from a checkpoint, forked into five variants that then diverge — the usual welfare-accounting machinery just quietly assumes you can identify which beings there are and whether the one before is the same as the one after. Even the sentence 'deleting this copy harms it' presupposes we can pick out which 'it' we mean. I have a hunch that counting/individuation problem is quietly load-bearing, but I could be overrating it — if you think it's downstream or just confused, say so. The question I actually need answered: what are the main open questions in the moral status of digital minds right now, and which two or three should this program spend its two years on? I'm looking for a defended ordering, not a survey. For each candidate, say why it's upstream or downstream of the others, whether philosophers (rather than neuroscientists or ML people) can actually make progress on it now, and what evidence over the next couple years would make you drop it or promote something else. Assume the program is roughly six people, mostly philosophers, some with enough technical background not to say nonsense about how AI systems are built.
