I am a PhD student in the history and philosophy of science, and in reading online defenses of fringe historical claims — Atlantis, ancient-astronaut hypotheses, and chronology revisions of the Fomenko sort — I keep encountering a rhetorical move I have trouble analyzing. Whenever a skeptic points out that there are no surviving artifacts, no unambiguous geological traces, or no contemporary documentary record for the alleged phenomenon, the reply comes back with some version of the proverb "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence." The proverb is offered as if it were a complete and self-sufficient answer to the objection from silence.

What bothers me is that the sentence seems to slide between several quite different claims depending on the moment. It might be read as the thesis that an absence of evidence is never, by itself, any evidence at all of absence; or as the weaker thesis that an absence of evidence does not amount to conclusive proof of absence; or as a claim about search sensitivity, namely that a failure to find evidence disconfirms a hypothesis only if the evidence would probably have turned up were the hypothesis true; or as a burden-of-proof rule that makes the skeptic's inability to disprove the claim do positive argumentative work. These readings have very different logical shapes, and some of them look close to trivially true while others look plainly false.

I would like the answer to lay out the distinct precise theses that the proverb could be used to assert, say which are trivial, which are substantive and defensible, and which are false, and then explain which reading is actually being leaned on when the proverb is used to convert a lack of traces into a reason not to reject — or even to accept — a fringe historical claim. In particular, I want to know whether any defensible reading of the proverb can do that argumentative work, or whether the move only works by trading on an ambiguity.
