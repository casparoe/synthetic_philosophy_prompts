I'm a software engineer without any formal background in philosophy, but a friend who reads a lot of philosophy of mind has spent the past few weeks building an argument that I now cannot get out of. His target is panpsychism — roughly, the view that the fundamental constituents of the physical world have some kind of experience, or at least proto-experience, rather than experience arising only in brains. He claims the view can be refuted on broadly ethical grounds, and he lays the argument out like this.

(1) If panpsychism is true, then quarks, electrons, and the fields through which they interact have experiential states: there is, in however thin or alien a way, something it is like to be them.
(2) Experience is what makes a thing morally considerable in the first place, and because suffering and enjoyment are themselves just experiences with positive or negative character, every experiential state — however faint — has at least a minimal positive or negative valence, a tiny pull toward or away.
(3) Any physical process that smashes, tears apart, dissolves, or otherwise disrupts an experiencing thing changes its felt state, and a disruption of a fundamental physical thing in the ordinary course of nature is overwhelmingly more likely to make its state worse than better, however slightly; that is a miniature harm.
(4) Every ordinary human action — typing, walking, digesting, or even the computation running inside a processor — changes the configuration or energetic state of an enormous number of fundamental physical things, and so involves an enormous number of such disruptions.
(5) Therefore, if panpsychism is true, every available human action causes enormous quantities of morally relevant miniature harm, no matter what the agent does.
(6) A moral framework in which literally every available action causes enormous morally relevant harm leaves no room for a usable distinction between better and worse actions; every option is a moral catastrophe, and no agent can avoid wrongdoing.
(7) So panpsychism entails the collapse of ethics into an impossible universal prohibition.

He takes (7) as a reductio: since an ethical framework that collapses in this way cannot be correct, panpsychism must be false. I find each step plausible enough on its own, and I do not want to accept that conclusion, but I also cannot locate where the argument overreaches. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? Once the argument has been repaired as charitably as possible, what does it actually succeed in establishing, if anything, about the ethical implications of panpsychism?
