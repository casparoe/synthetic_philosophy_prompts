Last night I had a long argument with a friend who has been working through the Nyāya debate about which means of knowing (pramāṇas) are genuinely distinct sources of knowledge. Here is her track record, as best I can reconstruct it. She counts perception (pratyakṣa): seeing the pot gives knowledge that the pot is on the table. She counts inference (anumāna): smoke on the hill gives knowledge that there is fire. She counts testimony (śabda) as a pramāṇa in its own right, not merely a form of inference. She counts comparison (upamāna) as its own pramāṇa: someone told only that a gavaya is like a cow can see a gavaya and thereby know that this is what the word "gavaya" names. But she will not count memory (smṛti) as a pramāṇa, since it only replays a cognition already received. And she will not count postulation (arthāpatti) as a distinct pramāṇa: knowing that Devadatta is alive but absent from the house, she says, is enough to infer that he is elsewhere.

Two candidates never came up. One is recognition (pratyabhijñā): seeing someone in the market today and judging that this is the same person I met yesterday. The other is non-apprehension (anupalabdhi): seeing an empty table and judging that there is no pot on it. Do you think she will say each of these is a piece of knowledge, and do you think she will say either rests on a distinct pramāṇa? More importantly, what rule best explains the verdicts she has already given, what alternative rule would fit those verdicts just as well, and what further case would most cleanly separate the two?
