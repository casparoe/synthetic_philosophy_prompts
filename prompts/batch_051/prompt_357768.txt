I've been reading about a puzzle in the philosophy of evolutionary biology that keeps reminding me of problems I've seen in reliability engineering, but I can't tell whether the resemblance is substantive or merely superficial. The puzzle is this: if the fittest organisms are simply defined as the ones that actually survive and reproduce the most, then the statement that the fittest survive and reproduce more starts to look empty — a tautology with no predictive content. Philosophers have responded, I gather, by distinguishing fitness from actual reproductive success. On this response, fitness is an objective probabilistic disposition, or propensity, of an organism, trait, or genotype to contribute offspring in a particular environment, in something like the way a biased die has a probability of landing six even before you roll it. Natural selection then amounts to the substantive empirical claim that differences in these underlying propensities causally explain, on average, differences in actual reproductive outcomes.

I work as a backend software engineer, mostly on reliability and observability for distributed systems. The vocabulary I actually think in is SLOs, error budgets, canary rollouts, synthetic checks, flaky tests, latency percentiles, incident postmortems, and the distinction between a service's real underlying health and the metrics we happen to sample from it. Please restate the tautology objection and the propensity interpretation of fitness in that vocabulary as faithfully as you can. I want a systematic translation rather than loose analogies: which concepts line up cleanly? Is a fitness propensity like a service's latent failure rate under a fixed environment, while actual reproductive success is like an observed availability measurement over some particular window? Which parts of the biological debate have to be bent to fit, and which have no counterpart at all in how I think about systems?

Finally, give me the residue report: what did not survive the translation, and is that residue a defect in the reliability-engineering vocabulary, or is it exposing something under-specified in the philosophical position? In particular, I want to know whether my intuition that the tautology objection would simply dissolve if biologists always distinguished an unobserved propensity from its observed outcomes is correct, or whether that response silently imports a software-engineering assumption — for instance, that behind every observed metric there is a stable, well-defined underlying parameter waiting to be estimated — that biology is not entitled to.
