I run a rationality meetup whose regulars are working programmers: fluent in Boolean logic and the sum types of Haskell and Rust, well-read in the usual blog canon, but none of them has worked through constructive mathematics. I have one hour to move them from "either P or not P is obviously true" to a gears-level sense of what changes when a proof has to produce a witness and a proposition's truth cannot be tested locally. Plan the session, not the explanation itself. What has to be in place first — I suspect a crisp split between decidable and undecidable predicates, and between proof of a negation and genuine proof by contradiction, but correct me if the order is wrong. Choose the single running example that should carry the whole session and say what it must have and lack; I would rather not lean on the classic proof that some irrational raised to an irrational power is rational (the √2^√2 trick) except as a cautionary tale. Give me the misconceptions to expect and the precise point in the sequence where each should be defused — at minimum "intuitionistic logic is three-valued or fuzzy," "constructive means no proof by contradiction anywhere," and "rejecting excluded middle means believing there are propositions that are neither true nor false" — then say what you would openly leave out at this level, and one closing question whose correct answer requires actually using the constructive reading rather than reciting a slogan. Assume the room is bright but allergic to formality that does not earn its keep.
