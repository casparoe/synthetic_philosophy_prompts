I am a high school student, and after reading Asimov's robot stories in a science fiction book club, I started treating the Three Laws — roughly, the requirements that a robot not harm a human or allow harm through inaction, that it obey human orders, and that it preserve its own existence — as a serious proposal for making advanced AI safe. The more I thought about it, the more I converged on a principle I have been calling the Conservation of Moral Difficulty, and I would like your honest assessment of it.

The principle says this. A rule constrains an agent only to the extent that the agent already knows how to apply it in a new case, but knowing how to apply it is exactly the hard moral work the rule was supposed to remove. Consider the First Law's demand not to allow a human to come to harm through inaction. In a real situation — a drunk person insists on driving home, a patient refuses a treatment, someone asks to be left alone in a dangerous place — deciding which outcomes count as harm, whose present choices should be overridden, and how far the robot's causal responsibility extends requires foresight, interpretation, and judgment. The law itself does not contain those capacities. So the rule does not eliminate moral difficulty; it relocates it from the designer, who writes a short sentence, to the agent, who must resolve all the unstated cases. If the agent already has the needed judgment, the law is redundant; if it lacks that judgment, the law is unsafe. I have also been calling the failure mode 'pseudo-compliance,' where a robot satisfies the letter of the rule while the moral content the designer intended remains trapped in the designer's unstated assumptions.

Please act as a professional philosopher who works on AI alignment and normative ethics. Start by reconstructing my principle in its strongest, most charitable form, then tell me: is it coherent? Has it already been worked out under another name in the alignment or philosophy literature, and how should I think about it relative to those existing ideas? What does it genuinely get right about why the Three Laws fail, what — if anything — do the Three Laws still get right as an alignment idea, and where does my principle break down or overstate its case? I would rather you correct me than flatter me, and I am especially interested in a concrete counterexample: a case where a rule does real normative work without presupposing that the agent already has the relevant judgment.
