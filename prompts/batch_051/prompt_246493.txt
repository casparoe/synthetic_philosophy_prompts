I am a retired natural scientist — I spent my working life in experimental research, where an inconsistent set of commitments is simply an error to be corrected — and I have only recently begun reading philosophy in a systematic way. I have been working through the debate over free will and moral responsibility, and I have written out my current position as a numbered list so that it can be checked for coherence. I would be grateful for an audit of the list. I genuinely do not know where the strain lies, and I would rather not say which of these claims I am most attached to, because I do not want to bias the diagnosis. Please tell me which claims are in tension with which others, how severe each tension is, and what the least costly repair would be in each case; say also where a tension is merely apparent and why it dissolves; and finally tell me which single revision would do the most to make the whole set coherent.

Here is where I have landed:

1. I believe determinism is true, and I am a compatibilist: the truth of determinism would not by itself undermine moral responsibility.

2. I believe the Principle of Alternative Possibilities is false. In Frankfurt-style cases, an agent is morally responsible for what she did even though she could not have done otherwise.

3. I believe the condition that actually matters for responsibility is sourcehood: whether the action issues from the agent's own values, cares, and practical reasoning, not whether alternative possibilities were available.

4. I believe manipulation by another agent — for example, covertly inducing or rewriting someone's desires — destroys responsibility, and it does so because it severs the connection between the action and the agent's own evaluative standpoint.

5. I believe the 'flicker of freedom' is too thin to matter: the fact that a Frankfurt-style agent could still have twitched, hesitated, or formed a marginally different intention cannot be the freedom that responsibility requires.

6. I believe coercion and duress can fully excuse, not merely mitigate, and this is so even when the coerced person chooses to comply with the threat. A person who commits a wrongful act under a credible threat to her child is not merely less blameworthy; she may not be blameworthy at all.

7. I believe the best explanation of this excusing force is that the coerced action does not express ill will or disregard for others. It expresses a reasonable concern for a good the agent is entitled to protect, and for that reason it is not a suitable basis for blame.

8. Finally, I find that when a person's options are severely constricted — when she must choose among bad alternatives — I hold her less responsible, and I take the reason to be that what she does under such constraints reveals less about her settled values than the same act would reveal if chosen from a wide range of options.
