A friend and I went back and forth for way too long last night about self-control -- specifically whether you can ever rationally set yourself up to do something you've already decided you won't do, and whether precommitment tricks actually work or are just ways of fooling yourself. He kept insisting there's a classic puzzle that settles it, and this morning he sent me this paragraph from a book he's reading:

"Kavka's toxin puzzle shows that a rational person cannot intend to do something they know they won't do. A billionaire offers you a million dollars if, at midnight tonight, you intend to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon. The money arrives tomorrow morning, before you drink, and you keep it whether or not you actually drink; the toxin just makes you sick for a day and causes no lasting harm. Since a rational person will simply not drink once the money is safely in their account, it follows that you cannot form a genuine intention to drink -- and if you somehow did, you would be irrational. Kavka's point is that intentions just are predictions about your own future behavior, plus a desire, so intending to drink would mean believing something you know will be false, and rational agents don't form false beliefs. The upshot is that precommitment and self-binding are confused ideas: if you can't even intend the later act, no commitment device can make you intend it."

I cant tell whether he's overreading the puzzle, but something about that last stretch feels too strong. Is this a fair summary of what the toxin puzzle actually shows? Walk me through the paragraph line by line: what would a philosopher who works on intention and practical rationality sign off on, what would they resist, and what would they call flat-out wrong? And does the puzzle really have the implication my friend drew -- that precommitment devices are pointless -- or does it leave room for them?
