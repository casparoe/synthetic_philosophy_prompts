I'm a junior taking AP U.S. Government and Politics, and we recently finished a unit on the history of the franchise. We read excerpts from John Stuart Mill's writings on representative government, and I was struck by how far his framework is from the one-person-one-vote assumptions we mostly take for granted today. Mill treats the vote not as an automatic individual right but as a public trust to be distributed so that everyone's major interests get represented, while more educated or skilled citizens receive proportionally greater weight through a system of plural voting. He also defends excluding people who cannot read, write, and do basic arithmetic, and people who are dependent on public relief, on the theory that the vote should not go to those who lack the basic tools for judging public questions or whose immediate private interest would dominate their use of it. At the same time, he was unusually expansive for his era in arguing that women and the working class should be brought into the electorate. I find it genuinely hard to work out what these commitments imply for voting questions that arose after Mill died, and I would like to see them applied rather than treated as museum pieces.

Suppose Mill were alive today and serving on a state commission that has to take positions on three current proposals: first, lowering the voting age from 18 to 16 for all state and federal elections; second, granting non-citizen permanent residents the right to vote in municipal and school-board elections, since they live, work, and pay property and income taxes in the district; and third, adopting compulsory voting, with a modest fine for non-voters, along the lines of Australia's system. Please give your best reconstruction of the position Mill would most likely take on each proposal, drawing on his recorded arguments and commitments rather than on a generic guess about what a nineteenth-century liberal might say.

Please organize your answer into the following four sections, in this order. Mill's likely positions (about 200 words): state a definite predicted position on each of the three proposals. The reasoning behind each position (about 250 words): connect each prediction to specific Millian commitments, including his view of the vote as a trust, his demand for universal education before universal suffrage, his plural voting scheme, his concern with representing interests rather than mere numbers, and his link between paying taxes and having a voice in how taxes are spent. Points of tension (about 150 words): explain where Mill's principles pull in different directions on any of the proposals, or where changed facts about modern society complicate the application. Bottom line (about 100 words): say which of your three predictions you are most confident in and which is most uncertain, and briefly explain why.
