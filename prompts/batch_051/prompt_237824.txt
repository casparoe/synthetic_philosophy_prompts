As a novelist, I have come to think that much of what we owe one another is an attitude rather than an outward action, and I have been using four criteria to isolate the specific attitude I call recognition. First, the other person's point of view is allowed to make a real claim on me: I treat it as something that can oblige me to notice, explain, or change, rather than as one more factor in my plans. Second, I treat the other's self-understanding as authoritative about who they are; if they reject my account of them, I cannot unilaterally override it. Third, I hold myself answerable to the other — willing to give an account of myself, not merely to manage the other's reactions. Fourth, I respond to the other as this particular person, never as an instance of a type.

The case that unsettles me is my own relation to my central character while I draft a novel. The character appears to satisfy all four criteria. The character pushes back against a scene with the same felt force as a friend correcting me: certain endings feel like something the character "would not do," and the resistance strikes me as binding rather than optional. I can feel that I owe the character the version of the story their terms demand, and I catch myself saying that I am trying to get a particular other right rather than to improve my material. But the character is my own projection; there is, by construction, no one there to be answerable to. Does my relation to the character qualify as recognition in the sense those four criteria define? Work through the criteria one at a time and say where they underdetermine the verdict. Then tell me whether the case exposes a missing requirement — that the other be real — or whether the other's reality was doing less work in the concept than I had assumed.
