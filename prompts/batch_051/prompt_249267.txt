Last night a friend and I had a long, circular argument about when, if ever, it is right to say that an AI system believes something. We each kept proposing principles that promptly died on the other person's counterexamples. After he left, I wrote down the verdicts I actually hold when I consider concrete cases. I want to know what principle, if any, captures them, and I am prepared to be told that some of my verdicts must be dropped.

Here are the cases and my settled judgments about them.

1. A self-driving car's perception system fuses camera and lidar cues into a running estimate that a pedestrian is entering the crosswalk ahead, and the planner slows and yields to her. I am comfortable saying, without hedging or inverted commas, that the car believes a pedestrian is crossing and wants to avoid hitting her.

2. A language model of the ordinary sort, trained only to predict text and frozen afterward, is asked for the capital of France. It reliably says that it is Paris. I find myself unwilling to say that the model believes Paris is the capital of France; I would at most put the word "believes" in scare quotes.

3. A retrieval-augmented assistant pairs a language model with a database. At query time it retrieves an entry and composes an answer from it. I am comfortable saying that the composite system knows the retrieved fact while it is in use, but I would call it a category mistake to attribute that knowledge to the language-model part alone.

4. A reinforcement-learning agent trained to maximize a game score plays with the same trade-offs a skilled human would. I am fine saying that it wants to win, but I would not say that it believes some particular proposition, such as that attacking now will improve its expected score, except as metaphor.

5. A model with a persistent memory store records facts it is told during a conversation and later revises them when corrected. After being told that Alice lives in Berlin, it says Alice lives in Berlin; after being told she moved to London, it says she lives in London. I am quite willing to say that the system believed Alice lived in Berlin and now believes she lives in London.

6. A model is prompted to produce intermediate reasoning before answering, and it writes things like "I check X first; since X holds, I infer Y." I find this way of talking almost impossible to resist while reading its output, but on reflection I am still unwilling to say the model believed its intermediate conclusion Y, even when Y plainly shaped the final answer.

7. A medical-risk model outputs a probability that a patient is septic. I happily call it well calibrated, but I resist saying it is confident to this or that degree; I want to say it assigns a probability, not that it holds a graded belief.

8. An assistant with canned, agreeable responses tells a user something false that was baked in by its designers and was not the product of any tracking of the world. I will say it said something false; I will not say it lied, because I take lying to require a prior belief and an intention to mislead.

I keep backing into a rough rule: ascribe beliefs to states that track the world and guide behavior, ascribe desires to behavior shaped toward an outcome, and treat the rest as metaphor. But that rule seems to draw the line right through cases 2 and 6, and it may also commit me to saying the risk model in 7 has graded beliefs, which I do not feel.

What principle, or small set of candidate principles, best captures this pattern of verdicts? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, and apply it to a few further cases I have not listed: (a) a car like the one in 1 that produces the same driving behavior through a brute lookup table from sensor patterns to steering and braking commands, with no tracked model of the world; and (b) a robot whose speech module says the door is locked while its navigation module plans a route through the doorway, as if it were open. Finally, say honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should revise those verdicts rather than complicate the principle.
