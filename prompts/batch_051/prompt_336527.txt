I serve on the nomenclature committee of a professional society that maintains the standard vocabulary for industrial inspection and materials testing. Four or five times a year we vote on names for newly described kinds of defects, instruments, measurements, and certificates, and because the approved terms then work their way into regulations, training syllabuses, and tender documents, they are expensive to change once published. The committee is made up of working inspectors and metallurgists; none of us has any formal background in linguistics or in the history of our technical language, and a decision usually has to be reached in one two-hour meeting and then ratified by the full membership at the annual conference, where it is rarely reopened.

I have been turning over a question that keeps appearing in our discussions. Many of our established terms are compounds coined from Latin or Greek roots, and nearly every new proposal follows the same pattern. I can see the standard case for it: the roots can be assembled to encode a definition; a dead language belongs to no living speech community, so the term carries less national baggage; and the classical form marks a term as technical and stable. I can also see the standard case against it: the terms are opaque to people entering the field; the "internationalism" is less convincing to someone from outside the European-language tradition; and a name chosen for what was once thought to be the essential feature can survive long after that feature turns out to be secondary or false. What I do not have is a way to move from that diffuse sense of the trade-offs to an actual decision we can execute in a meeting.

Please write me a decision procedure my committee could adopt and reuse — a flowchart in words, with the questions in order; for each question, what would count as a sufficient answer for us to move on to the next; the points at which the procedure stops with a no; and the points at which it hands the decision to a person because the remaining matter is one of values rather than facts. Be explicit about which distinctions you are deliberately dropping so the procedure can be run by non-specialists in two hours. Then run the procedure on three proposals currently before us. The first is a proposal to rename the plain-language term "cold-cracking" as cryoclasis, from Greek roots the proposer renders as "cold" and "breaking," on the grounds that a classical compound will standardize the term across languages. The second is a proposal to name a newly observed defect — a crack that repeatedly branches at roughly right angles as it propagates — ramogenesis, uniting a Latin root the proposer renders as "branch" with a Greek root the proposer renders as "origin." The third is a split in our subcommittee over whether a new measurement of surface roughness obtained by dragging a stylus should receive a newly coined Greek name or simply keep the English phrase "profile depth." For each case, say what the procedure returns and why; where it returns an answer the committee will find uncomfortable, say so explicitly. Close with an honest statement of what the procedure cannot settle, and any circumstances in which we would be better off setting the procedure aside.
