I've been reading Mill's The Subjection of Women and I've found myself pulled in two directions. The side that convinces me is the critique of the appeal to nature: the usual move is to defend a social arrangement by saying it fits women's (or anyone's) 'nature,' but the evidence for that nature was produced under the very arrangement being defended, so the argument is circular, and what it calls nature is mostly custom hardened into a just-so story. That gives me a clean, general principle: when an institution is defended by appeal to nature, I should treat the appeal as a rationalization of the status quo until proven otherwise. The problem is I also have a firm intuition that some appeals to nature are not rationalizations. They report real constraints — say, that only some bodies can gestate, or that there are robust, cross-culturally stable average differences in some traits — and those constraints seem genuinely relevant to how we organize things. This doesn't feel like custom dressed up; it feels like evidence. But I can hear Mill answering that the relevance of the difference, not just its existence, is exactly what's being manufactured by the institution, and I don't know how to answer him without abandoning a principle I still think is basicly right. I don't want to be handed a winner without the reasoning. Give me exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro or conclusion: (1) the strongest version of the Millian principle, (2) the strongest version of my intuition, (3) what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, (4) what it would take for the principle to be the thing that goes, and (5) whether there's a reading where both survive — or, if not, which move is least costly.
