I've got a policy I use whenever I'm reading biology or evaluating claims about adaptations, and I want to know if it's defensible or just a bias wearing a definition. The policy is: when someone says "the function of X is Y," I only treat it as a real function claim if it's etiological—that is, if doing Y is supposed to explain why X was selected for, or why X has been maintained. If the claim is only that X does Y and that doing Y contributes to some larger capacity of the organism—like a physiology textbook saying the heart's function is to pump blood, with no selection story attached—I mentally file it as loose talk about a current causal role, not a genuine function claim. Last week in a physiology unit, the lecturer said the function of the loop of Henle is to concentrate urine by countercurrent exchange, and I caught myself discounting the whole statement as not-really-a-function, because nothing was said about why the loop evolved. I do the same thing when evaluating arguments: if someone says a trait is an adaptation for Y, and there evidence is just that the trait currently does Y and the organism currently needs Y, I discount it heavily.

So: is this policy defensible? Where does it break—I'm thinking of exaptations, novel features with no selection history yet, or physiological functions that seem to hold regardless of how the thing arose? Is there a version of the rule that keeps the good of demanding selection stories without making me dismiss half of physiology as "not real function"? And how would I tell, from the inside, whether I'm using a decent philosophical distinction or just protecting a pet theory from counterexamples?
