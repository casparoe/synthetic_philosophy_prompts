I chair the treatment-review panel of a psychiatric hospital. A case is before us, and I need a decision memo I can circulate to the panel and act on.

The patient is a 38-year-old with bipolar I disorder and a history of severe manic episodes with psychosis, each marked by anosognosia. Eighteen months ago, during a period of sustained remission and after capacity assessments by two independent psychiatrists, the patient executed a psychiatric advance directive. The directive provides that, in a future manic episode with documented anosognosia, the patient consents in advance to involuntary hospitalization and to named mood stabilizers and an antipsychotic, and that a refusal of those interventions during such an episode is not to be treated as a valid revocation. It also provides that the directive may be revoked only during a sustained remission, and only by a written instrument countersigned by the patient's treating psychiatrist and an independent examiner.

The patient is now involuntarily admitted in a manic episode that satisfies the directive's trigger conditions, and is refusing the named medications. During a forty-eight-hour period last week that nursing staff logged as a marked but transient improvement in insight, the patient wrote and signed a revocation, witnessed by two nurses, stating that the medications named in the directive are no longer acceptable to them. The treating psychiatrist says the improvement was transient and falls far short of the sustained remission the directive requires; the independent examiner has not countersigned the revocation. The patient now tells us that the person who signed the directive is not the person before us, and that their present refusal expresses who they really are.

The panel is split. Some members see the directive as a paradigm Ulysses contract: honoring it protects the patient's longer-term autonomy and respects a competent, informed past self against the transient preferences of an impaired present self. Others hold that the only stance consistent with respect for the patient is to decline treatment on the authority of the directive, because the person now before us is trying to revoke it and opposes the medication. A third view, floated by one member, is that the right framework is constitutional precommitment: just as a constitutional order may bind future majorities only where it leaves them a fair amendment procedure, an advance directive may bind a future self only where that self retains a fair, realistic procedure for unbinding — and the question is whether this directive's revocation procedure is such a procedure or a trap.

Draft the panel's decision memo recommending whether to authorize medication over the patient's current objection on the authority of the directive: treat, do not treat, or treat under specified conditions. Your memo should give a clear, actionable bottom line; set out the reasoning; deal honestly with the strongest argument for the position you reject; and address whether the constitutional-precommitment analogy helps or distorts here. Close with a short paragraph stating which factual or philosophical consideration, had it gone the other way in this case, would most likely have changed your recommendation, and where you take your reasoning to be least secure.
