I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years, and I keep running into a disagreement that I suspect is partly verbal but that matters for downstream claims about biology, minds, and AI. One side says that DNA is a code — not as a loose analogy but as a literal, factual description — and treats this as licensing a whole software/hardware view of organisms: the genome is a program, the cell is an interpreter, mutations are corrupted data, aging is a bug, and engineering an organism or even uploading a mind is in principle a matter of decoding and reimplementing the relevant information. The other side replies that calling DNA a code is just a metaphor borrowed from human symbol use, and that nucleotides have no intrinsic meaning apart from the causal machinery of the cell. Then the first side accuses the second of deflationary taste disguised as conceptual hygiene, and the thread goes nowhere.

I want a careful disambiguation rather than a verdict. What are the distinct precise claims that the sentence "DNA is a code" could be making? For each reading, I would like to know: whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false; what kind of evidence or argument would be needed to support it; and whether that reading actually licenses the software/hardware conclusions that the slogan is being used to draw. In particular, separate at least the following dimensions if they come apart: the presence of a systematic mapping between sequence elements and products; the presence of anything like arbitrary conventions or a sender–receiver relation; the idea that the mapping is digital rather than analog; the idea that the genome contains instructions or representations; the idea that the cell implements an algorithm; and the idea that DNA carries semantic information in a sense that could exist independently of the organism that reads it. I would also be interested in a brief note on how Schrödinger already blurred some of these distinctions in What Is Life? (1944) by describing chromosomes as an architect's plan and a builder's craft in one object, and how that historical ambiguity still does work in these arguments.
