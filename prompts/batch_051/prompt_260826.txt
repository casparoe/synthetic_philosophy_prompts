I'm preparing a seminar essay on the ethics of museum restitution, and I want to argue with, rather than merely illustrate from, Ryan Coogler's Black Panther (2018). The conceptual background is the familiar set of positions: the universal museum defense, the wrongful-taking argument for return, and the stronger claim that some objects are not just alienable property but partly constitutive of a people's identity and therefore carry demands that ordinary loot does not. The film enters this debate through an invented African object and an invented African state. Early in the film, Killmonger and Klaue steal a Wakandan artefact from a London museum; before the theft, Killmonger confronts the curator over how such objects ended up in European collections and treats their continued display as an act of expropriation. The film then routes this critique into a larger fight: Killmonger's plan to end Wakanda's isolation by arming African-descended people worldwide, versus T'Challa's commitment to Wakandan sovereignty and, ultimately, his turn toward a different kind of openness. I want a reading that takes both the film and the restitution debate seriously. What does the film dramatize about the issue that the standard arguments, run purely as arguments, tend to miss, for instance about the relationship between returning objects and returning interpretive authority, or about the way possession of a stolen thing can quietly continue to structure the conversation about whether and how it should come back? Where does the restitution lens genuinely illuminate a scene or a character's choice, and where does the film resist the reading, for example by placing its most explicit statement of the case for return in the mouth of a figure whose broader program the film judges destructive? Address the complication that Wakanda was never colonized and that Killmonger is not Wakandan but of the African diaspora: what does that imply about who has standing to press a claim of return, and whether the demand is best understood as a claim on objects, on redistributive technology, or on something else? Finally, say what the film contributes to the philosophical debate that a well-constructed position paper on the Benin Bronzes or the Parthenon Marbles would not, and whether its genre framing quietly lets the museum off the hook by making the radical case sound like supervillainy.
