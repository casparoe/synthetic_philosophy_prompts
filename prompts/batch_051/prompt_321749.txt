I have been thinking about something that came out of a frustrating medical experience, and I would like a clear diagnosis of where it goes wrong. My doctor's online tool gave me one number for my ten-year risk of a heart attack; a second tool, into which I entered more details, gave me a number almost twice as large. I assume each tool put me into a class of people like me and reported the rate for that class. But the tools used different classes, and the classes had different rates.

I then built the following argument. Each step seems forced, but the conclusion is plainly false, so something must give.

(1) A probability claim about an individual event is meaningful only relative to a reference class: a group of similar cases for which we have an observed rate.
(2) For any individual event, there are many ways to draw the reference class, and different ways yield different rates.
(3) If the truth of a claim depends on a choice among equally legitimate alternatives, and the alternatives disagree, then the claim has no determinate truth.
(4) Therefore a statement like "my ten-year risk of a heart attack is 20 percent" has no determinate truth; there is no fact of the matter about my personal risk.

(5) But the next flip of a coin is just as much an individual event. The flip belongs to many reference classes: flips of fair coins, flips of coins by me, flips of coins under this ceiling fan, flips made on weekdays, flips of this particular coin, and so on. Those classes can disagree about the frequency of heads.
(6) By the same reasoning as step (4), the statement "the next flip has a 50 percent chance of heads" has no determinate truth either. That is absurd: the chance is 50 percent, and any account that denies it has stopped being an account of probability.
(7) So at least one of the premises must be false, though I cannot see which one.

I have already tried the obvious escape. Perhaps one should always use the most specific reference class with enough data. But "most specific" still requires a choice of which features matter—age alone, age plus cholesterol, age plus cholesterol plus smoking—and for the coin the most specific class containing this next flip is the class consisting of just this flip, for which no useful rate exists.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep the assumption that there is a single true answer to my personal heart-attack risk; but if that forces me to say the coin's 50 percent is a different kind of claim, I want to understand exactly what the difference is and why it is not special pleading.
