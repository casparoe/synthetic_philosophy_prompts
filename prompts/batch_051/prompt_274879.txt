I am preparing a teaching unit on the ethics of migration—open borders, integration, and the welfare state—and I want to use Neill Blomkamp's film District 9 as the central case, treating it as a work that can complicate the theory rather than merely illustrate it. The film strands a population of insect-like aliens in Johannesburg, confines them for two decades to a militarized slum, and then attempts a forced relocation, while the surrounding society responds with revulsion, exploitation, and fears about crime, disease, and resource strain. Against this, set the philosophical debate: the cosmopolitan case that persons should be free to move and settle and that coercively excluding them stands in need of justification; the communitarian or membership-based reply that a polity may control admission because shared identity and trust underwrite redistribution; and the narrower welfare-state objection that open movement would strain public services and the solidarity that funds them.

Read the film through these positions, in both directions. Where does the film give the welfare-state objection genuine dramatic force—for instance, in how the authorities justify relocation on public-health and public-order grounds—and where does it expose such justifications as rationalizations for cruelty or for economic exploitation? What does the arc of the protagonist Wikus van de Merwe, from bureaucrat enforcing eviction to a fugitive who is himself becoming alien, show about whether equal concern requires becoming one of the excluded, and is that the film's strength or a moral-psychological mistake? Does the film portray the aliens' failure to integrate as a cause or a consequence of their exclusion, and what follows for the position that a state may restrict entry because integration has failed or would fail? Finally, identify where the film's central metaphor—biologically distinct aliens standing in for human migrants—ceases to track the actual debate, and say what the film nonetheless adds to the philosophical arguments that abstraction alone would miss.
