Aldo Leopold's land ethic judges actions by whether they preserve the health, stability, and self-renewing character of the entire land community—soils, waters, plants, and animals—rather than by human convenience alone. Predict what this ethic, applied consistently, would say about two conservation proposals that became technically plausible only long after Leopold's death.

Proposal 1: Release, on an otherwise isolated oceanic island, a self-sustaining CRISPR gene drive—an engineered genetic element that biases its own inheritance so that a damaging trait spreads through a wild population—to render an invasive rat population infertile and eventually eliminate it locally, allowing a native seabird colony to recover from nest predation.

Proposal 2: Bring back the passenger pigeon, extinct since the early twentieth century, by reconstructing its genome from museum specimens and using a closely related living pigeon as a surrogate, with the eventual aim of releasing a self-sustaining flock into eastern North American forests.

Would the local extinction of an introduced species count as repairing the land community or as yet another act of human mastery over it? Would a genetically engineered approximation of a lost native species count as restoring the community's wholeness or as imposing a human artifact on it? Identify the principle or principles internal to the land ethic that would make the two cases come out the same or differently, and state where the ethic is indeterminate between them.
