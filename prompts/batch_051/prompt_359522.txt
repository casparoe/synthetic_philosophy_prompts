Please give me your credence that the concept of complexity at work when we call a bacterium or a jet engine complex—organized, many-leveled, produced only by a long causal history of selection or design—is closer to assembly than to the two more familiar measures. By assembly I mean the minimal number of joining steps needed to build the object from a fixed stock of elementary parts, where every already-built subassembly can be reused as a single unit; by the alternatives I mean Shannon entropy, a property of a probability distribution that is high for disorder, and Kolmogorov complexity, the length of the shortest program that prints the object, which can be small even for the first million digits of pi. Treat the claim comparatively: across cases whose causal history we already know, assembly better separates objects produced by selection or design from objects produced by chance than either alternative does. I want a number plus the reasoning that produces it, decomposed. Assign a rough probability and a one-line justification to at least these sub-questions: whether complexity in this sense is one objective feature or a family resemblance across physics, biology, and artifacts; whether the reuse of parts captures what entropy and Kolmogorov complexity both miss; whether the assembly measure is stable under different choices of elementary parts, allowed operations, and background environment; and whether a copy-number condition must be added for the measure to distinguish designed objects from one-off accidents. Then say how the terms combine to yield your overall credence, identify which term the final number is most sensitive to, and name the single observation or argument that would move it most. Answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: the overall credence; the decomposed terms with their probabilities and justifications; how they combine; the term you are least confident in; and the evidence that would most shift the number.
