As a professor working in the philosophy of law, I have been trying to teach my students a precise test for the absurdity doctrine, and I find my own formulations keep collapsing under the cases. I would like you to propose a working definition of "absurd result" — the kind of result that licenses a court to depart from a statute's plain text — that gets the following cases right. First, a criminal statute is enacted with a drafting error: the text criminalizes knowingly making a true statement of material fact, while the title, committee report, and every surrounding provision make clear the prohibition was meant to cover false statements. I take it a court may correct the text here without offending the doctrine. Second, a tax provision allows a credit to any taxpayer whose income exceeds fifty thousand dollars as part of a package explicitly titled the Low-Income Relief Act, so that literal application would give the credit only to those above the threshold and deny it to the poor. Third, a statute prohibits any person from carrying a firearm inside a federal building, and a uniformed federal officer assigned to guard that building carries her service weapon while on duty. Fourth, a statute requires that a will be witnessed by two persons, one of whom is also a named beneficiary; the literal text does not say the bequest fails, though a long background rule outside the statute would disqualify the beneficiary as a witness. Fifth, a statute grants courts jurisdiction over claims arising under federal law, and a plaintiff sues under state contract law but mentions a federal statute incidentally in the complaint. Sixth, a filing deadline runs within thirty days after the injury, and the injured person is comatose for the entire thirty-day period. I want a definition that classifies the first as correctable, the fifth as not correctable, and gives defensible answers, one way or the other, for the middle cases. The definition should be a test a judge could actually apply, not merely a list of examples. Please run it through all six cases, say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. I suspect the doctrine may actually be doing two different jobs, one about correcting legislative drafting mistakes and one about avoiding results no reasonable legislator could have intended, and I would like to know whether your definition treats those as one idea or two. I would also like you to tell me where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and which case or consideration would most likely change your mind. Please write the whole thing in continuous prose, as you would in a letter or an email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
