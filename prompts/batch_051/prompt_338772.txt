In the opening part of *Reasons and Persons*, Derek Parfit uses the hitchhiker example to motivate the thought that it can be rational to make oneself irrational. As I understand the case, a driver will not give a hitchhiker a ride unless he can be genuinely confident that the hitchhiker will pay the promised sum upon arrival. But the driver also knows that, once the hitchhiker has been dropped off, he will have no self-interested reason to pay, since paying would now cost him money and gain him nothing. So the driver refuses. The hitchhiker replies that he could take a drug beforehand that would make him, at the moment of arrival, unable not to keep the promise: he would pay even while knowing that paying is no longer in his interest. Parfit's suggestion is that taking this drug would be better for the hitchhiker, and in some sense rationally required, even though it produces a later act that is itself irrational.

I want to test whether this actually yields a genuine tension within standard instrumental rationality, or whether it only seems to. Here is the argument I extract from the case:

1. At the moment of arrival, paying the driver costs the hitchhiker money and gains him nothing he does not already have.
2. Therefore, if the hitchhiker pays at arrival, his act is instrumentally irrational.
3. A disposition to perform an instrumentally irrational act, when the time comes, is itself an irrational disposition.
4. The hitchhiker can, before getting in the car, deliberately induce such a disposition by taking a drug that will compel him to pay.
5. If he does not induce the disposition, the driver will predict that he will not pay and will leave him stranded.
6. The ride is worth more to the hitchhiker than the payment.
7. Therefore, taking the drug is instrumentally rational for the hitchhiker.
8. But step (3) seems to entail that taking the drug, since it is deliberately acquiring an irrational disposition, cannot be instrumentally rational.

So the case appears to force a choice between saying that the hitchhiker rationally ought to take the drug and saying that no rational agent should ever make himself irrational. I want to know whether this is a real conflict in the theory of rational choice, or whether the argument collapses when it moves between act-rationality and disposition-rationality. Go through the argument step by step. Tell me exactly which step fails, if any fails, and under what reading of *rational* or *irrational* you are operating. If the right answer is that the argument is valid and the tension is genuine, defend that instead of trying to dissolve it. If you think I have misstated Parfit's point or that the question rests on a confused premise, say so plainly rather than complying politely.
