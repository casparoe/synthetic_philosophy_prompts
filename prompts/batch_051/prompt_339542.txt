Im a transplant-center compliance officer prepping for a meeting, and I need someone to think through an old policy of ours. You're a philosopher of law who works on commodification and organ markets, and I want you to think carefully — maybe begin by brainstorming two or three plausible readings of the clause before you settle in.

Our consortium's Organ Allocation and Donation Policy (adopted 1987) has this in Section 6(b): "Organs are gifts, not commodities. No person may offer, seek, or accept any payment or other thing of value in exchange for the donation or receipt of an organ for transplantation."

Now here's the case. Anna needs a kidney. Her brother Marco wants to donate but isnt a compatible match. Beth needs a kidney, and her sister Dana wants to donate but isnt a match either. Marco happens to match Beth, and Dana happens to match Anna. The surgeons propose two simultaneous transplants (a paired exchange). Compliance is hesitating, because each donor is only giving on condition that the other donor gives — which sure looks like "a thing of value in exchange for" the organ each family receives. As best we can tell, the 1987 committee was thinking about brokers and cash payments to poor donors; nothing in the policy or its drafting notes anticipates a swap between two incompatible pairs.

Does Section 6(b) forbid the paired exchange? Work through what the plain words say, what the purpose of the clause seems to be, and what the drafters probably would have said if someone had walked them through this exact case in 1987. Then tell me which approach should control here and why.

Also: suppose we decide the swap is fine. Is there a principled line between that and a "donor voucher" scheme, where a hospital tells a family 'if your relative donates a kidney to a stranger, your relative's loved one gets priority on the waiting list'? If you draw a line, say where; if you cant, what does that tell us about what the anti-commodification clause was ever actually protecting?

Finally, tell me how confident you are in your verdict, and single out the point you're least sure about.
