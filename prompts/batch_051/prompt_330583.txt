I report on science and ideas, and I've been reading some Husserl on the side for a piece I keep putting off about how we cover contested claims. I can't stop thinking about his epoché and the reduction behind it. As I understand them: the epoché is the move where you set aside the natural attitude, the standing assumption that the world and its objects are just there independent of my take on them, and the reduction is the shift of attention toward how things actually present themselves in experience, without affirming or denying that they're real. In my case, that would mean attending to what actually shows up in my notebook and recordings, taken as showing up, rather than to the world I assume produced it.

Here's my work situation. I write one or two pieces a week, usually with a day or two of reporting and two or three hours to draft. My editor signs off but won't redo interviews or read the underlying papers, so I'm the only one who knows what was actually in the room. By the time I write, my assumptions about the topic have fused with what I observed, and I can't tell which sentances in my notes are reports and which are my gloss. The stakes are credibility, not defamation: I keep writing the story my prior already believed.

So give me a reusable procedure — not an essay about Husserl — based on what the epoché actually says, or the version that survives contact with a newsroom. I need to run it in under an hour before I draft. Steps in order: what to bracket and in what order; what counts as having bracketed a assumption rather than just saying "maybe"; how to handle the fact that the real epoché is supposed to be global, while I can only bracket one thing at a time; and where I have to switch the natural attitude back on to make the piece publishable. Be honest about which steps are real checks and which are just attitude adjustments wearing a procedure costume, and flag the cases it can't settle.

Then run it on two or three of these, including at least one where it's going to tell me something I don't want to hear:

- a thirty-person study claiming timed fasting lowers inflammation markers, funded by a company that sells fasting apps;
- a patient-advocacy group insisting a cluster of long-haul symptoms is a distinct post-viral syndrome, while two clinicians I interviewed say "syndrome" is too strong for now;
- my own draft lede, which says researchers have been "unaccountably slow to accept" a large meta-analysis that found a benefit, when what I actually have in my notebook is the meta-analysis and three conversations with skeptical researchers.
