During my career as a research scientist, I was trained to treat reproducibility of the reasoning as nearly equivalent to credibility of the result. In contemporary machine learning those two have come apart, and the disagreements about whether that matters look to me like a conceptual dispute rather than a settled empirical question. I would like you to write a decision tree, for a discussion group of scientifically literate people without philosophical training, that sorts respondents by what they actually think about one question: when an AI system's outputs are more accurate than any humanly interpretable alternative but its reasoning cannot be reconstructed by a person, is the gain in accuracy worth the loss of legibility?

Please construct the tree from the logical space of commitments, not from a list of authors or familiar labels. Each branch node should be a concrete case or a plain-language claim, phrased so that someone can answer it without knowing which position it leads to. Aim for roughly eight leaves, and build the branching around the following distinctions:

(a) whether legibility is a component of being correct in a consequential predictive task, or a separate virtue that can be traded off against accuracy;
(b) whether legibility is valuable only as an instrument, for audit, correction, recourse, or public trust, or also for its own sake;
(c) when an explanation is required, whether it must be faithful to the actual computation or whether a reliable and useful post hoc account is enough;
(d) whether the burden is on the model to be interpretable or on the surrounding institution and process to make outcomes auditable and contestable.

The leaves should include at least the following positions, with names supplied by you:

- no model may be deployed in a high-stakes setting unless its output can be traced through humanly understandable steps;
- legibility is only a tool, worth having exactly when it buys safety, auditability, or recourse;
- measured accuracy is the chief end and opacity is acceptable wherever black-box systems win;
- the correct resolution depends on the kind of decision, the stakes, and whether decisions can be contested;
- training models to be legible tends to improve generalization and robustness, so the conflict is mostly a short-run artefact;
- the proper unit of legibility is the audit process as a whole, not the inner workings of any single model.

Use at least three concrete scenarios as branch nodes. One should be a high-stakes medical allocation case in which the best predictive model is illegible. One should involve generated explanations that are fluent but not guaranteed to be faithful. One should involve a system that beats an interpretable baseline overall but makes a few severe, unexplained individual errors. For each major split, state what claim the question is testing. Mark any positions the tree cannot separate, and note unoccupied combinations of answers, with a sentence on why each is empty, whether because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely underexplored. Please provide no bibliography, citations, or reading list; the piece should be self-contained.
