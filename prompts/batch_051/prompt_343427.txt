A few of us have been trying to reverse-engineer how a small constitutional court decides which international arrangements count as transferring enough sovereignty that they need special domestic approval, and we've got its last five rulings listed out below. The same court has repeatedly said it isn't applying a fixed checklist, but it also claims to be consistent. Our records:

- A customs union with a common external tariff but no free movement of persons: no transfer of sovereignty; a member could exit by ordinary statute.
- A confederation with a joint army and a shared court for disputes between states: some sovereignty transferred; entry required a two-thirds vote in the national parliament but not a referendum.
- A monetary union with a shared central bank and no common fiscal capacity: partial pooling of sovereignty, but not enough to require a referendum.
- A standards agreement with a joint regulator whose rules are automatically binding on members: sovereignty transferred; entry required a referendum because the state lost its veto.
- A mutual defense pact with an integrated command structure that any member may leave at any time: no referendum required, since the command can be withdrawn.

Next week the court is deciding whether a proposed passport union requires a referendum or just parliamentary approval. The passport union would have a common visa list, a shared border force, a joint appellate body over migration decisions, and a provision that its rules bind all members unless a two-thirds majority of member states votes to opt out within six months of a rule's adoption. It does not create free movement of persons inside the union and any member may withdraw from the whole arrangement with two years' notice.

How should the court rule if it wants to be consistent with its own precedents? Which of the five cases is the closest analog, and what principle is the court adopting either way if the record underdetermines the new case? We're less interested in the actual outcome than in the candidate principles that fit the five old rulings and which additional case would most cleanly distinguish them.
