I retired from a career in the natural sciences and have been working through Aristotle’s *Nicomachean Ethics* slowly, with a reading group of other nonspecialists. Book VII has been bothering me. Near the start of the discussion of self-restraint and unrestraint, Aristotle reports as a reputable opinion that the temperate person is always self-restrained and enduring. A few lines later, however, while setting out the difficulties, he says: “if Self-restraint implies having strong and evil desires, the temperate man cannot be self-restrained, nor the self-restrained man temperate; for the temperate man does not have excessive or evil desires.” These two claims look as though they cannot both be true: either temperance meets the definition of self-restraint, or it does not. In my old line of work, an apparent contradiction like this usually meant a variable had been left unmeasured, and I suspect something similar is happening here, but I have not been able to state the hidden variable cleanly.

I am not asking for a general summary of Aristotle on temperance and continence. I want a rigorous reconciliation: what distinction is Aristotle drawing, or should be drawn, between having bad appetites and mastering them, and not having those appetites in the first place? Is the first passage simply a popular view that he goes on to replace, or does his final position preserve part of it? And is the resulting account a substantive psychological claim about self-control, or a stipulative narrowing of the concept to protect the superiority of temperance? Please set out the main candidate reconciliations, say which one you find most honest and which strike you as special pleading, and then end by telling me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration about appetite, character, or Aristotle’s terms would lead you to a different verdict, and where your own reconstruction is most likely to fail.
