I teach social epistemology, and I've sat through enough curriculum-committee fights about "viewpoint diversity" that I've started thinking about platforms as epistemic furniture rather than debating-club policy. I keep returning to the distinction between an epistemic bubble — where you simply never encounter the outgroup's best case because of how information is filtered — and an echo chamber, where you do encounter it but only after its sources have been preemptively discredited. Here's a mechanism a colleague floated for our campus discussion forum, and I can't tell whether its clever or naive. Every time someone posts on a flagged controversial topic, they must first write a two-sentence steelperson of the position they most reject. That steelperson is then scored 1–5 for faithfulness by a rotating panel of people who actually hold the rejected position, drawn from outside the author's usual network. If the score is below 3, the post still appears but with a banner saying the author could not restate the opposing view to the opposing side's satisfaction, and its default reach is halved; if the score is 4.5 or above, the post gets a cross-cutting boost and is shown preferentially to people who disagree with the author. Treat this as a mechanism, not as a moral proposal. I want you to walk through what this does to posting behavior, to who accumulates reach, to the kinds of arguments that get selected for, and to the composition of the forum over time. In particular, does it pop epistemic bubbles, soften echo chambers, both, or neither? Where does it get gamed first, and are the failure modes fixable without gutting the scheme? Act as an expert in social epistemology and mechanism design. Before you commit to a verdict, brainstorm at least three ways a rational user who doesn't care about truth could adapt to the scheme, and then analyze each.
