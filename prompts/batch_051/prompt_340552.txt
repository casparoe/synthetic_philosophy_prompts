I have a standing rule for attributing felt states — pain, fear, pleasure, anything it is like something to undergo — to nonhuman animals. I refuse to count an animal as feeling anything unless its behavior clears a checklist I settled on in advance: it must have the sort of nervous system that is associated with pain in cases I already accept; the response must not be fully explainable as reflex or tropism; and, where possible, the animal should act as though the outcome matters to it, for example by learning to avoid a stimulus. I apply the rule hardest to the animals I am told are simple: fish, insects, crustaceans. When I see a fish thrash on a line or a crab scald, I notice myself flinch, but I treat the flinch as poor evidence. I have seen wind-up toys and climbing plants do things that look like struggle, and I know how readily I project. My friends say the checklist is rigged: I have set the bar where it least disturbs my diet, and I would never ask this much of a human infant before crediting it with pain. But the alternative seems to be letting my intuitions set the boundary, one flinch at a time, with no principle at all. Is my rule a defensible epistemic policy rather than motivated skepticism? Where does a rule of this kind most plausibly fail? And is there a principled restatement of it that keeps the discipline against anthropomorphism without, in effect, deciding the question in advance?
