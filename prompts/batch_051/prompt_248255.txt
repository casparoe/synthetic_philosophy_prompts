Next month I'm presenting a working paper to a mixed seminar at a policy research institute, and I'd like a Q&A prep sheet rather than a polished defense. My thesis is that the default release posture for frontier AI systems should be closed: for models at or near the current capability frontier, the burden of proof should rest on the releaser to show that open release won't meaningfully lower the cost of catastrophic misuse, rather than on everyone else to show that it will. My argument runs roughly as follows. The most serious risks from frontier models — helping nonexperts design biological or chemical agents, or sharply lowering the skill needed for offensive cyber operations — depend on capabilities that are hard to verify at release time; once weights are public the release is irreversible, so the error is one-sided; and most of the claimed benefits of open release, such as external evaluation, competition, and downstream innovation, can be had through staged access, structured third-party evaluations, and API-based research access without publishing the weights. So the asymmetry of irreversible downside against largely replaceable upside justifies closed-by-default above a capability threshold, even if that threshold is admittedly fuzzy and can only be specified operationally rather than by a crisp metric.

Here's the room. It's not a monolithic pro-open-source crowd, but it's not on my side either: two ML engineers who have shipped open-weight models and believe openness is how the field stays honest; a security researcher who thinks the real action is in the misuse of existing open models and that I'm solving last year's problem; two AI governance researchers broadly sympathetic to my conclusion but worried the argument proves too much, or collapses into a moat for a few large labs; and an economist who will press on whether closed-by-default just entrenches incumbents and slows the diffusion that produces safety-relevant expertise. Please give me the objections I'll actually get from this room, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage each can do, phrased the way these people would actually put them. For each, give me a two-minute reply and a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and tell me what I should concede up front so the room doesn't unite against me. Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence — the single most important strategic takeaway for how I should handle the Q&A — and then give the prep sheet, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
