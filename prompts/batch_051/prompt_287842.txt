I'm a working theoretical physicist, and I keep a running list of commitments about the role of elegance in science and mathematics. Before presenting it to a philosophy of science reading group, I'd like an audit of whether the set hangs together. Here it is:

(a) The historical record shows a robust enough correlation between elegance—symmetry, simplicity, unification—and later empirical success in fundamental physics that elegance can legitimately justify devoting years, money, and attention to a theory before it has made a distinctive novel prediction.

(b) But elegance should carry almost no weight in deciding whether to believe a theory that, even in principle, makes no new predictions distinguishing it from its rivals.

(c) In pure mathematics, elegance in a definition or proof is a reliable guide to importance and to what will generalize, but it is not evidence for the truth of an unproven conjecture.

(d) The historical correlation between elegance and success is plausibly explained by selection effects: inelegant theories are rarely developed far enough to be tested, and failed elegant theories are forgotten.

(e) Still, when a new equation or symmetry is strikingly simple and strikes me as right, I take that to be a genuine epistemic reason to expect it to survive, not merely an aesthetic preference or a research-prioritization heuristic.

Which of these are in genuine tension, which only look like they are, and what is the cheapest revision that restores the most coherence? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and for each say exactly what would have to give. Then tell me how confident you are in the audit and which single point you are least sure of.
