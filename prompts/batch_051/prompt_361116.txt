Back when I was in the lab I use to think game theory was just for economists, but now I run a little reading group on decision theory for science grad students with a philosophy itch — maybe a dozen of us, 70 minutes a week. We've done ordinary Nash equilibrium and I want one session that makes them feel why program equilibrium is a genuinely different idea and not just a formalism. The bit that grabs me is that if players can submit programs instead of one-off actions, and the programs can read each other, then mutual cooperation in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma can be an equilibrium, whereas in the usual game cooperating is just a dominated move. But I don't want to lecture that at them; I want them to run into it. Design an in-class game or activity around this. It should probably have two rounds: one where they choose actions the usual way, and one where they write little programs or conditional strategies ahead of time — maybe on cards — and the facilitator runs them against each other. Give me exact rules, a materials list, a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money, a timeline for roughly 70 minutes, a debrief that maps what happened onto the formal difference between Nash and program equilibrium, and an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and which feature of the idea the activity can't reproduce so I'll have to supply it out loud. By the end I want them to see that being able to commit to a program changes what counts as a rational response — not just that it's a cheap trick.
