Two mates of mine got into it at the pub the other night, and I'm still turning it over. A friend of ours, Sam, is moving to New York for a year and had asked whether she ought to tip, queue and cross roads 'the local way'. Hester said yes, roughly: these aren't just manners. In New York, tips are counted as part of a server's pay, so not tipping doesn't merely violate a custom, it leaves someone worse off against a background of reasonable reliance. Queue-jumping is unfair wherever queuing is the settled practice that everyone else is following. And jaywalking in a city where drivers aren't expecting it is dangerous, not a harmless personal choice. So a visitor can't just import her own habits and call it a clear conscience.

Marcus said that's too fast. Tipping, queuing and jaywalking are conventions, not moral duties in themselves, because the very same action is fine in one place and wrong in another depending on custom. So what actually does the work is the underlying values: exploitation, fairness, expectation, risk. A visitor should ask directly whether her action exploits anyone, treats anyone unfairly or endangers anyone, and then decide. She doesn't owe some extra duty of deference to localness as such.

Hester replied that he'd just shown why localness matters rather than why it doesn't. What others can reasonably expect of you, how they rely on you, and what your action signals all vary with local practice. If you don't tip in New York, the harm isn't fixed by your private intentions; it's fixed by the practice as it actually operates. You can't declare your tips optional and thereby change what the convention means to the server.

Marcus said that's partly right, but now she needs a rule for why some local expectations bind and others don't. Tourists are expected to pay bribes in some places, or to accept queue-jumping, or to join customs that are plainly unfair; no one thinks 'when in Rome' settles those. So an independent test is needed. Hester answered that not every local expectation is binding, only those that already track fairness, welfare or legitimate reliance. But then, Marcus said, the visitor is not really obeying the convention; she's applying the underlying values, and the localness adds nothing. Hester said it adds the facts: localness changes what fairness, welfare and reasonable reliance amount to in the situation. A universal rule like 'tip no one' or 'queue everywhere' can't work, because the same values point different ways in different places, and a visitor needs local knowledge to apply them. Marcus then asked whether a local can criticise a foreigner who did the calculation wrong but meant well. Hester said yes, within limits, because part of entering a practice is having a duty to find out how it works, and some ignorance of local conventions is itself culpable. Marcus said that's only a duty to be careful, not a duty to follow a rule as a rule.

By then I'd been asked to referee and I genuinely couldn't. Could you go through their exchange and say who's right about which point, and what both of them are missing?
