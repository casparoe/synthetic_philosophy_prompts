After a career in the physical sciences, I have recently begun reading Hume in earnest, and I am trying to get clear about the logical force of his discussion of miracles in the Enquiry. My working understanding, which I suspect may be wrong, is this: Hume's maxim states that testimony to a miracle is rationally credible only when it would be a greater miracle for the testimony to be false than for the reported event to have occurred. Translating this into Bayesian terms, it seems to me that an agent who assigns any nonzero prior probability to a miracle could always in principle be moved by a sufficiently large and independent body of testimony, since each new witness should push the posterior upward. So if Hume's argument is meant to show that no possible accumulation of testimony could ever justify belief in a miracle, he must have been tacitly assuming that the prior probability of a miracle is exactly zero. If that is right, the argument appears to beg the question by ruling miracles out in advance; if it is not right, I do not see how the maxim does more than delay belief rather than forbid it. Which is it? I would be grateful for a precise account of where this Bayesian reconstruction goes wrong, and exactly what the maxim does and does not license. Please also say how confident you are in your reading and flag the point you are least sure about.
