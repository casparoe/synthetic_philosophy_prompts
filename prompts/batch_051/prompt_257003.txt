I teach a course on the metaphysics and ethics of virtual worlds, and I am preparing a session on a question my students keep coming back to: whether the objects, relationships, and harms inside a virtual environment are real, or only real in some diminished or second-order way. I plan to screen the Black Mirror episode "San Junipero" as the central case—the one in which a shy woman and a dying woman meet in a simulated 1980s beach town that turns out to be a system the elderly can visit while alive and then inhabit permanently after death, and which ends by cutting from the sunlit town to the physical server facility that houses the uploaded minds, so that the two women appear, on one reading, to be reduced to rows of hardware. I want a philosophical reading of the episode, not a plot summary.

Here is the tension I want the reading to work through. On one interpretation, the episode is a defense of the full reality and value of virtual goods: the romance, the friendships, the second chance at a life, and the marriage are presented as genuinely meaningful, and their computational substrate is treated as morally irrelevant. On another interpretation, the closing image of the server room quietly deflates that romance—what persists is not the town or the persons but a set of states in a machine, and the apparent virtual objects are nothing over and above data and hardware. Which interpretation does the episode's own storytelling most support, and where do particular scenes or decisions pull in the other direction? I would like you to be specific: how does the dying woman's initial refusal to stay—because her husband and daughter are absent from the simulation, and she does not want a virtual companionship to substitute for them—bear on the question? Then take the ethics a step further: if someone inside San Junipero were deceived, manipulated, or made to suffer in the way an embodied person can be, would the episode's implicit metaphysics count that harm as real, and real in the same sense? End by saying what the episode adds to this philosophical debate that bare argument does not, and where an honest critic should concede that the episode stacks the deck in favor of one answer.
