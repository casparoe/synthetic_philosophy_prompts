William James, in "The Dilemma of Determinism" (1884), writes:

"If a certain formula for expressing the nature of the world violates my moral demand, I shall feel as free to throw it overboard, or at least to doubt it, as if it disappointed my demand for uniformity of sequence, for example; the one demand being, so far as I can see, quite as subjective and emotional as the other is."

James here is trying to make room for genuine chance by denying that the demand for deterministic uniformity has any special objectivity. Now take a standard Frankfurt-style intervention case: Black wants Jones to decide A, and Black can detect a prior sign in Jones's brain; if the sign indicates Jones is about to decide B, Black will intervene and cause Jones to decide A; if the sign indicates A, Black does nothing. In the actual sequence, the sign indicates A, Black does not intervene, and Jones decides A on his own. The usual conclusion is that Jones is morally responsible for A even though he could not have done otherwise. Vary the case as follows: the prior sign Black detects is a genuinely indeterministic quantum event in Jones's motor cortex—say, whether a particular ion channel opens at the relevant moment. If the channel opens, Jones will decide A on his own; if it does not open, Jones would decide B and Black would intervene. In the actual sequence the channel opens and Jones decides A. Work through what the introduction of Jamesian chance at the level of the sign does to the Frankfurt-style argument. Does it restore an alternative possibility of the kind the principle of alternative possibilities requires, or does it show that "could have done otherwise" was not doing the relevant work in the original case? More specifically, distinguish the modal status of the sign, the decision, and Black's intervention, and say whether the chance James wants to defend is the kind that could ground moral responsibility or merely an irrelevant piece of microphysical noise.
