I'm a software engineer building a small tool for aggregating people's prefrences into a group ranking, and I fell down a rabbit hole on Sen's liberal paradox. The way I understand it: you want to turn individual preference orderings into a social ordering, you insist on weak Pareto (if everyone prefers x to y, the group prefers x to y), and you also insist on a minimal 'liberalism' condition (each of at least two people gets to be decisive over some pair of alternatives that concerns only their own private business). Sen's result is that no acyclic social ordering can satisfy both once you allow 'meddlesome' preferences, where people care about what others do in their private choices. The standard patches I've already seen are: (1) weaken or drop Pareto so individual rights trump it; (2) treat rights as constraints on which options can even be put to a social vote, rather than as conditions on the social ranking itself; (3) rule out meddlesome preference profiles from the domain; (4) redraw the alternatives at a finer grain so a personal choice and the Pareto conflict don't get represented as the same pair. Give me one more way out that is not just a rewording, combination, or special case of one of those four. Then tell me straight whether it actually holds up, and what it costs in a real group-decision setting. And please write the whole answer in British English.
