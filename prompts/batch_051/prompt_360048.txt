I have been trying to think about deplatforming, firings that follow online pressure campaigns, and other informal social sanctions through the philosophy of punishment, and I find myself caught between a theory I am drawn to and an intuition I cannot shake. The theory is an expressive account of punishment extended to informal sanctions. On this view, punishment — whether a court fine, a dismissal, or a platform ban — is fundamentally one kind of thing: the communication of censure for violating a norm, justified when the norm is itself justified, the attribution of the violation is accurate, the severity is proportionate, and the target retains some channel through which to answer the charge. The view has real systematic virtues. It refuses to treat state punishment as a mysteriously special case; it presses the same questions across the whole continuum from legal sentence to social ostracism; and it yields a tidy decision procedure: for any given sanction, ask whether the censure is warranted and the response calibrated.

Yet I also have a firm intuition that something qualitatively different occurs when a person loses a livelihood or a public platform because a crowd has assembled around an old remark stripped of its context. In such cases no one is obligated to test the evidence or hear the accused; there is no assurance that the powerful will be spared while the accidental target is made an example; and the sanctioners themselves bear no reciprocal accountability, since each contribution is small and anonymous even though the accumulated damage is enormous and precise. It feels wrong to call that a defective or hasty punishment. It feels more like banishment, or scapegoating, or collective self-defense run amok; the procedures seem not like optional add-ons but like part of what distinguishes punishment from mere social force. I can see three ways to go. I could debunk the intuition as proceduralist romanticism, on the grounds that informal sanctions fill accountability gaps the state structurally cannot. I could amend the theory by adding a principled institutional-procedure requirement, restoring a bright line at the cost of the unified criterion. Or I could seek a compatibility reading on which social sanction and punishment are neighboring but distinct normative kinds, with the theory explaining the genus and the intuition tracking a species difference.
