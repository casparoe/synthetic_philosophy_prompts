I head the legal service of a national parliament. Several times a year, my office is asked by the Speaker what should happen when the written rules run out. Recent examples include sitting days where the Speaker and Deputy Speaker have both died, a vacant presidency with no named caretaker, and a demand to postpone elections because the electoral roll has been destroyed. Each time we improvise; each improvisation is later cited as a precedent; and I have come to believe we are quietly writing constitutional law without anyone noticing.

My working view, assembled from constitutional and political theory and not asserted as settled law, is what I call the repair-not-redraw rule: when the rules run out, a gap authorizes at most the least disruptive provisional measure needed to keep the constituted bodies able to act. A durable settlement of the gap must be routed back through the ordinary amendment track or, in the most extreme case, an explicit act by the people. Using a gap to create a lasting rule is governing the gap rather than surviving it. In practice I treat a measure as a redraw, not a repair, if it changes who holds power, for how long, or under what limits.

Please turn this into a decision procedure my office can actually run. Our constraints are these. We often have between two and six hours. My colleagues are capable legal drafters but not philosophers or constitutional theorists. The Speaker signs our advice, but the House, and later the courts, tend to treat what we advise as law. The procedure must tell us what to do when the least disruptive repair is itself not lawful under any reading of the rules, and it must mark every point where judgment cannot be eliminated.

Use exactly these sections, in this order, with roughly the indicated lengths.

1. Summary — three or four sentences stating the bottom line of the procedure. (about 10%)
2. Definitions and tests — what counts as a gap; at least distinguish procedural, competence, and succession gaps; and give operational tests for the key terms: least disruptive, provisional, and durable. (about 20%)
3. The decision procedure — numbered questions in the order we should ask them. For each question, say what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a no, and where the procedure hands the decision to a person or body instead of pretending that a fact-finding step settled it. (about 35%)
4. Worked applications — apply the procedure to these three cases, taking them in order, and say plainly where the procedure blocks something my office would prefer to approve: (a) both the Speaker and Deputy Speaker die the week before the budget sitting, the standing orders say only that the Speaker presides, and tomorrow the House must elect a new Speaker with no rule saying who presides over that election; (b) the President resigns, the constitution is silent on who acts in the meantime, and the outgoing President's chief of staff argues that necessity authorizes the Cabinet to appoint an acting President for the rest of the term; (c) a cyberattack destroys the electoral roll three weeks before the constitutional deadline for elections, and the government asks the Speaker to certify an emergency decree postponing the elections and extending parliament's term for two years on the ground that the systems must first be restored. (about 25%)
5. Limits and misfires — where the procedure will go wrong, including the risk that a seizure of power gets blessed as a mere repair, and what the procedure deliberately does not decide. (about 10%)
