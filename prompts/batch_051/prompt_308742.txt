I've been studying the universalizability principle: roughly, that an action is permissible only if one could will that everyone act in that way. I recognize this principle in Kant's categorical imperative, and I've read that something close to it is central both to R. M. Hare's universal prescriptivism and to Peter Singer's argument that we ought to donate far more to famine relief than we do. But the principle seems to produce absurd results. Suppose I decide to become a prosthetist because I have the ability and find the work meaningful. If I universalize that maxim, I get a world in which everyone becomes a prosthetist; no one farms or maintains water systems, and the outcome would be catastrophic. So the test says my career choice is wrong, which seems plainly incorrect. Yet universalizing the maxim behind Singer's argument seems to yield exactly his conclusion: if everyone who could gave a substantial share of their income, extreme poverty would be nearly eliminated. Why should I trust the test when it supports Singer's conclusion but condemns an ordinary career choice? Doesn't this show that universalizability reasoning, from Kant through Hare and Singer, proves too much and should be rejected?
