Here's one I keep turning over. Leibniz spent a lot of effort on the idea that reasoning could be made mechanical: he wanted a universal language in which you settle disputes by calculating instead of arguing, and he messed around with binary arithmetic and calculating machines and so on. Now suppose somebody gives him a weekend with a modern laptop and walks him through a language like Haskell or Lisp, slowly enough that he actually gets what a program, a data structure, recursion, and a type are. My question is what he'd make of the claim you often hear from programmers and some philosophers that programming is a language of thought — that coding is just explicit thinking, that the mind is some kind of symbol-manipulator, and that a good programming language is basically thought with the sloppiness cleaned up. I can see one reading where he says yes, obviously, this is what I was after, only with better hardware. But I can also see him objecting that a programming language is a notation for telling a machine what to do, while his universal characteristic was meant to be a notation for concepts and inference — related, but not the same thing, and you could have either without the other. Which way do you think he'd actually break, and how much of it turns on what you mean by language of thought? I'm not after a biography; I want you to reconstruct his likely position from his commitments — where he'd get excited, where he'd hedge, and which versions of the claim he'd endorse versus which he'd call a category mistake.
