I teach Kant's political philosophy, and I'm currently on leave working with a policy institute that screens proposals for new international institutions and security arrangements. The analysts don't have philosophy backgrounds. I want to hand them a Kantian screen — a practical, step-by-step procedure, not a lecture — for deciding whether a proposal advances or undercuts the conditions Kant lays out in Perpetual Peace (1795). I want you to act as the expert on Kant's political philosophy and think carefully before you write the procedure.

The passage I want the whole thing built around is in the Second Section, Third Preliminary Article. To paraphrase: Kant says standing armies should in time be abolished entirely, because they are themselves a permanent threat of war by being constantly ready for it; the cost of maintaining them makes peace more burdensome than even a short war; and they turn soldiers into instruments. I'm going from the idea rather than the exact wording. I want that specific argument operationalized, not just used as a slogan.

So write a screening procedure my analysts can run on a proposal in about 30 minutes. It should: ask questions in order; for each question, say what counts as a good enough answer to move on; where the procedure stops with a no; and where it hands the decision to a person because the answer is a value judgment or Kant's text underdetermines it. The procedure must respect the difference between Kant's preliminary articles (conditions that are stricter, immediate) and the definitive articles (conditions for the final peace, especially republican constitutions, a federation of free states, and cosmopolitan hospitality). But the spine should be the standing-army argument generalized: does this arrangement create a standing threat, or does it reduce the freedom to go to war by making war less likely and less tempting?

Also flag where the procedure will misfire. For example, Kant himself wobbles between saying the proper juridical condition is a world republic and saying we can only have a negative substitute, a league of states that stops short of coercive enforcement. A procedure that ignores that tension will either call every enforceable international regime 'Kantian' or call every one 'un-Kantian', and I want it to do neither.

Run the procedure on three proposals:
(1) a permanent standing UN rapid-response force, paid for by all member states and deployable by Security Council vote, to stop genocide;
(2) a regional human rights court whose rulings are enforceable against member states through trade sanctions;
(3) a mutual defense pact among several constitutional democracies, explicitly aimed at deterring an autocratic neighbor.

At least one of these should come out in a way that would make my own institute's leadership uncomfortable — probably (1) — but the procedure should give that answer honestly if it follows from the principles. Also the procedure should say what it cannot do: it can't tell us whether cosmopolitan right requires open borders, it can't quantify how much of a threat counts as significant, etc.

Make it usable by someone who hasn't read Kant. Use ordinary terms, but keep the distinctions that matter. Don't dumb it down so the standing-army point drops out.

Before you write the procedure, spend a paragraph thinking through the hardest translation problems: what counts as a 'standing army' today, what counts as a 'federation' given the UN, and how to handle Kant's claim that republican governments are less war-prone when actual democracies have fought plenty of wars.

At the end, after the worked applications, give me a one-sentence summary as the last line, starting 'Summary:'.
