Alright, I cover science and ideas for a living, and lately I keep going back and forth between two things that shouldnt have anything to do with each other: Leibniz on the best of all possible worlds, and the claim that Occam's razor can be made mathematically precise with Kolmogorov complexity and Solomonoff induction. The formal story, as I understand it: the best theory of the data is the one with the shortest program, and a rational agent's prior over theories should weight each hypothesis by two to the minus its program length. People defend this on the strength of an invariance result — switching programming languages only changes the complexity by a constant — and then admit, in the next breath, that the whole thing is uncomputable anyway.

Heres my question. Leibniz spent his life trying to turn reasoning into a kind of calculation and he also believed the actual world is the one God picked by balancing simplicity of laws against richness of phenomena, with something like a principle that everything true must have a sufficient reason. So: what would Leibniz have thought of the Solomonoff picture, which came two and a half centuries after him? I want a real prediction, not a shrug. Specifically, get into the weeds on these: would he have accepted that the shortest program is what simplicity means, or would he have said a good explanation also has to be transparent — that a tiny program can hide a lookup table and that this is not the kind of reason God or a scientist should accept? Would the invariance result satisfy his demand that truth not depend on an arbitrary choice of representation, or would he have dismissed the whole framework as language-relative because the constant can be as large as you like? And would he have seen the uncomputability of Kolmogorov complexity as a vindication of his view that perfect reasoning is beyond any finite machine, or as a disappointment for the characteristica universalis he wanted to build? I want your best reconstruction of where he'd land, point by point, including the places where he'd have to quietly abandon one of his own principles to stay on board.
