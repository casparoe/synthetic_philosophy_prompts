I spent forty years in experimental biology, where a sound experiment was one that isolated a single variable so cleanly that a skeptic could not explain the result away. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy, and I keep returning to a debate in the theory of love in which I think that same experimental instinct would be useful. As I understand the lay of the land, there are three main positions. The appraisal view, which I associate with J. David Velleman, holds that love is a response to value the beloved already possesses — centrally, the beloved's rational nature or personhood. The bestowal view, which I associate with Harry Frankfurt, holds that this reverses the true order: the lover's caring does not answer to an independent value in the beloved but rather confers a special importance upon the beloved. The relationship view, which I associate with Niko Kolodny, holds that the reason for love lies in the value of the ongoing relationship one shares with the beloved — a shared history and a present relatedness that cannot be reduced to the beloved's intrinsic properties or to the lover's sheer projection. I am not asking you to adjudicate among these views. I am asking for a controlled experiment.

Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. The scenario should be one paragraph of ordinary life — a recognizable illness, injury, or biographical change, with no duplicates, memory transfer, personality uploads, or any other science-fiction device — and it should contain no philosophical vocabulary; it should simply tell a story. The story should show the lover carrying the same love across the change, not forming a new love for a new person. The case must be one in which the bestowal view and the relationship view agree that this particular continued love is fitting while the appraisal view denies that it is. This is the harder-to-construct direction, since the appraisal and relationship views are both response-to-value views and one might expect them to travel together. By fitting I mean rationally appropriate for the lover, not merely pleasant, useful, or understandable. After presenting the scenario, walk through what each of the three views says about it and why, showing that the verdicts come out exactly as specified. Then say which single feature of the case is doing the work, and whether removing that feature would make the three views converge. End by explaining how a defender of the appraisal view would resist the case. This is for an essay I am sketching on whether the reasons for love are located in the beloved, in the lover, or in the history between them, so the case must generate the verdicts from the theories; a case that builds the desired verdicts into its description, or that stipulates what the beloved really or ultimately is, would be useless to me.
