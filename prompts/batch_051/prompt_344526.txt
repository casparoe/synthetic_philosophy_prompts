I'm a software engineer, and the closest I come to this material in my day job is deciding how long an A/B test should run and whether the p-value on a dashboard is actually telling me anything. Over the past few years I've read a fair amount about the replication crisis in psychology and medicine, and about the recurring argument that p-values and null hypothesis significance testing are themselves part of the problem. I don't have any formal background in philosophy of science, so I want the story told in mechanism-level terms rather than school-of-thought labels.

Here is the counterfactual I keep returning to. Suppose that in 2012 the major empirical psychology journals had all adopted a new default for confirmatory studies. To be published as a discovery, a study would have had to be pre-registered, with a pre-specified Bayesian model using a default or previously justified prior, and the evidence would have had to reach, say, a Bayes factor of at least 10 in favor of the tested effect against a point null. A p-value below .05 would no longer have been sufficient, and the rule would have been enforced rather than eroded through exceptions. Also suppose that within a few years researchers and reviewers had become genuinely fluent in the machinery, so the new rule was being applied with ordinary competence rather than as a box-ticking exercise.

Predict how the next fifteen years would have looked relative to what actually happened: the period in which many prominent findings failed to replicate, large-scale replication projects found that a substantial share of published effects did not hold up at their original sizes, and reforms such as pre-registration and registered reports arrived only slowly and unevenly. I'm less interested in a flat 'replication would have improved' or 'it wouldn't' than in a chain of reasoning about which parts of the problem — publication bias, the file drawer, undisclosed flexibility in analysis, underpowered designs, the broader incentive to find positive results — this policy would actually change, and which it would leave untouched because they are incentives rather than inference methods. In particular: would the researcher degrees of freedom simply migrate to prior selection and model choice, and would that be more or less damaging than the practices it replaced? How much of any improvement would come from the pre-registration requirement rather than from the Bayesian layer, and how much from the shifted publication threshold? Try to assign explicit confidence to each major link and say what would have to fail for the prediction to be wrong.

While you're at it, please separate what is now well established about the replication crisis — the things people who study this would treat as settled — from what is still genuinely contested among statisticians and methodologists, such as whether Bayesian decision rules would fix the problem or merely relabel the same flexibility with priors and models. Don't present a contested position as the field's consensus.
