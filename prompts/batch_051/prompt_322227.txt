I am a community member of the ethics committee at a small residential hospice. We have been asked to rule on a new standing option for patients with terminal diagnoses who are still some weeks or months from death: an "emotional quieting" protocol. Under the protocol, a patient who asks for it would receive a daily combination of low-dose medication and structured routines designed to keep their feelings in a narrow, calm band. It would not be like our usual end-of-life sedation, which leaves a patient asleep and is used only in the very last days; this would keep the patient awake and able to talk, but muted. Panic and despair would be damped, but so would delight, sharp grief at goodbyes, and the urgency that families often describe as the most honest part of those weeks. The effect would be reversible within a few days if the patient changes their mind, and it would be available to any patient who requests it, including those who are not now in severe distress but who fear future panic.

The committee is divided. Some members see the protocol as plain mercy: nobody should have to spend their final weeks in terror, and if a calm, muted mind is what a patient wants, it would be cruel to refuse it because we have romantic ideas about suffering. Others worry that this kind of calm would not relieve suffering so much as remove the person who suffers. They draw a line between equanimity that comes from making peace with one's situation and a flatness that comes from having one's feelings switched off, and they say the second kind of calm lets a patient drift away from the very relationships that make the last weeks worth living. I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have been reading on my own about the old argument over whether freedom from disturbance is the best thing we can hope for, or whether the price of it is the capacity to care. This case feels like that argument with an actual death in the middle of it.

I have been asked to draft the committee's decision memo before our meeting next Thursday. Please write the memo. It should recommend a concrete course of action—approve the protocol, reject it, or approve it with specific conditions—and give the reasons so the committee can actually vote from it. It should include a clear bottom line and, if relevant, a timeframe for review. Above all, it should take seriously the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than merely restating the proposal or the law.
