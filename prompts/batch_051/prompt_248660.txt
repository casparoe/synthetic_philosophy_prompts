Im a junior and our school board is voting next month on whether to cut basically all the money for new fiction in the school library and the one drama class, and move it into a new STEM lab. I signed up for a three-minute public comment slot at the meeting. The room will mostly be board members, some parents, a couple teachers. One board member said at the last meeting that if kids want stories they can stream them at home on their own dime, and that taxpayers shouldnt fund other peoples hobbies. Another one keeps saying show me the test scores.

I dont want to give the standard speech—"reading raises your SAT scores" or "fiction teaches empathy"—partly because I think those arguments fall apart the second the numbers get squishy, and partly because I actually think something different: that a town paying for stories is more like a town paying for parks. The point isnt what the park does to your step count; its that theres this thing everyone in the town can have in common, whether or not they personally use it. Ive been trying to figure out how to say that without sounding like I swallowed a philosophy podcast, and without ever actually saying "public good" or "intrinsic value" out loud.

Write the speech. It has to argue that funding fiction and one drama class is worth it not because it makes us better students or future employees, but because the stories a community decides to keep and pass around are part of what makes it a community at all—and it has to land with people in that room who think thats nonsense. If theres a way to answer the Netflix guy directly in one line, do it, but keep it warm not mean. And make it actually sound like a seventeen year old giving public comment: a little nervous, a little angry, not a lecture.
