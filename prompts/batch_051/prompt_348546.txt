i'm in a philosophy of mind course and we just finished the unit on personal identity, and for our final project a few of us are running a student podcast. Next weeks guest is a philosopher visiting the dept who defends the view that if you gradually replaced every neuron in your brain with functionally equivalent synthetic neurons, you'd survive: there's never a gap, no duplicate, and the causal-functional pattern that is you is preserved. Her stock reply when people bring up teletransportation is to say gradual replacement keeps the stream going, while teletransportation starts a new one.

I need you to write me eight to ten interview questions that don't let her just restate the position. Order them so each answer narrows her room to maneuver, until by the end she has to either bite a bullet or revise something.

Make sure there's at least one question on each of the following, with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give:
- why teletransportation (scan, destroy, rebuild elsewhere) is supposedly different from gradual replacement when both preserve the same functional pattern — if she says it's the gap, ask where the cutoff is (a five second gap? an hour? a copy made while the original is still alive but about to be destroyed?);
- the fading qualia thought experiment: since each synthetic neuron does the same causal work, it would be bizzare if your experience gradually dimmed and vanished, but does that show you'd survive, or only that there'd be a conscious being with your memories at the end? — if she says those are the same thing, ask her how she can tell the difference is empty rather than just invisible from the outside;
- a duplication case where the removed biological neurons are kept alive and reassembled into a second working brain, so by the end there are two candidates — if she bites the bullet and says you'd survive as both, ask her what happens to ordinary ownership, promising, and anticipation (can it be rational for you to fear your own survival?);
- and one question pressing on what the gradualness itself is doing: at the end not one original neuron remains, so why should the slow route matter more than the fast one if what she cares about is the pattern? — if she says the slow route preserves an unbroken stream, ask whether the stream is a thing over and above the pattern, or whether she's quietly giving up patternism.

Also put in a question about whether she's really claiming numerical identity or just that what matters in survival is preserved, and which one her position needs to avoid the teletransportation result. End with a question about what sort of case or evidence would count against the view — not a gotcha, but something she couldn't dismiss as just the biological-continuity intuition wearing a costume.

Make sure the questions sound like a curious but stubborn undergrad, not a cross-examining lawyer.
