I have spent the past year arguing about survival under mind uploading on rationalist forums, and my verdicts on particular cases have drifted into a pattern I cannot articulate—and I am no longer sure they are jointly consistent. I want you to find the principle behind them, or tell me which verdicts have to go. Here are the cases as concretely as I can state them.

1. Destructive scanning. A machine records the full physical state of my brain to whatever precision is needed to run a faithful emulation, destroys the original in the process, and the emulator boots a few hours later with all my memories, dispositions, and quirks. Verdict: this is me; I would step into the scanner.
2. Gradual neuron replacement. Over the course of a year, one neuron at a time is replaced by a functionally identical artificial unit while I remain awake and talk throughout. I feel continuous, and I regard the person at the end as me.
3. Copy-and-keep. The same scan as in (1), but the original body and brain are not destroyed; the emulator boots while I sit up. Verdict: the emulator is not me, even though it remembers being me and will insist it is me.
4. Overlap. While I am under a general anesthetic, I am scanned; the emulator boots and lives a full afternoon; the original is then painlessly destroyed without ever regaining consciousness. Verdict: this feels no better than being killed—even though I said (1) was survival. The timing troubles me more than I can justify.
5. All-at-once replacement. Under a general anesthetic, my entire brain is removed and replaced with a silicon device that implements the same computations, and I wake with my memories intact. Verdict: I do not know what to say. It does not seem obviously worse than (2), but I cannot explain why if (2) turns on gradualness.
6. Hemisphere-by-hemisphere replacement. My left hemisphere is replaced over one month while I remain conscious, then my right; I remember the operations, and the person at the end seems to be me. Verdict: I survive.
7. Multiple emulations. Two emulators boot from a single scan. Verdict: neither is strictly me, or both are equally good successors, but my survival could not branch; in any case I would not value this the way I value my own survival.

I can see pieces of a principle—psychological continuity, a single unbroken stream of consciousness, a no-branching clause, gradualness or spatiotemporal overlap, substrate independence—but case (1) breaks continuity of consciousness, cases (3) and (7) sit badly with any pure pattern view, and case (4) appears to contradict (1). I would like you to state the candidate principles precisely, say which of my verdicts each one must reject or reinterpret, test them against a few fresh cases (a faithful non-biological replacement of only the hippocampus; an upload that runs only while the original sleeps and is halted when the original wakes; a scan-and-beam device that keeps the original alive for five minutes and then destroys it), and tell me which of my verdicts I cannot keep. I care about the forced choices, not about a label.

Structure your reply in exactly these sections, in this order and roughly these lengths: Candidate principles (about 300 words; state three to five principles, each with the case or cases that motivate it); Case-by-case test (about 400 words; run each principle against my seven cases); Forced choices (about 250 words; which verdicts cannot survive together, and why); Fresh cases decided (about 250 words; apply your preferred principle to the three fresh cases); Recommended principle (about 200 words; the principle you would adopt and which of my verdicts it requires abandoning). Please write the answer in British English.
