Two friends in my reading group got into a long argument about the color-phi effect and I had to leave before they finished. Quick background: in the standard setup, a red flash appears at one location, then a green flash appears a moment later at a nearby location, and people report seeing a single red dot glide across and turn green about halfway between the two spots, even though the green light was not yet on when the color change would supposedly have happened.

Sam said the cleanest reading is that the brain edits the experience itself before it ever reaches consciousness, so the person never actually has the red-moving-dot experience; the whole thing, including the early color change, is assembled after both flashes have arrived. Maria said no, the person does consciously see a red dot moving at first, and then the green flash makes the brain quickly rewrite the memory of what was experienced, so the report is a false memory. Sam shot back that the distinction doesn't really matter: since the subject gives the same report either way and introspection can't tell the two stories apart, it's a difference without a difference. Maria replied that it's only indistinguishable from the armchair; the two stories make different claims about what the person was experiencing at a particular moment, and a good enough neural marker of conscious content could in principle break the tie. Sam's answer was that any early neural readout she points to could just be pre-concious or pre-edited processing, so unless she already has an independent marker of consciousness, the proposed experiment is circular. That's where they were when I left.

I'd like a move-by-move adjudication. Act as a philosopher of mind who has worked on timing and consciousness and knows Dennett's discussion of this case. Start by getting clear on what the color-phi result shows on its own versus what the two interpretations are adding. Then, for each move above, say whether it's right, wrong, or half-right and why. Finally, say whether there is an empirically respectable way to settle the Orwellian-versus-Stalinesque dispute, or whether the question of when the person experienced what is ill-posed.
