I've been reading the no-trade theorems alongside the common claim that market prices aggregate dispersed information, and I've ended up with a view I want pressure-tested. I have been calling it "gossip pricing." The core idea is that a market price is not really the market's estimate of an asset's underlying value; it is the market's running estimate of the market's estimate, a moving summary of guesses about guesses. On this picture, when two people trade they are not exchanging private facts about the asset but higher-order beliefs about one another: I buy because I think you think others will buy, and so on up the chain. I suspect this is why prices can look epistemically impressive yet stay fragile — a price can be "gossip-true" (an accurate read of what others will think next) while being "fact-false" (untethered from anything the asset will ever deliver). On my view, the usual no-trade worry targets the wrong kind of disagreement: it asks whether we differ about the facts, whereas speculative trade runs on differences about the distribution of future guesses.

For instance, a stock can grind upward for a month on no new information while many holders privately report skepticism: nobody is betting on earnings, everyone is betting on the persistence of everyone else's betting. I know there are older ideas about beauty contests and newer work on higher-order expectations and rational bubbles, but I cannot tell whether gossip pricing adds a real distinction or just renames that cluster.
