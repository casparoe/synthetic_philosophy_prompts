You are a professional philosopher working at the intersection of epistemology and aesthetics, and you have been asked to serve as the neutral facilitator and scribe for an adversarial collaboration between two camps in the debate over aesthetic testimony.

The first camp — call it the Acquaintance camp — holds that a person cannot legitimately form or hold a full-blooded evaluative aesthetic judgment about an artwork, performance, or natural scene on the basis of another person's testimony alone. On their view, first-hand experience of the object is a necessary condition for distinctively aesthetic judgment, not merely an instrumentally useful way of gathering information. The second camp — call it the Testimonialist camp — holds that testimony can in principle ground aesthetic judgment much as it grounds other kinds of judgment, and that whatever resistance we feel to forming aesthetic judgments on others' say-so is a contingent psychological or social fact rather than a principled epistemic limit.

The two camps have spent a year arguing past one another, and the deliverable they have commissioned is a joint statement they can both sign. Your job is not to adjudicate the dispute and not to defend one side; it is to produce the document a successful adversarial collaboration would have produced, with the residual disagreement made exact and any merely verbal disagreements identified.

The statement should include:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section. These claims should be substantive rather than vacuous. They should cover at least: what both camps grant about testimony's ability to transmit descriptive, non-evaluative information about works; what both camps grant about the special value of first-hand experience; the relevant asymmetries or lack thereof with ordinary empirical testimony and with moral testimony; and what each side would have to be shown in order to concede.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should state a single residual disagreement. For each row, give: (a) a neutral statement of the disagreement, (b) the formulation the Acquaintance camp would sign, and (c) the formulation the Testimonialist camp would sign. The two formulations must be more than mirror images of one another; each should capture what its own side cares most about.

3. A cruxes section. Identify the sub-questions such that a determinate answer would move at least one side. For each crux, specify a concrete case, argument, or class of evidence that both sides would agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts the case or evidence will show. Build the cases around scenarios such as: a deeply knowledgeable critic who has access to a full score or complete technical description but has never encountered the work first-hand; an ordinary person who defers to unanimous expert opinion both before and after her own encounter with the work; and a comparison between aesthetic deference and moral deference.

4. A closing note that separates any disagreements you judge to be merely verbal from the substantive ones, with a precise account of where the verbal slide happens and what would need to be true for each side to give ground.

Throughout, keep these distinctions explicit rather than running them together: aesthetic belief (a state with propositional content, such as the belief that a building is graceful) versus aesthetic judgment in the fuller sense (a first-personal appraisal that normally draws on one's own perceptual or imaginative encounter with the object); testimony about the speaker's own response versus testimony about the work's merits; and questions of contingent reliability (how often critics agree, how manipulable taste is) versus questions of epistemic principle (whether even ideally reliable testimony could supply the right kind of reason). Do not include citations, bibliographic references, or attributions to particular authors; the document must stand on its own.

Begin by brainstorming the candidate cruxes and the cases you will use, and think carefully about which formulations each camp would actually sign rather than merely tolerate. Then write the final joint statement in polished, formal prose.
