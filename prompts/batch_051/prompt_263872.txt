Please act as a professional philosopher specialising in the philosophy of humour and moral psychology. Think through the question slowly and from first principles; begin by sketching the candidate principles you are taking seriously, and do not settle for a mere name for the phenomenon. Throughout your answer, write in British English.

I write comic fiction, and deciding where laughter is permissible is part of my craft. Over many drafts I have discovered that I hold a set of firm judgements about particular cases, and I would like to know what principle, if any, holds them together. Each verdict arrives quickly, but no simple rule I can state captures all of them.

Here are the cases.

1. A pompous bank manager, having just belittled a junior clerk, steps back onto a freshly polished floor, slips, and crashes to the ground in a flurry of papers. I write the scene so that the reader laughs, and I feel no impulse to spare the man. I take the laughter to be permissible.

2. The same fall, with the same choreography, but the character is a woman leaving a register office after recording her husband's death. I could not write it as comedy, and I would find a reader's laughter with the narration repulsive. I take the laughter to be impermissible.

3. A colleague slips on ice in front of me, falls hard, and breaks his wrist. I do not laugh, and I think worse of the person standing beside me who does. Yet I would happily stage an identical fall in a farce and let the audience laugh. The sequence of movements is the same; only the frame has changed. I take real injury to be no fit object of comedy, and the staged farce to be perfectly comic.

4. A character with a stammer makes a dry joke at his own expense — about the patience of strangers on the telephone, say. I intend it as charm, and I expect readers to warm to him because of the self-mockery. Another character repeats the same joke to a table of strangers with the first character present and mortified. I would write that repetition as cruelty. So I take self-mockery to be permissible and third-party mockery of the same trait to be impermissible.

5. A junior employee deflates his overbearing manager's pretensions, and I treat it as wit; the reader is on the junior's side. The manager deflates the junior employee's stammer. The mechanism of derision is the same, but the first feels fair and the second feels abusive. So I take mockery directed upward to be permissible in a way mockery directed downward is not.

6. In an animated farce a character is struck on the skull with a mallet and briefly sees stars; I find this funny. Written realistically, the same blow would be a scene from a horror novel, and I would not find it funny at all. What matters is not simply that no real person is harmed, since in both cases no real person is harmed. I take the cartoon violence to be comic and the realistic depiction of the same act not to be.

I know the familiar one-line explanations: that all comedy is disguised cruelty at another's expense; that laughter is only violated expectation; that humour is a discharge of nervous energy; that a joke requires a wrongness that is simultaneously felt to be safe. I do not want these voted on as slogans. I want whichever of them, or whichever hybrid, actually does the work when tested against these six cases.

Your task is to state the best candidate principle, or a small set of candidate principles, as precisely as you can; show which case motivates each clause; distinguish your position from the slogans above; then apply it to the following cases about which I am genuinely unsure: (i) a small child laughs when a sibling stumbles but is unhurt; (ii) a videogame lets the player arrange a minor mishap for a simulated pedestrian, with cartoon physics and no possible suffering; (iii) a piece of satire mocks a vice in the abstract — snobbery, say — but is overheard by a person who is trying, and failing, not to be snobbish. Finally, say honestly which of my six verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, if any, and whether that is a reason to revise the verdict or to complicate the principle.
