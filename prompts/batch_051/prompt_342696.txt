For an advanced undergraduate seminar in metaontology that I teach, I would like you to draft the document one would expect from an adversarial collaboration between a quantifier variantist and a heavyweight ontological realist concerning whether ordinary composite objects such as tables and chairs genuinely exist. The quantifier variantist takes the dispute over composites to be, in large part, verbal: different linguistic communities can legitimately use existence-talk and object-talk with different meanings, so the claim that tables exist and the claim that reality contains only simple particles arranged in table-like fashion can be simultaneously true in their respective frameworks, with no further fact of the matter selecting between them. The heavyweight realist denies this. She holds that some quantifier meanings and kind terms are more natural, joint-carving, or structurally privileged than others, and that it is a substantive, non-verbal question which composites the world contains, if any.

The deliverable should begin with a numbered list of agreed claims that both parties could sign in good faith: claims about the surface linguistic data, about what each position is and is not committed to, and about what each party would count as evidence capable of moving her away from her own view. The agreed claims should be informative without being slanted toward either side. It should then present a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is stated first in neutral language and then restated in wording that each side would accept as a fair articulation of its own view. After the table, identify the cruxes—the sub-questions whose settlement would genuinely move at least one side—and for each crux specify a concrete case, argument, or kind of evidence that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. If, by the parties' own lights, part of the dispute turns out to be merely verbal, say exactly which part and why.
