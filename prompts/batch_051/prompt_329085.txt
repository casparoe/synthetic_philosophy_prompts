I'm a high school junior and I've been going down a rabbit hole about whether disgust and sacredness actually belong in ethics. I've landed on a pretty specific position: feelings of disgust or desecration are never by themselves reasons to judge something as wrong. They can be useful clues that something might be wrong, like a smoke alarm that goes off around mold or betrayal, but the actual wrongness always has to cash out in harm, unfairness, or a violation of someone's autonomy. So "that's revolting" or "that's sacred, don't touch it" is at best a red flag, not a moral reason, and when people judge the same act as worse just because it's gross I think that's a bias rather than a moral insight. One thing I like about the view is that it makes a prediction: if you hold harm and autonomy constant, how disgusted people feel shouldn't drive how wrong they think something is. The thing that would move me most is a cross-cultural case where a whole practice gets judged wrong only because it's gross or sacred, with no harm or unfairness story anyone can actually tell, and people's judgments don't look like rationalization.
