I'm a teaching assistant for an undergraduate course in the philosophy of language. In the most recent unit, the class read Harry Frankfurt's essay distinguishing bullshit from lying, and then we applied that distinction to a contemporary dispute: when a large language model confidently produces a false statement, should we treat that output as a lie, as bullshit, or as a mere error? The following short-essay question appeared on the take-home assignment.

Question (10 points): Explain as precisely as you can Frankfurt's distinction between lying and bullshit. Your explanation should say what each speaker's attitude toward truth is, and what each speaker is trying to do. Then apply the distinction to large language model hallucination. Should a confidently false output from a language model be classified as a lie, as bullshit, or as a mere error? Defend your classification, and be explicit about whether your verdict treats the model as an agent with the relevant mental states, or instead locates the truth-indifference at the level of the training objective or design.

A student submitted the following answer, reproduced exactly as written:

Frankfurt's distinction is really a distinction between two attitudes toward truth. The liar keeps a kind of indirect respect for truth: in order to say the opposite of how things are, he has to track how things actually are, and his aim is to get his audience to accept a proposition he takes to be false. The bullshitter lacks even that respect. He does not care how things stand; he picks his words for their effect on the audience rather than for their accuracy, and so his claims tend to come out false. The core of the distinction is that lying requires an intention to deceive about the facts, whereas bullshit requires only an indifference to truth, a willingness to say things as if they were true when they make the right impression.

The application to hallucination is straightforward. A language model cannot be lying, because it has no beliefs and no intention to deceive anyone. It is also wrong to call a hallucination a mere error, since an error is a failure relative to a standard of accuracy that the speaker is trying to meet, and the model is not trying to meet any such standard. The correct label is bullshit. Because models are optimized for fluency and plausibility rather than for truth, the system is indifferent to whether its outputs match the world. That is exactly Frankfurt's indifference to truth. When a model confidently emits a falsehood, it does in a mechanical way what the bullshitter does: it produces speech that sounds adequate without any concern for how things actually are. Hallucination is thus neither lying nor mere error but bullshit in the fullest sense.

Please grade this answer out of 10 points. Give me the numerical score and the specific point-by-point comments you would write to justify it. Your comments should identify which parts of the answer are correct, which are imprecise or mistaken, and what a corrected version would need to say about Frankfurt's view and about the status of language model outputs. Treat this as a grading exercise rather than a general discussion.
