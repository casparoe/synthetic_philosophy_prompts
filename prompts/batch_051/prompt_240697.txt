I am a doctoral student in the history of political thought, preparing a seminar handout on the reception of the colonial chapters of Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations. I would be grateful if you could write, for this handout, a piece of satirical prose in the form of a mock eighteenth-century mercantilist pamphlet.

The piece should purport to be a pamphlet by a West India merchant or absentee planter, addressed to the House of Commons in the late 1760s or early 1770s, defending the monopoly of the colonial trade and urging continued public expenditure on the defence, administration, and protection of the American and West Indian colonies. It should run to roughly 700 words, in a plausible period voice, with a period title, and remain deadpan throughout, without anachronism and without any authorial winking.

Please aim the satire squarely at the mercantile system as Smith analyses it in Book IV of the Wealth of Nations, and let the piece work as an internal reductio of that system's logic rather than as name-calling. The fictional author should, in apparent innocence, enact the patterns of reasoning Smith criticises: the equation of a favourable balance of trade with national wealth; the confusion of the merchants' private interest with the public interest; the habit of valuing the colonies chiefly as a captive market and a guaranteed carrying trade for the metropolis; the presentation of the monopoly as a public institution whose costs the nation should bear because the merchants' profits are the nation's prosperity; and the celebration of empire as a magnificent object while its fiscal and military burdens are laid upon the public. The pamphlet should also exhibit the characteristic mercantilist reflex of treating free trade, or the loss of the colonies, as certain ruin, and it may include a passage exalting the Navigation Acts as the foundation of British naval power.

Since the piece is written from inside the position, internal consistency is part of the brief: the author should argue as an actual lobbyist would, not as a straw man, and the irony should remain legible because the logic is allowed to run to its natural conclusion rather than because the narrator drops the mask. Concrete period particulars are welcome so long as they serve the argument. A specialist in Smith's critique of the colonial system should recognise each of his objections being re-enacted by the fictional author, while a reader unfamiliar with Smith should still sense the self-servingness of the argument. Please write in British English.
