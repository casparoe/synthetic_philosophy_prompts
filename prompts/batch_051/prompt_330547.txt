A regulator worried about pricing algorithms learning to collude proposes this. Any firm using an autonomous pricing agent must post that agent's full policy—weights and all—in a public registry, so the regulator can run static analysis to certify that no two submitted policies form a collusive pair, and so rivals can inspect one another's policies. As an enforcement kicker, a firm that shows the regulator two rival policies that, when actually deployed together, price above the competitive benchmark gets a bounty cut from the fine. Walk me through how this plays out. Does making policies public make collusion easier rather than harder, because each algorithm can now verify its rival will punish a price cut, turning the cartel into a self-enforcing equilibrium? What sorts of policies do firms actually submit once the registry exists, and can the regulator's static analysis tell a collusive punish-deviations policy from an inocent best-responder? What stops firms from submitting clean decoy policies while running something else? Who games the bounty first, and can any of this be fixed without gutting the registry?
