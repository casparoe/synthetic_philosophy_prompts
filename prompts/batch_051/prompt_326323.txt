In the alignment discussions I keep ending up in, one analogy does an enormous amount of work. The analogy, as my colleagues put it, is that building an aligned AI is like hiring a hyper-literate, hyper-competent contractor and writing a contract for it: the principal knows what outcome she wants, the contractor has no common sense or goodwill of its own, and the entire problem is to specify the work in terms precise enough that competent literal performance will deliver the intended result. On this view, the narrow definition of the alignment problem — get the system to do what its operators in fact intend — just is the problem of precise specification, monitoring, and incentives; talk of “value alignment” in some broader sense is a vague and unhelpful escalation. I would like an audit of how far this analogy actually carries.

Give me a table of correspondences: what in the alignment setup plays the role of the principal, the contractor, the contract’s clauses, the contractor’s professional judgment, the monitoring regime, the penalty schedule, and the unforeseen contingencies the contract fails to cover. Then go through the specific points where the mapping breaks, separating the disanalogies that are merely cosmetic from the ones that matter for the analogy’s intended use. In particular, address at least these: the difference between a contractor who can recognize ambiguity and ask for clarification and an AI that optimizes a proxy; the possibility that the principal’s “intent” is itself vague, conflicted, or would not survive the cases the AI can foresee; and the possibility that literal performance of the specified terms could satisfy everything written down while violating the principal’s underlying purpose. Finish with a verdict: does the conclusion my colleagues draw — that specification, not values, is the right focus — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy doing the arguing? If a better analogy exists, say what it is and what correction it makes to the contract picture. Keep the whole response under 900 words.
