Two friends of mine got into it about a proposal in the news to replace part of parliament with a citizens' assembly chosen by lot.

Ben said it was definately better than what we have now. Career politicians are a professional class that mostly answers to donors, and even the people who hate them only propose term limits, which just rotates the same class through faster. Nobody thinks a jury made up of professional career jurors would be an improvement on twelve random citizens, so sortition just extends the jury principle to lawmaking: a chamber that actually looks like the public.

Anya said he was conflating two different jobs. A jury answers one bounded question after being walked through the evidence by a judge and two advocates; a legislature sets its own agenda, drafts technical law, amends budgets, and bargains with the civil service for years. Random citizens would be captured by the permenant staff on day one — you'd end up with rule by unelected advisers, which is the very thing he says he hates.

Ben replied that a fresh MP is just as captured by civil servants and party whips, and you don't solve that by electing better people; you give amateurs enough support and time. In any case the current lot isn't exactly covering itself in glory.

Anya came back that elections at least give ordinary people the power to throw the bastards out. A sortition chamber can't be voted out, so it's actually less accountable than what it replaces.

Ben said accountability to voters is part of the disease: politicians pander to short-term polling instead of doing the right thing, and being chosen by lot breaks that dependence. That's where they stopped.

Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right about what, which objections actually land, and what both Ben and Anya are missing — not just who wins overall? And please write the answer in British English.
