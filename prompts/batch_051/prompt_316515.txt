I've been teaching Moore's paradox and theres a list of conditions I hand out for when a sentence counts as a case of it. The conditions are: (1) it's got to be of the form "p, but I don't believe that p" — or the contrarian version, "p, but I believe that not-p" — with the exact same p on both sides. (2) The speaker has to put both halves forward in one assertive act, not just report one half as a stray thought. (3) It shouldn't be a plain contradiction: "p and not-p" can't both be true, but "p and I don't believe p" can, since it can be raining while I fail to believe that it's raining. (4) The oddness has to come from the first-person, present-tense combo, because "It's raining, but she doesn't believe it" and "It was raining, but I didn't believe it at the time" are fine.

Now here's the case that's bugging me: "It's raining, but I don't know that it's raining." It has the same surface shape, it's asserted in one go, and both halves can be true (I might be going on a lucky hunch, and in any case I can believe it's raining while still not knowing it's raining). But the second half says "don't know," not "don't believe." Some days I think it's close enough in spirit that my list just needs widening to "belief or knowledge"; other days I think it's a different animal — a norm-of-assertion problem rather than a Moore-paradox problem. Walk me through my four conditions one by one, say which it clearly meets and which are genuinely a judgement call, then give me a verdict: is it Moore's paradox, a close cousin, or neither? And if my list needs fixing, tell me exactly where.
