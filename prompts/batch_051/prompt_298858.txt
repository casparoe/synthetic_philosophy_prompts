After thirty years running a mass spectrometry lab and grudgingly teaching a graduate methods seminar every other fall, I've finally started reading philosophy properly, and Whitehead's essays on education have got me stuck on a movie I half-remember from when my kids were teenagers: Dead Poets Society. The way I remember it, Welton Academy is a machine for manufacturing exactly what Whitehead complains about — ideas that sit there unused and inert, drilled in for the exam and never put to work in a student's own life. And then Keating shows up and does the pure opposite: all excitement and first love of the material, carpe diem, ripping the prissy introduction out of the poetry textbook, standing on the desk to see things from a new angle. On the face of it, a Whiteheadian should cheer. But the more I read the rhythm-of-education stuff, the less sure I am.

Whitehead's claim, as I'm reading him, is that real learning has a rhythm: a first stage of vague, excited encounter he calls romance, then a discipline-of-detail stage he calls precision, then a freer stage of synthesis he calls generalization — and the stages keep cycling. Keating looks to me like he lives entirely in romance and almost sneers at precision, while the school is nothing but precision with no romance to make the discipline worthwhile. So here's what I'd like you to take seriously: if you read the movie through this framework, is Keating actually a Whiteheadian teacher — supplying the missing first stage so that some of the boys (Todd, maybe Neil with his acting) can finally enter precision on their own terms — or is he a cautionary example of what happens when you set romance loose without the later stages? And does the movie itself seem to know the difference?

I'm not asking for a film-studies essay, and I dont need a plot summary. What I want is a reading that pushes both ways: which scenes show Whitehead's stages actually doing their work, where does the movie resist the Whiteheadian reading, and what would a Whiteheadian make of Neil's suicide and the final desk-standing scene — is that genuine intellectual independence, or just solidarity dressed up as freedom? Also, in my lab courses the thing I never solved was how to keep the disciplined, tedious part of technique connected to the excitement that made students want to learn it in the first place. Does the film, or Whitehead, actually tell me anything about that that a pedagogy textbook wouldn't? Give me the strongest version of the pro-Keating and anti-Keating Whiteheadian readings, then tell me which you'd land on and what would change your mind.
