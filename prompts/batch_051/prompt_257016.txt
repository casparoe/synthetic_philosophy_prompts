I recently moved onto a team building a semantic search product, and I have repeatedly encountered a particular way of talking about what our models are doing. Embedding spaces are described to me as maps, or geographies, of meaning: each word, sentence, or document is a point; distance between points is semantic relatedness; and the directions one can move through the space correspond to genuine conceptual dimensions, in roughly the way latitude and longitude correspond to positions on a globe. The standard illustration is that taking the vector for term like "king," subtracting the male-associated direction, and adding the female-associated direction lands near "queen," just as moving west and then north brings you to a different named place. This is not idle imagery in our discussions; it is made to do real work. It is used to justify retrieving a query's nearest neighbors as though they were the closest towns, treating cosine similarity as though it measured as-the-crow-flies distance, and concluding from the fact that a linear classifier can find a direction separating two categories that the model itself has stored that category as an axis of its internal map.

I would like you to audit this geography-of-meaning analogy rather than merely restate it. Begin with an explicit correspondence table: what corresponds to the map, the points, the distances, the directions, the coordinate axes, the landmarks, and the act of navigation. Then identify the places where the mapping breaks, paying particular attention to the features a geographic map quietly imports that an embedding space may not possess: that each item has a single fixed location; that distance is symmetric and obeys the triangle inequality; that nearby points are nearby for one coherent reason; that directions are globally consistent and composable; that the territory is stable over time; and that the map preserves something already there rather than constructing it. Finally, assess how much of the engineering and interpretability reasoning above survives these disanalogies, and say what a better analogy would be, if one exists.
