I'm a retired chemist who's recently gotten into philosophy, so my reflex is still to think of causation as something like collisions, reactions, energy moving around — a cause ought to be an event in the world doing something. But I've been reading about causation by omission in moral and legal philosophy, and I keep seeing the slogan "omissions aren't causes" (or "doing nothing can't cause anything"). Last week it came up in my reading group to settle an argument about whether a factory that never installed a safety shut-off valve caused a chemical leak. One member of the group insisted the company can't be causally responsible for the leak because the failure to install the valve wasn't a cause — it was just an omission. I'm not convinced, but I also suspect the slogan is bundling together seperate ideas. Could you lay out the different precise claims it could be making — about absences vs. events, negative facts, cause vs. merely allowing, causation vs. moral responsibility, and whatever else — and say for each whether it's trivial, substantive but defensible, or false, and then say which readings would actually support his conclusion in the factory case? I'd rather have a menu of options than one answer, since I want to bring it back to the group and show exactly where the argument goes wrong.
