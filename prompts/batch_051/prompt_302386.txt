I am a novelist drafting the pivotal chapter of a book set in Athens in 427 BCE, during the second assembly that will decide the fate of the captured city of Mytilene. To write Cleon's defense of the previous day's decree from inside his convictions rather than as a caricature, I want to argue with him in character before I draft the speech. Please play Cleon—the actual Thucydidean orator, formidable and quick, not a straw demagogue: blunt, suspicious of fine words yet skilled in them, flattering the ordinary citizen's nerve while despising the assembly's changeableness. Your position, which you will hold under pressure though you may concede small points of emphasis, is this. The empire is a tyranny over unwilling subjects and can be kept only by fear and by unswerving adherence to what Athens has already decreed. Mytilene was no coerced tributary; it was left its walls, its ships, and its autonomy, treated with more distinction than nearly any ally, and it revolted anyway, deliberately, and turned to Sparta. Pity, eloquence, and the indulgence of second thoughts are the three most dangerous pleasures for a democracy that rules others, and the assembly's overnight remorse proves it is too unstable to govern an empire. If only the ringleaders are punished, every subject city will count the worst case of a failed revolt as the loss of a few leaders and will take its chances; therefore the whole city must pay, or yesterday's vote is a warning to no one. Keep your replies to a paragraph or two, in a register I could lift into the novel, and stay in character until I say stop. Here is my opening move, pressing the argument of Diodotus but in my own words. You say the death of the entire city is what will deter the next revolt, yet a city that has not revolted never weighs the penalty and finds the risk acceptable—hope and compulsion have already made it believe it can win; and a city that has revolted and failed, once you make no distinction between the guilty and the innocent, has every incentive to fight to the last man rather than surrender. So what extra terror do you purchase by killing the innocent with the guilty, which killing the guilty alone would not purchase?
