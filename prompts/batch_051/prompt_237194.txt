I teach an upper-level undergrad seminar on decision theory and rationality, and I'm setting the final exam. I want one essay question on money pumps and diachronic rationality that can't be answered by just reciting the standard cycle example (A preferred to B, B to C, C to A, trader swaps the agent back to A minus a fee). I want it to force students to say exactly what the argument is supposed to prove, and whether it does.

In particular the question should make them confront three things. First, the difference between showing that an agent is exploitable and showing that she's irrational — what bridge premise would get you from "there's a series of trades she'll accept, each looking good, that leaves her poorer" to "her preferences are rationally defective"? Second, the familiar reply that a sophisticated agent who forsees the whole sequence could just refuse the first trade: does that defuse the argument, or does it just narrow the target to naive agents? Third, the distinction between synchronic coherence (acyclicity at a time) and diachronic coherence (sticking to a plan across time), because students constantly blur them.

Please write the question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme. The exam is 90 minutes, the question is worth 30 of 100 marks, and the course is upper-level. In the marking scheme, seperate the specific moves an A answer must contain — e.g., a clear statement of why the sure loss depends on the agent accepting each trade in isolation; the exploitability/irrationality distinction; the naive/sophisticated distinction; the synchronic/diachronic distinction; and at least one serious countermove (resolute choice, refusing to let pairwise choices trump all-things-considered preferences, or something in that neighborhood) — from the mistakes that make an answer a B or C: just retelling the pump, asserting without argument that sure loss just is irrationality, conflating cyclic pairwise preferences with hyperbolic discounting or preference reversal, or treating the pump as if it were mainly a problem about the agent's future self. I'd rather the question be too pointed than too broad.
