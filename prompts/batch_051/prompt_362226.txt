I am presenting a paper next month at an interdisciplinary workshop on constitutional design, and I want a question-period prep sheet rather than an abstract of the paper. The thesis is that the Ulysses-contract model of constitutional precommitment is systematically misleading whenever it is used to justify entrenched substantive values such as bills of rights or eternity clauses, by which I mean provisions that purport to be unamendable. My argument is that legitimate constitutional precommitment extends only to what I call procedural insulation: shielding a discrete mechanism, such as a central bank, an electoral calendar, or judicial tenure, from short-term political pressure. The reason is that procedural insulation solves a coordination problem that nearly every future citizen, whatever their substantive values, has reason to preserve. Substantive entrenchment, in contrast, requires the further premise that a founding generation's identification of values is authoritative over later generations who never shared in the act of adoption. The paper develops this through three claims: a parallelism claim, that every load-bearing part of the Ulysses analogy maps onto procedural insulation and none onto substantive entrenchment; a symmetry claim, that any substantive value a founding generation might entrench has an equally entrenchable disvalue, so the framing lends spurious authority to a contingent historical choice; and a functional claim, that the goods usually credited to substantive entrenchment, including stability, predictability, and minority protection, are either procedural-insulation goods or are achieved by ordinary constitutional practice without precommitment.

The audience matters for the objections I will receive. About half are constitutional lawyers who treat the Ulysses picture as the standard justification for entrenched rights and supermajority rules. Several are democratic theorists who already regard any entrenchment as a dead-hand constraint on self-government. Two or three are behavioral economists who model time-inconsistent choice and will press on whether a collective body can even be described as present-biased, and on what a collective discount model would have to look like for my argument to go through.

Prepare the prep sheet. For each expected objection, reproduce it in the idiom of the audience member who would raise it, identify their camp, estimate how likely and how damaging it is, and order the sheet by expected damage. For each, supply a reply I could deliver in about two minutes and note where that reply is weakest. Also tell me what I should concede in my opening remarks so that point is not used against me, what I should refuse to concede, and what question I should most hope nobody asks. Conclude by stating how confident you are in the overall map of objections and in the specific characterizations, and identify the single point in the sheet you are least sure about.
