I have been trying to work out whether it is really true that DNA is a code, or whether this is just a metaphor that has gotten out of hand. I recently finished a popular science book that argues the second view with great confidence, and I would like help deciding how much of it to trust. The author's position, as I understand it, runs as follows. The phrase genetic code is a metaphor we have mistaken for a fact. DNA is a molecule like any other, and its behavior is fully accounted for by ordinary chemistry: bases pair, genes are transcribed, and ribosomes and transfer RNAs translate the sequence, but nothing in this process is writing or reading a message. A code in the proper sense—Morse code, a language, a cipher—is a system of rules deliberately established by agents so that a sender can convey information to a receiver. No agent established the base-pairing rules or the assignments between nucleotide triplets and amino acids, and no component of the cell understands the sequence. We may therefore talk about DNA carrying information as a convenience, but that is a useful fiction. It follows, the author says, that the popular claim that life is fundamentally about information rests on a category mistake.

I have no training in biology or philosophy, so I cannot tell where this argument is solid and where it slides. One thing nags at me: I have read elsewhere that the genetic code was deciphered rather than invented, which makes it sound like something real that was discovered rather than a metaphor someone chose. Could you give me an accuracy audit of the author's summary, separating what a careful molecular biologist or philosopher of biology would accept, what they would qualify as a fair simplification, and what a defender of information-talk about DNA would reject as overstatement?
