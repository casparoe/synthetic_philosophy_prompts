I have been trying to reconstruct an argument I heard at a workshop, because I can neither accept it nor locate its mistake. It is meant to show that the project of attributing consciousness to some animals but not others is untenable. The conclusion seems to prove too much, and I want to know whether there is a precise step to reject.

(1) For any subject other than oneself, the only available evidence of consciousness is behavioural and physiological: nociception, responsiveness to stimuli, associative and flexible learning, and integrated control of action.
(2) If this kind of evidence is sufficient to justify attributing consciousness to a dog or a crow, then the same kind of evidence, present to a comparable degree, is sufficient to justify attributing consciousness to any animal that displays it.
(3) The relevant traits do not appear as a single package at one point in the animal kingdom; each trait is graded and the traits dissociate, since there is nociception without associative learning, associative learning without flexible planning, and so on.
(4) Consequently, wherever one draws the line—mammals, vertebrates, animals with a central nervous system—there will plausibly be animals on both sides with nearly identical total evidence profiles.
(5) A boundary with nearly identical evidence profiles on both sides is arbitrary, and an arbitrary boundary cannot be a justified boundary.
(6) So either consciousness must be attributed to every animal showing even minimal nociception or learning-like adjustment, down to the simplest invertebrates, or it must be denied to all non-human animals; there is no principled intermediate position.
(7) Both alternatives are unacceptable: the first attributes too much to nervous systems far simpler than ours, and the second denies too much to creatures that are behaviourally close to our own case.

I am inclined to keep the verdict that a dog is conscious and a jellyfish is not, but I want to know what principle makes that more than an arbitrary line. Which step or inferential move should be rejected, why did that step look forced, and what does rejecting it commit one to in the wider debate about the distribution of consciousness? Please answer in British English.
