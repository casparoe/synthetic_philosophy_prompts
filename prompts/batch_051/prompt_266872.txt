I spent four decades as a geochemist before retiring, and only in the past few years have I given philosophy sustained attention. In the reading group I now attend, two members, Peter and Sonia, got into a running argument over whether the relation expressed by "one state of the world is better than another" must be transitive; the argument survived several meetings, and since neither would budge they asked me—as someone trained to expect relations in nature to be well behaved—to lay the exchange out for an outside referee. Peter set up a spectrum of possible populations. State A contains one thousand people, each living a very good life. State B contains fifteen hundred people, each living a life that is slightly less good. State C contains twenty-five hundred people, each living a little less well still. The sequence continues for many steps down to State Z, which contains one hundred thousand people, each living a life that is only barely worth living. At every step the population and the total well-being increase, while each person's quality of life falls only slightly.

Their exchange unfolded as follows. (1) Peter argued that each adjacent state in the chain is better than the one before: there are many more lives, total well-being is substantially greater, and the loss to any one individual is small and spread thin. But State A is clearly better than State Z. If "better than" were transitive, the chain would force the conclusion that Z is better than A; since that conclusion is false, "better than" is not transitive. (2) Sonia replied that transitivity is not a dispensable extra but part of what makes a relation a ranking in the first place. If the two endpoint judgments are as Peter reports them, then at least one of the adjacent-step judgments must be wrong, whether or not we can say which one; our powers of discrimination are simply too coarse to tag the failing link. (3) Peter answered that this misdescribes the evidence. Each link is not a bare intuition but follows from an explicit criterion: total well-being rises steeply while the cost to any given person is tiny and diffuse. If every link satisfies the criterion but the endpoints contradict it, the criterion is inconsistent, and the better diagnosis is that betterness works locally rather than globally. (4) Sonia said this proposal makes betterness unable to guide choice. If A is better than B, B better than C, and C better than A, then a chooser could cycle among the options with no option counting as best, and a notion that cannot rank alternatives for action is not what we mean by "better." (5) Peter responded that choice need not presuppose one global ranking. Comparisons can be path-dependent: from each adjacent pair the local comparison decides, and there is no obligation to combine all the local comparisons into a single all-things-considered best. (6) Sonia came back that, if the chain contains only local comparisons along the dimension of total well-being, then the relation in the chain is not "better than, all things considered" but only "better along one particular dimension." The transitivity of the all-things-considered notion is then untouched; the spectrum shows only that local improvements along one dimension do not aggregate, which is not a counterexample. (7) Peter replied that the phrase "all things considered" was now doing all the argumentative work, since no one could say what it adds to the local comparisons except the demand for transitivity itself. The meeting ended with each accusing the other of changing the subject.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each numbered move, say whether it strikes you as clearly mistaken, mostly mistaken, mostly right, or clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then say which specific points each disputant is right about, which point each of them ought to have conceded, and what the exchange as a whole is missing. I want an intellectual scorecard rather than a simple verdict. Please write the answer in American English.
