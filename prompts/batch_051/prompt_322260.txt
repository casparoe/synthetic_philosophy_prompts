"The argument from illusion starts with cases like a stick in water that looks bent. What you're aware of is something bent; the phsyical stick is straight; so the thing you're aware of can't be the physical stick, it has to be a non-physical sense-datum. Since an illusory experience can be subjectively indistinguishable from a veridical experience of a genuinely bent stick, veridical perception has to be awareness of a sense-datum too, rather than of the object itself. So direct realism is false: we only ever directly perceive sense-data. Disjunctivists reply that this argument assumes experiences that feel the same are the same fundamental kind of state. They deny that assumption: veridical perception is a direct relation to the object itself, whereas illusion is a different kind of state. The argument from hallucination is basically the same argument but with no physical object present: a hallucination of a stick can be subjectively indistinguishable from really seeing a stick, and since in the hallucination you aren't aware of any mind-independent object, the real perception must be the same sort of state. The disjunctivist can reply in the exact same way by denying that the hallucination and the veridical perception are the same fundamental kind, even though they feel the same. Actually the hallucination case is even easier for the disjunctivist: there's no physical object there to get wrong, so they can just say it's a state that isn't a perception of anything. So the reply to illusion carries over to hallucination without any extra problems."
