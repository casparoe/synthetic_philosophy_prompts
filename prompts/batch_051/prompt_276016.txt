I've been following a cycle in campaign-finance regulation that keeps producing the same shape, and I don't know whether the shape has a name. Round one: a legislature bans direct corporate contributions to candidates, and the money reappears as independent expenditures. Round two: the legislature imposes disclosure requirements on independent spending, and the money reappears as donations to intermediary groups that do not disclose their donors. Round three: those groups are brought under disclosure rules, and the money migrates again. At each stage the official indicators improve: reported direct contributions fall, reported independent expenditures fall, and reporting of the new intermediary channel falls. Reformers cite those improvements as evidence that the reform worked, but the total amount of money seeking political influence seems roughly constant; what has changed is not its quantity but its position relative to whatever the regulator happens to be measuring. The actors are not falsifying reports or gaming the metric in the ordinary sense. They are lawfully rerouting around an obstacle, and the improvement in the metric is caused precisely by the activity leaving the part of the world the metric samples. In an important sense the reform has made the underlying problem harder to observe, and harder to regulate next.

I am after the specific evaluative trap, not the displacement itself. I know the displacement is often described in water-and-pressure metaphors or under the heading of whack-a-mole, and I know Goodhart's law and Campbell's law concern what happens when a measure becomes a target and gets corrupted. But this case seems different: the numbers remain truthful, yet they mislead because the regulated activity has moved outside the frame they sample. The trap is that a reform gets assessed by exactly the indicator its own passage is most likely to push out of view, so failure masquerades as success. Does this configuration have an established name in political science, regulatory theory, or the measurement literature? If so, how exactly is it distinguished from Goodhart's law, Campbell's law, and the hydraulic account of money in politics? If it has no established name, I would want a defensible coinage with a definition and boundary cases, because a bare label will not help me—I need to know where this phenomenon begins and ends and which features are doing the work.

Finally, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who study this. If the empirical claim that money in politics behaves hydraulically is itself disputed, say so and do not present one side of that dispute as the consensus.
