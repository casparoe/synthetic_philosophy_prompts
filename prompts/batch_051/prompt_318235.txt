Ethics committees let investigators randomize patients between an experimental treatment and standard care only when the expert community is in genuine, honest disagreement about which is better — a state people call clinical equipoise. But the doctor enrolling a given patient can privately think the experimental drug is likelier to work, and the patient may still be assigned to the arm their own doctor suspects is worse. Outside a trial, a doctor who let a coin decide while leaning toward one treatment would be criticized for it; call it a randomized trial and it becomes standard practice. What view of a doctor's obligation to the individual patient does this practice presuppose? Does it presuppose that uncertainty can live in a community rather than in individual minds, or is the community's uncertainty just a tidy way of saying a bunch of doctors with different hunches are overriding each other's judgment? Lay out the implicit picture of medical knowledge and of the doctor-patient relationship that licenses randomization, separate the parts the practice strictly needs from the parts it merely happens to fit, and say which rival views about evidence and a doctor's duty could each justify the same trial design. Also tell me where your reconstruction is least certain and what argument would change your mind about it.
