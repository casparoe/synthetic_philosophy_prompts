Ok so I just had one of those hour-long walk-back arguments with a friend about the Dutch East India Company, and I want an actual case analysis out of it, not vibes. Her line: it's basically melodramatic to call the VOC a state. It was a profit-seeking corporation with a monopoly charter; the States-General delegated all those powers and could in principle take them back; nobody treated a shareholder as a citizen. My line: that's the point. The clean line we now draw between 'private company' and 'sovereign state' simply didn't exist yet, and the VOC raised its own armies, signed treaties, minted coins, ran its own courts, and governed Batavia and much of the East Indies for nearly two centuries. That sounds like a state to me.

Here's what I want. Take the VOC from the 1602 charter through the 18th century, focusing on how it governed territory and used force, and analyze it through the lens of the state-as-a-corporation / corporate sovereignty thesis. Map where the thesis fits and where it strains: what was genuinely state-like about the company, what stayed commercial and shareholder-driven, and whether the 'delegated powers' objection actually dissolves the puzzle or just moves it back to the Dutch Republic and its admiralties. Then run the analysis the other way: does the VOC confirm the idea that early modern soverignty was partly corporate, or does it show the thesis is overblown — that sovereignty really stayed with the States-General and the company was always just an instrument? Be explicit about where the theory has nothing to say instead of making it speak.
