in my African philosophy class we've been on ubuntu and relational personhood, the idea that you arent born a full person but become one through other people recognizing and including you. anyway a guest lecturer floated a proposal that i cant stop turning over. suppose a town replaces residency/citizenship as the basis of full civic standing (voting in town meetings, standing to bring complaints, access to certain public goods) with a voucher system. to be a person in good standing you need three vouchers from unrelated adult residents who are themselves in good standing, recorded in a public registry and renewed each year. any resident can file an objection to someones vouchers, which triggers a hearing. existing residents are grandfathered through the first year; everyone else has to build their voucher network from scratch.

i want to treat this as a mechanism, not ask whether its right in the abstract. what does it actually incentivize among residents, newcomers, and the people doing the vouching? how would it get gamed, voucher markets, cliques renting out their signatures, people cultivating a web of mutual obligations just so nobody dares withdraw? who gets frozen out and how, night-shift workers, housebound or disabled people, someone fleeing an abusive partner who cant maintain a public social front? what does the public registry turn into over time, a social credit score by another name? and does the grandfather clause just bake in whatever exclusions already existed? walk me through the second-order effects, and then tell me whether some version of this could keep the ubuntu insight, that personhood is something we do for each other, without turning into a machine for enforcing conformity, or whether the failure modes are built into the relational picture itself.
