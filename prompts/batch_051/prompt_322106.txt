I work on distributed systems, so I think about replication and mutation for a living, and I have been drawn to the cultural analogue. Human cultural transmission is noisy: stories drift, techniques lose detail, recipes get approximated, and small unconscious errors compound across generations. I want to isolate what that noise is actually doing.

Here is the counterfactual. Starting tomorrow, in every human population, whenever one person deliberately transmits a cultural item to another—a story, a melody, a recipe, a technical procedure, a legal formula, a ritual, a scientific claim—the recipient ends up with exactly the item the transmitter intended to pass on. There is no copying error, no unconscious drift, no accidental blending of two sources, and no loss of detail no matter how many times the item is passed on. Deliberate invention, deliberate recombination, and deliberate rejection remain fully possible; people can still choose among sources, lie about what they received, and accept items for self-serving reasons. Nothing else changes.

Predict what human culture looks like two centuries later. I want the mechanism visible, not just a story: which domains of culture would keep changing and which would freeze; whether cumulative refinement would accelerate because information no longer degrades, or collapse because the low-level variation generated by copying error is gone, leaving only whatever people can think up on purpose; how systems that currently seem to feed on drift—language, fashion, legal tradition, ritual—would adapt; and whether the world becomes more homogeneous or more sharply divided into deliberate schools. Be explicit about which predictions are robust given the counterfactual, which depend on background assumptions about how much real cultural change comes from copying error rather than intentional design, and what would have to be true in our own world for the strongest version of your prediction to be wrong.
