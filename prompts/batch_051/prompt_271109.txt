After forty years as an experimental biologist, I have been reading philosophy in retirement with more patience than I could manage during my working life, and I find myself detained by a passage in John Stuart Mill's *On Liberty*, Chapter III, "Of Individuality, as One of the Elements of Well-Being":

> If it were felt that the free development of individuality is one of the leading essentials of well-being; that it is not only a co-ordinate element with all that is designated by the terms civilization, instruction, education, culture, but is itself a necessary part and condition of all those things; there would be no danger that liberty should be undervalued, and the adjustment of the boundaries between it and social control would present no extraordinary difficulty.

I read Mill here as claiming something stronger than the familiar case for liberty of conscience: the free development of individuality is not merely one good alongside civilization, instruction, education, and culture, but a necessary part and condition of all of them. Yet in the paragraph immediately before this one, when Mill urges that "different experiments of living" should be given free scope so that "the worth of different modes of life" may be "proved practically," the argument seems to shift its centre of gravity. That sounds closer to the fallibilist logic I knew in the laboratory: keep several lines of inquiry alive because you cannot know in advance which will pay, and treat variety as an engine for discovering better forms of life rather than as something precious for its own sake. I am left uncertain which is Mill's real argument—the constitutive reading, on which a self-governed life is itself an ingredient of well-being, or the instrumental reading, on which individuality is chiefly a search procedure under ignorance. Nor is the question merely exegetical. If a person or a community could show now, with high confidence, that an unconventional mode of life is inferior to the customary one, would Mill's principles still give any reason to leave the experimenter alone? Or does the defence of individuality collapse into a bet on future social learning?

Write an essay on this controversy. Weigh what each reading explains in Mill's text and what each struggles with, and then defend a verdict rather than ending in mutual concession. A hybrid verdict is acceptable only if it states a principled rule about when each reading governs.
