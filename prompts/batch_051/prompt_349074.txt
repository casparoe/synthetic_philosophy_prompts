Last week in a reading group on the ethics of collective action, someone deployed "there's no such thing as a free lunch" in the middle of a debate about free riding, and I've been turning it over ever since. The debate was about whether it's wrong to enjoy the benefits of a functioning society—roads, scientific knowledge, herd immunity, the legal system—while contributing very little yourself (not paying much tax, not voting, not doing the unpaid work that keeps institutions going). The person who used the slogan said it basically closes the case: if you're consuming benefits without contributing, someone else is footing the bill for you, and that's parasitic. But that seems to bundle together a bunch of different claims. One is just an accounting point: every real benefit was produced at some cost to somebody. Another is a stronger empirical claim that my enjoying the benefit imposes a cost on particular other people. Another is a moral claim that taking without contributing is exploitative or unfair. Some of these might even be false when the good already exists and my using it doesn't take anything away from anyone else (the costs are sunk). What are the distinct precise readings of "no free lunch" that could be in play in that move, from the boringly true to the radical? And which of them would actually support concluding that the non-contributing person is doing something wrong? I'd like a menu of readings with verdicts on each, so I can go back next week and explain why the slogan is doing more than one possible job.
