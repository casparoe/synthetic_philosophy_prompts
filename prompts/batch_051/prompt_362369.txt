Before retiring, I spent my working life in the natural sciences, and optimization — in one guise or another — was never far from what I did. Since turning to philosophy of science I have been trying to get straight about the increasingly common claim that natural selection is an optimizer, or a poor one, or that gradient descent is what natural selection aspires to be. I built the following argument to settle my own thinking, and I now distrust it because its conclusion seems to prove too much.

(1) A process deserves to be called an optimizer only if there is a single, time-invariant objective function such that the process's successive moves can be explained as improving that objective.

(2) The only plausible candidate objective for natural selection is fitness.

(3) But fitness is not time-invariant, and not a fixed property of a genotype or trait: it changes with the frequencies of the other types in the population and with features of the environment, many of which are themselves products of the evolving population.

(4) Therefore natural selection has no single, time-invariant objective function.

(5) Therefore, by (1), natural selection is not an optimizer.

(6) The same reasoning reaches into machine learning. Gradient descent truly optimizes only when the loss is fixed and independent of the trajectory followed. But as soon as the loss depends on the current distribution of parameters, on data that earlier iterations selected or generated, or on a learned reward model — curriculum learning, model-generated data, population-based training, distribution shift — there is no single, time-invariant loss. By parity with (4) and (5), gradient descent in those settings is not an optimizer either.

(7) Since nearly every interesting case of learning and adaptation involves feedback of exactly this kind, the argument entails that almost nothing we ordinarily call an optimizer — except a thermostat and a few textbook cases — is one. That conclusion I cannot accept.

Yet each premise, taken singly, looks right to me. If forced, I would rather keep the requirement in (1) than surrender it for a permissive notion on which a process can be an optimizer relative to a moving, self-modified objective; that sounds like it drains the term of content. So: where does the argument go wrong, and what does giving up the offending premise, or the step that fails, actually cost?
