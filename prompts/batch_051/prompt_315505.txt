I want you to analyze the run on Northern Rock as a case study in common knowledge of rationality and the game theory of bank runs, and I want it to run both directions: the theory explaining the episode, and the episode stress-testing the theory.

Here's the episode as I understand it, so you don't need to look anything up. Northern Rock was a UK mortgage lender that had grown by funding long-term mortgages with short-term wholesale borrowing, not just retail deposits. When interbank markets froze in August 2007, that funding model stopped working; it went to the Bank of England for emergency liquidity support. When news of the support got out in mid-September, retail depositors started queueing outside branches to pull their money, and instead of the lender-of-last-resort announcement calming things down it made the run worse: people took the support itself as evidence the bank was dying. It was nationalised in early 2008.

Map this onto the coordination-game account of bank runs. I already know the baseline model: illiquid but solvent bank, demand deposits, the sequential-service constraint, and the bad equilibrium where everyone withdraws because everyone expects everyone else to withdraw. What I want is the gears-level version, not the summary you'd get from Investopedia:

- Who are the actual players, and what do their payoffs look like? Seperate retail depositors, wholesale funders, and the Bank of England; do they even prefer the same thing, or is the two-player payoff matrix hiding that?
- The weird thing about Northern Rock is that a policy tool that is supposed to eliminate the bad equilibrium (public last-resort lending, the central bank saying "we'll back the bank") seems to have created the common knowledge that picked out the bad equilibrium instead. Work through the higher-order beliefs. Before the announcement, what did a depositor know about what other depositors knew about the balance sheet? After it, what was common knowledge that hadn't been before? Try to state the shift as a change in common knowledge, not just "panic atmosphere."
- Then be honest about where the model breaks. Were the people in the queues doing anything like the rational thing the model describes, or were they watching TV footage of queues, confused about deposit insurance, and minimizing regret? Does the fact that depositors could withdraw online—so there wasn't only a physical first-come-first-served line—damage the sequential-service part of the story?
- Finally, what does the episode do to the theory? Is it a confirming case of multiple equilibria with a public signal selecting the bad one, an anomaly, or evidence that the common-knowledge-of-rationality machinery was solving the wrong problem all along? I want you to say explicitly where the model is silent, rather than forcing it to speak.
