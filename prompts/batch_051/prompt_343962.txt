I'm a novelist, not a forecaster, but the book I'm working on is set in a made-up empire in its long slide down — think Rome after the third century, except it's my own world so I dont need the particular dates to be true. A friend who works in insurance keeps telling me to take the outside view on my worldbuilding. His claim, as close as I cant pin it down, is: if you want to know how long an empire like mine has left, you shouldn't focus on its local politics, institutions, or the personality of its emperor; you should put it in a reference class of comparable empires and use their base rates. He says that if I told him my empire had held together for four centuries and had just lost a peripheral province to a revolt, he could give me a median remaining lifespan from similar cases, and that this rough forecast would beat the judgment of most historians who actually studied the place.

I'm torn. On one hand this sounds testable. On the other it dissolves exactly the stuff I write novels about — the specific pressures, the particular decisions, the texture of decline.

So here's what I want: how would you actually test the claim that an outside-view forecast from a reference class of similar empires beats, or at least adds information beyond, an informed inside-view judgment about a particular empire's remaining lifespan? Design the test concretely: what data would you need, how would you pick the reference class without gerrymandering it after the fact, what would count as the outside view winning, what would count as losing, and where do decisions about when an empire 'ends' or what counts as 'comparable' smuggle in the very judgment the outside view was supposed to replace? And then be honest about the residue. If the outside view won, would that show that local knowledge is useless here, or just that people are bad at point predictions and need base rates as a prior? Would it show empires are governed by base-rate regularities, or just that a crude checklist does okay on coarse outcomes? What would the test leave untouched even if it came out strongly one way?
