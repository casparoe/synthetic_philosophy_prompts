I am taking an upper-division seminar on the origins of social inequality, and next week I am leading the discussion on the debate over whether the Neolithic transition from foraging to farming should be understood as a mistake. The class has read the usual fault lines: the thesis associated with Marshall Sahlins that many foraging peoples were in an important sense affluent because their wants were modest and easily satisfied; the more sweeping claim that the adoption of agriculture was a net loss for human welfare until quite recently; the position that the real rupture was not cultivation itself but the storable grain surpluses that made durable hierarchy and states possible; and a progressivist reply that treats the transition as a costly but necessary precondition for the division of labor, cities, writing, and science.

For the session, I want to build a sorting instrument rather than another discussion question: ideally a decision tree with a short accompanying scoring key, or a questionnaire that feeds into such a tree, that assigns a classmate to one of the positions above on the basis of what they believe rather than which label they recognize. Please construct the tree from the logical space, not from a list of names. I care especially about three axes: the evaluative standard (individual welfare in health and leisure, equality, sheer numbers supported, or long-run cultural capability); the scope of the causal claim (all agriculture versus only state-friendly cereal agriculture); and the modal status of the transition (a free choice that could have gone otherwise versus a demographic or ecological ratchet). Write the branch nodes as concrete cases or claims a student can answer without already knowing the positions: for example, a vignette about a sedentary foraging group facing the decision to adopt cultivation, or a farming village two generations in whose population has grown past the point where returning to foraging is realistic.

Where a combination of answers is logically possible but no named position occupies it, say so and briefly diagnose why it is unoccupied, whether because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Also flag any positions the tree cannot fully separate; I suspect the original-affluence position and the worst-mistake position may collapse into each other unless an additional question distinguishes them, and I would rather have that limitation acknowledged than papered over. The target size is between six and eight leaves, with no more than five branch questions before a leaf, and the audience is undergraduates who have not read the primary literature, so any technical terms should be defined in passing.
