I spent my career as a natural scientist, mostly running an experimental group, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science with the attention I could not give it while I was practicing. The question I keep coming back to is whether elegance in mathematics and science is genuinely evidence of truth, or only a professional aesthetic that sometimes runs ahead of empirical success and sometimes against it. Rather than ask you to settle that in the abstract, I want you to analyze a concrete institution that would place a large standing bet on elegance, so that the incentives reveal where the practice would actually bend.

I sit on the board of a private research foundation that is considering the following scheme. Each year the foundation commits a fixed sum to an Elegance Fund for theoretical work selected solely for its elegance. Applications are anonymous and limited to three pages; they may not mention data, experimental support, technological promise, prior results, or the applicant's identity. Each application must state a problem, a formal or conceptual approach, and an explicit self-assessment of why the approach is elegant, using a published rubric with four dimensions: symmetry or invariance, economy of assumptions, unifying power, and surprise or depth. A rotating panel of twelve judges — theoretical scientists, mathematicians, philosophers of science, and at least two working artists or critics — rates applications on those four dimensions alone. The top tenth receives five years of unrestricted support, with no reporting requirement and no expectation of empirical payoff. The foundation retains all applications and scores, and after ten years a separate panel that cannot see the original scores classifies each funded project as a genuine advance, a productive dead end, or a failed project; the original judges are then named and their scores published beside those outcomes.

Walk through how this mechanism would actually behave. I want to know who would apply and with what kind of project; how applicants would adapt to the anonymity and the ban on empirical support; how judges would actually rate under the rubric; where the scheme would be gamed first and by whom; what it would do to the status of elegant work and to the ordinary funding lines that still demand empirical promise; and whether the predictable failure modes can be patched without turning the fund into something that no longer tests the underlying question. Treat it as a mechanism to be understood, not a proposal to be approved or rejected.

Please structure the response into the following sections, in this order, with approximate lengths:

1. Working model and stated rationale (150 words): restate the mechanism as you understand it, flagging the features that will most affect behavior.
2. Incentives for applicants (300 words): what a rational researcher does differently under this fund — in problem choice, framing, and follow-up work.
3. Incentives for reviewers and the retrospective panel (250 words): who volunteers to judge, how the rubric and the eventual publication of scores shape their ratings, and what norms emerge among judges.
4. Gaming strategies (300 words): specific exploits, including laundering empirically motivated work as purely formal, rubric overfitting, and collusion, and which groups are best positioned to use them.
5. Second-order effects (300 words): knock-on effects on status hierarchies, on ordinary funding lines, on what early-career researchers choose to learn, and on what elegance comes to signal.
6. Failure modes and fixes (350 words): the worst likely outcomes, realistic patches (for example, calibration bonuses, mixed panels, or pre-registered success criteria), which patches would help, and which would introduce new games or defeat the scheme's purpose.
7. What this shows about elegance as evidence (200 words): what the likely behavior of this institution would establish, or fail to establish, about whether elegance is truth-conducive rather than merely heuristic or a matter of taste.
