I sit on the ethics committee at a memory-care facility, and I need to circulate a written opinion before Thursdays meeting. Our resident, L., is 71 and has advanced Alzheimer's. Twelve years ago, when she was fully competent and shortly after watching her own mother die of the same disease, she signed a detailed advance directive. It says that if she ever reaches a point where she cannot recognize her family or communicate in full sentences, she refuses artificial nutrition and hydration. It's unusually explicit on one point: if she should ever appear happy or content in that state, the directive says, that is not to count as a reason to override the refusal—because the woman writing it knew she might seem happy in that state and still did not want to live that way. Her daughter is her healthcare proxy. L. now meets the directive's criteria exactly: she doesn't recognize her daughter, speaks only a few stock phrases, but she eats with obvious pleasure when spoon-fed, brightens at music, and the staff describe her as content most days. Recently her swallowing has deteriorated; her physician says a feeding tube could keep her alive for years, whereas without it she will likely die within weeks. The daughter wants the tube. Her argument is not that the directive is invalid or coerced; she says the person who signed it no longer exists, and that the woman in front of us clearly wants to keep living and is still capable of joy. Several committee members find this persuasive; others say honoring the directive is exactly the point of having one.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before the meeting: a concrete recommendation—honor the directive or place the tube—with a time frame and a bottom line the facility can act on. Engage the daughter's position seriously, including her claim that the earlier self has no authority over this later person, rather than just reciting that valid directives are binding. Keep it under 700 words.
