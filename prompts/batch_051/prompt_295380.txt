I keep coming back to a thought experiment after reading about oaths in Greek religion, and I want someone to actually run it properly instead of hand-waving. The bit that caught me: the Styx was the one oath with real enforcement behind it, even for the gods. When a god swears falsely by the Styx they fall into a year of breathless sleep and then get cut off from the feasts and councils of the gods for nine more years, which is supposed to be why Zeus himself uses Styx-oaths when he wants a promise to stick. So the Greeks had a working, visible commitment device sitting right there in their mythology—and then never seem to ask what would happen if mortals could actually use it.

Here's the counterfactual. Starting around 500 BC, throughout the Greek-speaking world—a patchwork of city-states with written lawcodes, long-distance trade, and oaths already woven through court procedure and diplomacy—any mortal who swears a false oath by the Styx suffers the standard penalty immediately and visibly: a year-long deathlike sleep, then nine years of obvious divine disfavor (sacrifices fail, oracles won't answer them, that sort of thing). After the first few well-attested cases its common knowledge that this is automatic, applies equally to Greek or foreigner, slave or free, and can't be faked or interrupted, though it only triggers on a properly sworn, aloud oath in good form and, presumably, only on what the gods count as a false swearing. What actually happens over the next century? I want the mechanism-level story, not a novel: which existing institutions change first—contracts, loans, treaties, marriage, trial procedure—and which turn out to be surprisingly robust to it? Do people engineer around it (hedged formulas, swearing by proxies, written oaths, a market for professional oath-takers), and does that mostly preserve or mostly unravel oath-taking as an institution? Does everyday social life get better because promises are now credible, or worse because casual oaths become too expensive to ever say out loud? Where you have to guess, say so, and be explicit about the hinges that would flip the story—whether the gods track intent or objective breach, whether evasive wording slips through, whether everyone knows the exact boundary of "false." And at the end, flag the single prediction you're least confident in.
