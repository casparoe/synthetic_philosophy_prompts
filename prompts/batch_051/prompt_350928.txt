I am a graduate student in metaphysics, and I keep returning to the presentism–eternalism debate in the philosophy of time because it seems to me a case study in a dispute that has gone on for decades without either side moving. The positions are familiar: presentists hold that only present objects and events exist, and that this is the only view that takes the felt passage of time and the specialness of the present seriously; eternalists hold that past, present, and future objects and events all exist tenselessly, and that this is the only view that fits the physics of spacetime, which gives no frame-independent present. The striking thing to me is that both sides accept the same physics, agree on the truth of ordinary tensed statements, and disagree only about what the world has to contain for those statements and that physics to be true. The internal costs are also well rehearsed: presentism struggles to say what makes true claims about the past and future true when only the present exists; eternalism struggles to explain why the present seems privileged without covertly reintroducing the moving spotlight it officially rejects. My hypothesis is that the debate has stalled because the two sides are not answering a single question. The presentist is answering the question of what the world must be like if time really passes in the way it seems to; the eternalist is answering the question of what the physics of spacetime already commits us to, treating the experience of passage as an indexical feature of our location in the block. If that is right, much of the back-and-forth is a mismatch of explanatory targets rather than a head-on ontological disagreement — but I worry this diagnosis is too deflationary, because many presentists and eternalists plainly take themselves to be contradicting each other about one fact: whether non-present things exist. I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement rather than a survey of positions or a verdict on who wins. In particular: is this a substantive ontological disagreement, a verbal dispute about the words exist or real, a difference in explanatory standards, a bedrock clash of intuitions about the privilege of the present, a hidden empirical or formal question, or two groups answering different questions under a single name? And what, if anything, would actually count as progress given that diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or a reason to expect none? Do not present a contested reading as the consensus: where the field is genuinely unsettled, say so plainly and separate what is settled from what is disputed among people who work on the topic.
