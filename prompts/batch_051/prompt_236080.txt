I have been arguing with a friend about whether personal identity over time can be analyzed in terms of memory, and we have worked ourselves into a knot about what exactly Reid's brave-officer objection shows. My friend thinks the objection refutes only the crude version of the memory criterion—the one that requires direct memory—and that the core idea survives once we allow chains of overlapping memories. I think the chain repair is substituting a different relation and merely restoring formal transitivity, and I reconstructed the dispute as the following numbered argument. I want to know whether the argument is valid and, if not, exactly where it goes wrong. The steps feel individually plausible to me, so I want a surgical diagnosis: identify the step or steps that fail, say whether the failure is an equivocation, a false dilemma, a scope shift, or something else, and say whether the argument does genuine damage to the chain repair or to the memory criterion itself.

(1) On the memory criterion as we have been using it, a later person-stage is the same person as an earlier one just in case the later stage's consciousness reaches back to the earlier one's experiences—that is, just in case the later stage directly remembers the earlier one's experiences from the inside.

(2) Reid's brave-officer case then puts the criterion under strain. The old general directly remembers the officer's taking the enemy standard, and the officer directly remembers being flogged as a boy for stealing apples; but the general does not directly remember the flogging. So the direct-memory criterion yields: general = officer, officer = boy, and general ≠ boy—which cannot all be true, because identity is transitive.

(3) The obvious repair is the chain criterion: two stages are the same person if there is a finite chain of stages between them, each adjacent pair linked by direct memory.

(4) The chain criterion handles the case: the general and the boy count as the same person because the chain runs through the officer.

(5) But this verdict arrives precisely where the Lockean condition is absent. Nothing in the general's consciousness directly reaches back to the flogging; the boy's punishment belongs in the officer's consciousness, not the general's.

(6) So the chain criterion saves formal transitivity only by abandoning the thing that was supposed to do the identity-making work—a single consciousness extending back to the earlier experience. It quietly substitutes "connected by a series of overlapping bits of consciousness" for "being one and the same consciousness."

(7) Therefore the chain repair is not a rescue of the memory criterion but a replacement of it, and Reid's target remains standing: one must either give up the transitivity of identity, which is unacceptable, or give up the memory criterion.

I suspect the weak point is (5), on the grounds that it may beg the question by insisting on direct reach when the whole point of the repair was to let consciousness extend through intermediate stages. But if (5) is innocent, I do not see what blocks (6). Am I right to locate the failure there, does the real problem sit elsewhere, or is the argument valid?
