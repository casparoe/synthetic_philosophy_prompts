ok so in my gov class we were reading John Stuart Mill's Considerations on Representative Government, the chapter where he talks about the mode of voting. He makes this argument that voting is not really a private right, its a public trust. Like he says a voter should act as a trustee for the common good, not use the vote for his own interest, and that he should vote as if he were the only voter and the whole election depended on his judgment alone. He even compares it to being a juror: you dont get to vote your personal wishes, you have a duty. And because of this he thinks voting should be open and not secret, so people are held accountable as trustees.

Anyway my two friends got into this whole argument about it after class. I'll try to recap it because I want to know who's actually right at each point.

Alex said Mill basically proves that you should vote for the lesser evil. If you're a trustee for the public good, then in a two-party system where one of two candidates will win, you have a duty to pick the one who will do the least harm to everyone, even if you dont like them, because that's what a trustee would do. You dont get to use your vote as self-expression.

Sam said no, that's actually backwards. Mill says vote according to your best judgment of the public interest, not your private interest. If your best judgment is that a third-party candidate has the right policies, then Mill says vote for them, even if they have no chance, because you're supposed to vote as if you were the only voter and the election depended on you alone. That thought experiment literally kills the lesser-evil argument: if your vote were the only one, you'd just pick the best candidate, period.

Alex said that's exactly the problem with Sam's reading. If you're a trustee, you have to consider the actual consequences of your action. In a real election, a vote for a candidate who can't win has no effect on the public good; it just makes a statement. A trustee doesn't get to make expensive statements with someone else's money. So the only-voter thing is about not free-riding on other people doing the moral thinking, not about ignoring the strategic situation.

Sam said if you're going to bring in actual consequences, then you should also consider that voting for the lesser evil signals to the party you voted for that they can keep moving away from you, and that has long-term public costs. Mill didnt say trustees have to be short-sighted. And anyway, if you treat voting as purely strategic, you're turning it into a game theory problem, not a public trust. A juror doesn't vote guilty because the other jurors are going that way; he votes based on the evidence.

So I want someone to go through there exchange and say who is right about what. Specifically: is the lesser-evil interpretation of Mill correct? Does the only-voter thought experiment actually rule out strategic voting? And is Sam's point about long-term signaling compatible with Mill's trustee idea, or is that sneaking self-expression back in? I'm not asking who to vote for in my life; I just want the argument adjudicated point by point.
