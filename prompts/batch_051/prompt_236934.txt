I teach a philosophy of mathematics course that spends a week on Greek arithmetic, and a student sent me a clip from a podcast that summarizes Euclid's notion of a unit like this: "For Euclid, a unit is just the number one — the indivisible atom out of which all other numbers are built. Every number is a heap of these units, so his whole theory of numbers is really just elaborate counting. Because numbers are built from indivisible building-blocks, Euclid couldn't treat zero, fractions, or irrational quantities as numbers, which is why the Greeks shoved all of those off into geometry." I then looked up the passage they were paraphrasing. Book VII of the Elements opens: "A unit is that by virtue of which each of the things that exist is called one. A number is a multitude composed of units." My own sense is that the podcast summary is half right and half caricature, but I don't fully trust my feel for the Greek context and I'd rather not pass a sloppy impression on to my class. Could you go through the summary sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a careful historian of ancient mathematics would accept, which parts they'd wince at, and whether the bit about zero, fractions, and irrationals actually follows from the parts that are accurate?
