So I've been rereading the old LessWrong archives and ended up down the rabbit hole of the fast vs slow takeoff debate, and it's striking how little the arguments have moved in, like, fifteen years even as actual capabilities have gone way up. The fast-takeoff people basically describe intelligence as a software problem: once a system can read and improve its own code, each improvment makes it better at making the next improvement, so you get a feedback loop where the jump from human-level to superhuman happens in days or weeks, before outside actors can react. The slow-takeoff people argue that even a very smart system still has to run experiments in real time, wait for chips to be fabricated, gather data from the world, and deal with physical bottlenecks, so the returns to each round of self-improvement shrink and what you get looks more like an economic transition dragging on for years or decades. Both sides seem to accept pretty much the same empirical facts about scaling laws and compute trends, but they slot them into totally different pictures.

What's weird to me is that the disagreement doesn't look like a normal empirical one. People claim it's about whether intelligence has diminishing returns at the high end, but neither side has a way to test that before the thing happens, and both sides cite the same historical cases (agriculture, the Industrial Revolution, evolution, Moore's law) as support. My current hypothesis is that they're mostly arguing past each other: "takeoff" is being used to mean an algorithmic event, an economic event, and a capability threshold all at once, and the real disagreement is about what the right reference class is for a one-off event. But I could also see it being a substantive disagreement about how much of intelligence is software versus how much is wedded to physical experimentation, or about how much weight to put on inside-view models versus base rates from history. I'd like a diagnosis, not a survey: what kind of disagreement is this actually, and is there anything — a distinction, a model, a bounded empirical question — that would move either side, or is this just what an unfalsifiable disagreement looks like until someone builds the thing?
