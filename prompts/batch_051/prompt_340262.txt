In the chapter "Of the Government of Dependencies by a Free State" in his Considerations on Representative Government, John Stuart Mill argues that a free state may govern a dependency despotically when the dependency is, in his terms, not yet fit for self-government, but only on the condition that the despotism is conducted for the improvement of the governed and directed toward raising them to the point where self-government becomes possible. He treats a people's right to its own government as something that can be held in abeyance while it is still, in his view, in an earlier stage of civilization.

Write a dialogue between a defender of Mill's position and a critic who thinks the criterion Mill offers is too accommodating in practice. The defender should argue that Mill's condition is a real constraint: rule must be for the benefit of the governed, and must be judged by whether it actually develops the local capacity for self-government, not by the ruler's intentions or by the assertion that the time is not yet ripe. The critic should press the opposite view: that the condition has no independent bite, because whoever rules a dependency can describe whatever they are doing as tutelage, and "not yet fit" is a judgment made from the ruler's own standpoint with every incentive to postpone the test indefinitely. Have them engage directly with each other's arguments, rather than talking past each other, and make neither character a strawman.
