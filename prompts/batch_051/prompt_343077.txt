Over the past several months I have been working through David Lewis's account of convention, and I have arrived at an argument from it that strikes me as far too tidy, although I cannot pinpoint where it goes wrong. I would like to know whether the argument is valid and, if not, which step or transition fails and how that step ought to be restated. I am using a stripped-down version of the account. A regularity R in a group is a convention when (i) everyone conforms to R, (ii) everyone expects everyone else to conform to R, (iii) each person prefers to conform to R on the condition that the others do, and (iv) R is arbitrary in the sense that some alternative regularity R* would have solved the same coordination problem had it become established instead.

Now consider any norm N that is a convention in this sense, and the following argument:

(1) If N is a convention, the group is at a coordination equilibrium: given the shared expectation that others follow N, each individual does best by following N.
(2) If each individual does best by following N, given what the others do, then no individual has a reason to depart from N unilaterally.
(3) If no individual has a reason to depart from N unilaterally, then no individual can reasonably complain that the group follows N rather than some alternative regularity.
(4) If no individual can reasonably complain about N, then N is unobjectionable for the members of the group, and its continued existence is at least a defeasible justification of it.
(5) Therefore, any stable convention is self-justifying: the bare fact that it persists is itself a reason for each participant to accept it.

I have a standing suspicion that this is a disguised move from 'is' to 'ought', or from the claim that an arrangement is an equilibrium to the claim that it is an optimum, but I cannot locate the precise point at which the normative conclusion slips in. Is the argument valid? If not, say exactly which step or transition fails, and give the strongest corrected version of that step.
