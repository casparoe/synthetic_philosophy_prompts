I'm setting the take-home essay for a third-year political philosophy module on exploitation, and I need a question on sweatshops that actually seperates the students who get the structure of the problem from the ones who've just memorised the usual "sweatshops are bad" talking points. They've already been through the standard moves in class — consent under poverty, living wages, the worry that boycotts hurt the very people they're meant to help — so the question should make reciting any of that insufficient.

The crux I want the essay to turn on is this. Call it the benefit defence: if a worker freely accepts a job and ends up better off than she would have been without it, then the exchange can't be seriously criticised as exploitative, whatever a middle-class observer thinks of the wage. A strong answer should have to explain why that defence is tempting — it treats the worker's actual alternative as the right baseline for judging the offer — and then attack it in ways that don't just fall back on "she wasn't really free" or on stipulating that the wage is below a living wage. I'd also like the question to include a further wrinkle, call it the intervention problem: even if the terms are exploitative, someone who blocks the exchange might leave the worker worse off, so the verdict "the terms are exploitative" and the policy "we should stop it" can come apart. What I'm after is a question that makes them take a stand on whether "mutually beneficial" and "exploitative" are genuinely compatible, and on what a fair baseline for judging an offer could even be.

Please draft (a) the essay question itself — it'll be answered closed-book in about 1,200 words, (b) a model answer of around 700 words written for the marker, and (c) a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves a first-class answer must make, which of those a 2:1 usually misses, and the particular mistakes that should cost marks. Everything in British English, and the question shouldn't ask for or reward citations or reading lists.
