I am a retired natural scientist—I spent most of my career in geophysics—and I have recently developed a serious interest in the philosophy of probability. I convene a monthly philosophy seminar, and next month the guest will be a philosopher who has devoted much of his career to defending the doomsday argument: roughly, the claim that our place in the sequence of all human beings who will ever live gives us reason to think that the total number will not be vastly greater than the number already born, and thus that human extinction may be considerably nearer than our ordinary estimates of risk suggest.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions for this evening. The audience will already have read a short statement of the argument, so none of the questions should merely ask him to restate it. I want questions that press the view where it is genuinely strained. Please include at least one question on each of the following, expressed without specialist jargon:

- the choice of reference class: why the relevant class should be exactly the set of beings the argument assumes—humans, sentient creatures, civilisations—and how much the conclusion moves if the class is drawn differently;
- the rival thought that one's own existence is itself evidence for there being many observers, which appears to cancel or reverse the doomsday shift;
- the worry that any earlier generation could have run the same argument from its own place in the sequence and mostly would have been wrong about its own impending end, so it is unclear what our own generation should conclude;
- the practical upshot: whether the argument should change how we weigh extinction risks, and how it should be combined with ordinary probabilistic estimates from areas such as nuclear or biological risk.

For each question, give the follow-up you would put if the guest gives the reply he is most likely to give. Order the questions so that later questions make his earlier lines of defence harder to reuse. Avoid biographical questions and any question that simply asks him to explain the argument.
