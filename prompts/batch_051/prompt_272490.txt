Last night I got into a long, circular argument with a friend in law school about how precise a criminal law has to be before it can count as valid. He was defending the void-for-vagueness doctrine as basically coherent: a statute fails when it doesn't give ordinary people fair notice of what's banned, or when it invites police and judges to fill in the edges case by case. I kept pushing back that almost every real statute has fuzzy edges — think reasonable force, dishonest appropriation, conduct likely to cause serious annoyance — and courts uphold those all the time, so the doctrine can't literally be about drawing a sharp line. Around midnight he said something that stuck with me: demanding that a law draw one sharp boundary is like demanding that a rational person have one sharp probability for every proposition. I haven't been able to get the comparison out of my head since.

The parallel, as I'd reconstruct it: in formal epistemology there's a live argument about whether a rational agent's degrees of belief must be representable by a single precise probability function or whether a set of probability functions — imprecise credences, credal sets — can be the fully rational state, especially when the evidence is sparse or messy. In law, a standard that's too vague gets struck down as void. So both domains seem to be asking how much precision a norm needs before it can guide conduct, but they answer in what look like opposite directions: the imprecise-probability crowd thinks forcing a single sharp credence can misrepresent the evidence, since the set might be the honest state, while the vagueness doctrine treats a standard that can't be drawn sharply as no law at all. I'm genuinely unsure wether this is a deep structural analogy with the normative sign flipped, or just two fields using words like 'precise' and 'vague' in ways that don't really touch.

Could you work through where the mapping between legal vagueness and imprecise credences holds up and where it snaps?
