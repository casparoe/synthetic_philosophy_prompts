I've been trying to work out whether delusions are beliefs—the doxasticism-versus-anti-doxasticism fight—and I keep flip-flopping between two strong pulls.

On the theory side, the two-factor account of monothematic delusions feels like the best story going. First factor: some neuropsychological glitch produces an odd experience or thought—in Capgras, a familiar face no longer carries the usual autonomic jolt, so your spouse feels alien. Second factor: a failure of the system that would normally reject implausible thoughts, so instead of shrugging it off, you accept that she's been replaced. What I like about this is that it explains why delusions have the specific contents they do, why the content tracks the underlying glitch, and why the person doesn't just dismiss it the way I do when my brain burps up something weird. It also keeps one unified notion of belief: delusions are just beliefs with an unusual causal history. That tidiness is genuinely attractive.

On the intuition side, though, I can't shake the feeling that this streches "belief" past the point where it's doing any work. Beliefs are supposed to regulate affect, inference, and action. But there's this double-bookkeeping quality to so many delusions: the Capgras patient who insists her husband has been replaced by an impostor, then makes the "impostor" lunch and signs his birthday card with real warmth; the Cotard patient who says she is dead but still minds the ward being too cold and asks for another blanket. If a state is that quarantined from the rest of her cognitive life, I want to say it isn't a belief—it's an avowal, a vivid imagining, a label stuck onto an uncanny feeling. And if it doesn't do belief-work, then "she believes it" isn't carrying explanatory weight.

So I'm caught. Walk me through the negotiated verdict. Should I debunk the intuition as a folk-psychological over-reaction that demands more consistency from beliefs than ordinary beliefs actually have? Should I amend the two-factor theory so the second factor produces something belief-like but not fully doxastic, or go for a degrees-of-belief or fragmentation view? Is there a reading on which these are compatible, where delusions count as beliefs in a minimal or partial sense and the argument is partly verbal? What would justify each move, and what does each one buy and cost?
