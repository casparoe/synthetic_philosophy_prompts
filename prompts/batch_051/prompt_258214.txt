Epistemologists have debated for decades whether assertion is governed by a knowledge norm, a truth norm, or a belief norm. The three accounts agree on a wide range of ordinary cases but part ways over the harder ones: lottery propositions, lucky true guesses, sincere false assertions, and assertions made on expert authority without corresponding belief. Each camp has a well-rehearsed way of discounting the cases the others treat as central, and the literature has largely settled into an exchange of conflicting intuitions. My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is not best explained as a first-order dispute about which norm is correct, but as a disagreement about the structure of the inquiry—whether the word assertion picks out a unified speech act that could bear a single constitutive norm, whether the data should be ordinary judgments of propriety or the conversational function of assertion, and whether the three candidate norms make sufficiently different predictions for the choice to matter. I want a diagnosis, not a survey or a verdict. Is this a verbal disagreement, a difference in starting intuitions, a difference about what counts as data, or a hidden empirical or formal question—or is it simply a hard substantive question after all? Given the diagnosis, what would genuine progress look like: a new kind of case, an experimental result, a formal constraint, a sharper distinction—or should we conclude that no progress is to be expected, and why?
