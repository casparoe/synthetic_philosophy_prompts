Ok so a friend and I just spent an hour arguing about Parfit's hitchhiker and we couldnt settle it, so I'm bringing it here. Heres the original as I remember it: you're dying of thirst in the desert, a driver stops and says he'll rescue you only if you promise to pay him a thousand dollars once you're back in town. You're a rational egoist and the driver knows it, so he figures that once you're safe you'll have no reason to pay, and he drives off. Parfit's move is that you should make yourself into someone who keeps promises even when it's against your self-interest to keep them — the trustworthy disposition pays for itself, so its rational to be in a certain sense "irrationally" reliable.

My friend thought that shows you should try to become an actual promise-keeper. I thought the case only works because the driver has no way to secure the debt, and a hostage would do the same thing without any self-modification. So here's the variant I want yu to work through carefully.

Suppose the hitchhiker is carrying one thing the driver can hold as collateral: a little heirloom locket with zero resale value but huge personal value to him — the only thing left from his grandmother, say, and he'd never willingly part with it. The driver says: give me the locket before the ride, and I'll give it back only after you pay the thousand dollars in town; if you don't pay, I keep it. The driver has no use for the locket and doesn't want to hurt the guy, he just wants his money. Assume he can tell how much the locket matters to the hitchhiker, and both sides are honest about the return-clause.

Now: does a rational egoist pay up in town, and should the driver trust him? Work out the exact condition on how much the locket is worth to the hitchhiker, relative to the thousand dollars, for the promise to become credible without the hitchhiker changing his dispositions at all. Then drop the assumption that the driver can tell, and say whether the whole result depends on it, and what (if anything) the hitchhiker could do to make the locket's value to him credible.

Then the part we actually fought about: does the "make yourself into a promise-keeper" conclusion survive this version? Is Parfit's move just one credibility technology among others — a second-best for when youve got nothing to post as a hostage — or does it still do something the collateral can't? If the locket does all the work, whats left of the rational-irrationality point; and if there are cases where you still need the disposition, what exactly has to be missing from the collateral situation to make it needed again?
