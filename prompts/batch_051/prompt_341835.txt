I've ended up a dialetheist — not just a paraconsistent logician in the weak "keep explosion out of the logic" sense, but genuinely of the view that some contradictions are true, with the liar and its relatives as my main motivation. I've made some peace with what this costs: no disjunctive syllogism in full generality, no modus ponens as a general rule for the material conditional, all that. What I want is the honest case against it. Which formal results, paradoxes, or cases should a committed dialetheist treat as genuinely counting against the view, and which are the ones critics trumpet but defenders can reasonably shrug off? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, including anything that has already come close. I've heard the real threat is supposed to be Curry and its cousins rather than the plain liar, because a paraconsistent logic can absorb contradictions but not arbitrary triviality — tell me how much weight that should carry. And I want an honest verdict on whether the view has been insulated: do the standard dialetheist moves about negation, about truth being inconsistent at the limits, about "true contradiction" meaning something else than the rest of us mean, amount to making nothing count against it? If so, is that a problem for a view of this kind? Also: if the deepest objection is that "true contradiction" doesn't mean what ordinary speakers mean by "contradiction" and the whole thing is a verbal redefinition, explain why that's substantive rather than a quibble. American English, please.
