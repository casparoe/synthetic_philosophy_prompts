Here's the working definition of societal collapse I've been using, built mostly from the three standard cases (Late Bronze Age, the Classic Maya lowlands, and Easter Island as it's usually told). A society counts as collapsed when it satisfies all of these:

(i) a large drop in population over a fairly short window,
(ii) a loss of political complexity, going from a multi-tiered hierarchy with regional elites to something like small autonomous villages,
(iii) abandonment of major monumental centres and the public works that went with them,
(iv) loss of specialised knowledge or technologies it previously had, like a script, a craft, or long-distance voyaging,
(v) and the whole thing happens on its own, before and independent of conquest, epidemics, or incorporation by an outside power.

Now run Easter Island/Rapa Nui through that definition, but using the revisionist account rather than the Diamond-style one. On that account the deforestation is driven more by seed-eating rats and a tiny founding population on a fragile island than by people cutting down the last tree to drag a statue. The moai-building does stop and gets replaced by the birdman cult at Orongo before Europeans arrive, but that reads more as a shift in ritual and authority then as a loss of social complexity. The catastrophic population decline comes mostly after 1722, especially with the Peruvian slave raids and introduced disease, not from a pre-contact ecocide. And there's decent evidence of adaptation afterwards, like the rock gardens used to farm a deforested island.

So the question: on the revisionist account, does Rapa Nui still qualify as a collapse under my five criteria? If it doesn't, which criterion is doing all the real work? Does the case show that criterion (v) is smuggling in the idea that collapse has to be self-inflicted, so the same society could meet (i)–(iv) and still not be called a collapse when the trigger is external? Or does it show that 'collapse' is bundling together two things I should keep separate: a demographic/ecological crash on one hand, and a political and ritual reorganization that only looks like failure from the outside?
