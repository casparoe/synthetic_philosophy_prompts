I’ve been trying to pin down when introspection turns into something harmful. My working criteria for useful self-examination are: (a) it’s answerable — the question can actually be settled with the evidence you have; (b) it has an exit condition, so it stops when you reach a decision or change a behavior, not just when you run out of steam; (c) it stays specific and situational (“what did I mishandle in that meeting”) rather than global (“what is wrong with me”); and (d) it’s able to come up empty, so it doesn’t treat the fact that a thought keeps returning as proof that it’s deep. Rumination, as I’m using it, is self-examination that violates most of these: it loops, it goes global, it asks questions you can’t answer, and it treats recurrence as insight.

Now the borderline case. A friend of mine does a nightly “moral inventory.” She writes down every interaction from the day where she suspects she was selfish or insensitive, and for each one asks why she did it, what it says about her character, and what she’ll do differently. She’s done this for years, has a whole taxonomy of her own recuring patterns, and says it’s painful but she’d be a worse person without it. On my criteria this sits right on the edge. It’s specific (situational), it has an exit condition (the night’s list ends, and each item gets a “do differently” line), and it’s capable of finding nothing on a given day. But it’s also repetitive, it keeps asking global “what does this say about me” questions, and she admits the insights haven’t felt new for a long time — she mostly rediscovers the same three or four patterns.

So is her moral inventory useful self-examination, rumination, or a third thing my criteria can’t see? If the criteria leave it on the edge, does that mean I’m missing a dimension — like time horizon, or whether the practice actually changes later behavior — or is the useful/rumination split just too clean?
