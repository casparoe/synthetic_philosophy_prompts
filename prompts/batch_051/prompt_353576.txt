Here are the criteria I take to justify a statute of limitations for a serious offense, understood as a moral matter rather than as the law of any particular jurisdiction:

1. Evidentiary decay: with the passage of time, memories fade and physical evidence degrades, so the risk of convicting the innocent grows.
2. Repose: a person should eventually be able to arrange their life without a permanent, open-ended threat of prosecution.
3. Reform: a long period of lawful conduct after the offense is itself morally significant.
4. The state's waning interest: the deterrent and denunciatory force of punishment weakens as the temporal connection between act and punishment stretches thin.

Now consider this case. In 1985, a 28-year-old engineer signs off on a chemical storage design that he privately knows violates the relevant safety standards. The facility begins leaching solvents into the groundwater almost immediately, but at concentrations too low for the detection methods of the time. For decades, no one detects anything. In 2024, a new monitoring technique reveals the plume, and epidemiological work links it to a cluster of cancers in a nearby town first observed a few years earlier. The evidence against the engineer is now far stronger than it would have been in, say, 1990: the design documents have been digitized and archived, the supplier's records are intact, and the plume chemistry is more diagnostic than any contemporaneous witness would have been. The engineer is now 67, retired, and has an otherwise spotless record. He knew the design was deficient at the time, but he never came forward, corrected it, or reported it; instead he has lived quietly for nearly four decades, supporting his family and doing volunteer work.

Set aside what any actual jurisdiction's statute would say. Does the engineer's 1985 act fall within the moral rationale for a limitations period, or outside it? Work through each of the four criteria, and for each one say whether it pulls toward or against a limitations defense here. Where a criterion underdetermines the verdict, say whether that reveals a defect in the criterion or a genuine moral impasse. Then reach an overall verdict — should the moral case for a statute of limitations protect this engineer from prosecution now? — and finish with one sentence on how, if at all, you would amend the criteria.
