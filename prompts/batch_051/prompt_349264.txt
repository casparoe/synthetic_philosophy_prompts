Since I started my PhD in modern European history, I have followed a rule about causal-historical questions. When a seminar or a draft paper asks who started something—the Cold War is the case I encounter most—I refuse to answer the question as posed. I believe "who started it" runs together at least four tasks: fixing an origin or first move, asserting a counterfactual about what would otherwise have happened, establishing which actions were necessary or sufficient or how much each contributed, and assigning blame. A senior colleague says this is evasion: historians answer questions like this all the time with hedged, evidence-based judgments of responsibility, and my rule lets ordinary grammar dictate metaphysics. Is my rule defensible? If it is, specify when it applies and what, if anything, a historian may do with the vulgar question; if it is not, restate it so it keeps what is right in it without the evasion. Then commit: is "who started the Cold War" a defective question, a legitimate elliptical one, or something in between? Defend that verdict using the Cold War as the running case, and state and answer what you regard as the strongest objection to your own position.
