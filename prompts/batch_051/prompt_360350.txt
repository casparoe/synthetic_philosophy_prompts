The people coming are fiction writers, mostly, and I'd bet my advance that none of them have read Capital. The theme they gave me is "labour and work in fiction," but what I actually want to do is teach one idea: that the wage-form hides exploitation, and that the alienation a character feels about their job is a symptom of that hidden structure rather than just a mood or a personality defect.

I want too build the whole thing around a passage from Marx's Capital, Volume One, chapter 19. Here it is:

"In the expression “value of labour,” the idea of value is not only completely obliterated, but actually reversed. It is an expression as imaginary as the value of the earth. These imaginary expressions, arise, however, from the relations of production themselves. They are categories for the phenomenal forms of essential relations. That in their appearance things often represent themselves in inverted form is pretty well known in every science except Political Economy."

I'm not going to assign readings or turn this into a seminar on Marxist economics. I want a teaching plan, not the explanation itself. Help me sequence it so the quote doesn't feel like jargon but like a twist, like the moment the detective explains that the butler wasn't in the kitchen.

I need to know which of these to head off before the quote, which to let them voice and then dismantle after, and how the sequence should absorb the pushback without turning into a political argument.

What should I deliberately leave out at this level and say so?
