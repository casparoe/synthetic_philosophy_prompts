I keep trying to explain to a friend what an oath adds to a promise, and I've realized I can't even pin down what makes an oath binding in the first place. Give me a working definition of a binding oath — one that says which of the following are binding, and which are oaths at all, and why. I'll call something binding if, after you've taken it, you have a reason to follow through that isn't just "it would be nice to keep your promises": something closer to a debt you owe.

The cases:

- I promise a friend I'll pay him back next week (no swearing formula). I'm confident this is binding but I'm not sure it's an oath.
- The same promise, but I say "I swear to God I'll pay you back." My gut says the swearing adds something; I'd like to know what.
- An actor playing a character who swears an oath onstage. Clearly not binding.
- A kid swears on her life to clean her room, and she knows while she's saying it that she won't. Binding at all?
- A soldier forced at gunpoint to swear loyalty to an occupying army.
- A person swears to help a friend hide a body. Can an oath to do something wrong generate any real reason to do the thing? If not, is the oath still binding?
- Someone who doesn't believe in God or any supernatural witness swears "on my own honor" to show up to court.
- A Greek god swears by the river Styx. In the myths at least, even Zeus treats this as binding, and the reason looks mechanical: break a Styx oath and the punishment falls on the oath-breaker automatically. I don't know where this one goes. Either it's the purest case of binding force — if binding is about an outside enforcer — or it's exactly what binding isn't, if binding is about your internal commitment.

Propose the definition, run it through all eight, flag where it gives a wrong answer or no answer, and either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. And try to seperate what's settled from what people who study oaths genuinely disagree about; don't present one contested view as the consensus.
