I serve on the board of a small housing cooperative. We just approved a one-time special assessment for a roof replacement, and a minority of member-owners refuse to pay while continuing to use the shared facilities. Another board member keeps calling them parasites on the body of the cooperative: biological parasites take from the host and give nothing back, and if the host does not mount an immune response, they kill it; so, he argues, our enforcement rules are the immune system and we should use them to expel the non-payers. I can feel the rhetorical force of this analogy, but I cannot tell how much argumentative weight it can actually carry.

As a professional philosopher working on collective action and conceptual analysis, please audit it for me. Start by brainstorming possible correspondences, then produce an explicit mapping of what corresponds to what—a table is fine—and identify where the analogy breaks down. Please distinguish among biological cases, such as an acute pathogen, a chronic parasite that keeps its host alive, and a tolerated commensal, rather than treating parasite as one undifferentiated category. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the expulsion conclusion from those that are merely rhetorical, and say whether the underlying claim—that tolerating free riding tends to undermine a cooperative and that some enforcement is justified—survives once the disanalogies are admitted. If some other analogy would support that conclusion more honestly, say what it is; if none does, say that too. Write your answer in American English.
