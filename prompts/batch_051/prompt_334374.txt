I moderate a large technical question-and-answer community whose reputation system gives each user a karma score based mostly on how often their past answers were accepted or upvoted. For years I have followed a standing policy in how I handle corrections and disputes: when a new or low-karma account contradicts a factual claim made by a long-standing, high-karma contributor, I do not evaluate the claim on the merits. I assign the contradiction a low prior, treat the newcomer as likely confused, and move on unless a second established contributor vouches for the correction or the newcomer supplies evidence too specific to dismiss. I adopted this because I cannot open every dispute myself, and low-karma accounts are, in my experience, wrong more often than regulars on technical matters. Last month the policy failed in a way I cannot get out of my head: a first-time poster corrected a technical claim that three respected regulars had repeated, and I brushed it off, only to learn later that the regulars had all copied the same upstream error while the newcomer had checked the underlying system directly. I now cannot tell whether this was an acceptable cost of a rule that is still right on average, or whether I have dressed up status bias as an epistemic shortcut.

Is the policy defensible as a heuristic for forming beliefs under time pressure? Under what conditions—concerning how accurately karma tracks truth, how independent the regulars' errors are, and whether checking a claim myself is genuinely available—would it be justified? And what would a defensible restated version look like, one that keeps the benefit of screening out noise without systematically screening out corrections? Please treat this as a question about whether the rule works, not about fairness to new users, and write the answer in American English.
