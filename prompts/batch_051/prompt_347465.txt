I keep turning over a parallel between Hobbes's social contract and the way compilers and formal systems get grounded, and I'd like someone who knows both sides to tell me if it's genuine structure or just pattern-matching.

On the Hobbes side: in the state of nature covenants have no force, because each person keeps the right of nature to judge what conduces to their own preservation, and no one can be expected to perform first on the strength of a bare promise. The way out is for everyone to lay down their rights in favor of a sovereign who is not a party to the covenant. The sovereign stands outside the structure he enforces, and for Hobbes that outside position isn't a scandal but exactly what stops the regress: if the sovereign were a party to the agreement, there'd be no one left to hold him to it, and you'd need a further power above him, and so on. So the final enforcer has to be placed outside the compact he guarantees.

The same shape keeps showing up in more formal places. To get a compiler for language L written in L, you can't start from nothing: the first L compiler needs an already existing L compiler or an implementation in some other language. Once that exists the system can compile itself, but the chain still has to be grounded somewhere outside itself. And Gödel's second incompleteness theorem says a formal system that's consistent and strong enough can't prove its own consistency from the inside; any proof of its consistency has to use resources outside the system, and then that outside system raises the same question about itself. In each case you have something that works on a structure but can't be justified by the structure it works on, so the regress gets stopped by placing some ground outside it.

Here's where I worry the similarity is only skin-deep. The compiler case is diachronic: once you've bootstrapped, the L compiler is written in L and the outside starting point is no longer doing any work, whereas a Hobbesian would insist the sovereign can never be brought inside the covenant, no matter how settled things get, without falling back into the state of nature. And in the Gödel case the outside proof sits in a stronger system whose own consistency is then unproved, so there's no final top; Hobbes, by contrast, seems to want exactly one final top that is not upgrated or re-grounded. Are these just different ways of stopping the same kind of regress, or is the regress different in kind in each case? What exactly is doing the work in the analogy, and does the formal version predict anything about sovereignty that turns out false?
