I find myself both convinced by and repelled by the expected utility argument for cryonics. The theory is hard to resist: when probabilities are well defined, maximizing expected utility is the cleanest decision rule I know, and it gives a definite answer in every case. The application seems equally straightforward. Revival is not literally impossible, so the probability of success is not zero; the cost of a policy over a lifetime is finite and modest; and the payoff—additional decades or centuries of conscious life—is astronomically large. The expected value is therefore positive, and declining looks like refusing to act on one's own credences. Yet I cannot make myself believe that a careful reasoner should, on the strength of this argument alone, actually buy a policy. Something in me says this is Pascal's mugging in a lab coat: the enormous payoff does all the work, the probability is not small-but-known but radically unmeasurable, and treating 'not impossible' as if it were a usable probability confuses unbounded ignorance with risk. I am not asking for the empirical numbers, and I am not asking which side 'the literature' takes. I want a negotiated verdict: what would it take for the decision-theoretic argument to survive and the intuition to be debunked—say, as loss aversion or status-quo bias; what would it take for the theory to be amended or abandoned here—say, through bounded utility, a principled floor on probabilities, or rejecting fanaticism; and is there a reading of 'rational' on which the calculation and the reluctance are both tracking something? Please say how confident you are in the main claims and single out the point you are least sure of.
