I lead a five-person policy team at a large platform that hosts user-generated content, and I want to replace the way we resolve our hardest moderation cases with a reusable procedure built on reflective equilibrium. Once a month we get 90 minutes to clear a backlog of edge cases under three rules: a ban on graphic violence, a ban on coordinated harassment, and a ban on harmful health misinformation. Each rule has an open-textured exception or threshold — documentary or public-interest value, publicly available information, an identifiable false or misleading claim — and in practice we decide by a mixture of precedent, intuition, and anticipated press reaction. I have watched our group reach opposite conclusions on near-identical facts in different meetings. I sign the decisions as policy lead, legal reviews them for risk after the fact, and a VP who only sees a one-page summary can block a rule change; none of those people is in the room during the working meeting.

My working understanding of reflective equilibrium, roughly in Rawls's sense, is this: specific judgments about cases and general principles are both fallible; where they conflict, you revise whichever is less credible in light of the rest; you iterate until the set is coherent; and in wide reflective equilibrium you also bring in background beliefs about how the platform actually works, what harms are likely, and what the platform is for. The result is always provisional and revisable.

What I need from you is the operating version my team can run. Give me a decision procedure — a checklist or flowchart in words — with the following:

- The inputs the meeting needs in advance, who prepares them, and how a case gets into the backlog at all.
- The sequence of questions to ask in order, with the decision points explicit: when the procedure loops back, when it revises the rule, when it revises or discounts the case judgment, and when it stops and escalates on the ground that the remaining step is a normative choice rather than an application.
- An anti-rationalization step: how to tell a principled exception from a post hoc patch, and where the anchors outside the two moving parts come from so the procedure cannot just reproduce whatever we already wanted to do.
- What counts as a sufficient answer to each question, given that no one in the room has formal philosophy training and we cannot extend the meeting.
- The points where judgment cannot be eliminated, and which named role should make it: the policy lead, the legal reviewer, the VP, or the full committee.

The deliverable should be usable by a new team member reading only the page you write.

Then apply the procedure to these three cases from our backlog. Be explicit about each turn of the loop rather than jumping to a verdict. At least one application should produce an answer my team will not like; identify it and say why the procedure commits us to it even though our current method would not have.

1. A user posts unedited footage of a violent police encounter in which a person is seriously injured, with no commentary beyond a timestamp and location. Our graphic-violence rule has an exception for documentary or public-interest value. Half the team reads the exception as covering exactly this; the other half says the exception cannot mean any real violence with news value, because then the rule is empty.

2. A popular creator has spent months posting clips that never assert a false medical claim outright but ask pointed questions about a widely used treatment, spliced with anecdotes from people who say it harmed them, leaving viewers with the clear overall impression that the treatment should be avoided. Our health-misinformation rule requires an identifiable false or misleading claim. External pressure is building to treat measurable viewer harm as sufficient for a violation even though no single clip crosses the literal line.

3. A user in a long-running personal dispute reposts publicly available court filings about another private individual, including an old address and details of a family dispute, with commentary that does not directly threaten the person but tells readers to look into them. Our harassment rule has an exception for publicly available information.

Close with an honest note on what the procedure cannot do: which steps are real normative work dressed as a mechanical test, what the procedure would misfire on, and what information a person running it would need but may not have.
