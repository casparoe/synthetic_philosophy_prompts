You are a philosopher of science who works on the epistemology of prediction, especially long-run and catastrophic forecasts. I am an economist, and I have been reading a series of historical controversies that all seem to exhibit the same move, and I want to know whether the move itself has a name and a precise analysis.

The clearest case is Malthus. He argued, on what looked like simple arithmetic, that population tends to grow faster than the food supply, so any rise in living standards would be checked in the fairly near term by rising mortality or falling fertility. In nineteenth-century Britain, roughly the opposite happened: sustained growth, industrialization, imported food, rising population, and rising real wages at the same time. Defenders did not abandon the underlying argument. They said it was right but merely early: the escape was real but temporary and special, made possible by fossil energy and the opening of new lands, and the long-run logic still held even if the reckoning had been postponed rather than cancelled. I then saw the identical pattern in the twentieth-century debates over resource exhaustion and overpopulation: dramatic predictions tied to rough dates failed on schedule, and were reissued with later dates and an extra clause explaining why the earlier failure should not count. Each clause was individually reasonable, which is exactly what makes the pattern hard to criticize.

I am not asking for a bibliography, and I am not satisfied with a label by itself. What I want to know is whether this specific maneuver has an established name. I know there are broad concepts for theories being shielded from evidence, and the word unfalsifiable occurs to me, but it feels too blunt, because the theory does make predictions; they just keep getting rescheduled, and each failed timetable is absorbed as a special case rather than counted as a disconfirmation. I also want to know how this differs from the ordinary use of auxiliary assumptions in good science, from legitimate cases where a theory is genuinely right but early because an unforeseen factor really did delay the mechanism, and from plain moving of the goalposts. If the established term exists, I want a definition and a careful account of its boundaries; if no single term fits, I would like you to coin one and define it precisely, including tests for when a rescheduled prediction is still doing real theoretical work and when it has become a way of never being wrong. Please start by brainstorming several candidate concepts before settling on your best diagnosis, and then give me the distinctions as cleanly as you can.
