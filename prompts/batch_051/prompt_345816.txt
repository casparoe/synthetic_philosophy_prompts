Last night a friend and I argued for hours about generics — statements like "ducks lay eggs" and "mosquitoes carry malaria" — and his whole position rested on a single analogy. He maintained that a generic is just a statistical generalization with the quantifier left implicit: "ducks lay eggs" says, in effect, that most ducks, or most ducks of the right sort, lay eggs, and the oddity of "mosquitoes carry malaria" when almost no individual mosquito does is merely a matter of choosing the correct reference class and threshold. His conclusion was that there is no distinct phenomenon of genericity to explain, only loose talk about frequencies, and that the philosophical puzzles about generics are manufactured.

Audit his analogy as an argumentative instrument. Lay out the exact correspondences he is relying on — what plays the role of the population, the quantifier, the threshold, the property, and the evidence in the generic as he reads it. Then show where the mapping breaks, separating the disanalogies that genuinely threaten his reductive conclusion from any that are only superficial. Identify what the statistical picture quietly imports that generics lack — about exceptions, about whether the statement predicates something of individual members or of the kind itself, and about prevalence as the measure of truth. Finally, say whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and what a better comparison would be if one exists.

Give your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. The five items should cover, in order: the correspondence he assumes; the first disanalogy that matters; a second disanalogy that matters; what the analogy smuggles in; and your verdict on whether the conclusion survives, with any better analogy.
