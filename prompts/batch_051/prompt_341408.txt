Here's an asymmetry in how we treat photographs that's been bugging me, and its really a question about whether photos are transparent—whether we see through them—or whether we just have inconsistent habits.

A photojournalist who deepens the shadows after the fact, composites out a bystander, or moved the water bottle before pressing the shutter gets treated as having faked the image. The photo is pulled, their career takes a hit, everyone says the photo lies. But the same photographer on the same day can choose the long lens that compresses a scattered crowd into a menacing mob, shoot from a low angle so a sparse protest looks huge, underexpose for menace, and pick the one second when fists are up instead of the five minutes when people are laughing—and those choices generally count as craft, not deception, even though they change what the image says at least as much as the cloned-out bystander. And zoom out one more level: a drawing or painting of the same event, made from imagination or from someone else's description, isn't treated as a lie at all; nobody expected it to be evidence.

So the asymmetry is: photographs are treated as transparent traces of the scene—the scene itself caused the image, the way a shadow or a fossil is caused—so tampering with the image after the fact is a scandal, while selecting the lens, angle, exposure, and moment beforehand is fine, and a hand-made image isn't held to that standard in the first place. Is there one principle—something about the causal chain of light, about the photo being of the scene in a way a drawing isn't—that justifies all of that at once, and draws the line exactly where practice draws it, between moving objects and framing, between photo and drawing? Or does the principle either let too much in (the telephoto mob shot, the staged water bottle) or exclude too little (the heavily processed photo that is only partly a trace)? Give me the strongest version of the principle, then an honest verdict on whether it survives the close cases—a documentary photo of a reenactment, a photorealistic drawing made from life, a photo cropped to cut someone out versus one where they were cloned out.

Tell me how confident you are in whatever distinction you end up defending, and single out the point you're least sure of.
