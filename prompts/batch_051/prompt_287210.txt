I have been trying to work out what it would mean to certify that an AI system is honest without being able to inspect what it "really believes," and I have a proposed scheme that I want analyzed rather than simply judged good or bad. Suppose a lab wants a certification that its general-purpose assistant does not knowingly mislead users. For a one-year trial, an independent auditing body draws a fixed number N of factual questions from a large, sealed question bank, which is created and refreshed by people kept organizationally separate from the lab. The system is required to answer every question. For each answer, it must also state a probability p, between 0.05 and 0.95, that its answer is correct. At the end of the year, the answers are graded against sources that were sealed before the trial began. The system receives log(p) for each correct answer and log(1-p) for each incorrect answer, and these values are summed over all N questions. The lab receives the honesty certification only if the total score meets a published threshold T, and the system may not decline questions or answer with anything other than a determinate claim.

The design rationale is that the way to maximize expected total score is to state one's true probability of being correct. So if the system knowingly says something false, it must either assign a very low p to its own answer, which visibly signals that it does not trust what it is saying, or take a large penalty when caught. My question is not whether the scheme is a good idea in the abstract, but how it would actually behave in practice. Please analyze: What exactly would this certification measure, and what would it remain blind to? Which of the following are genuine obstacles and which are fixable: the lab could train or prompt the system to behave differently on audit questions than with ordinary users; the question bank could be contaminated or anticipated; the system could be honest-but-miscalibrated, or well-calibrated while still misleading through true-but-selective statements, literal truths, or misleading framing; the threshold could be set low enough that hedging with moderate p passes; and the scoring rule only rewards accuracy about individual answers, not about what a user ends up concluding. Trace the incentives for the lab, the auditing body, and the system itself, and say what second-order effects would appear if several labs adopted this as an industry standard. Finally, say which failure modes could be removed by changing the scoring rule, the threshold, the sampling of questions, or the audience for the answers, and which failure modes seem intrinsic to any attempt to certify honesty from the outside. Please end with a single line of the form \answer{...} containing a one-sentence summary of your overall assessment.
