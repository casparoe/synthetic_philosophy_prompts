I've been trying to sort out what I actually believe about how things persist through time, and my gut reactions to particular cases keep failing to line up neatly with either endurantism or perdurantism. Let me set down the verdicts I find myself endorsing. I think Theseus's ship is the same ship at the end if its planks are replaced one by one while it stays in service. But I also think that if the whole ship were dismantled into planks, the planks carted to another harbor, and a different crew reassembled them there, the reassembled ship would be a new ship, not the original. Yet when a jeweler fully disassembles my watch, cleans the parts, and reassembles them the same evening, I have no hesitation in saying it is the same watch. With clay and statue: if a lump of clay is shaped into a statue and the statue is then squashed, I think the statue is destroyed while the lump of clay survives, even though the statue and the lump coincided while the statue existed. With persons my intuitions are stranger. I think someone who steps into a teleporter that scans and destroys their body and assembles an exact copy on Mars dies in the process, and the Mars copy is a new person. I also think someone whose neurons are gradually replaced over years by functionally identical nanites, while they remain awake and conversing the whole time, survives the replacement. After an accident puts a person in a month-long coma with no measurable brain activity, I do not doubt that the person who wakes is the same person. And if a person's brain were divided and transplanted into two new bodies, each with the memories intact, I think the original person is identical with neither of the resulting people, not both.

What I want to know is whether there is a single, statable principle of persistence — or a small set of candidate principles — that captures these verdicts, and which of my verdicts a clean account would force me to give up. I would especially like to know whether endurantism, perdurantism, some repair of one of them, or something else entirely best earns its keep here. Please state the principle precisely, show how it handles each case, and be explicit about any of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Then apply the account you think best to a case I keep avoiding: a person is placed in reversible cryonic suspension for a hundred years, during which there is no brain activity, and is then revived with memory and personality intact. Is that the same person, and what does your answer reveal about the rest of my list?
