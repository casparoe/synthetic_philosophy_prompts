Yesterday I spent a long evening arguing with a friend about anachronism in the history of mathematics, and we kept returning to an asymmetry in how old and new work get read. When a seventeenth-century argument is rewritten in modern notation and vocabulary and then assessed as if that were the original, most of us accept this as charitable reconstruction, even as evidence of what the author "really meant" or "anticipated." Yet no one would rewrite a modern proof in seventeenth-century notation and vocabulary and assess that, and someone who insisted on doing so would be treated as perverse rather than charitable. The past is translated forward into the present and credited for the result; the present is not expected to make sense when translated backward into the past. What, if anything, justifies treating one direction of translation as clarification and the other as distortion? Give me exactly five numbered candidate justifications for the asymmetry, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion; test each candidate against a nearby case in the history of mathematics or physics where it would give the wrong verdict, and use the fifth item for your overall verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
