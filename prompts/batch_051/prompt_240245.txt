A friend has been pressing an argument he calls the fanatic's proof, and I can't find the step that fails. It runs as follows. (1) An act is rational only if no alternative open to you has higher expected value. (2) The expected value of an act is the sum, over its possible outcomes, of probability times value, with value measured on a single cardinal scale. (3) If value has no upper bound, then for any act A whose outcomes are all of finite value, and for any positive probability p, there is a possible act B with probability p of a payoff so large that B's expected value exceeds A's. (4) Value has no upper bound: however good an outcome is, a better one is possible. (5) Therefore, for any ordinary act that promises a finite good with high probability, expected value reasoning says that some long-shot alternative—an act with a tiny probability of an astronomically large good—is better, and that we act irrationally whenever we take the near-certain good instead. (6) But that conclusion is false: it can be perfectly rational to give to a reliable charity rather than spend the same money on a one-in-a-billion chance of an enormous payoff. (7) So at least one of (1), (2), (4), or the move from (3) to (5) must go. I would most like to keep (1) and (2), though my friend insists that acts with tiny probabilities of astronomical goods are genuinely among our options. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? Please answer in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
