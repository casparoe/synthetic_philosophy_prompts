I've noticed something odd in how we judge people's beliefs, and I can't tell wether it's a defensible line or just an inconsistency we live with. Take two cases. A juror votes to convict a defendant on a strong gut feeling plus evidence that no careful person would call beyond reasonable doubt; everyone agrees the juror did something wrong — the word "rash" gets thrown around, and we'd say he had no right to believe it. Now take a woman who finds some ambiguous texts on her husband's phone. Her friends generally don't think she's being unreasonable if she trusts him anyway and decides to believe he's faithful, even though the evidence, honestly weighed, is maybe fifty-fifty at best. Or a patient with a serious illness whom everyone admires for refusing to accept the grim statistics and believing she's going to beat it. We praise the last two as loyal, hopeful, even courageous; we condemn the first as irresponsible. All three are cases of believing beyond — or at least not proportioned to — the evidence. So what's doing the work?

I know James and Clifford fought over roughly this: Clifford is remembered as saying it's always wrong to believe on insufficient evidence, while James thought that when the evidence can't settle a forced, momentous question — and when believing the thing might help make it true — you're allowed to let passion or practical interest decide. But I don't want a book report on the two of them. I want to know whether the asymmetry in ordinary judgment survives. Is the juror case different because the costs of error fall on a third party, while the wife and the patient bear their own costs? Does it matter whether the belief is about the past or the future? Whether it might be self-fulfilling? Whether it's private or acted upon publicly? None of those seems obviously right to me, and maybe the truth is that the juror is the consistent one and we're just sentimental about the other two. Or maybe the asymmetry is right and there's a principle that draws the line in the right place.

Answer as a professional philosopher working on the ethics of belief. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming candidate principles before committing. Then lead with your bottom line up front: keep the asymmetry, revise it (and say how), or reject it. Keep the whole answer under 500 words, even if that means being blunt. I want the strongest case for a real line — or an honest admission that their isn't one.
