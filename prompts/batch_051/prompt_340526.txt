I just got back from a long argument with a friend about whether an AI system should be able to own property, sign contracts, or be sued, and he kept pressing me on why I was fine with saying a corporation is a legal person but not willing to say the same thing about a robot. I realized my intuitions are more a list than a view, so I want to write them down and see if there's one principle behind them, or whether some of them have to go.

Here are the verdicts I found myself defending.

A corporation can own a building, bring a lawsuit, be sued, and be bound by contracts. I have no problem with any of that. But I don't think a corporation can be sent to prison, and I think it's at best a figure of speech to say it has dignity in the way a human does.

A river that has been given legal personhood with two guardians appointed to sue on its behalf: I can accept that as a legal device for giving someone standing to protect it, but I don't think the river itself has interests or moral standing. The guardians are the ones who have duties and who act; the river is a placeholder.

A dog that mauls someone. The dog is not the defendant; the owner is liable. Even though I want cruelty to the dog to be a crime, I would never say the dog can sign a contract or hold property in its own name.

An AI system that runs a small online trading operation, makes offers, takes payment, and delivers the goods without a human reviewing each transaction. I would not call it a legal person. I want the company or the humans who set it up to be the ones answerable for what it does, not the system itself.

A human newborn who inherits a house. The baby can hold the property through a guardian even though it can't negotiate, understand, or consent. I am obviously fine with that.

A hypothetical AI that has preferences and a strong sense of itself (whatever that amounts to). I keep flipping on this one, mostly because I dont know whether legal personhood is grounded in having a mind or in being a useful unit for a legal system.

The point my friend made that really got under my skin is this: my main reason for accepting corporate personhood was that it is a useful fiction for bundling assets, decisions, and liability into one thing. But that exact rationale seems to cover the trading AI just as well, or better, since the AI keeps generating obligations without a human signing off on each one. So either there is a principled difference between the corporation and the AI, or I'm treating the corporation as more person-like than my own rationale allows. I also feel pulled toward saying legal personhood is just whatever the law finds useful, but I don't believe that either, because I'd refuse to make a toaster a legal person no matter how useful it became.

So: is there a principle that captures most or all of these verdicts? Say it precisely, and tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Then apply it to a case I haven't listed: an AI system in a hospital that writes certain routine orders under its own identifier, with a doctor required to sign off later. And be honest about how confident you are in the principle, and what point you're least sure about.
