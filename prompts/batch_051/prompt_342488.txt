I’ve been working through Soares, Fallenstein, Yudkowsky, and Armstrong’s paper ‘Corrigibility’, and I’m stuck on an early point in their argument. They observe that corrigibility problems arise only when an agent is capable enough to consider options such as disabling its shutdown code, physically preventing the button from being pressed, psychologically manipulating the programmers, or constructing new agents without shutdown buttons. What I don’t understand is why this dilemma is not dissolved by making the agent simply not care about the shutdown button. If the button and the fact of its being pressed are kept out of the agent’s utility function, then the agent would have no terminal reason to interfere with the button. It would only need to be corrigible in the sense of being indifferent to shutdown, and that seems like a straightforward design choice rather than a hard open problem.

Is the difficulty just that we don’t know how to specify a utility function that truly omits the button while still capturing everything else we want the agent to value? Or is there a more fundamental reason why the agent would still end up resisting shutdown, even if shutdown itself has zero utility for it? I suspect my picture of what it means for an agent to ‘care about’ a state is subtly confused, but I can’t yet locate the mistake.
