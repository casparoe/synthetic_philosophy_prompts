Consider the absent-minded driver problem. A driver is on a road with two exits that are visually indistinguishable from inside the car. The driver is absent-minded in the following sense: whenever he reaches an exit, he cannot tell whether it is the first or the second exit, and he cannot remember whether he has already passed one; no clock, odometer reading, or landmark gives him information. His payoffs are these: taking the first exit yields 0, taking the second exit yields 4, and continuing past both exits yields 1. Before setting out, he must choose a single probability p of taking an exit whenever he finds himself at one, since at the wheel the two decision points look identical and no plan that treats them differently can be executed.

The standard analysis runs as follows. A plan with exit probability p has ex ante expected payoff p·0 + (1 − p)p·4 + (1 − p)²·1 = 1 + 2p − 3p², which is maximized at p = 1/3, for an expected value of 4/3. Moreover, if the driver at an exit assigns probability 1/(2 − p) to being at the first exit — the posterior obtained by noting that the first exit is reached with probability 1 and the second with probability 1 − p — then exiting has higher expected value than continuing exactly when p < 1/3, continuing has higher expected value exactly when p > 1/3, and the two are tied when p = 1/3. So p = 1/3 is both the ex ante optimal plan and the unique plan that is stable under on-the-road reassessment.

I find this standard resolution convincing, and not merely as a fixed point of the arithmetic: I think it is the right account of what a rational agent should do when forced to act under ignorance of his own location in an otherwise simple decision problem. But I want a serious map of its exposure. What would have to be true for the correct answer to be something other than p = 1/3 — a different mixing probability, a different solution concept, or the conclusion that the problem has no determinate answer at all? In particular:

- Which assumptions are load-bearing? Consider the payoffs, the specific memory limitation, the indistinguishability of the two exits, the assumption that there is a single driver who can plan in advance, the self-locating posterior 1/(2 − p), and the requirement that a single p govern both decision points. What does the case look like as each is weakened, and which relaxations change the answer rather than merely renaming the problem?
- What rival treatments of rational choice under imperfect recall, or of self-locating belief, would have to be correct for the standard analysis to fail? What would a defender of such a rival need to establish before a rational onlooker should switch?
- Which possible observations, formal results, or conceptual arguments would count decisively against the standard analysis, and which would be merely awkward for it?
- What do the standard analysis's own defenders concede is artificial or troubling about the case, and why do they think those concessions do not overturn the conclusion?
- Does p = 1/3 genuinely risk being wrong, or has the standard analysis been so thoroughly tied to this exact setup that it is insulated from any possible counterexample? If it is insulated, is that the sign of a stable result or of a claim that has stopped doing real work?

Sort the considerations by how much weight I should give them, and close with an honest overall assessment. For what it is worth, I would be moved most by a formal argument showing that ex ante expected utility and on-the-road expected utility genuinely come apart under a defensible treatment of self-locating belief, or by a case in which the fixed-point reasoning is shown to mischaracterize what the driver should believe about his own location.
