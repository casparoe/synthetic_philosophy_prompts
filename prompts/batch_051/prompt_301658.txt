Here's one I keep coming back to when I teach this stuff, and I want you to actually argue it rather than give me the standard line. Socrates, in the Protagoras, holds that what people call weakness of will — knowing the better and doing the worse — can't really happen. His story is that the akratic person is making a measurement error about the relative sizes of pleasures and pains, not acting against their own better judgment. Most of the tradition rejected that as too strong, but nobody had a clean formal alternative for a long time. Now along comes the hyperbolic discounting model: people discount future rewards at a rate that depends on how far in the future they are, steep for near-term tradeoffs and shallower for long-term ones, and that produces preference reversals. Tonight I prefer that morning-me exercise; at 6 a.m., with the run an hour away and the warm bed right now, I prefer to sleep; by noon I again prefer that I had exercised. Does the Socratic argument survive this development, is it dead, or does it need repair? And the part I really want you to pull apart: is the 6 a.m. version of me someone who knows the better and does the worse, or is it better described as a somewhat different agent, with a different discount rate, than the one who made the plan — so the apparent akrasia dissolves into a problem about how many selves their are? Which specific premise of the Socratic argument does hyperbolic discounting put pressure on: the one about what a single agent is, or the one about what a single synchronic judgment of "better" is? Give me your best argued verdict, including what would have to be true for the other side to be right. Oh, and please write in American English.
