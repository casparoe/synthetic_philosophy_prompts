I have been collecting my own verdicts about a set of cases in which preferences change over time, or conflict with themselves at a single time. Some cases strike me as clear rational defects, others as ordinary change, and at least two are hard to reconcile. I want the principle, if there is one, that captures the pattern—or a clear statement of which verdict I must abandon.

Case 1 — The lunch menu. At one sitting, I prefer the soup to the salad, the pasta to the soup, and the salad to the pasta. Offered pairwise trades for a small fee, I will pay to move around the cycle and finish where I began, poorer by a few dollars. Verdict: this is straightforwardly irrational, and the defect is the cyclical pattern of preference itself, not merely the fee-paying or the money lost.

Case 2 — The dated rewards. At the start of the month, I prefer $110 on the thirty-first day to $100 on the thirtieth day. On the thirtieth day, with the $100 now available and the $110 a day away, I prefer the $100. I therefore predictably end up with less than my earlier self would have chosen. Verdict: no individual choice seems wrong when I make it, and yet the pair feels like a defect. I want to say the person over time is irrational, even if no momentary choice is.

Case 3 — The city apartment. At twenty I prefer late nights, cramped city living, and spontaneity; at forty I prefer quiet, space, and routine. Each self is sincere and reasonably well informed. A broker could have profited from the differences between my earlier and later preferences, but I do not think that shows any rational defect. Verdict: this is ordinary change, and neither self is mistaken.

Case 4 — The bottle of wine. Throughout the week I judge that I should keep an expensive bottle for a dinner next month. I know that on Saturday evening I will sincerely prefer to open it, and that I would later regret having done so. I pay a small fee to have the bottle locked away until after Saturday. Verdict: this is rational, even a paradigm of rational planning. But then I owe an account of why Saturday's preference does not deserve the same deference as the forty-year-old's in Case 3.

Case 5 — The thermostat. Between any two adjacent settings on a thermostat, from 18°C to 24°C, I have no preference; yet I strongly prefer 24°C to 18°C. No single comparison feels like a mistake, but the overall pattern feels defective to me, and I suspect it is the same defect as in Case 1 in a weakened form. Verdict: the pattern itself is rationally defective.

The hard pair is Case 2 and Case 3. Both involve a person whose preferences vary across time in ways a broker could exploit, yet I condemn one and exonerate the other. Case 4 makes the difficulty sharper: I treat one future preference as a temptation to be bound against, but I would not say that about the forty-year-old's preference in Case 3. I can feel the shape of a principle nearby—something about whether all the preferences involved can be represented as belonging to a single, temporarily extended agent, or about whether the later preference is one the earlier self would disown—but I cannot make it precise, and I do not know whether it extends to Case 5.

Please state the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, precisely enough to test. Motivate each clause against the cases, and say what it implies about cases I have not yet considered, including this one, on which I do not have a settled verdict:

A person deliberately avoids an experience—say, having children—partly because she knows the experience would change her preferences in ways she currently does not want, and she currently values the preferences she has. She also knows, on good evidence, that after the change she would be genuinely glad of it and would not wish she had avoided it. Is it rational for her present self to act to preserve her present preferences against the future self's endorsement? And would a broker who profits from exploiting her predictable change be showing that she is irrational, or merely that her preferences are genuinely changing?

Finally, if no principled account can accommodate all of my verdicts, say exactly which ones it overturns, and whether the gain in simplicity or coherence is worth the cost.
