I got into a long argument with my roomate after we read about a museum that destroyed a painting by accident during a cleaning job — the kind of story where everyone quietly asks what, exactly, was lost. He said that if the museum had made a molecule-for-molecule copy of the painting beforehand, nothing of value would have been lost. I tried to make his argument precise, and this is the clearest version we could come up with:

(1) Let P be the original painting and Q a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of it, such that no examination of the two canvases themselves can tell them apart.
(2) If two things are molecule-for-molecule identical, then they share all the same intrinsic properties.
(3) Everything that contributes to a painting's aesthetic value is an intrinsic property of the painting — the colors, composition, arrangement of pigment on the surface — and not a relational property like who made it or where it has been.
(4) So Q has all the same aesthetic value-making properties as P, and therefore Q is exactly as aesthetically valuable as P.
(5) If Q is exactly as aesthetically valuable as P, then a museum that destroys P and keeps Q loses nothing of aesthetic value.
(6) But a museum that destroys the original and keeps only the duplicate does lose something of aesthetic value.
(7) Therefore (3) is false: at least part of the aesthetic value of an original must lie in a relational property after all.

I keep going back and forth on whether this argument is even valid. It feels like there's a slide between 'aesthetic value' in the bare sense of how the painting looks as an object, and 'aesthetic value' in some broader sense that already includes the fact that this is the original, but I can't tell whether that slide is a genuine equivocation or just me refusing to accept a conclusion I don't like. Which step, if any, fails? If none fails, say so and defend the conclussion.
