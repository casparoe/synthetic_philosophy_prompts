I'm a backend engineer and self-taught on the philosophy side, so forgive me if I use terms wrong. Something's been bugging me about the reliability culture at work. We run blameless postmortems for every incident: after an outage, we reconstruct the call the on-call engineer actually made with the information they had at 3am, and we judge the decision on that, not on whether it happened to take down prod. The stated reason is that the same action can cause an outage or not depending on things no one controlled — a race condition that only triggers under load, a vendor's rate limiter firing at exactly the wrong moment, etc. So the process deliberately refuses to judge by outcome.

That sounds a lot like the rejection of what (I think) philosophers call resultant moral luck: the idea that in ordinary life we do judge people morally for outcomes that weren't fully under their control. Blameless postmortems look like resultant-moral-luck skepticism turned into an engineering practice.

But Nagel also has three other kinds of moral luck — constitutive (the temperament you were born with), circumstantial (which situations you happen to face), and causal (how prior conditions determined the action). And my parallel only obviously maps onto the first one. I can't tell if the postmortem is silent about the other three, or whether "we don't blame for temperament or situation, we just treat those as facts about the system" is itself a substantive position on them.

So: is this a real structural parallel or am I pattern-matching? Its probably pattern-matching, but I'd like to know. If it is real, what's the exact mapping between the pieces of a postmortem and the pieces of a moral-luck position? What would have to be true on both sides for a result to transfer — e.g., if blameless postmortems actually make systems safer, does that count as evidence against ordinary outcome-based moral blame, or is that a category mistake because engineering has a different goal structure? And give me at least one thing the parallel would predict that turns out false.

Also please separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this; don't present a contested view as the consensus.
