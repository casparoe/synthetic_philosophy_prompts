I have no background in linguistics and have never studied writing systems formally, but I have learned several languages on my own and have spent a long time noticing how their scripts line up, or fail to line up, with the way the languages are spoken. I keep coming back to a question I cannot answer to my own satisfaction, and I would like you to work through it with me.

I want a working definition of what it means for a writing system to be phonemic — or, if that term is too narrow, a definition of what a script encodes, with being phonemic as one special case. I am using the word phonemic rather than phonetic, though I am not sure I could define the difference; if that distinction does real work in the cases below, please draw it. The definition must not be a slogan like one letter per sound. It should do real sorting work when applied to the cases below. Please give your best definition, run it against each case, say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and revise it or defend the surprising verdict as you go.

The cases I have been puzzling over are these.

Spanish. To my eye it is nearly phonemic: most letters have a fairly stable value, and a reader can usually pronounce a written word correctly. But there are exceptions — silent h, b and v, and dialect differences involving c, z, ll, and y. Should Spanish count as phonemic, and how much do the exceptions matter?

Turkish. It has been written since the 1920s in a Latin alphabet deliberately designed to match the spoken language, and it feels even closer to one-symbol-per-sound than Spanish. Is this the model case, or does it have hidden departures?

English. Clearly not phonemic in any simple sense — consider the ough words: rough, through, though, thought. But is English phonemic in some weaker or more abstract way, or should we say it is an alphabet whose spelling has drifted away from the phonemic principle?

French. Many letters are silent in isolation but reappear in liaison or in related forms such as grand and grande. French spelling seems to encode grammar and word families as much as pronunciation. What exactly does it encode, and how does that affect the definition?

Chinese characters. A character typically stands for a meaning-bearing unit and a syllable, with sound only partially hinted at by components. This is plainly not an alphabet, but it is a writing system, so the definition must say what it encodes rather than simply exclude it.

Korean Hangul. The letters are arranged into syllable-sized blocks, and the letter shapes are said to depict the speech organs that produce the sounds. Is Hangul an alphabet? Is it phonemic? Can a system be both featural and phonemic, or do those descriptions compete?

Arabic, and Hebrew if it helps. Consonants are written, while short vowels are omitted or marked only by optional diacritics. Can a system that leaves out vowels still be called phonemic? If not, what is the right category for it?

Devanagari. A consonant letter carries an inherent vowel unless another vowel is marked, and vowel signs attach to the consonant. Where does this fall, and what kind of unit does it encode?

Japanese kana. Each symbol stands for a mora, a unit that is not quite a syllable and not quite a phoneme. Is kana phonemic, non-phonemic, or is phonemic simply the wrong axis for it?

I suspect the word phonemic is being asked to do several jobs at once: how much pronunciation information a script records, what kind of unit it records (phoneme, mora, syllable, morpheme), and how regular the mapping between marks and sounds is. If you agree, separate those jobs and define each; if you disagree, show me how a single definition can do all the work. Either way, I want a definition that yields a defensible verdict for every case above, including the borderline ones — even where the honest verdict is that the case exposes a flaw in the definition.
