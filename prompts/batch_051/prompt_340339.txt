I've been turning over an asymmetry in how people — including me — talk about politically engaged art, and I can't tell whether it's defensible or just self-serving. When a film or novel explicitly argues for a cause I already believe in, with characters who are either awakened or destroyed by the injustice it depicts, I reach for words like courageous, unflinching, clear-eyed. When a work uses the same overtly didactic methods to argue for a position I reject, I call it propaganda, agitprop, or a message novel with literary pretensions. The reviews I read do exactly the same thing, only with the politics rotated. The respectable opinion seems to be that there is a real distinction between art and propaganda; the observable practice looks more like: propaganda is the other side's tendentious art, and art is my side's.

What makes this uncomfortable for me is that W. E. B. Du Bois, defending the use of art in the struggle for racial justice, argued — at least as I read him — that the distinction is partly an illusion: all serious art embodies values and is aimed at moving an audience, and the pretense of aesthetic purity is itself a value-laden stance. He did not, I think, mean that beauty and craft don't matter; he meant that political purpose and aesthetic excellence aren't opposed, and that refusing to name one's purpose is not neutrality. If he's right, then the asymmetry can't be a distinction between art and propaganda; at most it's a distinction between better and worse advocacy.

So here is my question. Is there a principle that generates this asymmetry — that the same work, with the same formal choices and the same intent, is praised as true when it flatters our politics and condemned as propaganda when it doesn't — other than partisanship? Perhaps the asymmetry tracks something real, like a judgment that our side's message is true and theirs is false; but we usually make the call without checking the facts, and we would not grant the same license to an opponent. I want the strongest possible case for keeping the asymmetry, tested against this version: two films identical in script structure, rhetorical devices, emotional levers, and craft, differing only in which political conclusion they push. Then give me an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or abandoned. Please also say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you are least sure of.
