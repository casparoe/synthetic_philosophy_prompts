I am helping to develop materials for an interdisciplinary workshop called "Death at Small Scales," whose participants work in public health, stored-product pest management, conservation, and smallholder agriculture. In our preliminary conversations, we noticed that people who say nearly the same thing in the abstract—that pest control should be "humane," "proportionate," or "a last resort"—often answer concrete cases very differently. Some people fog a bedroom for bedbugs without hesitation but carefully carry a stray moth outside; others accept indoor mosquito spraying but refuse ornamental lawn treatments; still others think the sheer number of insects killed in agriculture is the most important fact and are uneasy even about veganic farming. We want an instrument that sorts people by their actual responses to cases rather than by the labels they choose for themselves.

Please write a nine-question sorting quiz, with a scoring key, titled something like "Which account of insect pest control do you actually hold?" Each question should present a concrete, realistic case—for example: indoor mosquitoes in a malaria-endemic region, head lice on a child, pantry moths in a grain store, a locust swarm threatening a subsistence crop, Japanese beetles on ornamental roses, non-biting midges around a porch light, bedbugs in a rental apartment, soil pests in an organic vegetable garden, and ants invading a hospital kitchen—and ask the respondent to choose among two or three permitted, required, or forbidden actions. Do not ask respondents whether insects are sentient, have rights, or matter morally; the answer options should be about what may be done in the case. The scoring key should map answer patterns onto positions defined by the following axes: (i) whether insect welfare matters directly or only through human or ecological interests; (ii) whether the number of insects killed is itself morally weighty or negligible; (iii) whether wildness, nativeness, or "pest" status changes what may be done; and (iv) whether human health and food-security interests defeat insect interests automatically, only subject to necessity and proportionality constraints, or never. Leaves should include at least a strong insect-rights view, a sentientist welfarist view with a de minimis threshold, a precautionary view grounded in uncertain insect sentience, a scale-sensitive utilitarian view that heavily weights aggregate insect suffering, an ecological or instrumental view that cares only about species- or ecosystem-level effects, and an anthropocentric nuisance view.

Finally, make the scoring key honest about its own limits. Flag any positions the quiz cannot really separate, and if some combinations of answers would place a respondent in an empty region of the logical space, say so explicitly and offer a brief diagnosis of whether the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Conclude the scoring key with one sentence for each position stating the most awkward commitment its defenders must accept. The instrument should be self-contained, and it should not include citations, reading lists, or bibliography.
