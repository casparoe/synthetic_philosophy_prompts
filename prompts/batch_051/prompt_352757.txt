For a while now I have been mulling over a recurring debate in rationalist-adjacent spaces about the WEIRD problem in moral psychology. The empirical picture is familiar: the populations most heavily sampled in psychology are Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic, and they turn out to be outliers in fairness intuitions, third-party punishment, and even some tasks well outside morality. People then split into two camps. One treats cross-cultural variation as evidence that our moral machinery is parochial enough to make any talk of objective moral truth a mistake. The other treats the variation as different parameter settings on a shared architecture: the same underlying capacities for reciprocity, kinship, hierarchy, and purity are weighted differently under different ecologies and institutions, so the variation is fully compatible with a single objective standard that the shared core approximately tracks.

Lately I have been wondering how David Hume would have adjudicated this, because he is the one figure in the canon who was trying to do both things at once. He insists that moral distinctions are not derived from reason alone but from sentiment; yet he also insists that there is a point of view — the general point of view, as he calls it — from which our sentiments are corrected for distance, partiality toward ourselves and our associates, and the accidents of our own situation, and from which we can meaningfully, and not merely parochially, approve or blame. His aesthetics does parallel work in the essay on standards of taste. That makes him a natural test case: a sentimentalist who nonetheless thought that correcting for bias could yield something like objectivity, writing two and a half centuries before anyone had cross-cultural data of the kind we now have.

I would like you to write a detailed prediction of how Hume would respond to the modern WEIRD findings and what he would conclude they imply for moral objectivity. Specifically, work through: (1) which results he would absorb without strain and which would genuinely surprise him; (2) whether he would treat cross-cultural moral variation as more like variation in taste, or more like an illusion experiment in which culture supplies different inputs to the same underlying faculties; (3) how he would amend, if at all, his doctrine of the general point of view now that the general point of view turns out to be heavily populated by WEIRD subjects; and (4) whether, on balance, the data would push him toward a stronger realism, a chastened universalism, or something closer to relativism about morals.
