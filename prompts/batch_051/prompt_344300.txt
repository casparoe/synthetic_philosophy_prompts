I've been rereading C. Lloyd Morgan's *An Introduction to Comparative Psychology*, and I keep geting stuck on the way he introduces his canon in the "Other Minds than Ours" chapter. He says, roughly, that if an animal behavior can be explained by a lower psychical faculty we shouldn't attribute it to a higher one; but then he immediately adds a caveat to the effect that the rule is about the order of inference, not a claim that animals have no minds or never think. That caveat makes the whole thing sound like a methodological tie-breaker, not a substantive ontology of animal minds.

Here's the development I can't get out of my head: large language models are trained with a single, extremely low-level objective, next-token prediction—on the face of it about as low a process as you can get. Yet they pass many tests that were supposed to require higher faculties: false-belief tasks, analogical reasoning, systematic generalization, and so on. If you applied Morgan's canon to these systems, it would force you to say all that competence should be interpreted through the lower-level objective. But then the lower-level explanation covers so much that the higher/lower distinction stops doing any work: almost anything can be reframed as statistical pattern completion.

Does Morgan's canon survive this development, or does it rest on an assumption that the lower-level faculties are less flexible and less powerful than the higher ones, and modern AI just showed that assumption is false? If the canon can be saved, what exactly would the repaired version say—in particular, about when a behavioral test counts as evidence for a distinct kind of cognition rather than a clever interpolation?
