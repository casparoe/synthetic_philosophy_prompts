As a novelist who writes in the first person, I fabricate what readers take to be the most unmediated thing they possess: a self at the center of experience. The view I keep circling, worked out most fully by Thomas Metzinger, is that the felt self is a transparent phenomenal self-model—a representational structure whose medium is phenomenally transparent, so that one experiences through it and mistakes it for an unmodeled thing itself. People grant this easily in partial cases: a phantom limb can feel vividly present and owned though no limb is there, and in the rubber-hand illusion a person can feel ownership of an object plainly outside the body; both are now standardly described as transparent models without a corresponding real object. But propose the same kind of transparency for the self as a whole, and most people treat the claim as absurd. I want to know whether anything justifies that asymmetry between the part-cases and the whole-case. Is there a principled difference between a transparent model of a hand and a transparent model of a self, or is the resistance only a failure to carry the reasoning through? Give me the strongest defense of the common view, test it against a full-body version of the illusion—say, an out-of-body experience in which the felt locus of the self relocates while the body stays put—and then tell me honestly whether the asymmetry should be kept. Say explicitly what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to the opposite verdict, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
