So I've been chewing on a parrallel between two things that don't usually get put side by side, and I can't tell whether it's substantive or just pattern-matching.

In frequentist stats, the validity of an inference depends on the rule you used to stop collecting data. Run a study fixed at N=200 and the p-value means one thing; peek and stop the first time p < .05 and the actual false-positive rate inflates badly, even when the data you end up with look the same as what the pre-registered design would have produced — this is one of the mechanisms blamed for the replication crisis. Bayesians mostly shrug: the likelihood principle says all the evidence lives in the likelihood function, so two experiments with the same likelihood support the same conclusion regardless of the stopping rule. For them, the procedure by which the data happened to arrive is irrelevant; only the evidential content matters.

On the legal side there's a similar-sounding doctrine: courts throw out evidence gathered through an improper search or coercion even when the evidence itself is perfectly reliable. The fruit of the poisonous tree rule means the collection procedure determines whether the evidence may be used, whatever the evidence shows. A judge doesn't say "well, the evidence is strong, so let's ignore how it was collected." And law dislikes fishing expeditions — keep searching until you find something incriminating — which sounds a lot like the peeking statistician.

So the proposed parallel: frequentism treats the stopping rule the way courts treat the collection procedure, as something that can invalidate an otherwise informative result, while Bayesianism plays the role of the evidentialist who says only the facts in hand matter.

I go back and forth on whether this is deep. The legal rule exists to deter police misconduct and keep institutions legitimate, not because tainted evidence is epistemically weaker; the frequentist rule exists to control error rates across hypothetical replications. Those are very different rationales. But maybe the shared structure is that in both cases a counterfactual — "what would have happened under a different procedure" — is doing real work, and the Bayesian is being too quick to ignore it.
