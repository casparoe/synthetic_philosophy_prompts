So here's the thing. I teach a seminar on the ethics of forgiveness every other year, and I also chair my university's academic integrity review board. The two keep colliding in an unhelpful way. The board—registrar, an engineer from the faculty senate, a student conduct officer, two students, me—meets every two weeks and gets maybe 15 minutes per case to decide whether a student goes to standard sanctions or to a restorative route, and lately the restorative route has picked up the language of forgiveness. Board members say things like 'he's apologized, we should forgive him' or 'the instructor says she forgives the student, so can we just drop the report?', which lumps together about five different things at once: forgiving, excusing, condoning, waiving the sanction, and restoring trust. I keep trying to explain that a group can decline to punish without forgiving anyone, that forgiving someone doesn't by itself settle what happens to their record, and that we may not even have standing to forgive on behalf of the instructor or the department—but I can't be at every meeting, and anyway the rest of the board shouldnt need me in the room.

Write me a one-page decision procedure the board can run on any case that raises a forgiveness question. Questions in order, starting from something like 'does this case actually involve forgiveness, or just a request for leniency or a grade appeal?' and moving through: who was wronged; who has standing to forgive what; whether the student's statement is an acknowledgment or an excuse, and how a non-philosopher can tell the difference; whether the student is asking to be forgiven or asking for the consequences to be removed—not the same thing; and whether the board is being asked to forgive on somebody else's behalf. It should seperate the board's decision about sanctions from any decision about forgiveness, and it should mark clearly where the procedure has to stop and say 'this is a matter of values, not a checklist item.' I'm thinking especially of whether forgiveness can ever be required of an instructor, and whether unconditional forgiveness makes sense in a setting like this at all—maybe as a standing disposition toward the student rather than one more thing the board does in a single vote. Plain language, not seminar language; the engineer needs to follow it, and whatever we land on goes up to the Dean of Students, who likes clear boxes.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show exactly where each one exits. (a) A student who plagiarized a lab report, fully admits it, names the lab partner they wronged, and asks to be forgiven; the instructor is still angry. (b) A contract-cheating case reported by a TA where the student denies everything and asks the board to 'be lenient.' (c) An instructor who says she has forgiven a first-year for using an AI tool in a way she considers naive rather than dishonest, and asks the board to remove the report so the student doesn't carry it forever. I suspect the procedure will give the instructor in (c), and maybe the board in (a), an answer we won't like, and that's exactly what I want it to be honest about. Finally, say what the procedure cannot do—what it can't determine, what it must hand to a person, and where my own philosophical commitments are doing the work rather than the argument.
