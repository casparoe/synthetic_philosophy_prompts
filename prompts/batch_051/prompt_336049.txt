I sit on the doctrinal review committee of a small confessional seminary. One of our systematic theologians has published an essay arguing that a punishment of unending conscious torment is disproportionate to any choice a finite creature can make, and that once this is granted only two positions remain defensible: the annihilation of the impenitent or the eventual restoration of all persons; in his lectures he has defended the first of these as the more workable. Several senior faculty now want him found in violation of the seminary's statement of faith. That statement affirms that the final punishment of the impenitent is everlasting without further specification of its nature, and it therefore leaves open whether the punishment consists in endless conscious suffering, irreversible destruction, or a remedial process that issues in restoration. Another bloc of the faculty argues that the statement rules only on the fact that the punishment is everlasting, not on what it is, and that disciplining the professor would settle by administrative fiat a question the tradition has left open. The provost has asked our committee to circulate, before next month's board meeting, a short written opinion recommending a concrete course of action with a time frame: find him in violation, find him within bounds, or appoint a study commission with a defined mandate.

Please draft that written opinion. The committee is split in a way I do not want the memo to paper over. The strongest case against the professor is that the tradition's retributive logic measures the gravity of an offense by the dignity of the party wronged, so that a sin against God can merit a punishment no finite duration would bound; the strongest reply is that this move severs punishment from the degree of the wrongdoer's culpable choice, which was the very thing a retributive justification of hell was meant to preserve. I want the opinion to face that disagreement directly, to say which account of what final punishment is for our statement of faith actually commits us to, and to give an honest statement of the strongest consideration on the side the committee ultimately rejects. I took this seat expecting to referee tone and collegiality; the case has instead reopened questions I thought I had settled years ago, and I want a document I could stand behind whichever way the vote goes.
