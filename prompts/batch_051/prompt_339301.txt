A friend of mine in cognitive science sent me the following summary of the modularity-of-mind debate, and something about it seems off to me, though I cannot pin down exactly where.

"Fodor argued that the mind contains a collection of modules — fast, automatic, domain-specific subsystems for perception and language — alongside a central, domain-general system that handles reasoning and belief fixation. What makes something a module is domain specificity, and it follows from being domain-specific that a module is informationally encapsulated: it can use only its own proprietary store of information and cannot draw on what you already know. That is why visual illusions do not go away even after you have measured the lines and know they are equal. Massive modularity took the next step and claimed that the whole mind, including the central reasoning system, is composed of nothing but such fast, automatic, encapsulated modules. This is now broadly accepted in cognitive science, because natural selection can only build domain-specific mechanisms, and the idea of a domain-general central system is mostly a philosopher's nostalgia for an immaterial soul."

I have since read that Fodor himself lists several criteria for modularity — domain specificity, informational encapsulation, mandatory operation, speed, fixed neural architecture, characteristic breakdown patterns, and shallow outputs — and I notice that the summary mentions only two of them. But that alone does not tell me which moves in the summary are fair renderings of Fodor or of the massive modularists, which are subtle conflations or overstatements, and whether the closing claim about massive modularity being broadly accepted follows from anything accurate in the passage. Please go through the summary claim by claim: identify what Fodor would accept and what he would reject, say what a careful defender of massive modularity would object to in the way her view is described, and assess whether the summary's conclusion survives correction.
