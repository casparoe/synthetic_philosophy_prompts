Here's a trolley variant I've been turning over, and I can't tell which of two standard-looking analyses is the right one.

Standard setup: five on the main track, one on the side track; pull the lever and the one dies, don't pull and the five die. The person at the lever has split creedences across two moral theories. She's 55% on simple act-utilitarianism: pull = +4 lives (five saved minus one killed), don't pull = 0. She's 45% on a threshold deontology that says killing an innocent as a means is forbidden unless it would save at least ten lives. Five isn't ten, so on that view pulling is forbidden, and not pulling is merely permissible.

The standard move under moral uncertainty is to maximize expected choice-worthiness. But the utilitarian hands you numbers (+4 and 0), and the deontologist hands you verdicts that carry no magnitude. One way to compare them is to normalize each theory so its better option gets +1 and its worse option gets −1, then take the credence-weighted sum. That gives: pull gets .55(+1) + .45(−1) = .1, don't pull gets −.1, so pull.

Another way is to use lives as the common unit. The threshold at ten seems to give the deontologist's verdict an anchor: they'd let five die rather than kill one, and they'd only switch at ten saved, so you could put 'forbidden' at about −5 lives and 'permissible' at 0. On that scale: pull gets .55(4) + .45(−5) = −.05, and don't pull gets 0, so don't pull.

So two ways of running the standard analysis come out on opposite sides. Which one, if either, is what the analysis actually says? Does a threshold deontology really have enough cardinal structure to justify placing it on a lives scale, or is −5 just made up — and if it is made up, is the +1/−1 normalization the only non-arbitrary move, even though it throws away the utilitarian's +4 and the deontologist's ten? Or does this case expose a hole in expected choice-worthiness when one theory is categorical and the other is cardinal?
