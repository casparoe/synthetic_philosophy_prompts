After much prodding, I got her to commit to a series of verdicts. She refused to state a general rule, insisting that the cases speak for themselves, but our argument left me convinced that she is running at least one tacit principle. 2. A naturalized citizen who has lived in the city for 25 years: yes.
3. A noncitizen permanent resident who has lived in the city for 15 years, works here, pays local taxes, and is raising children here: yes.
4. A citizen born and schooled here but living abroad for the past 20 years, owning no property here and visiting rarely: no.
5. A citizen who arrived five months ago for a two-year work contract, rents a room here, and plans to leave when the contract ends: no.
6. A noncitizen commuter who works here five days a week but lives in a neighboring town, owns a home there, and has no other tie to this city: no.
7. A tourist who rents an apartment here for one month: no.
8. An investor who lives in another city but owns a storefront and two rental units here and visits on some weekends to manage them: no.

Now the new case I want to predict her verdict on: a couple lives just outside the municipal boundary. Their two school-age children attend the city's public schools under a tuition transfer agreement, so the family spends most weekdays in the city — school runs, meetings, activities, the children's friendships — but they sleep and pay property tax outside the city, and neither parent's job is based in the city.

What does her track record predict she will say about giving this couple the municipal franchise? If her prior verdicts underdetermine this, identify the rules she could be running, say what each predicts here, and state which of her earlier cases is the closest analog.
