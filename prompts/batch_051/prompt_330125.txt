I'm a science journalist working on a feature about Maxwell's demon and what the arguments over it mean for the idea that information is physical. A reader sent me a chain of reasoning I keep turning over; it's meant to show something stronger than the standard line that Maxwell's demon doesn't actually violate the second law—the reader thinks the whole information-is-physical picture has been oversold. I'll lay it out the way he did, numbered:

(1) A Szilard engine can turn one bit of information about which side of a box a single molecule is on into at most kT ln 2 of work.
(2) Work is energy.
(3) So if an engine can turn a bit into kT ln 2 of work, a bit just is a certain amount of energy—information literally is a form of energy.
(4) But information gets destroyed all the time: overwriting a file, forgetting a password, wiping a hard drive. If information were literally energy, destroying it would have to release or consume that kT ln 2 each time, which it plainly does not.
(5) Therefore, since bits are routinely destroyed without any kT ln 2-scale energy exchange, the claim that information is physical must be false.

I half-suspect the move from (1) and (2) to (3) is doing to much, and I know Landauer's principle is lurking somewhere in here, but I can't quite say where the argument fails—or wether it actually does. Could you go through it step by step? Reply as a numbered list with exactly five items, one for each step above, each item a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. For each step, say whether it's right, wrong, or only right under a certain reading, and say which step is the main failure point.
