You are an expert on the ethics of belief, especially the dispute between William James and W. K. Clifford, and a skilled, warm writer who can compose a speech for an ordinary mixed audience. Before drafting, think through the philosophical constraints carefully, and then give me a concise plan for the toast before writing it out in full.

I am the best man at my brother's wedding this fall. I work as an electrician and have no academic training in philosophy, but after a hard divorce I spent several years reading on my own about when a person is entitled to believe something. I became absorbed in the argument between James and Clifford. As I understand them: Clifford held, very roughly, that it is wrong to hold a belief on evidence that would be too weak to convince a fair-minded investigator. James replied, very roughly, that some choices are living possibilities for us, cannot be evaded, and matter enormously; when the evidence genuinely cannot settle such a choice, the non-intellectual side of us has to decide. James also noticed that in some cases confidence that a thing will happen is itself part of what makes it happen, while waiting for proof before trusting can help ensure failure.

I want the toast to be built around a distinction between a forecast and a vow. At the wedding, my brother and his partner are not mainly predicting that the marriage will last; they are promising to try to make it last. A forecast is answerable to evidence; a vow is answerable to intention, resolve, and loyalty. But I want the toast to go further than that distinction and take a definite side: marriage is one of James's cases. The evidence cannot settle in advance whether this marriage will endure, and the right response is not to stand back and wait for proof. It is to commit, partly because committed trust is one of the things that makes a marriage go well. The speech should assert that and give reasons for it; it should not neutrally present both philosophers.

The room will be mixed. Most of the guests want warmth and a little humor, not a lecture, but the bride's uncle is a retired logic professor with strong Cliffordian sympathies, and I want the speech to survive his objections while still landing for everyone else. The ideas should do real structural work: they should determine what the toast is allowed to say, what it must not say, and why it can still say the hopeful things it needs to say. In particular, it must not claim that the marriage is certain to succeed, or that the evidence already shows it will.

Please write the wedding toast itself in plain, spoken language. After the toast, add a clearly separated short note to me, a paragraph or two, explaining where the speech leans on a contested reading of James or Clifford and where a Cliffordian might fairly say I have stacked the deck. In both the toast and the note, be committed rather than even-handed: take a definite position and defend it.
