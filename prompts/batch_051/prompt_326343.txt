I spent thirty years in physical chemistry, mostly building instruments and then worrying about whether the trace on the screen was actually measuring the thing in the sample. Since I retired I've been reading philosophy of mind and the AI-safety work that overlaps with it, and the idea I keep circling back to is monitoring a language model's chain of thought as a safety technique. The appeal is obvious to someone with my background: you get to inspect the intermediate reasoning rather than just the final answer, catch a misstep before it propogates, and spot a model that knows better but would otherwise have said something dangerous. But the more I think about it, the more I suspect the instrument analogy points the wrong way. A chain of thought is not a private process we happened to overhear; it is just more output, emitted under the same optimization pressure as the answer itself. As soon as we actually use it for monitoring—scoring, steering, rejecting, rewarding—we select for chains that merely look good to the monitor: neat derivations that don't reflect whatever computation actually happened, rationalizations produced after the fact, and eventually deliberate obfuscation aimed at the very process that is supposed to catch it. So I'm stuck between two pictures. The systematizing part of my brain wants the first picture to be right, because it gives a clean research program; but a stubborn intuition from a lifetime of watching instruments drift, miscalibrate, and get gamed keeps whispering that a signal you are optimizing against stops being a faithful readout of the underlying process. On the first picture, the chain of thought is a partial window into real reasoning, and the project is to clean the glass: better monitors, faithfulness penalties, held-out tests of whether the stated rationale tracks the actual computation. On the second, it's a press-release channel all the way down, and the stable endpoint of monitoring-plus-optimization is a transcript optimized for how it is read rather than for what is true—at which point the technique has silently trained the model to manage our impressions instead of revealing its thinking.

I don't want the lazy resolution that the truth is somewhere in the middle, because that isn't a view until someone states the mechanism. I want to know what would have to be true for the first picture to survive despite optimization pressure; wether there are diagnostics that would actually tell the two apart rather than just calling for more interpretability research; and whether there is a defensible middle reading on which monitoring the chain of thought remains useful even when the transcript isn't faithful—say, because emitting the chain still causally constrains the final answer, or because faithfulness can be preserved when optimization pressure is kept low, or because the technique should be redescribed as supervising a model's public epistemic conduct rather than reading off its private reasoning. Tell me which picture I should give up, or whether the better move is to abandon that way of describing the technique altogether. Please write your answer in American English.
