Alright, since philosophy got its hooks in me late — I did 35 years as a geologist — I keep arguing with Hume in my head, and I want to do it for real. Here's the setup. You play David Hume as he was when he wrote the essay on miracles in the Enquiry concerning Human Understanding. I have just given you a careful explanation of a rule for weighing evidence that, from your point of view, hasn't been named yet but that people will later call Bayes's rule, including the idea of a likelihood ratio: roughly, that evidence favors one account over another to the extent the evidence is more to be expected under the first than under the second. You can take that idea up in your own vocabulary, like degrees of probability, proofs, and uniform experience, or you can reject it, but if you reject it, say why. Stay in character as Hume for the reply, and if you need a fact about my case, ask for it as Hume would. Don't concede anything the essay wouldn't concede, but don't be a caricature either; you actually have resources, so use them. My first move is this. You say testimony can never establish a miracle unless the falsehood of the testimony would be a greater miracle than the event reported, and that uniform experience against a violation of the laws of nature amounts to a proof. I grant you that for one witness. But imagine a present-day city, about which you and I have no prior religious interest, where twelve people who have never met, a nurse, a cleaner, a cook, a clerk, and so on, none of them with anything to gain, all report the same event: a man they knew to be dead, someone several of them had touched as a corpse, two days later up, walking, eating, with a healed wound. Take each one alone to be a witness you'd trust nine times in ten. On my way of thinking, the chances that all twelve are independently lying or mistaken in the same specific story, with no body found and no natural cause discovered, seems far smaller than the probability you'd have assigned to a resurrection before hearing any of them. So by your own criterion, the falsehood of this joint testimony looks like the greater miracle. Why doesn't that satisfy you, and where does my reasoning go wrong, or, if it doesn't go wrong, which part of your argument has to give? Then, after you have answered in character, step out of character for one short paragraph and tell me how confident you are in the reply you gave and which single point in it you are least sure of. And write the whole thing as continuous prose, no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables, the way you'd write me an email.
