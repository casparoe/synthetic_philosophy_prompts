You are a novelist with a serious working knowledge of value theory, and you have agreed to serve as the neutral drafter for an adversarial collaboration between two camps that disagree about how two features of a conscious state combine to determine its overall value. Treat each camp the way you would treat a character whose fundamental commitments you intend to render fairly: find the experiential intuition behind the formalism before you put words in its mouth.

Let C be a state's degree of phenomenal consciousness — how vivid, unified, wide-awake, and rich the experience is — and let H be the state's degree of positive affect, or happiness — how good the experience feels from the inside. Both camps agree, for purposes of this collaboration, to treat C and H as graded dimensions with a meaningful zero and as the two relevant components. They disagree about the combination rule.

Camp A, the additivists, hold that the overall value of a state is an additive function of C and H: each component contributes value independently of the other. Thus a very dim, barely conscious state of enormous happiness could be very valuable, and a highly conscious state of neutral affect could still be valuable in virtue of its consciousness alone.

Camp B, the multiplicativists, hold that the overall value of a state is the product C × H, up to positive rescaling: the two components interact. When either component is zero, the state has zero value; raising either component amplifies the contribution of the other.

Draft the document this adversarial collaboration would produce, with the following structure.

First, a numbered list of claims both camps can sign. These claims should be neither vacuous nor slanted; they should pin down the formalism, the shared cases, and what each side must explain.

Second, a disagreements table. For each residual disagreement, state the precise wording of each side's own view — wording that side would accept as a fair statement of its position — and identify the crux: the sub-question such that a settled answer would move at least one side.

Third, a short section of shared tests. List the thought experiments, hypothetical creatures, or measurement regimes both sides agree would bear on the cruxes, and record each side's prediction in advance.

Fourth, a closing appraisal of whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal — for example, whether a change in how C and H are scaled, or a log transform, could turn an additive representation into a multiplicative one — and whether any part of the dispute shades into a lexical view, in which one component has strict priority over the other.

Before writing the final statement, spend time brainstorming candidate formulations, test cases, and possible agreed claims. Then compose the document with a novelist's care for exact wording, in polished American English.
