I have noticed what may be a structural identity between a passage in Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics and two theses that are now central to debates about artificial intelligence, and I would like a careful verdict on whether the parallel is deep or superficial.

The passage is Nicomachean Ethics, Book III, Chapter 3 (Bekker 1112b), in H. Rackham's translation:

"And we deliberate not about ends, but about means. A doctor does not deliberate whether he is to cure his patient, nor an orator whether he is to convince his audience, nor a statesman whether he is to secure good government, nor does anyone else debate about the end of his profession or calling; they take some end for granted, and consider how and by what means it can be achieved. If they find that there are several means of achieving it, they proceed to consider which of these will attain it most easily and best. If there is only one means by which it can be accomplished, they ask how it is to be accomplished by that means, and by what means that means can itself be achieved, until they reach the first link in the chain of causes, which is the last in the order of discovery."

The two modern theses are these. The orthogonality thesis holds that an agent's intelligence and its final goals are independent variables: in principle, any degree of capability can be combined with any final goal. Instrumental convergence holds that sufficiently capable agents pursuing almost any final goal will tend to converge on certain instrumental subgoals — self-preservation, acquisition of resources, and improvement of their own capacities — because those subgoals are useful means to a very wide range of ends.

The parallel I have in mind is the following. Aristotle says deliberation is only ever about means, never about ends: the end is taken for granted and supplied by something outside deliberation itself. That looks to me like an ancient statement of the orthogonality thesis, with deliberation in the role of intelligence and wish, or the agent's character, in the role of the terminal goal. And his account of how the deliberator runs back from the end through the chain of means to the first link in the chain of causes looks like the very structure of means–end reasoning from which instrumental convergence is supposed to follow: fix an end, and certain means turn out to be required or favoured for nearly any end whatever.

I want to know whether this is a genuine identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion. Please address, in particular: (a) whether Aristotle's claim survives as an account of deliberation, or whether it depends on features of human motivation that the orthogonality thesis deliberately sets aside; (b) what the parallel would predict and whether the prediction holds — for instance, does it commit Aristotle to the possibility of an excellent deliberator with a wicked end, or to the convergence of all competent agents on the same instrumental subgoals, and did he in fact say anything that bears on either point; (c) what would have to be true of both sides for results to transfer from one field to the other. I would be grateful for a precise mapping of the two structures, a clear statement of where they match and where they come apart, and a final judgement on whether the parallel is deep or superficial.

Please write in British English.
