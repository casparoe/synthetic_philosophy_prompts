I'm giving a seminar next month at a joint philosophy–statistics workshop, and I'm already dreading the Q&A. My talk defends a strong version of the Jaynes line: Cox's theorem shows that any system of rational inference that (i) represents degrees of belief as real numbers and (ii) satisfies two minimal-sounding consistency conditions—the plausibility of A-and-B is some function of the plausibility of B and of the plausibility of A-given-B, and the plausibility of not-A is some function of the plausibility of A—must make those numbers obey the probability axioms, so probability really is the logic of uncertain inference and not just a modelling tool. The audience is mostly formal epistemologists and Bayesian statisticians who are half-persuaded already, but there's two frequentists who think priors are just regularization, and one imprecise-probability person who thinks the real-valued degree-of-belief setup is the exact thing that should be questioned. I'm weak on how much the theorem leans on continuity or differentiability assumptions and on whether the domain has to be rich enough for the functional equations to close, so assume they'll find that.
