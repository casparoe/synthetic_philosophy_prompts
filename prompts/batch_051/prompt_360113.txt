I've been following the recent disputes over whether images produced with generative models can be copyrighted, and I'm struck by how odd the settled institutional practice looks when you stand back from it. In the United States, the Copyright Office takes the position that a work must have a human author to be registrable: an image generated entirely by a text-to-image system with no further human intervention receives no protection, even if the prompt was clever or the output is striking. Yet the same office will register a work when a human has made creative choices in selecting, arranging, or modifying the machine's output, even if the machine produced all the underlying imagery. The line, in other words, is drawn not on the basis of what the final image looks like, nor on how much skill or effort went into making it, but on whether some human act of selection or arrangement can be located somewhere in the causal chain.

I would like you to treat this as an exercise in reconstructing an implicit theory. What view of creativity — or authorship, or art-making — does this practice presuppose? Does it presuppose, for instance, that creativity lives in intentional acts like choosing and arranging rather than in the qualities of the finished object, and that no process or output can be creative unless a human mental act stands behind it? Which rival views does that quietly rule out, such as the view that creativity can be a property of a generative process or of a work judged on its own terms, or the view that machines could participate in authorship as collaborators rather than tools?

I am also curious whether the practice is internally coherent. Does it lean on one, fairly demanding notion of authorship when it excludes wholly machine-produced works, and then switch to a much thinner, almost procedural notion when it admits lightly curated machine output? How much of this is strictly presupposed by the practice, and how much merely fits a romantic picture of the lone human creator that the institution could in principle do without? Finally, spell out concretely what would have to change in the practice if we adopted the rival view that the creative properties relevant to protection belong to works or processes, not to the mental acts of human agents. Please write the answer in American English and keep it accessible to someone without a legal background.
