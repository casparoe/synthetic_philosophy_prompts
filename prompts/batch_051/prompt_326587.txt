I am a novelist, not a philosopher, and I have been pulled into a dispute I do not know how to adjudicate. The novel I am writing turns in part on the interior life of a dog—a herding dog in a dwindling mountain village—and I want the reader to treat her fear, grief, and loyalty as genuine psychological facts rather than as the narrator's sentimental projection. At a dinner party, a historian of science objected to this, invoking the old slogan that "animals are machines" and adding that Descartes had settled the matter centuries ago; on his account, a novelist who writes grief into a dog is only wrenching pathos out of a clockwork. I am willing to be corrected, but I cannot tell what correction is actually being offered. The slogan might mean that animals lack an immortal soul, or that they have no consciousness, or that they cannot reason, or that they lack language and therefore thought, or that their behavior is wholly mechanically explicable, or merely that their inner lives are inaccessible to us. These are plainly different claims, and they bear very differently on the scene I am trying to write. I would like the slogan taken apart and each candidate reading assessed rather than defended as a package.

Please structure your answer as follows. (1) Readings (roughly 600 words): distinguish at least five precise claims the slogan could be used to assert, ordered from least to most demanding, with a short name and a one-sentence formulation for each. (2) Verdicts (roughly 400 words): for each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or most likely false, and briefly why. (3) What Descartes actually needed (roughly 300 words): identify which reading or readings Descartes's own position required, and note which distinctions the slogan collapses even though he may not have needed or held them. (4) What the objection needs (roughly 300 words): determine which reading or readings the dinner-party objection would have to establish in order genuinely to undercut a novel written as I have described, and say whether any defensible reading gets him there. (5) Bottom line for the scene (roughly 200 words): recommend whether the slogan, once disambiguated, should make me revise the dog's grief, and if so how.
