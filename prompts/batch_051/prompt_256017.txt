At a reading group, two friends got stuck on a sentence from the Supreme Court's opinion in Brandenburg v. Ohio (1969), quoting Noto v. United States:

"the mere abstract teaching * * * of the moral propriety or even moral necessity for a resort to force and violence, is not the same as preparing a group for violent action and steeling it to such action."

Marco said the sentence basically gets the American rule right. Teaching a doctrine, even a violent one, tries to convince people of a proposition, and we can answer it with better arguments. Preparing or steeling a group is different: it works on the group's readiness to act, not on the truth of a proposition, so the state may step in. The boundary has hard cases, but the distinction tracks something real.

Priya said the case itself shows the boundary is unstable. The Court used that distinction to overturn the conviction of a Klan leader whose rally involved armed, hooded men and a burning cross. If that scenario counts as 'mere abstract teaching,' then the abstract/preparation line is so permissive that it practically collapses. She said the distinction describes the Court's choice about which risks it is willing to tolerate, not a natural feature of speech.

Marco replied that all legal lines are chosen. The question is whether the line tracks a morally relevant difference. A philosophy essay defending political violence and a manual telling people how to build a bomb are different in kind, and the fact that some cases are hard doesn't erase that.

Priya said even this concedes too much, because many European hate speech laws don't require a link to violence at all. They restrict public expression of racial contempt because it can harm equal dignity and social standing, not because it makes violence likely. So the Brandenburg test just talks past the rationale behind the European approach.

Marco countered that this is the problem: once "dignity" becomes a sufficient state interest, almost any speech that offends a group can be regulated. The American rule requires more—a real connection to unlawful action—because the alternative gives majorities a permanent tool to silence disfavored speakers.
