I direct the triage unit in a state attorney general's consumer protection division. We review standard-form consumer and employment contracts and decide which mandatory pre-dispute arbitration clauses should be escalated as an abuse worth formal attention—an enforcement referral or a proposed legislative amendment—as opposed to being treated as routine contract terms. We handle roughly forty clauses a month; the screeners are recent law graduates, each doing one pass in under an hour, and both false positives and false negatives cost us credibility. I need a reusable screening procedure, not a case-by-case intuition, that operationalizes the ideal that adjudication should remain a public, impartial, reason-giving process rather than a privately controlled service. The procedure should take a plain-language summary of an arbitration clause and produce a decision with reasons, specifying which features are warning signs—for example, confidentiality, one-sided selection or control of the arbitrator, cost or forum barriers, limits on remedies or public-court review—how those features compound or cancel, and which features screeners should ignore as red herrings. It must distinguish clauses whose defect is that consent was not genuinely voluntary from clauses whose defect lies in the design of the tribunal even granting genuine consent. It should say explicitly where a screener must make a judgment call despite the checklist. Apply it to two or three realistic clause summaries, including one where the procedure says not to escalate even though we would rather flag it, and state clearly what the procedure is not able to decide.
