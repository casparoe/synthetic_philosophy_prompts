I've been trying to figure out what one open-source community actually believes about free riding, because their practice seems to face three ways at once. A maintainer releases a small library under a permissive license — the kind that says, in effect: take this, build on it, sell it, no need to contribute back or pay, just keep the notice. A few years later a big company ships a paid product that is, under the hood, mostly this library. The company never contributes a line back, never sponsors the project, never files a bug report. The maintainer burns out and posts an angry essay about leeches, and the community — which had praised the license for getting the library adopted — mostly cheers. But the same community has no problem with a graduate student using the same library in her free app without giving anything back; nobody calls her a leech. So the practice seems to say three things at once: taking without contributing is fine (the license); taking without contributing is a wrong worth public anger (the reaction to the company); and taking without contributing is fine again when the taker is small and non-commercial (the grad student). I'm not interested in the legal side — assume the company is doing exactly what the license allows. What theory of free riding, fairness, or exploitation would make these three reactions hang together? Reconstruct the implicit view the practice presupposes, say whether it is coherent, and say what the maintainer should have done differently from the start — in licensing or in community norms — if they really held that view. I also want to know which parts of the practice are forced by the underlying theory and which are just optional.
