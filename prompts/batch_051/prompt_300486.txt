Last month the company I work for launched a new customer-onboarding system. It had been built by eight small teams, each of which tested its own module in isolation. Only after launch did it emerge that two of the modules interacted in a way that caused the system to reject almost every applicant over sixty-five. The effect was a predictable consequence of the design specification the teams had signed off, but no individual seems to have put the two requirements together before launch. The company must now state in its formal post-incident report whether the project team intended that outcome, and I have been asked to help draft that section.

Two members of the review committee argued about this in front of me and could not reach agreement. Their exchange, compressed, went roughly as follows.

(1) Anand maintained that the team plainly did not intend the outcome: intentions are mental states, a team has no mind, and so "the team intended" can only be metaphor or loose speech.

(2) Priya replied that this is too quick, because the same committee is about to issue findings and recommendations in the company's name. If talk of group decisions, judgements, and apologies is unproblematic, then group intention cannot be dismissed merely on the ground that a group has no mind; otherwise the committee's own findings are category mistakes too.

(3) Anand answered that the two cases differ. Decisions and apologies are statuses assigned to office-holders who perform them under rules; they do not require a collective mental state. The committee can decide without intending-as-a-group, so group intention can be dropped while group decisions are kept.

(4) Priya said this still leaves the causal centre unaccounted for. The harmful effect was produced by the interaction of modules, not by any individual's contribution; each contributor made locally reasonable choices. If no individual intended the outcome and the team cannot intend it, then the outcome was intended by no one, which makes the incident report unable to say what the company actually did.

(5) Anand responded that "intended by no one" is not the same as "caused by no one": eight people each failed to coordinate, and negligence can be apportioned among the eight without inventing a group intention.

(6) Priya replied that apportioning negligence misses the way the eight were acting under a shared plan: each person's choice was reasonable only on the assumption that others were doing their parts. The plan itself, and the decision to launch, are not equivalent to the sum of eight individual decisions, and it is precisely that "our decision" character that the report must capture.

They then turned to me and asked whether the report should say the team intended the outcome, or say that eight people chose badly. I genuinely could not tell. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each numbered move, say whether it is mostly right, mostly wrong, or mixed, and briefly justify each rating. Then say what both parties are missing and give your own view of how the report ought best to characterise the situation. Please write your answer in British English.
