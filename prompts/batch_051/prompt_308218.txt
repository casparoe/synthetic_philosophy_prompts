I've been using four criteria for saying an omission caused something, and I want to stress-test them. The criteria: (1) the person had the capacity, opportunity, and enough information to act; (2) if they had acted, the bad outcome probably wouldn't have happened; (3) there was some duty, role, or standing expectation that they, in particular, should have done it; (4) the omission, rather than some backround event, is what naturally gets singled out as making the difference. Now take this pair of cases. You pay a neighbor to water your plants while you're gone; she forgets; the plants die. Same plants, same dry week, but instead a burglar breaks in to steal your laptop, notices the plants are wilting, does nothing about them, and the plants die. Physically the same non-watering happend in both cases. Does the burglar's omission cause the plants' deaths? If you say no, that's probably because the burglar fails criterion (3) — but then criterion (3) is doing all the work, and "cause" for omissions starts to look like a moralized label rather than something in the world. If you say yes, then why does the identical physical non-event get treated as a cause in one case and not in the other? Go criterion by criterion, and say whether this case shows one of my criteria isn't necessary, or whether the way I've framed omissions is off from the start.
