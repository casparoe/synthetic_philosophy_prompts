I run a monthly discussion group for adults with no formal philosophy background, and the next session is on the antinatalist thought that coming into existence can be a harm even when the life in question is one you would rather go on living. The hinge of the session will be Benatar's asymmetry between pains and pleasures across existence and non-existence: roughly, the presence of a pain is bad, and the absence of that pain is good even where no one exists to enjoy its absence, while the presence of a pleasure is good, but the absence of that pleasure is not bad where no one is deprived of it. I am not asking you to explain the asymmetry to me. I am asking you to plan how I should explain it: the order in which the components must be introduced, what has to be in place before the four-box comparison can even be written on the board, and why the distinction between a life worth continuing and a life worth starting should be flagged early but not made to do the argument's work.

Give me a single running example or scenario that should carry the whole explanation, and say what it must have and what it must not have if it is going to support rather than distort the asymmetry. Then give me the misconception map for this audience: which misunderstandings they will predictably form (for instance, treating the never-existing person as if they had interests, collapsing the four boxes into two, or reading the asymmetry as the claim that all lives are miserable on net) and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. Tell me what I should explicitly refuse to defend at this level and why, and finish with one question I can put to the group at the end whose answer would show me whether they have grasped the asymmetry itself rather than merely assented to a conclusion they found edgy or counterintuitive.
