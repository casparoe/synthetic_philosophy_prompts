I'm a journalist on the science and ideas desk, and I've been turning over a thought experiment for an essay. Suppose that at midnight tommorow, every country with a judicially enforced bill of rights adopts a new constitutional rule. From that point on, whenever a court finds that a law or government action infringes a recognized fundamental right — where recognized means the right and the scope of its protection were already settled in binding precedent at midnight — the court must invalidate it. Full stop. The court may not balance the right against public health, public safety, national security, expense, decency, or other people's rights, and it may not run a proportionality test or a least-restrictive-means analysis. The recognized rights and their settled interpretations are then frozen: for fifty years they can be expanded but not narrowed. Nothing else changes.

This is the philosophers' idea that rights are trump cards written into hard law, with the rival pictures — rights as near-absolute constraints with catastrophe exceptions, rights as placeholders in a welfare calculation — removed from the judge's toolbox. I'm not asking whether the change is good. I want your best argument about what actually happens over the next twenty years. Where do the first crises come from: public health orders, speech regulation, surveillance, environmental rules? What workarounds do governments and courts actually adopt once balancing is off the table? I suspect the fights migrate into definitional questions — what counts as the right, what counts as an infringement, who is a rights-holder — but I want the mechanisms spelled out, not a narrative. In particular, what happens when two settled rights collide head-on and a court is forbidden to weigh either one? Then tell me which predictions you'd be genuinely confident in, which are guesses, and what would have to be different about how judges and bureaucrats behave for your whole prediction to flip. And one last thing: after two decades of this, does the concept of a right end up doing more real work in politics, or does it get hollowed out into a word governments fight over?
