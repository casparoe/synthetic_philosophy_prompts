Before I read one more paper on this I want the spec. Everywhere I look — a judge using a risk score at a bail hearing, an oncologist quoting five-year survival for 'stage II colorectal cancer with this mutation,' my car insurance quote, an election model saying a particular house district has a 38 percent chance — somebody is taking a rate from some class of similar cases and attaching it to a single case. But the same person lands in lots of classes that all feel natural, and they give different numbers. What would a theory of how to pick a reference class have to get right before I should trust it? Give me the requirements list, with the case or problem behind each one: what it has to explain, what it can't contradict, what distinctions it can't collapse. My hunch is it at least has to (a) not let two equally defensible groupings license two different probabilities for the same event, (b) respect the difference between a factor being statistically relevant and merely being easy to measure, (c) say something about the endpoint where the most specific class is just this exact person, whose history is one-shot or empty, so specificity alone can't be the whole answer — and also not just smuggle in 'use the most specific class that has enough data,' since 'enough data' is doing a lot of hidden work, (d) handle Simpson-style reversals where the direction of an assocation flips depending on how you partition. Are those right, and are any of them secretly optional or question-begging? Then tell me which single requirement rules out the most candidate views, and if any pair of them can't be jointly satisfied, say so — that's the part I actually want. A short table is fine.
