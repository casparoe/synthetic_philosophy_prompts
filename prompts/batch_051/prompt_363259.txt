My brother and I keep having the same fight, and it finally bled into something concrete: he wants to keep pushing our dad to sign up for a clinical trial for his back, and I said there's no point telling our dad "you have a reason to do the trial" when the trial doesn't hook onto anything Dad actually wants—he's risk adverse, he hates hospitals, and he's more or less made peace with the pain. I've noticed I apply the same rule everywhere now. I won't count a consideration as a reason for a person unless, after sound deliberation from what they already care about, it could actually move them. I treat talk of reasons that float free of anyone's motives as loose talk, and I won't argue from such reasons when I'm trying to convince somebody, or even when I'm trying to decide whether a person is being unreasonable. My brother says I've turned into a full-on Humean internalist and that I'm psychologizing reasons into oblivion.

The passage I keep coming back to is Hume, from the Treatise of Human Nature, Book II, Part III, Section III, "Of the influencing motives of the will":

Here then reasoning takes place to discover this relation; and according as our reasoning varies, our actions receive a subsequent variation. But 'tis evident in this case, that the impulse arises not from reason, but is only directed by it. 'Tis from the prospect of pain or pleasure that the aversion or propensity arises towards any object: And these emotions extend themselves to the causes and effects of that object, as they are pointed out to us by reason and experience. It can never in the least concern us to know, that such objects are causes, and such others effects, if both the causes and effects be indifferent to us.

That seems to me like exactly my view: reason traces the routes between someone's ends and their means, but the push has to come from the ends themselves. Am I reading Hume right, and is my rule actually defensible? Or am I just confusing "would motivate" with "has reason to," and building a whole conversational policy out of a psychological claim? If the rule is too crude, is there a version of it that keeps what's attractive about it—refusing to pretend reasons just apply to people from nowhere—without denying that someone can be rationally criticized for not caring about what they'd come to care about if they deliberated better?
