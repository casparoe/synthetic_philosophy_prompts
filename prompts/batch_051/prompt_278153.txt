I'm in a class this term on lying, bullshit, and misinformation, and we just read Frankfurt on bullshit. After section, two classmates got into an argument about LLM hallucination and I can't sort out whose right.

Maya started by saying it's obviously not lying: lying requires believing the claim is false and intending to deceive, and a language model believes nothing and intends nothing. Jonah said sure, but Frankfurt's point is that bullshit is different from lying because it doesn't require an intent to deceive. The liar at least cares enough about the truth to hide it; the bullshitter just doesn't care whether what they say is true. And that, he said, describes a chatbot exactly. It's optimized to produce fluent, plausible text, not to keep its outputs in line with the facts. So he called LLM hallucination industrialized bullshit.

Maya came back that indifference is a mental state. A rock doesn't care about truth either, but nobody calls a rock a bullshitter. Frankfurt's bullshitter is a person who misrepresents what they're up to: they present themselves as trying to say how things are while actually having no stake in that. A model isn't up to anything, so the concept doesn't apply. At most, she said, hallucination is an error in the plain engineering sense: a system produced a false string because it wasn't built to track truth in the first place.

Jonah replied that she was being too individualist. We can ask who stands behind the output and is answerable for it. The company put the thing in front of users as a helpful assistant, and users reasonably treat its confident answers as claims. If the company knows it makes stuff up and ships it anyway because fluency is what sells, then the company is running a bullshit operation, and calling the outputs bullshit is just tracking where the responsibility actually sits. He said the distinction between lying, bullshit, and error has to be applied to whoever stands behind the speech, not to the model's nonexistent inner life.

Maya said that move relocates the argument without settling it. Whether a company is bullshitting depends on its internal attitudes, which you can't read off outputs. And if you stretch "bullshit" to cover any truth-indifferent process, the term stops doing philosophical work: a random sentence generator would count, and then the difference between Frankfurt's bullshitter and mere noise disappears. The hallucination is still just an error by a system being used for something it wasn't designed to do.

Jonah wouldn't budge. The philosophical work is preserved, he said, because the norm applies wherever we hold a speaker to truth-telling. Users do hold chatbots to truth norms, and companies actively invite that when they market them as reliable. So the indifference to truth built into the system is exactly the kind of failure Frankfurt was pointing at, once you locate the speech act at the entity that faces the user.

At that point the TA told us to wrap up, and the whole exchange left me more confused then before. Can you go through this move by move and say who's right about what, where each of them overreaches, and who ends up more right overall about whether LLM hallucination is lying, bullshit, or error?
