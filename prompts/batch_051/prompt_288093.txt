Okay so I'm on the faculty governance committee at my university and we've been going back and forth for weeks about the student code of conduct. The specific fight is over the word "harm" — half the committee wants a capacious definition so that all sorts of emotional and ambient harms count, and the other half says the definition is already fine and we're just redefining a word to win a policy argument. Someone floated a proposal I can't get out of my head, partly because it sounds clever and partly because it sounds like it would explode. The proposal is this: every five years the university convenes a concept revision panel (faculty, students, administrators) which must pick exactly one contested term from a public list — harm, consent, merit, accessibility, academic integrity, and so on — and replace its official definition with a revised one, adopted by two-thirds vote. Any office or academic unit whose policies use the old definition then has two years to bring everything inline with the new one. Anyone in the university can petition to add a term to the list, and the panel votes on those petitions before choosing its one term for the cycle.

I've been reading a bit about conceptual engineering and ameliorative analysis — the idea that sometimes we shouldn't just describe how a concept like harm or consent actually works but deliberately replace it with a better one for the purpose we want it to serve — so I get the intellectual appeal: this is conceptual engineering turned into a bureaucracy. But I'd like to understand it as a mechanism, not as a nice-sounding idea. How would it actually play out? What would it incentivize among faculty, students, administrators, and departments? How would it get gamed — would the panel quietly pick the least controversial term every cycle, or would "harm" become a permanent campaign issue, or would the forced-revision rule produce a pile of brand-new definitions nobody uses? What happens to the terms that never get picked, and to ordinary usage that goes its own way while the official definitions sit there in the handbook? Does the fact that concepts are partly descriptive and partly normative give this thing a built-in failure mode, and does that track the distinction between fixing concepts and merely analysing them? Where exactly would it break, and are the failure modes fixable by tweaking the rules, or are they symptoms of trying to settle conceptual questions by vote in the first place?
