After a long argument with a friend about whether freedom from disturbance is an ideal worth wanting or merely a flattering name for feeling less, I have been trying to pin down the difference between the two. I would like you to use the following criteria for what I will call genuine equanimity, understood broadly as the kind of apatheia that is an achievement rather than a defect:

1. Global stability. The calm is not limited to a few rehearsed situations; it holds across ordinary insults, losses, reversals, and successes.
2. Intact cognition. The calm does not depend on false or self-deceiving judgments, such as denying that a loss is a loss or systematically underestimating the stakes.
3. Preserved evaluation. The person continues to judge that some things are genuinely good or bad and is practically moved to pursue or avoid them; what has been removed is turbulent feeling, not caring about outcomes.
4. Non-suppression. The calm is not bought by white-knuckled inhibition or by dissociating from experience; the person remains present to what happens rather than numb to it.

Now consider a case. A person has spent a decade in intensive meditation practice and more recently took an SSRI for anxiety. They report that events that once provoked anger, shame, or dread now rarely throw them; when a close relative died, they recognized the loss, rearranged their commitments, and wept briefly, but did not feel the familiar ache. They describe their inner life as clearer but quieter. Under pressure they are more reliable than they used to be, and they continue to work hard for the things they judge valuable. But friends who have known them for years say they have also become muted in their humor, enthusiasm, and spontaneous delight. The person insists those feelings are still present, only less loud.

Does this person exhibit genuine equanimity by the above criteria, or have they purchased calm at the cost of the very responsiveness the criteria are meant to protect? Work through the criteria one by one, and where a criterion does not settle the matter, say so explicitly. Then indicate whether the case shows that the criteria themselves need revision, for example whether an adequate account of equanimity should separately protect positive emotions, or should distinguish felt caring from practical caring. Finally, state how confident you are in your overall verdict and single out the point you are least sure of.
