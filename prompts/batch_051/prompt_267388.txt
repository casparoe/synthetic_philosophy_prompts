I write novels for a living, which means I spend half my day pretending fictional people matter and the other half wondering whether anything matters. I'm heading to an arts foundation residency where each fellow has to present a one-page statement of what they actually believe, and when I tried to write mine I realized my nihilism has never been stress-tested as a package. I'd like an audit rather than a lecture: tell me where the view cracks, not what I'm supposed to think to keep the residency people happy. Here's the list I keep endorsing, in no particular order.

(1) Moral error theory: there are no objective moral facts. A sentence like torture is wrong tries to report a fact, but there is no fact of that kind, so the sentence is false, not merely relative to a culture or a person.

(2) Fictionalism about the moral stuff: we should go on making moral judgments, blaming, resenting, forgiving, feeling guilt. These dont track anything real, but they're an indispensable fiction, like caring about a character while knowing she never existed.

(3) Existential nihilism: the universe does not assign purposes to us; there is no meaning of life out there to be discovered.

(4) Nonetheless, I think it can be true, not just consoling, that one life is more meaningful than another. A life with a genuinely well-made narrative shape—a real arc rather than one imposed by self-flattery—has a meaningfulness that a life of unconnected episodes lacks. And I don't mean only meaningful-to-the-person-living-it; I mean it is an actual feature of that life.

(5) Mereological nihilism: strictly speaking, there are no composite objects. There are no tables, books, or persons. There are only simples arranged table-wise, book-wise, person-wise, and ordinary object-talk is false but useful, like most of my other fictions.

(6) And yet when I finish a novel, I cant help thinking of it as one thing that persists. It isn't just a loose sum of paper, pixels, and memories, and it doesn't vanish when a draft is deleted. I also hold people accountable across the whole of a life; I refuse the line that the person who did something awful at twenty literally no longer exists at fifty.

Go through these and tell me which pairs actually conflict and which only seem to. Rank the real conflicts by how severe they are, instead of treating them all as equally bad. Then tell me the single cheapest revision that restores consistency, and what it costs me—what I'd have to stop saying in my novels and at parties. I don't need the view to be cheery; I need to know whether I can honestly live inside it.
