I've been kicking around a view about music that I don't have a totally settled name for — for now I call it "track-first" ontology. The core claim is that for studio-recorded popular music, the work isn't the song, and it isn't a repeatable type (like a score) that performances instantiate. The work is the released recording itself: the specific master, with its specific ambience, edits, and mix. So when people argue about whether a live version "changed the song," I think they're already leaning on a fiction. On my view, a live version of Strawberry Fields Forever is not a performance of the work at all — it's a cover of the 1967 studio single, even when it's the same band on stage. A re-recording of an old album by the same artist is a new work with a historical link to the old one, not another instance of it. Two pressings of the same master: same work. A remaster: same work, as long as the mix isn't actually changed. A remix: new work. A cover by someone else: new, derivative work.

I know this has wierd consequences. It demotes "the song" to something like a family resemblance across versions, and it gets uncomfortable for music that predates recording or for a song that has two equally canonical recorded takes. But for alot of rock and pop, the record really is the thing people care about, so maybe that's the point.

Before I go around defending this, I want an honest assessment. Is it coherent? Has someone already worked it out under another name (I half-remember hearing something about rock recordings being the works)? What does it get right that a song-first or performance-first picture misses? And give me the three strongest objections, including at least one that isn't just uncomfortable but actually fatal. Finally: does it break in a way that restricting it to recording-based popular music doesn't fix, or does that restriction just turn it into a different, safer theory — and maybe a less interesting one?
