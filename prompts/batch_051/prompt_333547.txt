Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique attacked the adaptationist habit of treating every trait as an optimal solution to a design problem. The critique had at least three components: that alternatives to adaptation—developmental constraint, correlated byproducts, drift, and exaptation—were routinely ignored; that adaptationist stories could be generated for almost any trait and were therefore cheap; and that the historical evidence needed to choose among these alternatives was usually unavailable. The evidential situation has changed considerably since the critique was made. Genome-wide data, phylogenetic comparative methods, experimental evolution, and statistical scans for signatures of selection now allow many adaptationist claims to be tested rather than merely asserted. Does the spandrels critique survive this development, or has it been absorbed into standard evolutionary biology and rendered obsolete? Be explicit about which component of the critique is actually under pressure. In particular, is its force chiefly methodological, in that adaptationist claims were unfalsifiable, or substantive, in that a large share of traits are not adaptations, and does the new toolbox settle that question or merely relocate it? If the critique survives, state its remaining form with as much precision as you can; if it is dead or absorbed, say what has replaced it. Address the specific worry that a statistical signature of past selection does not by itself show that a trait is an adaptation for its current function, since byproducts and exaptations can be favored by selection after they first arise. Please write your answer in American English.
