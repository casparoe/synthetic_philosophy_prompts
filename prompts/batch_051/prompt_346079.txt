I have a view about the relationship between two questions in the historiography of the First World War: what caused the war, and who is to blame for it. I hold that the two questions are largely independent, and that the Fischer controversy draws much of its heat from running them together. The causal question concerns how explanatory weight should be divided among German decision-making in the July Crisis, Austro-Hungarian aims toward Serbia, Russian general mobilization, the alliance system, and contingency; the blame question is about what kind of thing can bear moral responsibility for a war at all — individuals, a cabinet, a general staff, a government, a state, a nation. My position is that even a maximally strong version of Fritz Fischer's claim — that German leaders deliberately risked and then fought a major European war to secure hegemonic power for Germany — would not by itself show that Germany rather than certain individuals within the German state is the right unit of blame, and that a revisionist account spreading causal weight across all the powers would not by itself show that no one is to blame. I want to know what would count against this position. Please distinguish three kinds of consideration: historical findings about 1914 that would significantly weaken my claim that the causal and the moral questions come apart in this case; arguments about collective responsibility, from either direction, that would show the distinction cannot bear the weight I place on it; and objections that a committed defender of the position should themselves concede as counting against it, as opposed to objections that merely change the subject. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and include an honest assessment of whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that would be a problem for a view of this kind.
