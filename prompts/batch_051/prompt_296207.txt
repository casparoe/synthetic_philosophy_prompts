A friend and I spent most of last evening arguing over the status of HCH — the recursive picture in which a single human answers a hard question by factoring it into subquestions, passing each to a copy of themselves, and integrating the returned answers, with the same move available at every level. HCH is usually introduced as a way of making sense of amplification, but my friend insisted that treating it as an idealization of individual human deliberation is a substantive philosophical claim, not a harmless simplification. Real deliberation, he said, is not cleanly factored: judgments leak across subquestions, conclusions are path-dependent, and the integration step is itself a large part of the thinking. I countered that every idealization exaggerates some feature of its target, and HCH exaggerates exactly the feature — decomposition into local, recombinable judgments — that makes deliberation truth-tracking when it goes well.

Put the contested claim — that HCH is a faithful idealization of human deliberation, not merely a computational scaffold — into an operationalized form, and propose a concrete empirical test of it, behavioral or computational or both. I would rather have a committed answer than a survey: choose the operationalization and the experimental design you think are most probative, and defend a definite position on what such a test would show.
