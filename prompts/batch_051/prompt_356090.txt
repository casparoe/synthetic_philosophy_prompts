I've been trying to think clearly about whether a bigger family, city, or species is, just by being bigger, better. A colleague suggested that if we focus on cities the question becomes unstable, and she laid out the following argument. I find the conclusion unacceptable, but I can't locate the step that should be given up.

She begins with two thoughts. First, a city is not a thing over and above the people who live in it: to say a city has improved or declined is just to say something about the lives lived within it. Second, when we actually compare cities by quality of life, we compare averages — average well-being, average health, average satisfaction — not raw size. She thinks these two thoughts, each harmless on its own, force an absurd conclusion.

(1) A city is a better city to the extent that the lives lived within it are better.
(2) The lives lived within a city are, on the whole, better when the average well-being of its residents is higher.
(3) Therefore, other things equal, a city with a higher average well-being is a better city.
(4) When a resident whose well-being is below the current average leaves, the average well-being of the remaining residents is higher than it was.
(5) Therefore, other things equal, a city is made better by the departure of its below-average residents — through emigration, through exclusion, or through expulsion.
(6) But that is unacceptable: a city does not become better because its unhappiest residents have left it; such a policy, if anything, makes the city worse, not better.
(7) So one of the earlier steps must go. Yet the natural repair is unpalatable too. If we say that what matters is not average but total well-being, the same style of reasoning yields: a sufficiently large city in which every life is barely worth living would be better than a much smaller city in which every life is flourishing. That seems just as hard to accept.

I would prefer to keep (1) and (6), but I can't tell which step should bear the rejection, or what giving it up costs when we try to say whether a bigger city matters.
