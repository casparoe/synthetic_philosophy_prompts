I'm a backend engineer and never studied philosophy, but I've been reading my way through the consciousness debates on my own. Because of my job, I automatically think in terms of processes, threads, daemons, event loops, loggers, watchdog timers, health checks, and signal handlers. So when I read theories where a mental state becomes conscious by being the target of another mental state, my brain keeps trying to compile them into that vocabulary. As far as I can tell, the main ones are the higher-order thought view (a state is conscious because you have a thought about it), the higher-order perception or inner-scan view (it becomes conscious because some monitor-like state is pointed at it), and the self-representational view (the state includes some awareness of itself and doesn't need a separate watcher). I also keep seeing a first-order competitor that says the state is already conscious on its own, and a reentrant one that says you need a signal going back into the same circuit rather than a separate higher-level state. The objections I've picked up are the misrepresentation worry (the watcher can be wrong about what it's watching, and then what the experience seems like might float free of the state it's supposed to be about), and the regress worry (what makes the watcher conscious, and why doesn't this just stack forever).

What I'd like is a systematic translation of this whole debate into my process/watcher vocabulary, treating the programming concepts as the target language rather than a metaphor you dip into now and then. For each of those positions and for the main objections, tell me what carries over cleanly, what has to be bent or silently extended to fit, and what has no expression at all in process talk. Then give me your read on the residue—especially the felt, first-person quality of a state—whether it only looks untranslatable because I'm using the wrong home vocabulary, or wether it points to something the higher-order debate itself has never really nailed down.
