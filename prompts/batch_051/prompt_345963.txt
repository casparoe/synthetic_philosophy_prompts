I've been messing with a distinction I want checked against Strawson's picture of the reactive attitudes. Strawson says that when someone is exempt from ordinary responsibility—a small child, someone in a psychotic break, or really a broken machine—we can't hold the participant stance toward them, so we drop the reactive attitude and settle into the objective stance. I think that's too clean. I call what actually happens reactive residue: the resentment or indignation doesn't dissapear, it just gets redirected away from the person and toward the situation. You're not angry at the kid, but you're angry at something—at the fact that the participant stance has failed you, at the absence of anyone to hold to the demand. My claim is that exemptions don't suspend reactive attitudes; they anonymize them. That, I think, explains why people often feel worse, not better, when they learn an offender was incompetent or insane: the anger stays but loses its defendant. Is this a real distinction, or does it collapse into something Strawson already says about the objective stance? And where does it break—what does it wrongly count as a reactive attitude?
