I'm a PhD student in decision theory and I keep going back and forth on the evidential cooperation in large worlds arguement. The rough idea is this: in a big enough world, my deciding to cooperate in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma with a stranger doesn't cause the stranger to cooperate, but it is evidence that other agents who share my decision procedure will cooperate in their own one-shot dilemmas, because all our choices are generated by processes of the same kind. If there are enough such correlated agents out there, the expected evidential payoff of cooperating — the summed benefit from all those other agents' likely cooperation — can outweigh the direct cost of being exploited by the actual stranger in front of me. For an evidential decision theorist, then, cooperating in an ordinary, causally isolated one-shot prisoner's dilemma can rationally come out as the right move. Please argue that this whole line is undercut by the reference class problem: there is no principled, non-question-begging way to carve out which agents count as relevantly similar to me for the purposes of the evidential correlation, so the argument only works once a similarity metric has already been slipped in that favours cooperation. Could you write the answer in British English?
