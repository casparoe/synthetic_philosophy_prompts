You are an expert in legal and political philosophy. Please reason carefully and do not settle for the first answer: begin by brainstorming the candidate principles that might draw the line, test each one against nearby cases, and only then give a verdict.

I am a high school student, and I have been puzzling over an asymmetry in ordinary judgment and in the law. A landlord who refuses to rent to a family because they are Muslim, or an employer who will not hire someone because she is a woman, is widely regarded as having done something seriously wrong, and in many places such refusals are illegal. Yet the same people who condemn those refusals tend to shrug when the same landlord refuses a tenant for having a visible neck tattoo, for seeming unintelligent, or for being born in a particular month, and when the same employer rejects an applicant for being shy, for supporting a particular political party, for being a poor culture fit, or for having no sense of humour. One set of traits is protected; the other is not.

I cannot see what principle draws that line. The obvious candidate is that people should not be judged on traits they cannot change. But it does not fit the line: religion is protected even though it can be changed; marital status is protected in many places; while height, accent, temperament, and physical attractiveness are largely beyond a person's control and are routinely unprotected. A second candidate is that a ground is suspect when it is irrelevant to the decision. But except for a narrow range of jobs, race and sex are just as irrelevant as star sign or a poor sense of humour, so that principle seems to swallow the whole distinction. A third candidate is that protected grounds are simply those with a history of group subordination; but that would make the list a record of past politics rather than a matter of principle, and it is unclear why history singles out some disadvantaged groups and not others, such as the poor.

I want the strongest principle you can find, not a summary of the law. Is there any principle that generates the distinction between protected and unprotected grounds? If so, test it hard against appearance, accent, class, political affiliation, religion, maturity, and culture fit, and tell me whether the distinction should be kept, reversed, or given up. If not, say why it is most honest to treat the distinction as a psychological and historical accident we have decided to live with.
