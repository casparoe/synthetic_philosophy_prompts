I work on systems that fit Bayesian models, and I keep running into a version of a puzzle that seems to belong to the foundations of probability. The principle of indifference says that when no evidence distinguishes several possibilities, you should spread probability evenly across them. Bertrand's paradox is normally presented as a decisive objection: if you ask for a random chord of a circle and apply indifference to different parameterizations — two endpoints chosen uniformly on the circumference, a point chosen uniformly along a radius with the perpendicular chord through it, or a point chosen uniformly inside the circle and taken as the chord's midpoint — you get different probabilities (one third, one half, and one quarter) for the same event, namely that the chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Indifference therefore appears not to determine a distribution at all, because treating possibilities equally depends on how the possibilities are carved up. In modeling terms, this is the familiar fact that a uniform prior over a parameter does not stay uniform under a nonlinear transformation of that parameter.

Now consider a development that is often described as fixing exactly this flaw. In various modern treatments, naive indifference is replaced by a rule meant to pick out a privileged prior: use something like Solomonoff-style algorithmic probability, which assigns more probability to hypotheses with shorter descriptions, or require the distribution to be invariant under the symmetries of the problem in the style of maximum-entropy or transformation-group arguments. In the discrete prediction and model-selection settings where these approaches are usually applied, they can yield a canonical or near-canonical prior where classical indifference does not. They look like repairs aimed at closing the hole Bertrand exposed.

My question is whether Bertrand's paradox still goes through against one of these repaired rules, or whether it is dead once indifference is replaced in this way. It seems to me the paradox could reappear at a deeper level: algorithmic probability depends on a choice of reference machine or encoding, and an invariance rule depends on which symmetry group is taken to define the problem, and those choices look structurally similar to the choice of parameterization that made classical indifference ambiguous. Does the paradox survive the repair in that form? If it does, what is its lesson now — is it that no indifference-style rule can pick out a distribution without some external anchor, or is there a reason to think the new choices are fixed by the problem itself? If the repair genuinely dissolves the paradox, say which premise of the original argument it rejects.
