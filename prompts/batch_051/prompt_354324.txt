I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I keep running into this while designing a value function for comparing simulated outcomes. Here's the question. Most people will say a mild headache can be outweighed by some very large but finite amount of good — pleasure, money, lives improved. But many of the same people want to say that severe, below-some-threshold suffering is not like that: no amount of happiness makes an outcome containing it all-things-considered better than an outcome that avoids it. On what I've seen called the lexical threshold view, sub-threshold suffering behaves like negative infinity in a cost function — it dominates everything else rather than being traded off against it. More precisely: there is some level of suffering such that any outcome containing an instance of it is worse, all things considered, than any outcome that avoids it, no matter how much other good the first outcome contains.

What is your credence that the lexical threshold view is true as a claim about the all-things-considered betterness relation between possible outcomes, not merely as a useful heuristic or a rule of action? Give me one number between 0 and 1.

Then decompose the reasoning. I can see at least these moving parts: (a) whether pleasure and pain are genuinely commensurable on a single ratio scale, in which case the question is just how large the weights are; (b) whether an apparent threshold is better explained by very large finite weights plus failures of imagination; (c) whether the threshold, if real, belongs to the betterness relation itself rather than to a deontological constraint on choice. Assign a rough probability to each sub-question, show how they combine into your number, and tell me which one moves the final number most. Then commit: if forced to implement it tonight, do I model sub-threshold suffering as negative infinity or as a very large finite weight, and why?
