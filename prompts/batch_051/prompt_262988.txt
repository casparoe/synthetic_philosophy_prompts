In the science fiction circles I move in, I keep hearing a soothing analogy: gradually replacing a person's neurons, one at a time, with functionally identical artificial units until the brain is wholly synthetic is just the Ship of Theseus on a neural scale, and since the ship remains the same ship, the person remains the same person. I am a novelist and want to use this argument in a novel, but I suspect the analogy is carrying more than its materials allow. John Locke, in An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, Book II, Chapter XXVII, writes: "That, therefore, that had one beginning, is the same thing ; and that which had a different beginning in time and place from that, is not the same, but diverse." Locke there is treating the identity of substances generally, not consciousness. I want you to audit the analogy: give me the explicit correspondence table between the ship and the gradually replaced mind, identify where the mapping breaks, and tell me whether the identity conclusion the analogy is used to support — that the person survives — survives once Locke's principle about beginnings is brought to bear. If there is a better analogy for gradual replacement, say what it is; I am not looking for a survey of the literature.
