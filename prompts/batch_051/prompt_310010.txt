I work as a computational neuroscientist, and the framework I think in is predictive processing and active inference. The core notions I work with are these: the brain as a generative model that produces top-down predictions; bottom-up prediction error; precision weighting, where the brain tunes the gain on prediction error according to estimated reliability; free-energy or prediction-error minimization; active inference, where action changes the sensory stream to fit the model; and interoceptive prediction, where the same machinery runs over signals from inside the body. I have enough fluency to follow a systematic translation into this vocabulary, and enough honesty to want to be told when the translation is being bent.

I know the philosophy of emotion only in outline. The main positions seem to be: (i) emotions are judgments or evaluative appraisals about what matters to us; (ii) emotions are perceptions, or quasi-perceptions, of value or of bodily changes; and (iii) emotions are essentially feelings, especially felt bodily states, where a James–Lange-style view holds that the feeling follows from bodily changes rather than causing them. Please restate each of these positions, one by one, in the vocabulary of predictive processing.

For each of the three views, do the following: first, say which claims translate cleanly, and give the counterpart claim in predictive-processing terms; second, say which claims translate only under distortion, where fitting them into the vocabulary alters what the view was originally committed to; third, say which claims have no counterpart in the target vocabulary at all. Do not flatten the positions into a single story, and do not smuggle in the assumption that the predictive-processing description is automatically the correct or complete one.

Then give me a residue report. Across all three translations, tell me which parts of the philosophical debate survive the move into predictive-processing vocabulary, which parts drop out as artifacts of a particular philosophical idiom, and which parts resist translation in a way that suggests the predictive-processing framework is missing something the philosophers could see. Be explicit about the direction of failure in each case: sometimes the residue is a defect of the target framework, and sometimes it is a sign that the original claim was less determinate than it looked.

Finally, offer a verdict: after this exercise, are the three positions best understood as competing claims about what emotions are, as different choices about where in a single predictive-processing architecture to locate the emotion, or as partly incompatible explanatory strategies that cannot be made to share a single description? Throughout, separate what is broadly settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this topic, and where you are giving one plausible interpretation among several, flag it as such rather than presenting it as the consensus.
