I teach an upper-level undergraduate epistemology seminar — 14 students, 75-minute block — and we're on rational belief under uncertainty: the lottery paradox and the preface paradox. I've done the boring version before: I hand out a page of innocuous factual claims, we stitch them into a booklet, and I ask who will sign a preface saying at least one claim in this booklet is false. Almost everyone signs, then we argue, and it goes flat, because signing costs nothing and the claims aren't theirs. I want a version where endorsing the conjunction—or the preface—actually bites, so the tension shows up in what students do rather than in what I say afterward.

Could you design an activity for that group and time slot that makes them feel the pull of believing each item individually while also believing the collection contains at least one error—and that lets the lottery case and the preface case come apart? Rules, materials, a scoring or payoff scheme that dosen't use real money, and a debrief mapping the moments of the activity onto the formal point: in the lottery case, that some ticket wins is a known truth, while in the preface case, that some claim is false is itself a fallible belief, and both cases put pressure on closing rational belief under conjunction. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which feature of the paradoxes the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply by lecturing. If possible, have the activity surface whether the preface case feels different because the author wrote the claims, because each was independently checked, or because there's no guaranteed winner.
