I'm a retired neurophysiologist (visual cortex, mostly), and in retirement I've read my way into philosophy of mind. The entry point was a dumb-sounding question from my old work: when we ported a model of a cortical column from one simulator to another and it produced the same dynamics, was it the same model? That led me to a position I didn't expect to hold. The position: a faithful whole-brain emulation of me would be the same mind as me, but only in the case where it is, or could be, the endpoint of a gradual replacement of my neurons by functionally exact artificial units — not the case where you scan me, leave the original intact, and run off a copy. I think the gradual replacement argument does the real work: at no single step does anything like death or duplication occur, and the endpoint just is an emulation, so whatever matters about being me must have been preserved. The copy case still bothers me in a way I cant ground, so I keep it outside the claim, which may already be a problem.

What would count against this? I want the actual pressure points, not just the stock it's-a-copy-not-you intuition: which possible cases, conceptual results, or findings about what brains actually preserve would force me to reject or seriously revise the position. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which of them a defender of my view should concede as genuinely counting against it and which should be dismissed as begging the question, and be honest about whether I've hedged the view into unfalsifiability with all the "could be the endpoint of a replacement" talk — and if I have, whether that matters for a view of this kind. Because im more moved by the causal-dynamical story than by folk intuitions, also flag which of these I personally should find most persuasive.

Format: exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, no introduction or conclusion.
