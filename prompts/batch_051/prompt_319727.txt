I'm currently taking a course on causation and responsibility in the law, and we have been discussing the problem of causation by omission: for any given harm there seem to be indefinitely many absences or failures that preceded it, yet we single out only a few as causes. I have been using a principle I call the economy criterion to explain this selection. The criterion says that an agent's omission counts as a cause of an outcome just when the omitted act would have prevented the outcome and the act lay within the agent's normal-cost range, meaning that a typical person in that agent's position could have performed it without taking on burdens beyond those they were already reasonably expected to bear. So the gardener's failure to water the plant meets the criterion, while a neighbour's failure to water someone else's plant does not, because routinely caring for another person's plant is above normal cost. My stronger claim is that there is no further causal fact behind this selection: causal talk about omissions is just the application of a cost-and-normalcy threshold, which is why legal duty requirements and ordinary causal judgments so often coincide. I am unsure whether this is a genuine account of causation, a confused account of blame, or a restatement of something already worked out under another name, and I would like an honest assessment of where the criterion succeeds and where it breaks.
