I keep reading proposed solutions to the paradox of phenomenal judgment, and the disagreements usually come down to unstated standards for what an account of phenomenal judgment actually has to accomplish. Before I read any further, I want the full set of desiderata, on the working assumption that phenomenal properties — what it is like to feel pain, see red, or taste salt — are epiphenomenal: caused by physical states, but causing nothing physical, including beliefs, utterances, and other behavior. Adequacy here means sufficient for some version of epiphenomenalism to remain a coherent position about our own mental lives, not merely a verbal position.

Write the desiderata such an account must meet. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a genuine constraint rather than a prejudice of one side, and indicate whether the requirement is negotiable or non-negotiable. The list should at least cover the following.

First, the correlation: normal subjects judge that they are in pain roughly when they are in pain, and the judgment tracks the phenomenal state far more closely than brute coincidence would allow. The account must explain this covariation without letting the phenomenal state itself do any causal work in producing the judgment.

Second, the apparent first-person authority of these judgments: that they present themselves to us as immediate, non-inferential, and unusually resistant to ordinary error. The account must say how much of this appearance it can preserve and how much it must debunk, and it must not simply ignore the appearance.

Third, the self-stultification problem: if the existence of a phenomenal state never makes a causal difference to the belief that it exists, then the belief looks as though its truth is an accident, and the epiphenomenalist's own judgment that epiphenomenalism is true seems to undermine its claim to track the truth. The account must say what distinguishes a justification-apt judgment from an accidental verbal output, and how an epiphenomenal process could produce the former rather than merely the latter.

Fourth, the dissociations. The account must return the right verdicts, or explain why the cases are impossible, for at least the following: a subject who dispassionately reports 'it hurts, but it doesn't bother me'; a subject or perfect physical duplicate who issues fluent and accurate pain or visual reports while the corresponding phenomenal state is absent; and a blindsight-style subject who can perform above chance on visual tasks while sincerely reporting that she sees nothing. For each, say whether the phenomenal state, the judgment, and the report come apart, and what that teaches about their normal connection.

Fifth, the distinctions the account must not collapse: between the phenomenal state itself, the judgment that one is in it, and the outward report of it; and between the causal explanation of a phenomenal judgment and its epistemic standing, if it has any.

Then do two further things. First, flag any pair of these desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied on epiphenomenalist assumptions, since an unsatisfiable pair would itself be a result. Second, identify the single requirement that does the most to exclude the standard moves — brute correlation, a common-physical-cause story, or a deflationary account that denies introspective judgments ever had the authority they seem to have — and say why it excludes them. Keep the weight on the requirements and their motivating cases; score the standard moves only briefly.
