A friend and I just spent two hours going in circles about whether games can be art, and he left me with an argument I cant shake. Here's my reconstruction of it:

(1) If a game is a work of art, the thing that makes it art — the only thing games have over film and novels — is the agency it gives the player: the designed space of possible actions, and the fact that playing means choosing.
(2) When the player chooses, those choices determine which work gets realized. The playthrough wouldn't exist without them, so in picking among possibilities the player is doing part of what an artist does — co-authoring the piece.
(3) But that structure isn't unique to games. Any designed possibility space works the same way: a park path lets a visitor pick a route and get a different sequence of views; a tasting menu lets a diner choose the order in which things run into each other; a branched tax form lets a filer select their own route; a lobby lets you walk it thirteen different ways.
(4) So if agency is what makes games art, then menus, park paths, tax forms, and lobbies are all art in just the same way.
(5) That's absurd. A well-designed tax form is not a work of art — or if it is, 'art' is doing no work.
(6) So agency can't be the art-making feature of games. And since agency is the only thing games have over other media, games arent art.

I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than swallow (5). Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
