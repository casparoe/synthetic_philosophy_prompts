Consider the procreative-complementarity argument against same-sex marriage in something like its strongest form. The argument holds that marriage is not merely a legal arrangement or a commitment of affection but a comprehensive union of two persons, a union of their lives and also of their bodies. The distinctive bodily union, on this view, is coitus, understood not as a merely pleasurable act but as the one kind of act by which two human beings are jointly ordered toward reproduction as a single biological unit. Because only a male–female pair can perform that act, only such a pair can achieve the comprehensive union that marriage is; same-sex couples can share affection, a household, and child-rearing, but they cannot form this specific kind of union. Procreation enters the argument through the idea of bodily union, not through a claim that every marriage must actually produce children, since infertile couples can marry on this view.

Now suppose ectogenesis, the complete gestation of a human being outside any human body from gametes to term, becomes safe, routine, and widely available. In that world a child can be produced from two people's gametes without any sexual act between them, and reproduction no longer requires the act the argument identifies as the bodily union constitutive of marriage.

Does the procreative-complementarity argument survive this development, or does it lose the premise that was doing the work? Identify precisely which premise ectogenesis bears on, and then assess whether the argument can be repaired into something weaker but still significant, for example a claim about the intrinsic normative significance of the human body as it is, or about the distinctive good of begetting a child through a shared bodily act. Say what the repaired version would still show and what it would no longer show. I am interested in the fate of the argument, not in whether the technology is desirable.
