Consider Aristotle's argument that a complete explanation of a natural thing must mention four kinds of cause: the material out of which it comes to be, the form or essence that makes it the kind of thing it is, the source of change that brings it about, and the end or that for the sake of which it exists. Part of what makes the fourth cause, the final cause, seem indispensable to him is that natural processes display regular, end-directed order—teeth grow in a way suited to chewing, an acorn reliably develops into an oak—and this order is not easily explained by matter and antecedent motion alone. The appearance is one of nature acting toward ends.

A familiar modern development is Darwinian natural selection. It is often said that Darwin showed how end-directed biological order can arise from undirected variation plus selection, without any irreducible tendency toward a goal. On this picture, functions are explained historically: a trait exists because past instances of it had effects that promoted survival or reproduction, not because the organism or nature aims at those effects now. Some philosophers conclude that final causes have been eliminated; others conclude that they survive only in a deflated, selected-effect sense.

Does Aristotle's argument for the irreducibility of final causes survive this development? More specifically: which premise in the argument is actually put under pressure by natural selection, and does selection refute that premise or merely relocate the explanatory work? If the original argument fails, is there a repaired version that preserves something important about teleological explanation in biology, such as the claim that function attributions cannot be fully replaced by physical descriptions? If the original argument survives, what exactly is left of the final cause, and how does it differ from the kind of teleology Aristotle defended?
