I've been part of an interdisciplinary working group on generational analysis, and after I pushed back on a colleague's claims about "Gen Z voting behavior," she asked me to write down exactly what I think generations are. I drafted the following statement, and I'd like a consistency check before I send it around:

(1) Generational categories such as Boomers, Millennials, and Gen Z are not natural kinds; the cutoffs are conventional, and there is no deep psychological or social joint at the 1997/1998 boundary. (2) Still, I think they are real social kinds rather than mere labels: people are sorted into them from birth, institutions and marketers and policymakers use them, and—through the kind of feedback process sometimes called looping effects—being classified as a Millennial or as Gen Z can shape how people interpret and govern themselves. (3) Methodologically, I hold that any credible claim about a generational difference has to separate age effects, period effects, and cohort effects, and that in practice most available data underdetermine that decomposition. (4) So I regard most popular generational claims—that Gen Z is uniquely anxious, that Millennials are narcissistic, that Boomers are unusually selfish—as confounded or overblown. (5) But I am not an eliminativist: I think at least some cohort effects are real, for example in levels of institutional trust or in certain political attitudes, and that generational labels are often the best available shorthand for them. (6) At the same time, I lean toward the view that when an underlying mechanism is known—a particular economic or technological change, say—we should prefer an explanation in terms of that mechanism and treat the generational explanation as a placeholder, to be discarded once the real variable is in hand. (7) Finally, I think it is a category mistake to personify generations, as if "the Boomers" or "Gen Z" could do anything collectively; but I am comfortable saying that the members of a cohort faced a shared opportunity structure and that a cohort can be systematically advantaged by it.

Could you audit this set of commitments? I'd like to know which pairs are actually in tension and which only seem to be, with the genuine tensions ranked by severity; for each genuine tension, what the least costly revision would be and what it would cost me; and whether, taken as a whole, the package commits me to any position I have not explicitly stated.
