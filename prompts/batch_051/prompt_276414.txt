i'm in a philosophy of ai course and we're on interpretability, specifically the recursive idea: use a smarter model to explain a dumber one, and then an even smarter model to explain that explanation, hoping it keeps working as systems get superhuman. my labmate keeps deflating the whole problem with an analogy: a smarter model interpreting a dumber one is basically like an adult reconstructing what a toddler was trying to do — you don't need to be inside the toddler's head, you just need a better predictive model of its behavior, and adults are pretty good at that. that's supposed to license the conclusion that recursive interpretability bootstraps without any special breakthrough: as long as you always have an interpreter at least as skilled as the target, you can just keep going up the stack. i think the analogy might be doing all the work, but i want it audited properly. what corresponds to the adult, the toddler, the adult's theory of mind, and the interpretation itself? where does it break — for instance the adult and toddler share an evolutionary history, a common architecture, and a lifetime of practicing self-explanation, which two separate networks don't; and the adult's 'interpretation' is itself a story generated by another opaque system. does the optimistic conclusion survive once those disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? and is there a better analogy lurking somewhere, like translation between languages with no common ancestor, that gets the recursive case right?
