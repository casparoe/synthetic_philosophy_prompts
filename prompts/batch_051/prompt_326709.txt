As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I have been preparing a feature on the logic of "what if everyone did that?" and its use in arguments about small individual contributions to large collective harms. During my reporting I sat in on a discussion between two philosophers I have interviewed before, and I realised I could not referee it. They were considering a case drawn from a city under a voluntary water-conservation request during a severe drought: a resident continued to run his garden sprinkler each evening, maintaining that one household's extra use could not measurably affect the reservoir. I reproduce their exchange below as faithfully as I can from my notes.

(1) Leah: His reasoning fails the universalisation test. We should ask whether he could will his maxim as a law everyone could follow—something like "I may ignore a shared restraint whenever my own contribution is negligible." He could not: a world in which everyone acted on that maxim would be a world in which the restraint collapses, and the benefits he enjoys depend on most other people's not acting as he permits himself to act. That is the structure of free-riding, and the test exposes it.

(2) Tom: The test, as you are using it, is either empty or rigged. Its verdict depends entirely on how the act is described. Universalise "run this sprinkler on this garden for exactly these thirty minutes" and no harm follows; universalise "take more than one's share of a scarce resource" and the wrongness is built into the description. The test convicts only after we have already chosen an incriminating description. It supplies no independent criterion for choosing descriptions.

(3) Leah: The description is not arbitrary. The relevant maxim is the principle the agent is actually acting on—the one he would honestly avow to justify himself: "when others are restraining themselves during a shortage, I may ignore the request because my excess is negligible." That maxim fails as a universal law, since if everyone in his position adopted it the shared restraint would unravel. His unwillingness to prescribe his maxim to others in his position is enough to show he is making an exception for himself.

(4) Tom: That is a generalisation fallacy. From "if everyone did it, the outcome would be bad" it does not follow that his doing it is wrong. The harm appears only past a threshold, and his marginal contribution is, on your own description of the case, causally negligible—so you are convicting him of an outcome his act does not bring about. In any case, what is really doing the work in your argument is a fairness principle about shared practices, not universalisability as such, and fairness principles have conditions you have not shown are met.

(5) Leah: The threshold point is precisely why we must judge maxims rather than isolated acts. A pattern-level judgement is not a fallacy; it is the recognition that some wrongs exist only at the level of a pattern. His maxim asks others to bear the restraint while he takes the comfort, and nobody who understood that could prescribe it as a public rule.

(6) Tom: But the man might happily prescribe a different public rule—"each household may use a fixed modest amount for its garden during a shortage"—and might prefer that everyone were free to do the same. If so, the disagreement is not about whether he is inconsistent, but about which public rule ought to govern water use. That is a substantive question of harm, fairness, and coordination, and the universalisation test by itself will not settle it. It can catch someone who makes a private exception of himself; it cannot show that his preferred universal rule is the wrong one.

I came away unable to say, move by move, where each of them was on firm ground, and I am hoping for a careful adjudication.

You are a professional moral philosopher with expertise in Kant's universal law test, Hare's account of universalisable prescriptions, and the logic of generalisation arguments. Please think through the exchange carefully before reaching any verdict. It may help to begin by stating, in your own words, the principle each speaker is relying on at each numbered move, and to steelman the weakest point on each side. Then give a move-by-move adjudication: rate each of the six moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and say precisely which parts of a mixed move are sound and which are not. Conclude by saying who is more in the right overall, and identify the most important consideration that both of them are missing. Please write your answer in British English.
