The engineering lead on my team has begun invoking what he calls the ubuntu proverb "a person is a person through other persons" to argue that our company's performance-review system should stop rating individuals and instead evaluate only teams. His line is that a person becomes and acts as a person only through other people, so an individual rating measures something fictitious: it isolates a person from the very relationships that constitute them. I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I can't tell whether this is a substantive claim about what persons are or a vague proverb being stretched to cover a management decision.

I'd like you to disambiguate the slogan rather than pick one reading and run with it. Separate out the distinct precise theses "a person is a person through other persons" could be asserting: for example, theses about the causal shaping of skills and preferences, theses about the constitution of personal identity, theses about whether agency or responsibility can be located in an individual at all, and theses about who deserves credit or blame for what a person does. For each thesis, say whether it is a platitude that nearly everyone accepts, a substantive and defensible position, or something likely false, with a sentence or two justifying the verdict. Then identify which of these readings, if any, actually supports eliminating individual performance ratings, and say which transition from an uncontroversial reading to a much stronger conclusion someone like my lead is most likely making.
