I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about how communities decide which of their inherited rules are universal and which are local. The vocabulary I'm most at home in is the one from the generalization and replication debates in the behavioral sciences: internal and external validity, samples and populations, WEIRD-sample problems, boundary conditions, heterogeneous treatment effects, operationalization, and the gap between a principle and a context-bound finding. My working hunch is that an old theological question is really just a generalization problem in disguise, and I want to know whether that hunch holds up or misses something.

The question is the one many interpreters of the New Testament frame as the difference between cultural and transcultural commands. The position, roughly, is this: some commands in Scripture bind believers everywhere (transcultural), while others bind only within the cultural setting in which they were given (cultural); a competent interpreter can tell which is which by asking whether a command is grounded in creation or "nature" rather than in local convention, and whether the text presents it as required across all the churches. The passage I want the question to turn on is from Paul's First Letter to the Corinthians, 11:13–16 (King James Version):

"13 Judge in yourselves: is it comely that a woman pray unto God uncovered?
14 Doth not even nature itself teach you, that, if a man have long hair, it is a shame unto him?
15 But if a woman have long hair, it is a glory to her: for her hair is given her for a covering.
16 But if any man seem to be contentious, we have no such custom, neither the churches of God."

Please restate that cultural/transcultural distinction, as a systematic position in biblical interpretation, in the science-and-ideas vocabulary I described, as faithfully as you can. Then give me the residue report: which parts translate cleanly into the language of samples, populations, and external validity; which parts translate only with distortion; and which parts have no counterpart in that vocabulary at all. In particular, tell me whether "doth not even nature itself teach you" is best read as a claim of unrestricted generalization, whether "we have no such custom" is best read as a boundary condition, and whether the cultural/transcultural distinction is doing real interpretive work or is mostly a post hoc sorting rule. The residue is what I care most about for my piece: where the translation fails, is the failure a defect in my vocabulary, or a sign that the distinction is less principled than its defenders claim?

As you answer, keep separate what is settled background and what is genuinely contested among people who work on this material.
