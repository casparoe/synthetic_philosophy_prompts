Here's an argument my housemate laid out over dinner the other night, and it's been nagging at me since. He's an economist and has the philosophy of science all figured out, apparently, and his conclusion is so strong that I keep thinking I must be misreading one of the steps. It goes like this:

(1) A striking number of the really famous discoveries were made more than once, independently, at almost the same time: Newton and Leibniz on calculus; Darwin and Wallace on natural selection; Bell and Gray on the telephone; Priestley and Scheele on oxygen, with Lavoisier right behind them.
(2) When a discovery is a multiple like that, the state of the field had made it ripe — the tools, data, and pressures were all in place, so if the actual discoverer had never existed, somebody else would have found the thing within a few years anyway.
(3) So for each of these discoveries, the individual discoverer's personal contribution was not necessary for the discovery's happening — the discovery, considered as a type of event, was coming either way.
(4) Scientific credit — priority, eponyms, prizes, the whole honor system — is deserved only for a necessary contribution: for being what made the difference between the discovery happening and not happening.
(5) Therefore Newton, Darwin, Bell, Priestley, and the rest don't deserve the credit we give them, and the entire system of naming laws and species after people and awarding prizes to whoever got there first is systematically confused, not just occasionally unfair.
(6) But that is absurd. There plainly is an important difference between Darwin and the hundreds of naturalists who also noticed variation in finches and did not formulate natural selection, and a system that refused to mark that difference would be worse, not better.

I'd like to keep (1) and (2) — the historical pattern seems real to me — and I'd also like to keep (6).
