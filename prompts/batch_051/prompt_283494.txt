So I keep having this argument with my brother about whether the little stuff people do is even wrong when their one choice cant change the big total. Like he says a single burger doesn't raise the number of cows killed, so it's fine, and he also skips voting for the same reason. But then he'll get really upset about someone littering, which also doesn't add up to much. I think there are like four or five different positions hiding in our friend group and nobody can tell them apart because they're all using the same-ish arguments. I run a little climate/animal/food discussion group for my housing co-op and I want to put together a sorting quiz for them: ten concrete situations, each with two or three options, that sorts people by their answers rather than by asking them which side they think they're on.

Here's what I need. Don't ask abstract stuff like "do you believe your vote matters." Give them cases and let the answers sort them. The quiz should sort people into at least these four rough positions (and tell me if a fifth falls out): (a) tiny expected differences are still real differences and they add up, so the small choice is wrong; (b) if your choice can't actually be the one that tips anything, it's fine—only real differences count; (c) what matters is whether you're joining or propping up a practice that's collectively awful, even if your own contribution tips nothing; (d) the point is what the choice says about you and the habits it builds, not the exact causal bookkeeping. Build the quiz from a couple of underlying distinctions instead of just these four labels, and if some combination of those distinctions is empty, say which combo that is and whether it's empty because it's incoherent or just because almost nobody lands there.

Constraints: ten questions total, each with two or three options. Include at least one case from each of meat, climate, and voting, and at least one "last straw" case where a threshold is nearly met (like 19 people have already signed up for something that needs 20, or the butcher orders another batch once 10 more people order tonight). Also include at least one case where the thing was already bought or done by someone else, so the person has to say whether benefiting from it or going along with it is different from causing it. The audience is my co-op, so no jargon in the questions themselves. The scoring key is just as important as the questions: show how patterns of answers map onto the positions, and make sure positions that are supposed to be seperate actually come apart on at least one question.
