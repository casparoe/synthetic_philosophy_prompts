I am writing a novel set in a university that has replaced part of its research evaluation with a device I have called the Silt Index. The rule is this: every article, monograph, or edition published by a department earns that department one silt point for each full year in which no one cites it, self-citations included; the first citation freezes the counter for that item permanently. Each year the university converts each department's silt points into a supplementary budget, which may be spent only on new research whose application form states that the work has no foreseeable use; silt-funded work is exempt from normal productivity review for a decade, and the exemptions are public while the spending details are not. Treat this as a mechanism rather than as a position about what scholarship is for. Walk me through what it would actually do: what it pushes department chairs, individual researchers, journal editors, and doctoral students to publish, cite, and avoid citing; where it gets gamed first; what kind of work rises to the top of the supplement after ten or twenty years; and whether the worst failure modes could be fixed by changing the rules rather than abandoning the index.
