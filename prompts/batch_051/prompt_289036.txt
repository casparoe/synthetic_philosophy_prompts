I spent my working life in cell biology—senescence and telomeres, of all things—and have only recently started reading philosophy. I've been going through the life-extension debate, and the term that keeps snagging me is "deathism." As I understand it, the charge is that much of the high-minded talk about mortality giving life meaning, urgency, or shape is really rationalization: people make peace with an unavoidable deadline by deciding it's secretly a feature, when a life of open-ended health could just as easily make its own meaning. That's a live question for me. But then I reread Tuck Everlasting, which I hadn't looked at since it was assigned to me as a kid, and it struck me that the novel is basically a sustained brief for the deathist view. The Tucks accidentally become ageless after drinking from a spring, and the father, Angus Tuck, gives Winnie a speech about how a proper life is a turning wheel and immortality is being a rock stuck outside the stream, watching everything move on without you. Winnie ends up choosing a mortal life, and the epilogue all but tells us that was right. The elegiac tone does a lot of the arguing.

What I'd like is a philosopher's reading of the novel as a work of deathism. Does it actually argue the position—premises, distinctions, something that could be pressed—or does it mostly stage the position and let the sadness carry the conclusion? Be specific about where the wheel-and-the-rock imagery does genuine philosophical work and where it is just pathos. And I want it to cut both ways: is there something in the book that a defender of life extension ought to grant, perhaps about narrative shape or the difference between a life's ending and a life's having a point? I'd rather have an honest reading then a takedown. Start with a two- or three-sentence bottom line, assume I've just reread the novel (no plot summary), and keep it to 850 words at most.
