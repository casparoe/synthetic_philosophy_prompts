Consider two pieces of bookkeeping that look, at first, like they belong to different centuries.

In the physics of information, the Szilard engine converts one bit — knowing which half of a partitioned box contains a single molecule — into at most kT ln 2 of work. Landauer's principle then says that erasing that one bit from the device's memory, to complete the cycle, must dissipate at least the same quantity of heat. This is the bookkeeping that exorcises Maxwell's demon: the demon can lower the gas's entropy only because it acquires a record, and the account closes exactly because the record has to be paid for when it is erased. The moral usually drawn is that information has to be counted as part of the physical world, and that the bit's value in the thermodynamic account comes entirely from what the bit lets the engine do.

In decision theory there is a structurally similar piece of bookkeeping. The expected value of a signal is defined as the gain in expected utility from making a decision after observing the signal rather than before; it is never negative, because one can always ignore the signal. And Blackwell's theorem states that a more informative signal yields weakly higher expected utility for every possible decision maker. Here, too, a bit of information is worth something only through the decisions it improves — utility is the currency, and the information is assigned no value in itself.

I am tempted to read these as two instances of a single idea: a bit is a fuel whose value is always instrumental, convertible into work in one framework and into expected utility in the other, and its "never worthless" status comes from the fact that it can always simply not be used. Under this reading, the familiar conviction that truth is worth seeking for its own sake — even when it changes no decision and produces no downstream benefit — would be a free lunch, a value without any conversion into the currencies the frameworks recognize.

Is this parallel deep, or does it dissolve once one notices that both frameworks define value instrumentally by construction, so neither can even state the question of intrinsic value? What would have to be true on both sides for a result to transfer — for example, for the Landauer bound to carry over into a substantive constraint on epistemic norms, such as a price that must be paid somewhere for holding true beliefs that never guide action? And does the parallel make a prediction that fails — for instance, that a person who pursues truth without regard to its use should look irrational from inside both frameworks, when in fact that person may be operating under a different sense of value that the analogy treats as free only because it has chosen to ignore it?
