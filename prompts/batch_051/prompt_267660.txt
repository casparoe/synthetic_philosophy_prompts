Take the controversy from the late 1970s over the planned neo-Nazi march through Skokie, Illinois, and analyze it through Mill's harm principle as laid out in On Liberty. The factual outline is familiar enough: an avowedly Nazi organization announced a demonstration in a village with a large population of Holocaust survivors, the village sought to prevent it, and the litigation ended with the courts declining to let those prohibitions stand. I know the First Amendment side of this episode. What I want is the philosophical mapping beneath it, with the village's proposed ban treated as the coercive act in need of justification. What, precisely, would the harm to Skokie's residents have to amount to for Mill's principle to license that coercion? Does the principle, as Mill framed it, actually apply to expression of this kind, or was it aimed primarily at coercive interference with conduct, while Mill's defense of free speech rests on a separate set of arguments about fallibility, the partiality of received truths, and the way uncontested belief hardens into dead dogma?

Then give an honest account of where the mapping strains. Say whether the foreseeable distress of Holocaust survivors confronted with the symbols of their persecutors counts as harm in Mill's sense, or whether the framework is forced to reclassify that distress as mere offense and therefore as one of the costs of liberty, and what it would mean for the principle if it had to absorb an injury that severe on that side of the line. Consider, too, whether the epistemic arguments that Mill actually gives for open discussion do any work in a case like this, where the marchers contribute no premises or arguments, only a display of allegiance. Finally, run the case in the other direction: does Skokie show the harm principle doing genuine independent work in free speech disputes, or does it reveal that the principle mostly announces a conclusion that has to be reached on other grounds first?
