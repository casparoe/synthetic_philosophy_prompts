I keep running into a weird thing in discussions about language, and I'm wondering whether there's a name for it. People treat the alphabet as if it's a neutral record of how a language sounds, but the alphabet seems to quietly decide in advance what counts as "one sound." English speakers will tell you English has five vowels, because there are five vowel letters, even though the vowel noises in "cat," "father," "about," "call," and "cake" are at least five different things, and that's before you leave the letter A. Or people insist that the "t" in "top" and the "t" in "stop" are "the same sound," just because both are spelled with a t, even though one comes with a little puff of air and the other doesn't — and in some languages that puff difference would force a different letter. I've noticed it in arguments about how many tones a language has, too, where people count tones by the accent marks a missionary or linguist happend to write down. It's as if the writing system is doing theory of language under the hood, and everyone then treats its categories as facts about speech.

So my question: is there an established concept for this specific thing — not just "learning to read changes how you hear spoken words," and not just "non-specialists have folk theories about language," but the way a particular kind of writing system (an alphabet) builds a discrete, invariant model of speech into how a language gets described? If there is a term, I'd want to know exactly how it differs from the broader idea that writing influences how people think about language, and how to tell an instance of this from a linguist deliberately using simplified notation as a practical shorthand. If there isn't one, can you name and define it so that someone could tell where it stops — does a syllabary do the same thing at a different level, or is this something specific to alphabets?
