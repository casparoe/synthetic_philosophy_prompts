I teach moral and political philosophy, but I have been asked to act as a respondent in a colleague's graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and in preparation I have been working through van Fraassen's Judy Benjamin example. As I understand the setup, Judy has been dropped at night into a territory divided into four cells—Red First Company, Red Second Company, Blue First Company, and Blue Second Company—and she initially regards all four as equally likely. A radio message then tells her only that, conditional on her being somewhere in Red territory, the probability that she is in the Red Second Company is three in four.

What puzzles me is why this case is treated as a serious problem for Bayesian updating at all. The message constrains a conditional probability. It tells Judy how to distribute probability between the two Red cells if she is in Red; it says nothing, as far as I can tell, about whether she is in Red rather than Blue territory. I would therefore have thought that P(Red) and P(Blue) should remain exactly one half, and that the update should proceed entirely within the Red cells—raising the probability of the Red Second Company until the conditional on Red reaches three in four. On that reading, the example is a small arithmetic exercise rather than a test of updating. I have, however, seen it claimed that the standard treatment requires Judy to become more confident that she is in Blue territory than she was before, which seems to me so counterintuitive that I suspect I have misunderstood either the setup or the rule that Bayesianism is supposed to supply here. Could you help me locate the mistake in my reasoning?
