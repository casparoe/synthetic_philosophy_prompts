I have been reading about anthropic reasoning after stumbling onto the doomsday argument while working on a school paper, and my own reactions to the smaller puzzles behind it do not seem to line up. I have seen the terms self-sampling assumption and self-indication assumption, but I cannot tell which of my reactions belongs to which, or whether either one is really what I believe. I would like to lay out my intuitive verdicts on a few cases and have someone work out whether a single principle captures them, and if not, which of my verdicts have to go.

Case 1. A fair coin is flipped. If it lands heads, exactly one person is created in a windowless room. If it lands tails, a thousand people are created in a thousand windowless rooms. You wake up in one of the rooms knowing all of this, with no way to tell which side landed. My gut says the coin almost certainly landed tails, because there are a thousand times as many people in the tails world, and therefore a thousand times as many chances for me to be one of them.

Case 2. Two cosmological theories fit all our current data equally well. Theory A says the universe is small in the relevant sense and contains only a few thousand intelligent observers in total. Theory B says the universe contains trillions upon trillions of intelligent observers, though we cannot currently observe the difference. Here my reaction runs the other way: it would be absurd to believe Theory B overwhelmingly simply because it says there are more minds. Whether a theory is true should not become vastly more probable merely because it entails a larger population. I believe this is the scenario I have seen called the presumptuous philosopher, though I may have gotten the details slightly wrong. But I worry it is the same move I made in Case 1.

Case 3. Imagine a person who knows two things. First, they are roughly the one hundred billionth human being ever born. Second, the final total number of humans who will ever exist is either around two hundred billion or around two hundred trillion, with no other differences between those two possibilities. My instinct is that this strongly favors the two hundred billion total, because under that hypothesis the person is about halfway through the species, whereas under the two hundred trillion hypothesis they would sit at the very beginning of the whole species. Yet in Case 1 the hypothesis with the larger population felt more likely, and here it feels less likely. I cannot tell whether I am being inconsistent.

Case 4. A fair coin is flipped. If heads, one person is created in Room 1. If tails, ten people are created in Rooms 1 through 10. You wake up and see that your door is labeled Room 1. My first thought is that heads is now much more likely, because if it had landed tails, I would probably have been in some other room. But then I remember that, if it landed tails, someone was always going to wake up in Room 1, and that person would have exactly the same experience I am having. I end up uncertain whether I am the guaranteed Room 1 person under heads or the one Room 1 person under tails. My gut still wants to raise the probability of heads, but I cannot state the rule that licenses it.

Is there one coherent principle about how to update on facts about how many people exist, where one ranks among them, and which specific position one observes, that delivers these verdicts? If there is, state it precisely and tell me what it predicts about some further case I have not listed. If there is not, say which of my verdicts your preferred principle overturns, and explain why a simpler principle that revises some of my intuitions is better than a patchwork that saves all of them. Please answer in American English.
