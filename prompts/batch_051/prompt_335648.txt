Secular mindfulness training, as it appears in clinical stress-reduction courses, workplace wellness programs, and meditation apps, standardly instructs people to observe their thoughts, emotions, and bodily sensations come and go without getting caught up in them, and to notice when attention has wandered and gently return it to the breath. The practice is almost always presented as a religiously neutral technique adapted from Buddhist insight meditation. Yet its core instruction seems to presuppose an inner observer that stands apart from the mental states it watches, chooses not to identify with them, and persists as the same observer across the session. That looks like a substantive picture of a self. What philosophical commitments about the self and personal identity does this practice actually presuppose, and which rival views does it quietly rule out? In particular, does it commit its users to exactly the sort of permanent, independent self that the Buddhist doctrine of anatta denies, or can the observer role be read as fully compatible with no-self? And does the practice genuinely depend on that presupposition, or could the same technique function without it? I want a committed answer: take a definite position on what the deepest presupposition is and on whether it is in tension with anatta, and argue for that position, rather than surveying possible interpretations and leaving me to choose.
