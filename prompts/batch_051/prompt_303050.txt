You are an epistemologist who works on self-knowledge, higher-order knowledge, and the debate over whether any mental or epistemic states are "luminous," where a state is luminous just in case, whenever someone is in it, they are in a position to know that they are in it. Please think carefully, lay out any candidate principles explicitly before committing, and make your final recommendation clear and well-motivated.

For years I have kept notes on the case verdicts of a colleague, Professor M, in a graduate seminar on knowledge and self-knowledge. The cases all ask whether a subject knows some proposition, and usually also whether the subject is in a position to know that they know it. Here are three representative entries.

1. The botanist. Ava, an experienced field botanist, sees a plant and correctly judges that it is poison ivy. She can point to the leaf shape and the clusters of three leaflets and explain how those features identify poison ivy. Professor M's verdict: Ava knows the plant is poison ivy, and she is in a position to know that she knows it.

2. The lucky hunch. Ben, who knows nothing about plants, takes a wild guess that a plant is poison ivy and happens to be right. He reports no visible feature, memory, or other ground for his answer. Professor M's verdict: Ben does not know.

3. The inarticulate birder. Cora, an experienced birder, identifies a bird from a brief glimpse as a Blackburnian warbler. She has a clear visual impression of the bird's orange throat and facial pattern, but cannot say in words exactly which features drove her identification. Professor M's verdict: Cora knows it is a Blackburnian warbler and is in a position to know that she knows it, because she can attend to the conscious visual impression she had even though she cannot verbalize the field marks.

M's larger record matches the pattern of these three: M has ascribed knowledge whenever the subject had a reliable and available basis, and denied it whenever the subject lacked any available basis and merely got it right by luck. No case in the record combines high objective reliability with a total absence of any conscious or reportable basis.

Now I have such a case.

4. The blindsight-like guesser. Noah has a condition of the kind discussed under the heading of blindsight: in part of his visual field he reports no visual experience at all, and when he answers questions about targets presented there, he says he is only guessing. In controlled trials, with no feedback, he identifies the orientation of a line as horizontal or vertical correctly in 19 out of 20 cases, far above chance. Noah has never been told his accuracy and has no idea that his answers are better than chance; from his own point of view, each answer feels exactly like a blind guess. On a new trial, he says the line is vertical, and it is. Does Noah know that the line is vertical?

I can see at least two principles that each fit every one of M's recorded verdicts and that pull apart on Noah. One is a KK-style or luminosity-style principle: knowledge that p requires being in a position to know that one knows that p. The other is a safety or reliability principle: a true belief counts as knowledge when it is formed in a way that could not easily have gone wrong, even if the subject has no access to the basis or to its reliability. There may be further candidates worth identifying.

Please do four things. First, identify the candidate principles that would each generate M's record, and show exactly how each one handles Ava, Ben, Cora, and Noah. Second, say which of the three earlier cases is the closest analog to Noah's and why. Third, give your best judgment about what Professor M should say if she wants to stay consistent with her own record, and say what principle she would be adopting with each possible verdict. Finally, name one further hypothetical case, different from Noah's, that would do the most to reveal which principle actually governs her record, and state what each candidate principle predicts for it.
