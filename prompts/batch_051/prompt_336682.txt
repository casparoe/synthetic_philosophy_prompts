I lean toward the view that the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution is the best available normative default for two-player cooperative bargaining: the fair division is the maximal feasible point lying on the segment from the disagreement point to the point that lists each player's individually maximum feasible payoff. I prefer it to the Nash bargaining solution, which maximizes the product of the parties' gains over the disagreement point. I find the monotonicity rationale compelling: if the feasible set expands in a way that raises one party's attainable ceiling while leaving the other's ceiling and the disagreement point unchanged, that party should not end up with less. And I am suspicious of Nash's independence of irrelevant alternatives: I think it is less a consistency condition than a license to ignore features of the feasible set that are morally relevant to what each party could have hoped for. I want to know what would count against the Kalai–Smorodinsky view. I want the argument itself, not citations, so work from formal properties, axioms, and cases.

Please sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and distinguish what critics would say from what a committed Kalai–Smorodinsky advocate would themselves concede. In particular:

1. Which assumptions or axioms in the Kalai–Smorodinsky characterization could fail, or turn out to be less compelling than they first appear, and what would a formal example or piece of evidence have to show for each to count as a genuine problem?

2. Are there bargaining situations where the solution gives a result its own defenders should find hard to accept — for example cases involving irrelevant alternatives, non-convex or unbounded feasible sets, shifts in the disagreement point, or parties strategically reporting what is feasible?

3. Is there a risk that the position collapses into the Nash solution, or into some other solution, once the axioms are pressed? Or does the monotonicity condition carry enough independent weight to keep the two apart? If it does, is that weight substantive fairness rather than plain rationality, and does that matter?

4. How much does the case depend on two players and a compact convex feasible set? What happens under n-player bargaining, uncertainty about the feasible set, or incomplete knowledge of each party's utility scale?

5. What would a committed advocate of the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution concede counts against it, and what would they dismiss as misdirected? Finally, is the view in danger of having been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that a problem for a normative claim of this kind?
