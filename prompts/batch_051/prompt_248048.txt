I have been trying to make sense of how professional history enforces the boundary between legitimate revisionism and pseudohistory, and the more I look at the practice, the less I can state the rule it appears to follow. On one side, the discipline funds and publishes extraordinarily fine-grained work on ordinary topics — a dissertation on tax collection in some minor medieval principality is unremarkable — while a paper arguing that the standard timeline of antiquity is off by a thousand years, or that ancient aliens built the pyramids, tends not to be refuted so much as not engaged: it is desk-rejected by journals, kept off conference programs, and treated as a symptom rather than a thesis. On the other side, the same discipline insists that claims be assessed by their evidence and methods rather than by who makes them or how strange the conclusion is, and it celebrates a handful of cases in which outsiders overturned a consensus. I am not defending Atlantis or Fomenko's new chronology; I am trying to reconstruct the principle that would make the discipline's actual behavior come out consistent, and I cannot. The question keeps presenting itself to me in the terms I know from reading about epistemic learned helplessness: when is refusing to engage a research program a noise filter, and when is it motivated stopping?

What theory of historical knowledge or evidence does this pattern of practice presuppose? Is the implicit view something like a Popperian criterion that rules out unfalsifiable research programs, a Bayesian prior that lost civilizations and thousand-year fabrications are vanishingly improbable, a methodological naturalism that excludes non-human or conspiratorial agency before looking at the data, or a Kuhnian point that a functioning discipline must ignore certain challenges in order to do normal science? Or is the practice philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories of demarcation would license exactly the same gatekeeping? I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory, carefully separating what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit well with, and to say where the practice is internally inconsistent — for example, where it treats evidence as sovereign and where it treats the presenter's credentials or the outlandishness of the conclusion as a legitimate reason to stop looking. Finally, tell me what would have to change in the discipline if the presupposed theory were replaced by a strong symmetry principle according to which every research program, however antecedently strange, is entitled to full engagement on the evidence.
