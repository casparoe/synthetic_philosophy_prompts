Before I lecture on it, I want to run an in-class game whose rules and incentives make students' own choices exhibit the Ellsberg pattern: they should find themselves preferring bets with known probabilities over bets with the same possible payoffs but unknown probabilities, and they should notice that pattern themselves, before I name it, as something worth explaining. Design the activity end to end: the rules, materials, a non-monetary incentive scheme (for example, points toward participation or a small quiz bonus), the ordering and framing of the choices within a timeline for the 75 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments of the game onto the formal distinction between risk and ambiguity, the sure-thing principle, and the reason a consistent preference for known-probability bets should puzzle an expected-utility maximizer. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run the activity and how to handle it, and identify which part of the ambiguity-aversion phenomenon the game cannot by itself make participants feel and will have to be supplied by the lecture.
