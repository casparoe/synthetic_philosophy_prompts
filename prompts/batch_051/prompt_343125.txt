Here's one I'd like to see a model actually think through rather than coast on. Al-Ghazali's occasionalism says the world doesn't run on created things having powers that make other things happen. He'd say that when fire touches cotton and the cotton burns, the fire isn't doing anything; God is creating the burning at that moment. The fire-then-burning sequence is regular only because God has a settled habit of creating it that way, and the habit is reliable for practical life but its never a necessity in itself. God could stop doing it, and the breaking of the habit is exactly what a miracle is. So he's not a skeptic about regularities — he's a skeptic about the idea that the regularities are produced by the natures of the things themselves.

Now drop him in a modern hospital journal club. The residents are walking through a big randomized controlled trail for a sepsis drug: random assignment to drug or placebo, blinding, the works. The trial comes back positive, and everyone reaches for the same conclusion — that randomization knocked out the confounders, so the study has identified that the drug causes the improvement, not just that the two happen together.

What I want you to do is predict, carefully, what al-Ghazali would say about that conclusion and about the method that produced it. Would he accept the inference from the trial to a causal claim, or would he redescribe the whole thing as God's custom treating the treated group differently? What would he make of randomization itself — does it strike him as a useful way to read God's habit, or as a weird rite? And if a follow-up trial comes up null, would he treat the null as a discovery about how the world works, or as a discovery about what God happened to create in that sample? Run his actual commitments — reliable custom, no created powers, miracles as broken habit — forward into this setting, and make sure the prediction isn't the same one you'd give for a generic theist who thinks God is in charge of everything.
