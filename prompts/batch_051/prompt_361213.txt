Suppose that, starting on the first day of next year, every adult speaker of a natural language acquires a complete, effortless, and infallible knowledge of the etymological history of every word they use or hear: its earliest reconstructable root, its attested sense changes with rough dates, its cognates, and the meanings it carried at each documented stage. They acquire no semantic rules along with this; they are not told which historical senses remain live in present usage, nor whether any earlier sense has authority over current use. Predict, over the following thirty years, what happens to (a) statutory and constitutional interpretation, (b) political and commercial rhetoric, (c) ordinary disputes about what a word really means, and (d) the rate and direction of semantic change. In particular, argue whether the original-meaning fallacy—treating the oldest or etymological sense as the correct or most revealing sense—becomes weaker or stronger, and in which settings. Make the causal chain explicit: distinguish the effects that follow robustly from the change itself from the effects that depend on further assumptions about how quickly speakers learn to separate a word's history from its present function, and state what observations would most undermine your predictions.
