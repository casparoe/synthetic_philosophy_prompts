I'm one of the elders at a nondenominational church of about two hundred people, and every few weeks someone brings us the kind of claim we don't have a consistent way to handle. A woman says she has a prophetic word for the congregation; a man says his tumor shrank and he wants it announced as a healing; someone speaks in tongues in the service and another member offers an interpretation. Our statement of faith is deliberately silent on the cessationist/continuationist question — that is, on whether the sign gifts stopped with the apostolic age or continue today — so the elder board just ends up trading proof texts and going with whoever argued loudest that night. Last fall we nearly split the church over one of these. I want a reusable procedure we can run in a two-hour elders meeting, with the training we actually have (two of us have some seminary, the rest are sharp laypeople). It should keep what the theological debate actually turns on — the difference between a gift that builds up the gathered body and a claim to apostolic sign authority, between a private encouragement and a public directive, between testing a prophecy by whether it lines up with Scripture and by whether it comes true — and it should tell us when we should receive a report publically, when we should quietly pass it over, and when we have to say no to someone face to face.

Don't just assume one side of the debate is right. I need a checklist that can be run whether the elders lean cessationist or continuationist: it should flag the steps where the two positions genuinely part ways and an elder has to make a theological call, rather than pretending those steps are neutral. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a trusted, prayerful woman brings a word for the congregation that is mostly encouragement but includes a specific prediction that a named couple who have struggled with infertility will conceive within a year; (2) a well-liked man reports that his oncologist says a tumor has shrunk unexpectedly and he wants us to present it in the Sunday service as a miracle; (3) a newer attender interrupts a service speaking in tongues, and the interpretation someone else offers is generic enough to apply to anyone. Make sure at least one case gives an answer the elders won't like, and be explicit about what the procedure cannot decide — for instance, whether God actually acted, as opposed to whether we are warranted in treating a report as credible.
