So I got into it last night with a buddy who did policy debate in high school — the kind where people talk at four hundred words a minute and keep a running shorthand of every argument on a legal pad — about wether argumentation could ever be a real sport. He kept insisting it already is one, and that at least debate has clear winners, unlike philosophy. I was pushing back that the way policy debate picks its winners quietly assumes a pretty specific and maybe weird theory of what makes an argument good, but when he asked me what exactly the system presupposes, I couldnt answer cleanly.

So take the actual practice. Two-person teams get a resolution, one side affirms and the other negates. Debaters read evidence cards at speed, everyone "flows" the round by writing down each argument as a short tag, and the crucial rule is that if you fail to respond to an argument in your next speech it's "dropped" and the judge treats it as conceded — even if the original claim was false or kind of deranged, you lose the point unless you can show somewhere that you answered it. Judges decide rounds by comparing what was left standing on each side rather than by weighing which side is actually right, and a team can win by spreading: reading more arguments than the other side can physically cover.

What theory of argument quality does that system commit you to? I want to know whether it treats an argument as good-by-being-unanswered rather than good-by-being-sound, whether it quietly rules out the idea that arguing is supposed to be truth-tracking rather than zero-sum, and whether the dropped-argument rule is defensible as a claim about conversational commitment — that in a dispute silence really is consent — or whether it's just a scoring artifact with nothing to do with reasoning. And I'd like the part where the practice is underdetermined spelled out too: where could both a truth-wins view and a coverage-wins view of argument license exactly the same judging behavior? Because I want to go back to my friend with something sharper than my vague "that's not real argumentation," and I'm also trying to figure out whether the sport model actually makes people better at reasoning or just better at spreading.
