I tutor middle-schoolers in math and science, mostly one-on-one sessions, and the thing I keep tripping over is what to do with wrong answers that doesn't feel random. Last month a kid told me that one third is bigger than one half because 3 is bigger than 2, and when I drew her two pizzas she said "but the rule works for whole numbers." She wasn't confused about what I asked; she was running a different rule, and it mostly works. Another kid in my science group kept saying plants get their food from the soil, and no matter how I explained photosynthesis he came back to roots eating dirt, because plants clearly pull stuff out of the ground. I've read that a lot of student errors are like this: not gaps or slips but stable little theories — internally consistent, reusable, predictive — and that some of them are even resources, the same reasoning that works somewhere else but over-applied. If that's right, you shouldn't always respond by correcting the error; sometimes you have to work through the theory. But my sessions are 45 minutes, I see four or five kids a day, and parents want to see errors corrected, not "explored." If I misdiagnose, I either burn twenty minutes interrogating a slip, or I 'fix' an idea that quietly comes back next week.

So: give me a step-by-step decision procedure I can actually run in the moment, with no cognitive-science background, for deciding whether a wrong answer should be treated as a theory to be worked through or as a mistake to correct and move past. For each step, say what I'm looking for and what to do when I can't tell. The procedure should be per student, not per answer — the same wrong answer might be a slip for one kid and a theory for another. Then apply it to three cases: the one-third-bigger-than-one-half kid, the plants-eat-soil kid, and a kid who says a rolling ball needs a steady push to keep moving at a constant speed. Include at least one case where your procedure tells me to do something I'd rather not do, and be straight with me about which steps are just my own judgment wearing a flowchart, and where following the procedure would lead me wrong.
