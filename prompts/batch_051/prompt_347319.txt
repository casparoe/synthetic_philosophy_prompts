alright, here's the package of views I've ended up with on self-binding and constitutions, and I'd like an audit of whether it hangs together. (1) Ulysses contracts are rational and basically unproblematic: if I know tomorrow-me is going to want a cigarette, I'm within my rights to make it impossible for tomorrow-me to have one, even when tomorrow-me will sincerely beg. (2) A legislature can entrench its own rules of order — say, a two-thirds vote to change them — and that isn't anti-democratic; it's part of what makes it a legislature at all. (3) Substantive constitutional entrenchment is much more suspect: a bill of rights enforced by courts against today's majority is just the dead hand of the past, since the voters being bound never consented. (4) But I also think there's no principled line between individual precommitment and collective precommitment: the fact that one is a single person over time and the other is a people over generations doesn't do any real work. (5) Involuntarily committing someone else is illegitimate paternalism — the state shouldn't put a gambler into a self-exclusion scheme they never asked for. (6) An advance directive should be honored even over the patient's later, heartfelt plea: if they refused the ventilator in advance, the earlier refusal is the consent that counts, and you keep them off. So: which of these are actually pulling against each other, which only look like they are, and what would the cheapest repair cost?
