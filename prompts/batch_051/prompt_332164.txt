In my introduction to political philosophy class, the assigned textbook summarizes Hobbes's argument roughly like this. In the state of nature, everyone is governed entirely by self-interest, and because there is no common power to enforce agreements, each person rationally attacks others or strikes preemptively. The result is a universal war in which life is insecure, miserable, and brief. To escape this condition, people promise to obey a common sovereign. Once that promise is made, the sovereign can use coercion to force everyone to keep their word, and this threat of punishment is what makes the social contract binding. The textbook concludes that Hobbes's central insight is to show how selfish individuals, recognizing that unrestrained selfishness defeats itself, would rationally agree among themselves to create a state, with the sovereign as one of the contracting parties.

Something about this summary strikes me as too tidy, but I cannot tell how much of my unease is justified. I think I remember that Hobbes insists the sovereign is not a party to the social contract, which would make the last clause wrong, but I do not know whether the rest is accurate or whether that misreading matters for the overall argument. Could you go through the summary and separate what a careful reader of Hobbes would accept, what is an overstatement or common simplification, and what Hobbes explicitly denies? In particular, does the textbook's claim that the binding force of the contract comes from the sovereign's threat of punishment survive once the accurate and inaccurate parts are distinguished?
