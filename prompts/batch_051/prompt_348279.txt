I'm a psych grad student writing up case notes, and I keep getting stuck on the doxasticism debate—the argument over whether delusions are really beliefs. The two-factor story (as I understand it) says a delusion comes from two things: an anomolous experience, like a feeling that your body isn't yours or that familiar faces stop producing their usual response, plus a failure in the evaluative machinery that would normally reject the crazy explanation. On that picture, whether the final state counts as a belief depends almost entirely on what "belief" means, and the literature seems to switch definitions whenever convenient. I need a working definition of "belief" I can actually use with cases, not a textbook definition that only handles the easy examples.

Here are the cases I want it to handle. (a) My standing conviction that Mars has two moons—pretty clearly a belief even though I rarely think about it and almost never act on it. (b) My visual impression, shown the Müller-Lyer lines, that one line is longer than the other even while I know they're the same length—I'd say this isn't a belief, but it is representational and it will make me answer wrong if I'm careless. (c) A friend refuses to eat a piece of chocolate fudge shaped like dog feces while sincerely insisting, a little annoyed, that she knows it's chocolate—her face and hands behave as if she holds a belief she also denies. (d) A patient with Cotard delusion who tells me every day, and writes in her journal, that she is dead, but who still gets hungry, asks for extra blankets, and at one point asks a nurse to fetch her slippers because her feet are cold; when a staff member once addressed her as though she were alive, she corrected him. (e) A physicist who treats Newtonian mechanics as true for bridges and pipes but denies, asked directly, that the world literally is Newtonian. (f) A man who insists his neighbor is poisoning his meals and will repeat this to his sister at length, but eats the meals anyway and doesn't avoid the neighbor; mostly he just seems sad when he says it. (g) A passing thought I entertain and immediately reject—did I leave the stove on? no, I checked twice. (h) A man who spends weekends and part of his savings building a shelter because he believes a solar storm will knock out the power for months.

My hunch is that the trouble in the delusion literature comes from running together what you'd sincerely assert and what actually moves you around, and cases like (d) and (f) show how far those can come apart. But I keep going in circles, and I don't trust my own lean. Could you propose a definition, run it through each case, tell me where it gives the obviously wrong answer or no answer at all, and then either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts? I care more about a definition that handles the delusion cases honestly than one that flatters my pre-theoretical hunches.
