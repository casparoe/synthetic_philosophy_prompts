A state commission I'm on has been kicking around a reform to how we handle regulatory offences — the kind of environmental and workplace-safety violations that can currently land someone in jail. The working group's proposal is to split the proceeding in two. At stage one, guilt is strict: the prosecution only has to prove that the defendant's act or omission caused the harm (the overflowed waste pond, the worker who looses a hand to the missing machine guard, whatever). No inquiry into knowledge, care, or control. At stage two, the defendant can put on a "fault mitigation" case; if they show by a preponderance that they took reasonable care or honestly didn't know, the judge must cut the sentence — but by no more than half, and the conviction itself stands regardless. The base sentence is fixed by the severity of the harm, full stop. The pitch is that this keeps the deterrent punch of strict liability while still giving careful actors a break.

I'd like you to walk me through how this actually plays out, not whether it's noble in the abstract. What does it do to prosecutorial charging and plea bargaining — do defendants just settle every case because the guilt phase is a formality? Who benefits most from the mitigation hearing, and what evidence do people start generating (or fabricating) once they know a 50 percent haircut is on the table? Where does it get gamed first — by wealthy defendants with compliance consultants, by papering the file with "good faith" documentation after the fact, by shifting blame onto a low-level employee? And does the mitigation hearing secretly turn into a mens rea inquiry in disguise, with the burden flipped onto the accused? If so, is that a bug or a feature? Which failure modes could be fixed without just reinventing ordinary mens rea?
