I need a working definition of "death," and I want it to actually earn its keep. The context is the organ donation and end-of-life debates, where the word is doing a lot of silent work — specifically, its supposed to mark the point at which it becomes okay to stop trying, take organs, bury someone, etc. But I don't want a definition that's just "whatever makes those practices okay," because that would be stipulating the conclusion.

Here are the cases I want the definition to handle. Run your definition against each one, tell me where it gives the right answer, the wrong answer, or none at all, and either fix it or defend the surprising verdict.

1. A body found cold, stiff, and clearly dead for hours. Nobody disputes this one.
2. A patient under general anesthesia during open-heart surgery on a bypass machine: heart stopped, not breathing on their own, but they'll wake up when the bypass ends. Obviously alive.
3. A patient with total brain injury on a ventilator: no brainstem reflexes, no drive to breathe, but the heart is still beating and the body is warm and pink. In the US this is called brain death.
4. A patient whose life support is withdrawn; the heart stops. After a five-minute "no touch" period the team declares death and begins organ recovery. But at that moment CPR could in principle restart the heart, and the patient might be revived if anyone tried. Dead, or just dying-but-not-yet-dead?
5. An patient on ECMO with no pulse and no cardiac output, awake and asking questions because the machine is oxygenating their blood.
6. A kid pulled from icy water after an hour with no heartbeat and a flat EEG, who recovers fully after long CPR and rewarming under the rule that you aren't dead until you're warm and dead. Was she ever dead?
7. An adult in a persistent vegetative state: eyes open, sleep–wake cycles, breathing on her own, but no awareness and no prospect of recovery.
8. A newborn with anencephaly — no cortex but an intact brainstem — who breathes and has reflexes, and will die within days.

I suspect "death" is doing two jobs at once: a biological one (the organism as a whole has stopped) and a social/legal one (now we may stop efforts, take organs, bury). If you agree, give me both definitions and say which cases change their verdict when you switch between them; if you think one definition can handle both jobs, defend that instead. Max 600 words, and spend the space on the borderline cases rather than the easy ones.
