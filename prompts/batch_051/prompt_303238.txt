I am a novelist, and the book I am currently writing turns on a decades-long argument between a theoretical physicist and a philosopher of science. The physicist maintains that the only true description of the world is the most fundamental one, and that chemistry, condensed-matter physics, and everything above them are at best convenient fictions. The philosopher maintains that the higher levels have their own causal and explanatory standing and are not merely convenient. I had assumed this was the familiar reductionism-versus-emergence debate, but the more I read about effective field theories, the more I suspect both positions miss something. The formalism appears to show that a low-energy theory can be exact within its domain of validity, insensitive to the details of deeper physics except through a finite set of parameters fixed at that scale, and connected to higher-energy theories only by matching conditions that are seldom needed in practice. It also appears to show the reverse: those few parameters are, in principle, computable from the deeper theory, so the autonomy is not total, and at a description's boundary the next scale's physics must be faced. I can see why physicists speak of the autonomy of scales, but I cannot yet tell which parts of that claim are forced by the formalism and which are matters of philosophical temperament.

What I would like from you is the full specification of an adequate philosophical account of the autonomy of scales, where adequate means faithful to effective field theory as it is actually used and sufficient to adjudicate the dispute between my two characters. Treat it as a requirements list rather than a defence of a particular view: the phenomena it must capture, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the established results it must not contradict, with the consideration behind each. It should say what an account is obliged to make of the decoupling of heavy degrees of freedom, renormalisation-group flow, universality, matching, and the way predictions become insensitive to ultraviolet detail; and it should keep apart distinctions such as epistemic limitation versus ontological closure, weak versus strong emergence, and explanatory autonomy versus downward causation. For each requirement, say whether it is non-negotiable or merely the preference of one camp, and name the camp. Then tell me which single requirement knocks out the most popular positions, and whether any subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — that is, whether the autonomy of scales obliges every available position to sacrifice something. That last point matters most to me, because it would give the novel its central conflict.

Please write in British English and deliver the answer in the following structure. First, a table whose rows are the requirements, presented in descending order of how much they exclude, with these columns: Requirement, a crisp one-sentence statement of the constraint; What it must capture, the concrete phenomenon or case from effective field theory that generates it; Why it is a requirement, the consideration that makes it a genuine constraint, or the camp that merely prefers it; Status, either Non-negotiable or Negotiable, with whose preference it is if negotiable; and Excluded positions, the popular views it rules out, briefly. Keep the weight on the requirements and their motivation rather than on the scoring column. After the table, give a short JSON object with the keys bottom_line (one or two sentences on the shape an adequate theory must take), most_exclusionary_requirement (the single requirement that rules out the most contenders, with a one-sentence rationale), jointly_unsatisfiable_subsets (any subsets of requirements that cannot all be satisfied together, the tension involved, or an empty list if there are none), and negotiable_requirements (the requirements that are camp preferences rather than universal constraints).
