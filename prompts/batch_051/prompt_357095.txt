I'm taking a course that runs through different kinds of nihilism, and I noticed a double standard in the room that I'm not sure is defensible. When we did mereological nihilism—the view that there are no tables, chairs, or even people, just simples arranged tablewise, chairwise, and so on—everyone treated it as a live option. People called it strange but coherent, said it made them think differently about composition, and argued over the arguments without acting like believing it would ruin your life. Then we hit moral nihilism, and the exact same students talked as if the view was barely thinkable: if nothing is right or wrong, the objection went, then you couldn't get out of bed, you'd have no reason to do anything, and you couldn't even mean your own moral judgments. I keep waiting for someone to say that both positions do the same kind of thing—they tell you a whole category of everyday talk and thought is systematically false—and that it's not obvious why one error should be more unlivable than the other. Existential nihilism, by the way, sat in between: people treated 'nothing matters' as dark but respectable, while treating 'nothing is right or wrong' as almost a reductio of the person who says it.

So here's what I want to know: is there a real difference that justifies this asymmetry, or are we just inconsistently panicking about the one that touches our feelings? The candidate distinctions I can think of are (a) moral judgments are action-guiding and tied to emotion in a way beliefs about mid-sized objects aren't, so getting morality wrong is more destabilizing; (b) you can smoothly live as if tables exist while believing only simples do, but you can't live as if things matter while believing nothing does; (c) we have independent pressure to think composition is weird, but no independent pressure to think morality is an illusion. Test these against the hard cases—someone who genuinely believes mereological nihilism but still talks about chairs, and someone who genuinely believes moral nihilism but still feels guilt and indignation—and tell me which, if any, survives. If the asymmetry is justified, say which candidate does the work and where it would break down. If it isn't, say whether that means moral nihilism deserves the same calm 'weird but liveable' treatment, or whether mereological nihilism should have felt more threatening than it did.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. And tell me how confident you are overall and which part of your answer you're least sure of.
