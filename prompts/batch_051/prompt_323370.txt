You are a neurophilosopher and experimental psychologist who works on conscious volition. Think carefully and start by reconstructing the argument before you diagnose it.

Here is an argument I have been turning over, and it seems to prove far too much. (1) In Libet-style experiments, a preparatory brain signal—the readiness potential—appears a few hundred milliseconds before participants report the conscious urge to move their finger or wrist. (2) So the conscious intention to move arrives after the neural preparation that determines the movement. (3) If conscious intention arrives after the determining neural preparation, then conscious intention does not initiate the movement; it is at best a readout of a decision already made nonconsciously. (4) There is no principled reason to restrict this order to arbitrary finger movements, so conscious deliberation in any choice should likewise be preceded by its own nonconscious decision. (5) Therefore conscious deliberation and choice are post hoc confabulations. (6) If conscious deliberation is post hoc confabulation, no one has ever really made a decision, and our practices of holding people responsible for their choices rest on a systematic illusion. (7) That conclusion is unacceptable.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I would most like to keep premise (1), because the basic finding seems robust, but I also cannot accept (7). Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, address whether the move from (3) to (4) can survive a distinction between spontaneous, content-light movements and genuinely deliberative choices.
