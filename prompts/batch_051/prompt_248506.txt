At a discussion group I have attended since retiring from a career in the earth sciences, two members recently debated whether an endless afterlife of perfect happiness could remain desirable. Neither is a professional philosopher; one worked in economics, the other in music. I will reconstruct the exchange as accurately as I can.

Robert opened with the Makropulos case as Bernard Williams used it: a person who has had her life extended for over three centuries by an elixir finds her existence emptied of significance and welcomes death. Robert took this to show that any life extended without limit, however pleasant, eventually succumbs to ennui, because human satisfactions depend on novelty, contrast, and striving. A heaven that removes all obstacles, he said, removes the very conditions under which anything can matter to a person.

Elena replied that this slides from a finite creature's exhaustion to a perfected creature's nonexhaustion. Boredom, she said, is a failure mode of finite attention and finite desires: it arises when a good is experienced too long by an organism built for scarcity. Eternal bliss, if the phrase means anything, is not merely an earthly life stretched out; it is a different kind of relation to the good, perhaps not made of sequential episodes at all. An atemporal or self-present joy would have no "too long."

Robert answered that this defense purchases coherence at the price of continuity. If heaven is not experienced as a series of moments, then it is not obviously experienced as one's own life continuing. And if the joy must be constantly self-renewing to escape satiation, then it either becomes incomprehensible or reintroduces time and change in order to avoid boredom, which concedes his point. Elena countered that a person can be absorbed in a great piece of music for a stretch that feels both full and outside clock time, and that this offers a faint model of joy that does not require scarcity or dissatisfaction.

Robert retorted that even the most absorbing music ends, and its value depends partly on its being a bounded whole. A quartet extended forever would not be the same good, and a mind that never needed to come up for air would no longer be ours. Elena said this again takes our limits as normative. A perfected mind, she insisted, would not be exhausted by richness, and we are in no position to assert that no possible mind could find an unbounded good permanently absorbing without our specific finitude.

At this point I confessed I could not referee the matter, and the conversation ended unresolved. I would be grateful if you would go through the exchange and assess it move by move: identify which of Robert's and Elena's claims are strong or weak, state which of the two is more right overall, and say what assumptions about time, personal identity, and value the dispute is really turning on. I am especially interested in whether either debater is entitled to the partial concessions they seem to make.
