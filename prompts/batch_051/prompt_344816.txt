I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and at a quantum foundations workshop last month a physicist walked me through an argument over drinks that I still can't shake. Here's his chain as I've reconstructed it:

(1) On the many-worlds interpretation, when a quantum measurement occurs, every possible outcome is realized in some branch.
(2) So in any life-or-death event whose trigger is a quantum event, there's at least one branch where I survive (assuming survival isn't flat-out impossible).
(3) I have no experiences in branches where I'm dead; my stream of consciousness continues only in the branches where I'm alive.
(4) So from my own first-person standpoint, I should expect to find myself in a survival branch.
(5) Iterate: across repeated quantum-suicide experiments, I should expect to observe myself surviving again and again — quantum immortality.
(6) But the same reasoning seems to apply to car crashes, poisonings, house fires, any near-miss. It seams to prove I should expect to be immortal, and that I should expect all the unlikely coincidences to pile up (every gun misfires, every clot happens to dissolve) without any account of how the branches are weighted.
(7) So one of these steps has to be wrong, but each one looked fine when I wrote it down.

I'd written this up as a curiosity for my notebook, but my editor wants me to turn it into a short piece, and I've realized I can't honestly say where the argument goes wrong. So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Explain why that step looked plausible, and what a clean diagnosis would commit us to giving up.
