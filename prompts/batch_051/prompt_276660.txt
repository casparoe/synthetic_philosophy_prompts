I've been working through Hume's essay 'Of National Characters' after a slightly heated argument with a friend who insists that all talk of national character is just licensed stereotyping, while I wanted to defend the thought that some generalisations about peoples are harmless and useful. Hume seems to want to split the difference — early in the essay he contrasts the 'undistinguishing judgments' of the vulgar with what 'men of sense' allow — and a few paragraphs later he writes:

"Where a number of men are united into one political body, the occasions of their intercourse must be so frequent, for defence, commerce, and government, that, together with the same speech or language, they must acquire a resemblance in their manners, and have a common or national character, as well as a personal one, peculiar to each individual."

I take the phrase 'as well as a personal one' to be doing real work: a national character is not a claim that every member has the trait, and it is not reducible to a particular person's character. But then what, precisely, is it? I want a working definition of 'national character' that makes sense of this sentence and that I can use to sort the following claims: (a) 'The Swiss are punctual'; (b) 'my Swiss cousin Felix is punctual'; (c) 'Singaporeans are efficient'; (d) 'the Kurds are hospitable'; (e) 'Londoners are rude'; (f) 'the Japanese have a culture of indirect refusal'. My own leanings: (a) is a paradigm national-character claim and (b) is clearly not; I cannot decide about (c) and (d), since one names a young, multi-ethnic city-state and the other a people without a state; and I suspect (e) and (f) are doing something subtly different — (e) may be about a city's interactional norms and (f) about etiquette rather than character. Propose the definition, run it through all six, and tell me where it surprises you or forces you to revise it. State the definition in your first sentence or two, and keep the whole answer to at most 750 words.
