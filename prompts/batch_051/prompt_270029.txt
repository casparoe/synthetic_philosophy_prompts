I have no academic background, but for years I have been thinking on my own about a practical problem: before an artificial general intelligence is built, people can try to lock in bargains about what will happen after it is built. A friend of mine has firm judgments about these arrangements, and I am trying to work out what she would say about a new one—and, more importantly, what rule her judgments are tracking.

Her track record is as follows.

1. A company's public charter that pledges one-fifth of any future AGI's profits to a global health fund. She called this mostly public relations. It imposes a small reputational cost now, she said, but nothing could force the entity that exists after a real transition to honor a sentence written before the technology existed.

2. An employee of a lab accepting stock options that vest once the board certifies that the company has built an AGI. She thought this was entirely sensible. It does not require any future intelligence to respect a contract; it is an ordinary claim against an existing corporation, and the employee can sell it or dispute it in court in the ordinary world.

3. An insurance policy from a normal insurer that pays out if an AI-related catastrophe destroys the policyholder's assets. She dismissed it. If the catastrophe were severe enough to trigger the policy, she said, the courts and the insurer itself would likely be among the things that had stopped working, so you could not collect. And if the world stayed intact enough to collect, you probably did not need the special policy.

4. A notarized personal statement asking any future AGI to restore and provide for the person who wrote it. She called it worthless: there is no present party under any duty, no asset attached, and no reason a future system would care about a document written before it existed.

5. An advance retainer paid to a law firm that has agreed to act in the client's interests after the transition by whatever means then exist. She thought this was only slightly better than the notarized statement. There is a real fiduciary duty now, she admitted, but the only leverage the firm would have later is ordinary legal process, which is exactly what you cannot count on after a genuine discontinuity.

The new case is this. The founder of a lab signs a non-binding internal document stating that if the lab builds an AGI, he will transfer control of the resulting system to a five-person safety committee rather than to shareholders, and that any three members of the committee may jointly declare him in breach. The five members have no right to sue to enforce the document. But they are the same people who set the founder's compensation, conduct his performance reviews, and effectively control whether he keeps running the company; the document gives them a public standard around which to coordinate.

I can see at least two principles she might be running. Under the first, an arrangement matters only if it can be enforced through ordinary legal and financial institutions that will still be functioning when the payoff arrives; everything else is aspiration. Under the second, what matters is whether some identifiable present party can impose real costs on the promisor while the world is still normal, through legal, internal, or reputational channels. These two principles agree about most of her record, but they seem to diverge on the new case: the first would call the founder's document toothless, while the second might call it one of the strongest arrangements on the whole list.

So my questions are these. Which principle fits her record better, or is there a third principle that fits better? What would she say about the new arrangement if she wanted to be consistent with herself? Is the new arrangement genuinely different in kind from the earlier ones, or is it just a blend of the public pledge and the law-firm retainer? And what further case, not on this list, would most cleanly distinguish the candidate rules? In your answer, please tell me how confident you are in what you claim and flag the particular point you are least sure of.
