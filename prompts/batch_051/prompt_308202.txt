I keep noticing a parallel between the rationalist theory of war (in the form associated with Fearon) and contemporary AI development races, and I cannot decide whether it is a genuine structural identity or just pattern-matching.

As I understand it, the rationalist account starts from a puzzle: since war destroys resources, two rational states should be able to find a negotiated bargain that both prefer to the same outcome achieved by fighting. The account explains the persistence of war through three mechanisms. First, private information: each side knows its own capabilities and resolve better than the other, and has incentives to misrepresent them, so the bargaining range remains uncertain and attempts to locate it can fail. Second, commitment problems: even when a bargain exists, at least one side cannot credibly promise to respect it after the balance of power shifts, so the other fears being exploited and fights preemptively. Third, indivisibilities: some goods at stake cannot be divided into packages both sides can accept, so no compromise is available.

The AI development race seems to me to have the same shape. Racing is costly: labs and states spend enormous resources, compress safety testing, and jointly generate risk; a credible coordinated slowdown or shared safety regime would plausibly leave most parties better off than the expected outcome of an unbounded race. Yet the race continues. I see the same three mechanisms. Each lab has private information about its own progress and incentive to exaggerate or conceal it in order to extract concessions, attract funding, or deter entrants. A promise to pause or to share the benefits of a leading system is not credible, because the leader cannot commit not to exploit a temporary monopoly after a slowdown. And the prize, decisive strategic or market advantage from being first, may be effectively indivisible, with no compromise division of the spoils.

Where exactly do the two cases come apart, and do the differences matter for explanation?
