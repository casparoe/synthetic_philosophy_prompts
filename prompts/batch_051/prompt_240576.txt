Next month I am giving a department colloquium defending the following thesis: human extinction would be bad not only because of the deaths it involves and the welfare lost by people who would otherwise have existed, but also because the continued existence of humanity after one's own death is a background condition of much of what one presently values. I will press a version of Samuel Scheffler's afterlife conjecture: many of our projects—long-term research, political reform, artistic creation, even ordinary practices like saving or teaching—derive much of their point from an implicit, rarely examined confidence that the human enterprise will outlast us; if we believed it would end shortly after our own generation, those activities would lose much of their value for us now, even though we ourselves would not live to see the end. My argument will be roughly this: the value of these activities cannot be fully analyzed in terms of the welfare of currently existing individuals or of people who would have existed, so a value theory that counts only losses to individual persons misses part of what extinction would destroy. The audience is a philosophy department with a strong ethics group: two act-consequentialists, a Kantian constructivist who holds that the value of our projects is grounded in rational agency itself, a virtue ethicist, and a philosopher of action who is suspicious of appeals to implicit background conditions. Draft a Q&A prep sheet for me: the six or seven objections I should expect, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way these people would actually put them, each with a two-minute reply, a note on what in the objection I should concede and what I should hold, and a note on where that reply is soft; include the question I am hoping nobody asks. Write the prep sheet in American English.
