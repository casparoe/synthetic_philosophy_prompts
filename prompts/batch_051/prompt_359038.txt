I've been teaching Nyāya epistemology and keep getting stuck on the standard gloss of Gautama's Nyāya Sūtra 1.1.7, where verbal testimony is described as the statement of an āpta — a trustworthy person who has personally apprehended the truth and is free from any desire to deceive. The Naiyāyikas take this to be an independent pramāṇa, not reducible to inference, because when you trust an expert you're not just inferring that they're usually reliable; the testimony itself supposedly transmits knowledge. I'm trying to think through whether that argument survives a specific development: suppose the bulk of what I now treat as verbal testimony reaches me through LLMs and recommendation feeds, where there's no identifiable āpta, no single knower I can point to, and the system is optimized for engagement rather than for reporting what it knows. Does the Nyāya argument still go through, or does the relevant speaker just become a gerrymandered aggregate? Or does this kind of reliance collapse back into inference from observed reliability, leaving śabda without a distinct role? I want to know which premise of the old argument is actually under pressure and whether there's a repaired version that still says something distinctive about testimony.
