I have a rule I follow in my own modeling work, and a friend and I spent a long evening arguing about it without either of us conceding. Whenever I need a probability distribution and all I know is a coarse summary of the evidence — an expected value, a finite range, the fact that two variables are independent — I select the distribution with the greatest Shannon entropy among all distributions consistent with those facts. My reasoning has been that entropy measures how far a distribution goes beyond its constraints, so the maximum-entropy distribution is the one that encodes the evidence and nothing more. And I have leaned on this as a rule for rational priors: uniform over a stated interval when only the range is known, exponential when only a mean is given, Gaussian when only a mean and variance are given. Until this argument I was confident that anyone who shared my information, and wanted to avoid smuggling in extra commitments, would be forced to the same choices.

My friend's objection was that the rule does not actually pick a unique neutral prior, because the maximum-entropy distribution changes in substance when the problem is redescribed. The prior that is uniform over the length of a cube's edge is not uniform over the cube's volume; a distribution that is exponential and maximum-entropy in one variable comes out a different, non-maximum-entropy distribution after a nonlinear change of coordinates. So, she argued, the quantity being maximized is only as neutral as the coordinate system, and choosing that coordinate system is exactly the kind of substantive assumption the method was supposed to eliminate. The rule, on her account, is a bias for whatever parameterization I happened to write down first. I found this genuinely unsettling, but I also could not see what better policy a person with finite information could adopt; if a prior has to be chosen for a decision or an estimate, refusing to choose is not on the table.

Is my rule defensible, or is it a bias dressed up as a principle? Is there a version of it that keeps the good consequences — say, one restricted to cases where the parameterization is physically or symmetrically privileged, or one combined with an invariance requirement, or one framed as a decision-theoretic procedure rather than a claim about rational neutrality — without pretending the rule quarantines me from substantive assumptions?
