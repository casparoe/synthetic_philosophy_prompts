I serve on a state curriculum commission that each year decides how our world-history standards should describe three or four contested narratives from the deep past. We are required to assign each narrative one of four classifications: history; legend with a historical core; legend or cultural memory whose primary value is what it reveals about the society that transmitted it; or not determinable on present evidence. These classifications drive textbook language, teacher preparation, and public statements, and every one of them will be contested by some constituency. I need a reusable procedure, not a one-off argument, that a staffer without training in ancient history, classics, or biblical studies can run in roughly an afternoon per case and that produces a defensible recommendation with all genuinely valuative steps flagged for the full commission to decide.

For this year's cycle, assume the following evidentiary baseline as common ground, rather than as debatable claims. For the Trojan War: a citadel at Hisarlik, widely identified with Homer's Ilion or Troy, shows one or more destruction horizons around 1180 BCE, which many scholars treat as the historical kernel of the tradition, but the epic's causes, duration, named commanders, and wooden horse have no contemporary attestation, and the poems were stabilized centuries after the events they depict. For King Arthur: no near-contemporary source describes such a ruler; the earliest clear literary appearances come in a ninth-century compilation and the full courtly Arthur is fixed by a twelfth-century chronicle, several hundred years after the post-Roman period in which any historical Arthur would have operated. For the Exodus: Egyptian and Levantine archaeological records offer no direct confirmation of a mass enslavement and departure narrated at that scale; the earliest extrabiblical reference to a people called Israel in the southern Levant is an Egyptian stele from around 1208 BCE, and the account as transmitted was written down and compiled centuries after the period in question. The commission takes no position on whether these stories are true in a theological or literary sense; the procedure's job is only to manage the shift from a silent, late, or ambiguous record to a label about historicity.

Please give me a step-by-step procedure that: (1) separates what the evidence can establish — a destruction at a site, a people named in an inscription, a period of political decline — from the narrative's larger claims about causes, individuals, and sequence; (2) states explicit tests for when a much-later text, a matching place-name, or an archaeological find licenses increased confidence in historicity, including tests for temporal gap, for the independence of the tradition from the later community's interest in it, for the difference between being consistent with the record and being positively supported by it, and for how much modification an event can absorb before it is no longer the same event; (3) assigns each narrative to one of the four classifications, or stops with not determinable and says what evidence would be needed to move it; and (4) marks every non-empirical decision point — about burdens of proof, about what counts as a continuous tradition, about how much weight devotional or national attachment receives — and hands those to a named person or body rather than resolving them silently. Then run the procedure on the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus, and report the classification each receives and the residual judgment each outcome depends on. At least one of the three results will probably be unwelcome to a significant constituency; do not soften it, and end by stating plainly what the procedure cannot do and where it is likely to misfire.
