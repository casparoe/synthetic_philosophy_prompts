I co-facilitate a reading group in philosophy of cognitive science — ten PhD students and postdocs, one 75-minute session with a whiteboard — and next week it is my turn to introduce predictive processing and the free energy principle. Most of the group can follow simple probabilistic language but have little neuroscience; two members are already skeptical that this is just a fashionable reframing of the old idea that perception is constructive. I want them to leave the session able to state the core claim in their own words: the brain implements a hierarchical generative model that continuously minimizes the surprise of its sensory inputs (in the information-theoretic sense) by predicting those inputs, and perception, action, and attention fall out as three different ways of managing prediction error. I also want them to hold apart the free energy principle — a formal result about using a tractable quantity that upper-bounds surprise — from predictive processing as an empirical hypothesis about how cortical circuits might do the minimizing, and to see exactly why the first does not by itself prove the second.

Plan the explanation rather than giving the explanation itself. Give me: the order in which the components must be introduced and why each depends on the one before, covering at least the generative model, prediction error, precision weighting, hierarchical organization, active inference, and the step from prediction error to free energy as a bound on surprise; the one concrete example or scenario that should carry the idea through the whole session, what it must have and what it must lack, and why it is better than starting from static visual illusions or from abstract Bayesian equations; the misconceptions the group predictably brings or forms and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off; a check for understanding that would show the distinction between the formal principle and the empirical theory has landed, not merely been repeated; and what I should openly refuse to explain at this level if time runs short, and say so. Hard limit: 900 words.
