I want you to assess the following argument step by step, because I find each premise plausible but I am not sure the conclusion is being reached honestly. The argument is about whether Frankfurt-style cases actually refute the principle of alternative possibilities, or whether they merely relocate the relevant alternative.

The case is the standard one: an agent is deciding whether to perform some action, and a counterfactual intervener has reliable access to what the agent is about to decide. If the agent is about to decide not to perform the action, the intervener will intervene and causally bring it about that the agent decides to perform it. In the actual sequence, however, the agent decides on her own and the intervener remains idle.

Now here is the argument:

(1) If an agent is morally responsible for a decision, then there is some alternative that she could have brought about, even if it is not an alternative decision.
(2) In the Frankfurt case, the agent could have brought about something other than what she actually brought about: she could have had the decision caused by the intervener rather than formed through her own deliberation.
(3) Having the decision caused by the intervener rather than forming it through one’s own deliberation is a genuine alternative.
(4) The alternative described in step 3 is the kind of alternative required for moral responsibility.
(5) Therefore, in the Frankfurt case the agent retains an alternative possibility, and the case does not show that responsibility is compatible with the absence of alternative possibilities.

I suspect the weak point is either step 1 or step 4, but I cannot say exactly where the argument fails. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what is the strongest version of the argument that could be built from the remaining steps?
