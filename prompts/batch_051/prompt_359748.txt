I'm a phd student working on institutional design and AI governance, and I keep running into the same argument whenever pre-AGI bargains come up — agreements made now about how power, compute, or the gains from AGI will be shared or constrained later. The argument, usually from people in the governance world, is that these bargains are basically Ulysses contracts. Before the sirens get here, we're still (relatively) sane and pluralistic and we actually have something to negotiate with; once AGI exists, whichever lab or state or coalition ends up on top will be hearing the sirens' song — the winner-take-all payoff of ignoring everyone else — and no promise signed now is going to outweigh that. So, the story goes, the rational move is to tie ourselves to the mast while we still can: treaties, verification regimes, compute escrow, tripwires, whatever. This is used to license the conclusion that pre-AGI commitments can be more than cheap talk, and that we should spend real political effort locking them in now.

I want an audit of how far the Ulysses picture actually holds. Give me the correspondance table first: what plays the role of Ulysses, the crew, the ropes, the mast, the sirens, the song, the order to ignore his later commands? Then the places where the mapping breaks, and which of those disanalogies really matter for the intended use versus which are just decorative. In particular I suspect the analogy quietly imports a unified principal and an external enforcement crew that doesn't itself get tempted, but I'm not sure how much weight that should bear. Does the conclusion — that pre-AGI bargains can be credible commitments — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy doing the whole argument? And if there's a better analogy for what these bargains actually are, tell me that too.
