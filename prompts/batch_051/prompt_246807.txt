I've been turning over an argument against laws of history, loosly adapted from Popper, and I can't tell whether it's sound or where exactly it comes apart. Here it is:

(1) If there were laws of history, then a suitably informed observer could in principle use them to predict future states of society.
(2) Future states of society depend heavily on future knowledge — new inventions, discoveries, ideas.
(3) To predict a future discovery, you would have to specify its content in advance.
(4) Specifying a discovery's content in advance just is making the discovery, so it would no longer be future knowledge.
(5) So future knowledge cannot be predicted.
(6) So no observer could predict future states of society.
(7) Therefore, there are no laws of history.

I go back and forth on where the weak link is: sometimes step 2 looks too strong, sometimes step 4 seems to conflate predicting that a discovery will occur with predicting its content, and sometimes the last step feels like a jump from what we can know to what there is. Is the argument valid? If not, say exactly which step fails on the strongest reading of the argument, and whether there's a nearby valid argument for a weaker conclusion that survives.
