I'm taking a course on cognitive liberty and neural data this term, and I keep getting stuck on one clause from a BCI company's privacy policy. It reads: "We collect neural data solely to calibrate the device and to measure attention and cognitive load. We do not use neural data to infer your thoughts, feelings, beliefs, preferences, or memories." The company added it after users got scared about mind reading, and their whole stance since has been that they only process "attention and cognitive load," nothing more.

Last week they rolled out a feature where the headset tracks frontal alpha asymmetry while you read messages and labels some incoming texts as "potentially upsetting," suggesting you wait before replying. The company says its not inferring any feeling: it calls the signal an "approach-withdrawal tendency," just a neurophysiological biomarker, and says the user is the one interpreting it as a feeling. I get that a biomarker isn't the same as an emotion, but the entire design is meant to catch the beginning of an emotional reaction before you would report one yourself. So either they're inferring something pretty close to a feeling, or the word "feelings" in the clause is doing almost no work.

I'm less interested in what a court would actually do and more in how the clause should apply to a case its authors weren't imagining. What do the different approaches—plain words, likely purpose, what the drafters would say if asked, and what a reasonable user expects when she reads the policy—each give? And if the clause doesn't reach this, is there a version that would, without also banning the ordinary attention and cognitive load metrics the device needs to work?
