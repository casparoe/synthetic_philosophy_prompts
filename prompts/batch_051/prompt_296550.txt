Consider Searle's Chinese Room. In the standard version, a monolingual English speaker who knows no Chinese is alone in a room with a stock of Chinese characters and a rulebook, written in English, that tells him how to match incoming character strings to outgoing character strings. The room performs so well that Chinese-speaking judges cannot distinguish its replies from a native speaker's. Searle's conclusion is that neither the man nor the system understands the Chinese: the man manipulates symbols as bare shapes, with syntax and no semantics. The main positions in the surrounding dialectic are the systems reply (it is the whole system, not the man, that understands), the robot reply (real sensory-motor contact with the world is what would ground the symbols), the brain-simulator reply (what matters is simulating the actual causal powers of a Mandarin speaker's brain, not just running an abstract symbol-shuffling program), and the combination reply (all three together).

Now vary the setup in one respect: there is no pre-given rulebook. The man starts with only the symbols and a feedback lamp. After he pushes a string of characters through the output slot, the lamp shows green if a panel of native judges would have accepted the string as an apt reply to the current input, and red otherwise. He may keep notes in English, revise his private manipulation scheme, and try the same input again. Over forty years of twelve-hour days, using nothing but this green/red feedback—with no translation of any input or output ever made available to him—he builds a scheme that passes the standard blind test for understanding Mandarin as well as a native speaker. Asked afterward what a reply meant, he can say only which of his character strings were usually green-lit, not what they said.

Does Searle's conclusion survive this change? Work through whether and how the learning history should matter, then say for each of the four positions above whether the revised case strengthens, weakens, or leaves untouched its force. Give a clear final verdict: at the end of the forty years, does the man understand Mandarin? If not, what exactly is still missing? If he does, say whether that commits you to one of the standard positions or to something further. Close by saying what your verdict implies about computationalism—the thesis that running the right computational process, in a brain or a machine, would suffice for understanding.
