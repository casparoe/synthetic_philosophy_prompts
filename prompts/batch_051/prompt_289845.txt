Me and some people in my philosophy of the family seminar got stuck on Jane English's friendship model of what adult children owe their parents. The standard move is to treat the friendship model as the soft option: kids with cold or abusive parents owe nothing, because there's no friendship to ground duties, whereas the debt model keeps them on the hook no matter what, since debts dont care about feelings. But it seems to me the friendship model could also cut the other way, and no one in the room could produce a clean case.

So: construct a thought experiment where the friendship model of filial duty ends up more demanding than the debt model. The adult child has, by any reasonable accounting, already discharged whatever quantifiable debt they incurred from being raised — so the debt model says they're done — yet the friendship model still says they owe something substantial going forward. Constraints: ordinary realistic family, no abuse, no estrangement, no catastrophic or terminal illness, no inheritance or guilt dynamics, and the parent must be a genuinely good friend to the adult child in the present day, not just someone who did their parental job well. The help in question should be burdensome but not ruinous. Keep the scenario to one paragraph. Then walk me through exactly what the debt model says in the case and what the friendship model says, and where the two verdicts separate. Finish by telling me which single feature of the case is doing the work — my hunch is it has to do with a debt being dischargeable while a friendship isnt, but make that precise — and how a defender of the friendship model should respond to the complaint that the case just stacks the deck by stipulating a warm relationship.
