I teach public law, but this problem has recently become personal, and I want an analysis I can put before an insurer. My sister and her husband used IVF; after preimplantation genetic testing, they selected an embryo that did not carry the marker for her husband's hereditary condition, but the clinic negligently transferred a different, affected embryo. Their son was born with that serious condition. The clinic's insurer maintains that the provision below bars any claim by the child, since the child would not have been born at all without the clinic's negligence.

The provision, enacted before embryo screening existed, reads: "No child shall have a claim against a health care practitioner based on the allegation that, but for the practitioner's negligent act or omission, the child would not have been born alive."

Act as a legal philosopher who works on the nonidentity problem and statutory interpretation. Think through the problem carefully before giving your considered view, and begin by brainstorming plausible readings of the clause. Then give the arguments from (i) the statutory wording, (ii) the purpose of the clause, (iii) what the drafters would have said if asked about a wrong-embryo case, and (iv) what a reasonable reader would take it to mean, and say which approach should govern here and why.

Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers working on the nonidentity problem, and do not present a contested view — for example, the claim that person-affecting moral principles can or cannot ground the child's claim — as the consensus. Where your answer depends on a contested premise, flag it and explain how much your conclusion depends on it.
