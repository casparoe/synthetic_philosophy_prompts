Lately I've been thinking about collapse because I moved back to my hometown after a decade away and it's eerie: the furniture plant shut down years ago, half of Main Street is boarded up, but there's still a public pool and Little League. Everyone calls the town 'collapsed,' but it clearly hasn't ended. Then I read some archaeologists saying similar things about the Maya or Easter Island — the people didn't disappear, the society changed, and maybe 'collapse' is just the wrong word. Around the same time I was reading about ecosystem dynamics, where there's this cycle of growth, conservation, release, and reorganization. The conservation phase is the most connected and efficient but also the most brittle, so a disturbance triggers release: the system simplifies, then something new assembles. That sounds to me almost exactly like Tainter's account of complex societies — they add layers of bureaucracy and infrastructure to deal with problems, those layers give less and less return, and eventually the whole hyperconnected thing comes apart. Here's the parallel I cant decide about: is societal collapse just the 'release' phase of an ecological adaptive cycle, and if so, what does that predict about what comes after? Test it against the Late Bronze Age, the Maya lowlands, and Easter Island. If the parallel is only superficial, where exactly does it snap — maybe because human societies can see the cycle coming, or because 'collapse' describes political institutions more than ecosystems? I suspect seeing collapse as part of a cycle might just be a way of sneaking in the claim that it's not really so bad.
