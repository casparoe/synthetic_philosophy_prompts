I'm a journalist who mostly covers science and ideas, and I'm trying to get my head round a language-revival dispute for a piece I'm writing. The shape of it is this: on a small island, the local language has about forty fluent speakers left, nearly all of them over seventy. The younger families switched to English a couple of generations ago and mostly don't see the language as part of their own lives. A council proposal would make it a compulsory subject for the first three years of primary school, taught by two adults who learned it as a second language. A heritage group quoted Mill at me to justify the scheme. The passage, from On Liberty (1859), chapter three, is:

"As it is useful that while mankind are imperfect there should be different opinions, so is it that there should be different experiments of living; that free scope should be given to varieties of character, short of injury to others; and that the worth of different modes of life should be proved practically, when any one thinks fit to try them."

Mill clearly wasn't picturing this: a mode of life that is disappearing not because its being suppressed, but because almost nobody is choosing to continue it, and a plan to keep it going by making schoolchildren learn the language. I'm not after a verdict on whether compulsory lessons are good policy. I want to know how the passage itself reaches, or fails to reach, this case under each of the usual readings: the plain words, the purpose of Mill's argument about individuality, what Mill would probably have said if I could put the case to him, and what a reasonable reader now should take him to mean. Does any of those readings give the heritage group what they want? And if none does, is that because Mill's framework has nothing to say about why a dying language matters, or because the group has misread him? If the readings pull apart, tell me which one should win and why.
