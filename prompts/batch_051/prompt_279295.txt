So I had a long argument with a friend last night about whether people actually have stable character traits. He thinks its obviously true that people are brave or stingy or whatever, and that we use these words because they pick out real, persisting features of people. I took the situationist side: a lot of the classic experiments seem to show that small features of a situation (being in a hurry, whether a phone call just happened, how many other people are around) swamp what we'd call character, and that people massively over-attribute behaviour to traits. Afterward I realised I don't just hold this as an abstract view — I run an actual policy. Whenever I catch myself or someone else explaining an action with a trait word ("she's generous", "he's unreliable", "they're just cowardly"), I refuse to accept it unless there's evidence the person behaves the same way across genuinely different kinds of situations, and in practice I discount one-off trait explanations almost to zero. The other day my friend said my flatmate was "just unreliable" after he missed drinks twice, and I immediately started listing situational pressures instead.

My friend thinks this is mad: he says I've turned a methodological caution into a blanket ban on character talk, that it leaves me unable to predict the people around me, and that it's the fundamental attribution error flipped — now I systematically under-attribute to persons. Is my policy defensible? I want to know whether it's a rational epistemic shortcut or a bias dressed up as a rule. When is it reliable, when does it fail, and is there a restated version that keeps the benefits without making me obtuse about my actual friends? For instance, maybe the policy is fine for broad global traits but overreaches for local, situation-relative traits, or maybe I should aggregate behaviour over time rather than demanding cross-situational consistency for every single attribution. I also wonder whether I'm sneaking in a moral preference — I don't like blaming people — and calling it an epistemic rule.
