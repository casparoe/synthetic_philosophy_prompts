I'm the chair of a small nonprofit board, and earlier this year we had to vote to eliminate a program that was the only thing getting some of our clients out of the house, because keeping it would have drained reserves and closed the whole organization by October. We all agreed it was the right vote, but I've never been able to shake the feeling that we didn't just harm those clients, we wronged them—and the wrongness didn't disappear just because the alternative was worse. So I've been reading around the problem of dirty hands in politics, and I built an argument to make sense of that feeling. Now I think it proves too much, and I cant find the exit.

(1) A leader who orders a strike that kills one uninvolved child as a side effect, when it's the only way to stop an attack that would kill many innocents, does the right thing all things considered. (2) But the leader still violates the child's right not to be killed. Violating a right is a wrong, even when you have overwhelming reason to do it. (3) So the act is both right and wrong: justified, but the child's claim still counts against it. (4) That combination—doing the right thing while also doing wrong—is what dirty hands means. (5) But then a parent who can save only one of two children from a fire has dirty hands too: saving one is right, and the other child's claim doesn't simply vanish. (6) If the parent has dirty hands, dirty hands aren't distinctive to public office; they're the ordinary cost of being a finite chooser. (7) Yet the concept earns its keep because it captures something distinctive about political roles: they liscense acts that would be forbidden in private life and leave a residue that calls for guilt and amends even when the act was right. (8) So either every hard tradeoff gives people dirty hands, which trivializes the idea, or the concept is empty. Both seem unacceptable, so one of the earlier steps has to be false.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I don't want a survey of possible diagnoses: tell me where the argument actually breaks and defend that verdict, even if it means biting a bullet. Two things I'd most like to keep, if I can: that the leader's act really is the right one (rather than merely excusable), and that there is a real residue when a justified decision wrongs someone. But if keeping both is what got me into this mess, say so plainly.
