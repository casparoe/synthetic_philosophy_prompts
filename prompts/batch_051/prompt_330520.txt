I am a PhD student working on South Asian epistemology, and I keep returning to a tension I want argued through rather than merely surveyed. I find the fourfold catalogue of knowledge sources associated with the Nyāya tradition compelling as a constrained theory of warrant: a cognition may count as knowledge only if it is produced by perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), analogy (upamāna), or the reliable testimony of a competent speaker (śabda). Its appeal lies in its exclusivity: the list refuses a residual whatever-else-lets-us-in clause, and each item has a distinct enough function that the catalogue reads as an explanatory theory of epistemic success rather than a collection of examples. Yet I also hold a firm intuition that resists this catalogue. There are things I know that do not, on their face, issue from any of the four. The hardest case for me is knowledge of an absence. I know that the cat is not on the mat, that a proof has no gap, that a debt was never repaid. Such knowledge is not obviously a perception of something present, an inference from an observed mark, an analogical recognition, or the uptake of a report, and it feels too much like a direct epistemic achievement to be reduced to inference in every case. Some rival schools address exactly this by adding a further instrument, non-apprehension (anupalabdhi), which yields knowledge that something is absent when an expected cognition or search fails. I want you to argue, with commitment, whether I should (a) debunk the intuition and keep the four means closed; (b) amend the theory by adding non-apprehension as a fifth means; or (c) reconcile the two by showing that the fourfold account already covers absence without a new instrument. Present each move at its strongest first, then tell me which move you would make, what it commits you to, and what it costs, and under what conditions you would reverse it.

- strongest_case_for_amendment: the strongest argument that absence knowledge forces a fifth instrument.
- strongest_case_for_reconciliation: the strongest argument that the fourfold account already covers absence.
