ok so i'm putting together my "decision theory profile" for a reading group and i'd love a consistency check before i present it. here's roughly where i've landed:

(1) i think good's theorem is right: an expected-utility maximizer with stable preferences can't be made ex ante worse off by costless information, since they can always ignore it and act exactly as they would have without it.

(2) i also think it's rational to decline a free, painless, perfectly accurate genetic test for a late-onset disease when no treatment or behavior change would follow—just because knowing would make you miserable.

(3) i think the reason (2) is rational is that dread, hope, suspense, and the like are real outcomes. information changes them even when it changes nothing you do. so a utility function can coherently care about whether you believe a bad thing is coming, not just about what happens.

(4) but i also want to hold that a version of good's theorem goes through for any stable, coherent utility function at all—including one that assigns disutility to bad beliefs themselves. the argument is just that "act as you would have acted without the news" is always available after you get it, and by (1) that strategy can't be worse.

(5) and i think that if someone declines costless, purely decision-relevant information for no reason whatsoever—not dread, not suspense, not a preference for surprise, just "i'd rather not know"—then they're being irrational, not merely nonstandard.

can you go through these and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and which only look that way? rank the tensions by severity, say what the cheapest fix for the worst one is and what it costs, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most.
