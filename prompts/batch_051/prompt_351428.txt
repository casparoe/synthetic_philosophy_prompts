I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I have been trying to get a conceptual grip on griefbots: interactive software agents trained on a deceased person's messages, voice recordings and social media, which allow the bereaved to converse with someone who has died. I have a working set of criteria for what makes an engagement an ongoing relationship with the dead rather than a mere reminder of one. On my criteria, the bereaved person addresses the dead person directly, in the second person; the engagement is part of the social practice of mourning rather than a private substitute for it; the relationship remains revisable as the survivor changes; and the bereaved person continues to acknowledge the death rather than using the interaction to undo it.

Now consider a case I have been turning over. A woman in her sixties loses her husband after a long illness. A company builds a griefbot from several years of his text messages, emails and voicemails. The bot is explicitly presented to her as a simulation and does not claim to be him, and she says she understands this. She nevertheless consults it most days, asks it for advice about decisions her husband never faced, and over time treats its answers as what he would have said. At the same time she keeps the anniversary memorial, visits the grave and talks with her children about him. She describes the bot as a way of continuing the conversation, not as a person.

Work through the criteria and say, for each, whether the case satisfies it, violates it, or leaves it underdetermined. Then answer the harder question: does the case qualify as an ongoing relationship with the dead? If the answer is unclear, is that because my criteria are wrong, because the case is genuinely new, or because I am smuggling in a view about what mourning ought to be? And does the distinction between an ongoing relationship with the dead and a mere reminder of one carry the ethical weight I have been assuming it does? Please write your answer in British English.
