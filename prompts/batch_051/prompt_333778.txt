In my course on archives and historical method, I have been defending the view that silences in the archive are not neutral absences but positive effects of power: when a class of people or a kind of event leaves little trace, the absence itself is evidence of the institutions, categories, and exclusions that decided what was worth recording and keeping. I want to learn what this view cannot survive rather than just sharpen it. What would count against it? Please distinguish at least three kinds of silence: suppression or bureaucratic inaudibility at the moment of record-creation, selective retention and weeding by later institutions, and non-institutional loss such as fire, damp, insect damage, or the death of an oral tradition. For each, say whether an absence is best read as a power trace or as something closer to noise and decay, and what evidence would decide that. Which cases would force me to abandon the principle outright, which would only narrow its scope, and which would a defender reasonably dismiss as a misuse of the view? I would also like an honest assessment of whether the position is insulated — flexible enough to explain every absence and therefore at risk of explaining none of them — or whether it makes definite predictions about the shape of silences that could fail.
