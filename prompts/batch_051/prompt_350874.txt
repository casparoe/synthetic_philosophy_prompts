I am a novelist, and I have been drafting a book in which a municipal government, a streaming platform's recommender, and a religious order turn out to be goal-directed systems of the same underlying kind. While trying to make their behavior legible to myself, I have developed a small theory, and I want an honest assessment of it before I build the book around it.

I have been calling it the negative-space method, or sometimes the exclusion profile. The central claim is this: the most reliable evidence about what an optimizer is actually maximizing, and about the space of options it is actually searching, is not what it does but what it never does. For any goal-directed system there will be moves that would be obvious and attractive if its stated objective were its real objective, but which the system does not merely reject: it fails to register them as options at all. The true objective, I want to say, is whatever function would be maximized by a search that is blind in exactly those directions; the true search space is the boundary between what the system can consider and what it cannot see. So instead of asking what a system is pursuing, I ask which sacrifices it declines to make even when they would serve its announced goal, and which nearby alternatives it treats as unthinkable. The shape of that excluded region is the negative space, and I claim the objective shows up there more reliably than in the system's successes, because successes can be faked, coincidental, or overdetermined, whereas the pattern of things never tried is harder to counterfeit.

Two examples from the novel. The recommender claims to optimize watch time, yet it never surfaces the option of ending a session early; encouraging someone to stop does not appear to be in its search space at all, even though it might build trust and yield more total watch time across a month. The municipal government claims to optimize public safety, but interventions that would plainly reduce injuries never appear in its internal debates whenever they would embarrass a contractor or cross a jurisdictional line. They are not considered and rejected; they are simply absent. My protagonist, a data archivist, notices this and begins reconstructing the true objective from the omissions.

I am fairly convinced this is a real insight rather than a metaphor, but I want to be checked. Is the negative-space method coherent as stated? Has it already been worked out under another name in the study of optimization, preference inference, or institutions? And where does it break: what does it count as evidence when it is not evidence?

Please be candid rather than encouraging, and take a definite position rather than laying out possible readings. Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
