I have been working through the idea of program equilibrium in one-shot games and find that I understand the examples without understanding what is really going on. The setup is this: two players facing a single Prisoner's Dilemma do not choose Cooperate or Defect directly; each submits the source code of a computer program to a referee, each program is given the other program's source as input, and the program's output determines the player's move, with the usual payoffs applied. The stock result is that if both players submit a suitable program that cooperates when it is paired with its own source and defects otherwise, mutual cooperation becomes a stable outcome, even though the underlying action game has a unique dominant-strategy equilibrium. I can follow the arithmetic of why a unilateral switch to an always-defect program is unprofitable, but I cannot put my finger on what conceptual change has happened: the payoffs and the number of encounters have not changed, so it is strange that merely changing the medium in which a strategy is expressed — a program rather than a bare action — should alter the set of stable outcomes.

My rough suspicion is that this is not simply an enlargement of the strategy space. A program lets a player condition on properties of the other player's strategy rather than only on the other player's move, and this seems to create a kind of one-shot commitment device: a would-be defector's code triggers defection from its opponent, so the deviation is unprofitable without any repetition or external enforcement. But I am not sure that formulation is illuminating, and I am particularly troubled by the more self-referential family of rules of the form "cooperate if and only if the other program will cooperate against me." There, each program's output appears to depend on the other's output in a way that could loop, or admit several mutually consistent fixed points, or fail to define a unique move at all.

Could you explain the conceptual issue rather than just rehearse the examples? In particular, I would like to understand: what a program equilibrium is, precisely; whether it is best understood as a genuine change in the game or as a cosmetic redescription; how to think about the circular rules and whether they are well-defined; and what the concept shows about cooperation being sustained by mutual transparency about strategies rather than by repetition or punishment. Please write your answer in British English.
