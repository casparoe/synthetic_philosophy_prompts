In my reading group, someone summarized a chapter on the underdetermination of theory by evidence from a popular philosophy-of-science book. Her summary went like this:

Any finite body of observations is compatible with indefinitely many mutually incompatible theories, so the data can never force a theory on us. Moreover, because every prediction draws on auxiliary assumptions about instruments, initial conditions, and background conditions, an apparently refuting observation can always be absorbed by adjusting one of those auxiliaries rather than abandoning the core theory. It follows that when two theories both fit the data we have, no future evidence could distinguish them; they are empirically equivalent for all practical purposes, and preferring one to the other can only reflect convention, taste, or social pressure. That is what philosophers mean by the underdetermination of theory by evidence, and it leaves scientific realism, the view that science converges on the truth, without rational support.

I have read just enough secondary material to suspect she has compressed several distinct claims into one — I gather the Duhem–Quine thesis is not quite the same thing as underdetermination, and that there might be a modest and a radical version of underdetermination — but I cannot locate the exact seams. Is this a fair summary? Please separate what is accurate, what is a defensible simplification, and what is an overstatement or conflation, and tell me whether the final conclusion about scientific realism survives once the distinctions are restored.
