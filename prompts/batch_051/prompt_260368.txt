Last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether a God who cannot suffer can still be said to love. He insisted that a love that cannot be wounded, grieved, or changed by the beloved is not love at all but something closer to an efficient decree, and he kept pressing me on passages in which the biblical God is said to repent, grieve, and be provoked. By the end I was less sure of my position than when we started. Next week I am moderating a colloquium Q&A with a philosophical theologian who defends the classical doctrine of divine impassibility: the view that God has no passions, is not acted upon by creatures, and cannot undergo emotional change or suffering. She is practiced at parrying the "then God cannot love" objection with some version of the reply that a God who suffers has a before and an after and is moved by something external, and that at that point one is no longer talking about God. Write me a sequence of questions that makes this deflection progressively harder to use, anticipating at least the following moves on her part — treating the biblical language as accommodation to human limitations, locating Christ's suffering in his human nature rather than in his divine nature, redefining divine love as unchanging beneficent willing, and treating passibility as a defect rather than a perfection — and include the follow-up to each. End with one question about what, in her view, could count against divine impassibility: what textual, experiential, or theological pressure would have to be real for her to revise the doctrine rather than reinterpret the data.
