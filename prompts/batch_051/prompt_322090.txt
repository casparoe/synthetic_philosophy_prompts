I'm a PhD student in philosophy of religion, and I've spent the last week stuck on something from a seminar on sacrifice. The Day of Atonement rite in Leviticus has the priest confess the community's sins over a goat, and the goat is sent off into the wilderness carrying them away. What bothers me is that the text doesn't seem to be speaking metaphorically: the people are described as genuinly unburdened afterwards, not just reminded of something. I tried to formalize my worry:

(1) If the rite really works, then the community's guilt is removed by being placed on the goat.
(2) To remove guilt by placing it on something is to transfer that guilt to that thing.
(3) Moral guilt is a relation between an agent and her own act; it is not a thing or a quantity that can be moved from one bearer to another.
(4) If guilt is a relation of that kind, it cannot be transferred.
(5) So the guilt the community has cannot be removed by being placed on the goat, and the rite cannot work as described.

My advisor says the interesting question is whether 'the guilt' in step (1) and 'guilt' in step (3) are the same thing, and whether step (2) is the only way removal could happen. But I keep feeling the argument has to fail somewhere, because the ritual clearly does something for the people involved. Is the argument valid? If not, which step exactly fails, and can the argument be patched to show the rite is incoherent? If it is valid, what is the strongest way for a defender of the rite to reinterpret it without conceding it is 'just' metaphorical?
