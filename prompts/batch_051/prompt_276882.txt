I'm preparing a short seminar on philosophies of education, and I want to pair John Locke's Some Thoughts Concerning Education with Peter Weir's Dead Poets Society. I'm struck by how much of Keating's classroom practice seems to echo Locke's advice: he treats the boys as capable of reasoning, tries to make literature an object of delight rather than rote assessment, and appeals to their curiosity, energy, and desire for freedom rather than to fear. But Locke also insists that a tutor's authority is indispensable, that children must acquire habits of self-denial before they can safely enjoy larger liberty, and that virtue, rather than intellectual brilliance or self-expression, is the chief end of education. Those are precisely the points where Keating's approach becomes most unstable, or where the film's institution fails him. I'm trying to determine what the film ultimately shows about a Lockean education. Does Dead Poets Society function as a vindication of Locke's child-centered methods, as a critique of them, or as a demonstration that Locke's approach only succeeds under conditions that Welton Academy never provides? Put another way, how would Locke's distinction between liberty and license apply to the film's final scenes, and which specific scenes or choices would you teach to make that comparison philosophically precise rather than merely thematic?
