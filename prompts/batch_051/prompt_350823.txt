I am an undergraduate taking a course in formal semantics and, in the same term, a course in knowledge representation and reasoning, and a parallel between the two has been nagging at me. In semantics we have been working on generics: bare plural statements such as *ducks lay eggs* and *mosquitoes carry malaria*. These are felt to be true despite tolerating exceptions in a strange way: the first holds even though only mature female ducks ever lay eggs, and the second holds even though only a small minority of mosquitoes actually carry the parasite. In the knowledge representation course we have been studying non-monotonic reasoning, in particular default rules of the form *birds fly*, which license the inference that a given bird flies unless something defeating is known, such as that the bird is a penguin. The resemblance is obvious: generics and defaults both support conclusions that go beyond what the evidence strictly guarantees, and both survive exceptions without simply being false. Yet the two seem to come apart precisely at the cases that interest me. Standard default rules are usually understood against conditions of majority or normalcy — the conclusion applies to typical or normal members of the class — whereas *ducks lay eggs* is accepted as true even though the property belongs only to a minority subclass of ducks. I cannot decide whether this is a deep structural identity (generics as a natural-language form of non-monotonic reasoning, with the minority cases as pressure on any adequate semantics) or a superficial analogy that distracts from what is distinctive about generics — for instance, that they may not quantify over individual ducks at all.

Could you help me settle whether the parallel is deep or superficial? I would like you to lay out the mapping between generics and default rules in both directions, say what would have to be true of both frameworks for results in one to transfer to the other, and name at least one thing the parallel predicts that turns out false once you look closely at either generics or default logic. What I most want is your judgment about whether the points where the analogy breaks are informative: do they reveal something important about generics, about defaults, or just that the parallel is loose and I should stop worrying about it?
