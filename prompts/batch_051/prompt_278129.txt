Next term I'm giving an invited seminar paper defending a qualified version of Fukuyama's end-of-history thesis: the claim that the end of history concerns ideological legitimation rather than the cessation of events, and that liberal democracy plus market capitalism remain the only models of modern political order capable of commanding belief as universal aspirations, while authoritarian-capitalist, populist, and religious-revivalist challengers are best understood as negatively defined by, or parasitic on, the liberal order they denounce rather than as workable rival futures. The audience is a political science department whose core is comparativists studying authoritarian durability and state capitalism, international-relations scholars of a realist bent, and several historically minded political theorists suspicious of teleology; several people in the room work on contemporary China, populism, or political Islam, and they will read the thesis both as an empirical claim about the post-1989 world and as a normative claim about what deserves to win. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the six to eight objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way a comparativist or realist would put them, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is weakest. Tell me what to concede at the outset, which part of the thesis not to retreat from even if it costs me the room, and include the question I should most hope nobody asks.
