I'm on my university's academic integrity board, and we're staring down a case that's split us down the middle, so I'd like a second opinion from someone who's thought hard about punishment. You're a philosopher who specializes in the ethics of punishment and has advised academic and professional bodies before; please think carefully and draft an actual policy memo I could circulate to the rest of the board.

The case: a senior with an otherwise clean record submitted a final seminar paper in which two long paragraphs closely paraphrase from a published article, with no citation anywhere. When called in, she didn't deny it, but says she pasted the passages into her notes months ago, forgot they weren't her own wording, and never meant to pass them off as hers. The professor filed a misconduct report and wants an automatic F in the course plus a permanant notation on her transcript. One faction on the board agrees: the rule was clear, the copying is plain, and an offense deserves a penalty that matches it — not a warning and a redo. Another faction wants her to rewrite the paper under supervision with no transcript entry, since the point of the process is to teach and protect the community, not to wreck someone's future over what looks like careless note-taking.

We need a deliverable the board can actually vote on: a clear recommendation for this case (what outcome should we impose?) plus the reasoning behind it, and a short set of criteria we can reuse when the facts are murkier. Please engage the strongest argument for the side you don't recommend and treat it honestly, don't strawman it. Do the retributivist-versus-consequentialist thing openly: say what each framework would make of this case and where you land between or beyond them. One board member keeps invoking something she calls negative retributivism — the idea that desert sets upper and lower bounds on acceptable punishment, but consequences pick within them — so tell us whether that's actually workable here. Start by brainstorming the considerations that should drive the decision, then narrow to your recommendation.
