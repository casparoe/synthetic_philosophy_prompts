I am a retired physical chemist who spent my career fitting quantitative models to experimental data—kinetics, spectroscopy, thermodynamics—and who has only recently begun reading philosophy of science seriously. I keep noticing that when philosophers argue about laws of nature, each side seems to be leaning on an implicit picture of what a scientific theory does, and I want to see how much of the disagreement survives translation into the vocabulary I actually trust.

Take the debate between Humean and anti-Humean accounts of laws of nature. As I understand it, the Humean holds that the laws are nothing over and above the total distribution of particular facts: a law is, roughly, an axiom or theorem of the most economical and informative systematization of everything that actually happens—something like an optimal data-compression of the entire history of the universe. The anti-Humean holds that this reverses the explanatory order: regularities obtain because laws govern, produce, or necessitate them, and laws do their work by supporting counterfactuals about systems that never in fact occur, not merely by summarizing what does occur.

Restate each position in the vocabulary I am fluent in: experimental data and the problem of fitting a model class to a finite or indefinitely extended data stream; the distinction between a trajectory through phase space and the dynamical equations that generate it; the difference between a symmetry or conservation law that genuinely constrains possible evolutions and a merely observed invariance that might, for all the data say, be a cosmic coincidence; the role of a Hamiltonian or evolution operator; and the practice of extrapolating a fitted model to initial or boundary conditions that were never measured. Take the two positions one at a time, and for each component tell me whether it translates cleanly, translates only with strain, or has no expression at all in this vocabulary.

Then give me the residue report. My suspicion is that the Humean position will translate almost too well into curve-fitting and model selection, while the anti-Humean's additional ingredient—that laws govern rather than describe—will have no native expression, or will appear only as a rough heuristic about how confidently one may extrapolate a fitted model beyond the measured region. But I am suspicious of that conclusion, because my own vocabulary may have been built to be Humean from the start: a phase-space trajectory already presumes a distinguished set of dynamical variables, and the very distinction between a symmetry and a cosmic coincidence presumes facts about what would have happened under other contingencies. So tell me honestly where the residue lies. Is it a defect of my scientific vocabulary, a sign that the anti-Humean claim is less clear than it first appears, or a genuine point of contact that my vocabulary can express after all? And what exactly would a working scientist have to add to this vocabulary before the anti-Humean position could even be stated without distortion?
