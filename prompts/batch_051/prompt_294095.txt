I have been trying to get to the bottom of an asymmetry in how ordinary judgement and anti-discrimination law treat different grounds of selection, and I would like the strongest case for the distinction, or an honest conclusion that it cannot be defended.

An employer who refuses to hire someone because of her race, sex, religion, or age is generally regarded as having done something seriously wrongful and, in many jurisdictions, unlawful. An employer who refuses to hire someone because she has a strong regional accent, is left-handed, is short, has a weak handshake, or supports a rival football club is usually regarded as exercising ordinary managerial discretion, even when the trait is irrelevant to the work and the decision rests on little more than a rough stereotype. I want to know whether the line between these two columns tracks anything principled rather than a historically path-dependent list.

The familiar candidate criteria strike me as having been built backwards from the list. Irrelevance to the job would make nearly every ground of selection suspect, including taste in music or the school one attended. Immutability would include left-handedness and height but exclude religion and much of age, both of which are protected. Historical subordination would cover many protected grounds but not, straightforwardly, age or religion; and it would force the question why a newly stigmatised group should have to wait for the list to catch up. Choice will not do either, since religion is chosen and protected while handedness is not chosen and unprotected.

So my question is this: is there a defensible criterion, or combination of criteria, that draws the boundary in roughly the right place and that also decides hard cases rather than merely gesturing at them? The hard cases I have in mind are left-handedness, obesity, a heavy regional or foreign accent, a surname strongly associated with a particular ethnic or national group, political affiliation, smoking status, and being unusually good-looking. If no such criterion can be specified, should we conclude that the distinction between suspect and non-suspect grounds is itself unjustified, and that the law should instead impose something like a general requirement that selection turn only on traits shown to be relevant to the task? I would rather be given a criterion that does real work than a list I have merely inherited.
