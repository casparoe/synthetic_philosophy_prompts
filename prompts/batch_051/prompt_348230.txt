I'm preparing a unit for an upper-level seminar in philosophy of mind and film on the question of which computations matter morally. I want to use Ridley Scott's Blade Runner (1982) not as one more discussion of whether a machine could be a person, but as a way into two harder, less-discussed problems. Let me set out some terminology before posing the question.

By the attribution problem I mean this: a single physical system — a body, a brain, a server farm — can in principle be given indefinitely many different computational descriptions, depending on which states one treats as inputs and outputs, which level of grain one selects, and which physical features one takes to be representational. If moral patienthood is meant to track the presence of the right kind of computation, then we owe an account of which of these computational descriptions picks out the computation that matters. By the boundary problem I mean the question of where the morally relevant computation begins and ends: within a single process, across a whole organism, across an organism embedded in its environment, or across some socially extended system.

The film offers a rich test case for both. The Nexus-6 replicants are organic, genetically engineered, and emotionally expressive, yet are treated as non-persons; the Voight-Kampff test purports to detect the morally relevant property — empathic response — through measurable physiological proxies under timed questioning. Please write a critical reading of the film through these two problems, addressing at least the following. (1) Where does the film dramatize the gap between the test's measurable proxies and the underlying capacity those proxies are supposed to track? Consider Rachel, whose implanted memories make the attribution of her responses especially unstable, and Roy's final scenes, in which he appears to exhibit exactly the kind of inner life the test is designed to register, or perhaps a kind the test cannot register at all. (2) Does the film supply any principled way to draw the boundary of the morally relevant computation, or does it systematically undermine each candidate boundary — species membership, biological substrate, the possession of genuine as opposed to implanted memories, the capacity for fear or mercy? (3) Where does the film resist a cleanly functionalist reading, for instance by making the replicants organic rather than silicon, or by making their exclusion largely a matter of legal and economic arrangements rather than of any computational difference?

I want a reading that is faithful in both directions: it should not reduce the film to an illustration of positions in philosophy of mind, and it should not distort the attribution or boundary problems to fit the plot. It may conclude that the film contributes something to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not — for example, by suggesting that the attribution problem is not merely epistemic, or that the boundary of the morally relevant computation is drawn in practice by institutions rather than discovered by inspecting the system. Assume the film's plot is common ground; I am not asking for quotations from the screenplay or the novel, and I am not asking for a bibliography.
