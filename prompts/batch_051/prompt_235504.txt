I'm a PhD student working on decision theory, and I keep getting stuck on something that looks like a weird asymmetry in how I treat my own choices versus other people's. If a friend is torn about whether to leave academia, I'm perfectly happy to put a number on it — I'll say I'd give it 70% she stays, and if I'm wrong that's just a bad prediction. If I'm the one deciding whether to leave, and I have the same kind of evidence about my own inclinations, it feels impossible to assign a probablity to my choice while I'm deciding. Not just hard: if I do manage it, it feels like I've stopped deliberating and started forecasting some guy who happens to be me. Meanwhile, I can do it easily with my own past choices and even with future choices if I step outside them — looking at my calendar for next month, I think "yeah, probably 80% I cave and go to that conference." But while I'm actually deciding, the 80% seems unavailable in a different way.

Do you think there's something that justifies this asymmetry, or is it just a psychological quirk — maybe a confusion between belief and intention, or a failure of nerve about acting under uncertainty? I want the strongest principle you can give me that either vindicates the asymmetry or says it should be dropped, and I want you to test it against cases where it gets strained: someone with a perfect predictive model of my choices tells me mid-deliberation what I'll do, or a case where I've in fact already decided but haven't admitted it to myself. Is the rule "don't assign credences to your own present choices" defensible, or should a fully coherent decision maker be able to do it and still act?
