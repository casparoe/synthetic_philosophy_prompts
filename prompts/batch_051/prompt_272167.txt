Here are the views I currently hold.

1. Loyalty is a virtue in its own right. A person who calmly trades away a loyal friendship or institutional commitment whenever a slightly better option appears is missing something of moral importance, even if everyone ends up at least as well off and no promise is broken.

2. Betrayal is wrong in a distinctive way, not fully explained by the harm it causes or the expectations it frustrates. At its core, betrayal is the violation of the trust that partly constitutes a relationship, and this wrongness remains even when the betrayal leaves everyone better off on balance.

3. Whistleblowing is sometimes not only permissible but required. Where there is serious, ongoing wrongdoing—say, fraud that endangers patients or clients—and internal channels are blocked, the fact that the wrongdoer is a close friend or family member does not release one from the obligation to report it.

4. Reporting a close friend's serious wrongdoing in such a case is nonetheless a betrayal of that friend, even when it is the all-things-considered right thing to do. The required act still damages something real, and a good person does it with a heavy heart and residual regret.

5. A wholly right act done for the right reasons leaves no moral remainder that one should feel bad about. If you did exactly what duty required, then guilt, self-reproach, or compunction after the fact is a mistake, not a sign of moral sensitivity.

6. Loyalty to a person is ultimately a matter of caring for that person's genuine good—roughly, what they would want if they were well informed and not corrupted. Because of this, exposing a friend's destructive wrongdoing can itself be an expression of loyalty to the friend, rather than an act against them.
