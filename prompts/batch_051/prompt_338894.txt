I am a novelist writing a scene in which a philologist character argues with a younger scholar about what my character calls the fallacy of original meaning. To make the scene intellectually serious rather than merely theatrical, I want to understand a particular passage from Nietzsche's On the Genealogy of Morality that I remember only in paraphrase, and I would prefer that you work from my paraphrase rather than from a possibly inaccurate quotation. The passage I have in mind is from the First Essay. As I recall it, Nietzsche observes that in many languages the words that came to mean good originated in terms designating the noble, the powerful, the high-ranking, or the privileged, while the words that came to mean bad originated in terms designating the simple, the common, the low, or the base. He then appears to treat this as evidence about how moral valuation actually arose: the good was first a self-designation of an aristocratic caste, the bad was their name for those beneath them, and the later moral opposition of good and evil was a kind of inversion of that earlier distinction. The part I need help understanding is the exact logical status of the etymological evidence in that passage. Is Nietzsche committing the very fallacy my character warns against, namely treating a words oldest recorded sense as if it revealed something authoritative about the concept? Or is he using original meanings only as traces of a historical event, namely who first applied the terms good and bad, and to whom? How does one distinguish those two uses, and does the strength of his conclusion depend on assuming that the earliest sense of a word is in some way privileged? I would like the answer in American English.
