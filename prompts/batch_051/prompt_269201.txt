For a feature I am writing about how philosophers actually argue, I want to understand why the debate over what thought experiments do in philosophy has remained so difficult to close. Three answers have coexisted for a long time. According to the first, the considered judgment that a well-designed case elicits is evidence: it counts for or against a general claim much as a carefully designed observation would, and the burden falls on anyone who rejects the judgment to explain it away. According to the second, thought experiments are only illustrations: they carry no evidential weight of their own and merely make vivid a conclusion that has to be secured by argument or by other means. According to the third, they are intuition pumps: they do not straightforwardly argue for or confirm anything, but reorder the reader's sense of what is possible, natural, or absurd, and they can push in either direction. My working hypothesis is that these are not three claims about a single object but three claims generalized from different samples of cases: the evidence view from cases where nearly everyone shares the judgment, the illustration view from cases where the narrative is clearly doing no argumentative work, and the intuition-pump view from contested cases in ethics and personal identity. What I want is a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a verdict on thought experiments themselves. Is it largely verbal, is it a substantive dispute about what can count as evidence in philosophy, or are the parties answering different questions under one heading? And given the diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a taxonomy of cases, a constraint on how much work a narrative may do, a better-posed question — or should the impasse be expected to persist, and why?
