I sit on my denomination's doctrinal commission. Our confession, adopted in 1911 and never amended, contains this article:

"The guilt of our first parents' transgression is conveyed to all their posterity by ordinary generation, so that every human being born in that manner is conceived and born in sin; and none born in that manner is freed from this guilt by the piety or baptism of his parents."

A member of our congregation, a reproductive biologist, has a daughter produced by somatic-cell nuclear transfer: the nucleus of one of the member's own cells was placed into an enucleated donor ovum, and the resulting embryo was gestated to term. There was no father, no union of gametes, and no sexual generation of any kind; the child is a human infant, genetically near-identical to the member. The commission must rule on what this article says about the child's condition with respect to the inherited guilt of the first transgression. We are deliberately not adjudicating whether any reproductive technology is licit, and we do not need to settle the wider metaphysics of the soul; we need an interpretation of our own confession in a case its authors never foresaw.

Please write the ruling in the following sections, in this order, with the indicated approximate lengths:

1. **The text** (80–120 words). Restate the article and the case, and isolate the terms that do the work: posterity, ordinary generation, and the clause about piety or baptism.
2. **The plain reading** (180–220 words). Apply the words in their natural 1911 sense. What does the article, read literally, require or imply about this child? Be precise about whether the article's language gives a necessary or only a sufficient condition for inheriting the guilt, and flag any ambiguity the text itself leaves.
3. **The purposive reading** (180–220 words). Reconstruct what the drafters meant to secure by attaching inherited guilt to ordinary generation — perhaps guarding the doctrine of the Fall, or excluding unusual cases and miracles — and assess whether applying the article to a cloned child advances or defeats that purpose.
4. **What the drafters would have said** (120–160 words). If the 1911 commission had confronted a case of a human being produced without ordinary generation, what would they most likely have decided, given their theological commitments? State your inference and its main weakness.
5. **My judgment** (100–150 words). Say which approach should govern the commission's ruling, what the ruling should be, and what your answer implies about why inherited guilt is tied to generation at all.
