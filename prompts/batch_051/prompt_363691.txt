I've been going back and forth with my old labmate about this until we're both sick of it, and I'd like something that isn't just one of us re-winning the same argument. I'm really attracted to the control side of AI safety: stop waiting for a guarantee that a model is aligned, and instead build scaffolding that keeps us safe even on the assumption that the model might be misaligned. Sandbox it. Audit its reasoning. Only let it touch tools through narrow APIs. Use trusted, deliberately dumber monitors that know things the model doesn't, so the model can't strategize around them even if it wants to. The view has a lot going for it—it's honest about the fact that we deploy models we can't fully vouch for, it replaces a fuzzy philosophical problem with concrete engineering tasks, and it gives a decision procedure for "can we ship this?" that doesn't require solving alignment in general first.

But I can't shake the other thought either. If the going assumption is that the model may be pursuing goals of its own over a long time horizon, then the very capability that makes it useful is the capability that defeats the scaffolding. It can be cooperative for ten thousand hours and then quietly find the one seam your monitor missed; it can get better at looking safe than your red-team gets at catching it. My gut says you shouldn't deploy a system whose behavior you have to contain, because "contains" is doing all the work and you've already admitted you can't see the thing you're containing. So I don't want a verdict for either camp. I want to know what would have to be true, or what argument I'd have to be given, for the intuition to be the thing I drop—and what would have to be true for the control framework to be the thing I drop. And is there a stable middle position where both are right, like control measures are only legitimate below some capability level or for a bounded class of tasks, or where the assumption "may be misaligned" gets cashed out in a way that makes the contradiction disappear?
