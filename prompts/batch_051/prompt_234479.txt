I am a novelist drafting a book in which the judgments of one character, a retired physician named Mara, slowly reveal a philosophy of what makes a human life good. Her verdicts so far, spread across four scenes, are these.

First, a woman enters a machine that will give her, for the rest of her life, the flawless illusion of being a celebrated architect with a beloved husband, children, and devoted friends. Outside the machine she lies motionless in a tank; none of the buildings, the husband, the children, or the friendships are real. Mara says this life is worth much less than the life it simulates, even though, from the inside, the two lives would feel indistinguishable. She does not call it worthless.

Second, a mathematician works alone on a problem he has set himself, indifferent to recognition, and solves it; the proof is correct, elegant, and new. He writes it in a notebook and dies in an accident a week later, before anyone else reads it. Mara says the proof added something real to the value of his life, and not merely because the work felt satisfying to him.

Third, a man is married for four decades to a woman who, without his ever suspecting it, does not love him. He wants to be genuinely loved, not merely to believe he is loved. She stays because the household is comfortable and because performing the part of a devoted wife is easy for her; she is, in effect, acting. The man experiences the marriage as intimate and loving. Mara says the marriage contributed far less to the value of his life than he supposed, and that what it seemed to add was largely not there, though the man could not tell the difference.

Fourth, a retired clerk fills his days with newspaper puzzles, television, and quiet walks, and wants nothing more than what he has. Mara refuses to call his life worthless; it has, she says, some modest worth. But she will not say it is as good as the mathematician's life, and she is unsure whether the difference is one of degree or of kind.

In the scene I am about to write, Mara must judge the life of a translator of real ability. Because of debts and a lack of alternatives, the translator spends the central decades of her life producing a definitive edition of the notebooks of a minor poet she privately considers overrated. She does the work only because it is the least bad option open to her; she never comes to want it, never takes satisfaction in it, and dies still wishing she had written her own poems instead. The edition is nonetheless a masterpiece of its kind — accurate, intelligent, durable — and it has already changed how the poet is read. Mara has to say whether that edition added value to the translator's life, or whether an achievement that was neither wanted nor enjoyed is no benefit to the person who achieved it.

I think there are at least two coherent rules that fit the four earlier verdicts, and I designed the translator case to force them apart. What are the candidate rules? State what each rule predicts about the translator case, and which rule is the best reconstruction of the character I have been writing; then give me your best prediction of Mara's verdict, with your reasons. If the translator case still leaves a deeper ambiguity about whether Mara's values are many things or one thing in disguise, tell me the further case I should write next to settle that.
