A colleague in our reading group has become convinced that inferring an optimizer's objective and search space from its behavior is epistemically hopeless, and I want a careful diagnosis of where his argument goes wrong. Here is the argument, numbered as he laid it out.

(1) An optimizer is only ever observed as a finite record of behavior: a sequence of choices, outputs, or moves through some space. What is hidden is the pair we want to infer — the objective function being optimized and the search space, meaning the set of candidates the procedure can visit together with the update rule by which it moves through them.

(2) Any finite record can be rationalized by many objectives. For any finite sequence of observed outputs x1, ..., xn, take L(x) = min_i d(x, xi) for some distance d; the observed outputs are then exact global minimizers, and the loss can be smoothed without losing that property. So a finite record never uniquely fixes an objective.

(3) The search space is, if anything, less fixed than the objective. The same observed sequence of moves can be redescribed as gradient descent on one loss in one coordinate system, as gradient descent on a different loss after a change of coordinates or preconditioner, as projected steps toward a constraint set, or as a nongradient update rule that happens to visit the same points. These rival descriptions agree on the record and diverge on unobserved inputs.

(4) Where candidates agree on everything observed and diverge only on the unobserved, the observed record cannot discriminate among them. By (2) and (3), any record leaves an infinite family of objective-plus-search-space rivals exactly as consistent with the record as the true one.

(5) So the evidence never genuinely selects among such attributions. And if the evidence never selects, then preferring one attribution over another can only be a matter of our taste for short or natural descriptions — not a discovery about the system.

(6) But this proves too much. We do make such attributions, and they pay rent: we distinguish a policy that is maximizing engagement from one that is executing a brittle input-output mapping, and we then make correct predictions under distribution shift — the first keeps finding longer sessions when the catalog changes, the second collapses. If attributions of objective and search space were mere projections of taste, they should not carry that predictive force.

The argument therefore cannot be right as it stands. I am most inclined to keep (2) and (3), since they look like mathematics, and (6), since surrendering it licenses a skepticism too wide for anyone to live with. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, say what exactly survives of the argument once the rejected step is removed.

Please keep the diagnosis to at most 600 words.
