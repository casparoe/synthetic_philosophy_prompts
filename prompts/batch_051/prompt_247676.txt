I have been arguing with two friends about complicity, and we keep using the word as though it named a single relation when it may be doing several different jobs at once. We agree at the extremes: holding the ladder while someone breaks into a house is complicity, and selling a hammer that a stranger later uses to smash a car window is not. But the middle cases break us apart. I would like you to propose a careful working definition of complicity, one that classifies the cases below the way I do where I have a view and commits to a definite answer where I do not.

The cases. (1) I hold the ladder for a break-in. (2) I sell a hammer to someone I have no reason to suspect, and she later uses it to break a car window. (3) I cast a yes vote on a four-person board resolution authorizing fraudulent accounting; the motion would have carried 3-1 without me, so my individual vote changes nothing. (4) I cook at a restaurant whose owner is laundering money through the books; I know about the laundering, but my work belongs entirely to the legitimate dining side of the business. (5) I laugh loudly at a bigoted joke told at lunch, intending to signal that I am one of the group, though I add nothing to the joke. (6) I continue renting from a landlord after learning he is harassing other tenants, because moving would be costly. (7) I keep shares in a company after it is revealed to rely on forced labor upstream, because selling is inconvenient and my holding is small. (8) A friend confesses a serious crime to me, and I neither help him nor report it.

My own intuitive verdicts: clearly complicit in (1) and (3); clearly not in (2); leaning not in (4) and (8); leaning yes in (5); genuinely undecided in (6) and (7). What I want is the principle behind the verdicts. I suspect the term tracks something closer to participating in or ratifying a wrong than to causing or even enabling it—the board vote and the laugh are the cases pulling me there, since neither makes a causal difference. Run your definition against all eight, say where it gives a wrong answer or no answer, and either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. If you conclude that ordinary complicity does two jobs, give me both definitions and say which cases move between them.
