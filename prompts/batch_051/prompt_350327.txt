I am building a taxonomy of the formal responses that decision theory has developed for the Ellsberg paradox and for ambiguity-averse choice more generally — the ways theorists have modified, constrained, or abandoned Savage's subjective expected utility so that Ellsberg's two-urn choices come out as coherent rather than as mere errors. So far I have six entries, each of which locates the departure from Savage at a different point:

(1) Choquet expected utility, where beliefs are represented by a non-additive capacity rather than an additive probability measure, and expectation is taken with a Choquet integral, so that the weights assigned to complementary events need not sum to one.

(2) Maxmin expected utility, where beliefs are a set of priors and each act is evaluated by the minimum expected utility over that set, so ambiguity aversion is built into the decision rule itself.

(3) The α-maxmin criterion, where an act is evaluated by an α-weighted average of the best-case and worst-case expected utilities over a set of priors, with α encoding the decision maker's attitude toward ambiguity.

(4) Variational preferences, where each prior is assigned a convex cost and the act is evaluated by minimizing over priors the sum of expected utility and that cost; maxmin and multiplier preferences fall out as special cases.

(5) The smooth ambiguity model, where the decision maker has a second-order probability distribution over the set of priors and applies a concave transformation to the resulting expected utilities before averaging, thereby separating ambiguity (the spread of the second-order distribution) from ambiguity attitude (the concavity).

(6) Source-dependent probability weighting, where decision weights are nonlinear and differ according to the source of uncertainty, so that the ambiguous urn is weighted differently from a familiar stochastic device without invoking a set of priors at all.

I have the strong sense that at least one further well-established approach is missing from this list — one that is not a restatement, notational variant, special case, or hybrid of the six above, but that represents the ambiguous decision problem in a genuinely different way. What is it? Please describe how the missing approach represents the situation, how it reproduces the standard Ellsberg choices, and where it sits in relation to the six entries on my list — in particular, what it predicts or assumes that they do not, and whether it is better thought of as a rival to them or as a response to a problem they leave unaddressed.
