Hey, I'm giving a talk at my hospital's psychiatry grand rounds next month. The thesis I'm defending is that delusions are real beliefs — doxasticism, not the line that they're just imaginings or empty avowals — and that the two-factor theory is the best account, at least for monothematic delusions like Capgras or Cotard. My argument is basically: an anomalous experience supplies the content of the delusion, and a second factor (a deficit in belief evaluation) is what turns a strange experience into a fixed belief instead of a passing weird thought. I'm going to defend this mainly for monothematic cases and admit up front that polythematic, bizarre delusions are messier.

The room matters a lot here. It's not a philosophy audience. It's mostly psychiatrists and clinical psychologists. A chunk of them are old-school and think delusions are by definition un-understandable and radically discontinuous with ordinary belief, so they'll hate the claim that a delusion is just a belief like any other. A few are CBT-for-psychosis people who think delusions are on a continuum with normal beliefs, but they mostly care about what therapy can change rather than what's literally true. And there's one neuropsychologist who works on Capgras and will know the experimental literature better then I do.

Draft me a Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this crew, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way these people would actually say them. For each one give me a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. And include the question I'm really hoping nobody asks.
