I've been fiddling with a knot in copyleft licensing and want to turn it into something readable. The question is: where does one program genuinely end and another begin when they share a given body of code? Most people rush to the legal language, but I think there's an under-explored conceptual point about compositional boundaries.

The part of GPLv3 I keep staring at is in section 5, where it says that a compilation of a covered work with other separate and independent works, which are not by their nature extensions of the covered work, and which are not combined with it such as to form a larger program, is an "aggregate" if the compilation and its accompanying copyright notices are not limited to the covered work. It then says the licence's terms do not apply to the separate works in that aggregate. I'm not a lawyer; my instinct is that concepts like "larger program," "extension," and "combined such as to form" are doing far more conceptual work than people admit. In particular, what marks the difference between "separate and independent works" and a "larger program" is, I suspect, not a purely technical relation.

I want you to write a short research proposal, maybe two or three pages, on the question: is the boundary between separate works and a larger program best understood as a function of opacity, intentional design, functional integration, or something else? My half-formed thought is that none of these standard candidates works alone. Opacity is too permissive, because two programs can be arbitrarily obfuscated at an interface. Functional integration overreaches, because a standard OS process can be deeply coupled to a GPL-covered text editor via a shell pipe, yet we don't usually treat that as one larger program. Intentional design is too psychologically fragile. I want a proposal that lays out the problem, identifies the hidden conceptual criterion, and makes progress on a more satisfying account. Don't give a bibliography, I'll sort that. And be honest about what would count as failure.
