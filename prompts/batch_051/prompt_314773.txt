I'm a novelist, and I'm deep in a book where I can't get the psychology of envy right. I've been reading around in political philosophy, which isn't my usual thing, and Rawls has this treatment in A Theory of Justice where envy is mostly a destructive vice, but then he says some envy is excusable when it's basically a reaction to losing your self-respect under conditions that are themselves unjust. I keep thinking this should change how writers draw a resentful character: the same resentment can be a petty personal flaw or a accurate read of an unfair situation, and often it's both at once. I run a writing workshop, 10-12 people, and I'd like a 90 minute session, not a lecture, that makes the group feel the difference between envy-as-vice and envy-as-signal in their own reactions before I ever name Rawls. Can you design a concrete, runnable activity — a role-play, a small-stakes game, a forced-choice scenario, whatever works — with rules, roles, pacing, payoffs, and a debrief afterward? The debrief should map moments in the activity onto the philosophical idea, warn me what will predictably go wrong when I run it, and be honest about which part of the idea the activity can't reproduce so I have to supply it myself. They should leave seeing that calling someone just jealous can be a cheap way to discredit their perception of injustice, while also seeing that envy genuinely distorts. Please think like a professional philosopher who also designs workshops; start by brainstorming two or three possible mechanics before you settle on one.
