I host a podcast on collective decision-making and legal philosophy. Next week's guest is a theorist — treat him as an unnamed defender of the view rather than any particular living person — who defends the premise-based procedure (PBP) as the correct response to the discursive dilemma. On his account, when a multi-member body such as an appellate court or a deliberative assembly must form judgments on a set of logically connected propositions, it should vote directly on the premises and let the conclusion follow logically from the majority-endorsed premises, rather than hold a separate vote on the conclusion itself.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having him restate the view. Press the view where the literature presses it, and give each question a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. Do not present the following as a mechanical checklist; work them into probing questions.

Include at least one question on each of these lines of attack:

(1) The objection that PBP can output a collective conclusion no individual member accepts — a majority for p, a majority for q, but only a minority for p and q together. Ask why this should count as a genuinely reasoned group judgment rather than an aggregation artifact, and press on whether collective rationality should require consistency alone or also closure of group attitudes under conjunction.

(2) Richer agendas. Ask how the defense fares when the agenda includes conditional premises (a modus ponens structure such as "if p then q; p; therefore q"), negations, or multiple independent routes to the same conclusion. Ask what his preferred reply is to the List–Pettit-style impossibility result over universal domain, collective rationality, independence (or systematicity), anonymity, and non-dictatorship — and which of those conditions he would relax or reject and why.

(3) Strategic behavior. Agents may vote insincerely on premises to shift the derived conclusion toward their own view, or reframe the division of an issue to alter later outcomes. Ask how PBP can be defended against insincere voting and agenda manipulation, and whether he thinks some vulnerability to manipulation is intrinsic to any non-dictatorial aggregation.

(4) The epistemic comparison. Ask under what conditions a majority is more likely to be correct about premises than about the upshot, how this trades on voter competence and correlation in the manner of Condorcet-jury-theorem arguments, and whether PBP actually does better epistemically than a direct vote on the conclusion once these assumptions are made explicit.

(5) Legal and democratic legitimacy. An appellate court using PBP could issue a judgment that a majority of the panel would reject if it voted on the outcome itself. Ask whether that compromises adjudicative or democratic legitimacy, and whether the requirement that courts give reasoned decisions rescues PBP or deepens the problem.

Where suitable, work in one question that forces him to handle a hard case for the view, such as a stratified decision in which sub-conclusions must themselves be aggregated before the final conclusion, or a common-law case in which the relevant "premises" are contested principles that only jointly entail the rule of the case.

Please write the questions and follow-ups in American English.
