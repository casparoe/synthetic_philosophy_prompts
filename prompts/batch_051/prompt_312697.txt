I help run a small philanthropic fund — on the order of eight figures a year, a staff of two, a board that meets quarterly — and our niche is high-variance, long-shot projects where the median outcome is zero but a success would be worth many times the grant. We keep facing the same decision: how much of the annual portfolio should any one proposal receive when the expected value is positive but the estimated probability of success is a guess built on a tiny, selected reference class? Our current practice is to fund the things we are most excited about at whatever size feels serious, and I have come to suspect this is intuition dressed up as allocation. I want a reusable grant-sizing procedure, grounded in Kelly betting and geometric rationality, that takes an estimated success probability, an estimated upside, and a project's correlation with the rest of the portfolio, and returns a fraction of the annual budget. It should be something a non-specialist can run in an afternoon with rough numbers, and it should say explicitly what to do when the numbers cannot be had. I want the procedure to be honest about the places where it is a judgment call wearing a formula, especially the choice of log utility, the reference class behind the success-rate estimate, the handling of returns whose impact does not actually compound, and whether a noisy edge should force a fractional-Kelly shrink. Then run it on three proposed grants: one claiming a five percent chance of a hundred-to-one return that would consume forty percent of the annual budget; one near-certain project with modest impact that the board favors for reputational reasons; and a pair of long-shot projects that fail together under the same external event, so that treating them as independent would double-count one risk. Walk through the questions in the order a grant officer would actually ask them, and mark where the procedure stops with a no or hands the decision back to a human. I expect it to tell us to do some things we will not like, so do not soften those. Finish with a plain statement of what the procedure cannot settle. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you would write an email or a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
