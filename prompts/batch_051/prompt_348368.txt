As a retired ecologist who came to philosophy late, I keep running into a claim that strikes me as half scientific and half conceptual: that the destruction of a habitat is on balance bad for the animals themselves, because an intact ecosystem has higher net welfare than the degraded system that replaces it. The competing claim is that an intact habitat is a machine for producing enormous numbers of short, painful, parasite-laden lives, and that removing or simplifying it might reduce total suffering even while it offends conservationist values. I am not asking which side is right. I am asking how one would go about testing the claim that an intact system has higher net animal welfare than its degraded replacement—and what any such test would actually establish.

Design the test concretely. What would you measure (mortality schedules, energy expenditure, stress physiology, parasite load, something else), in what kind of system, over what timescale, and how would you aggregate across individuals and species? What result would count for the claim, and what result would count against it? Then be precise about the residue: does moving from measurements to welfare require a theory of well-being that the field data cannot supply? Does aggregating the welfare of a mouse and a hawk require a weighting choice that is moral rather than empirical? Does a positive result show that habitat destruction reduces suffering, or only that it changes the demographic facts? I suspect the claim is testable in a weak sense but that the most interesting part of it is not empirical at all. Confirm or correct that, and say exactly where the testable part ends and the conceptual part begins.
