i've been playing with an idea i call franchise sovereignty, and i want someone to kick its tires. the claim is that we talk about the state as if it owns sovereignty the way you own a backpack - one thing, indivisible, you either have it or you don't. but actual states keep lisencing out the pieces. panama lets a cargo ship it will never inspect fly its flag and call itself panamanian for a fee. a gulf state sets aside a district where foreign judges run commercial courts under english law and the state's own courts stay out, while it still insists the district is fully its territory. a government contracts a private company to run its visa interviews, or a foreign firm to run a port and its security. my theory: sovereignty was never one thing, it's a bundle of separately licensable functions - courts, tax, defense, registration, recognition - and a state is less like an owner of territory and more like a franchisor. it owns the brand (the flag, the passport, the name) and the right to revoke the franchise, and it constantly subcontracts the day-to-day bits. on this view a 1600s charter company and a modern special economic zone are the same object with different licensing terms. i realize this could be a metaphor doing all the work. so: steelman it first, then tell me whether it's a real theory or just a word game. is it already out there under a name i should know? and hit the hard cases - actual violence, treaties, secession, a franchisee that gets more popular than the franchisor - and tell me where the theory makes a false prediction or quietly changes the subject. be specific about which bits of sovereignty refuse to be put in the bundle.
