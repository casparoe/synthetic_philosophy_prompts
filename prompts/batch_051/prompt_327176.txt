Okay so I keep going in circles on the paradox of fiction, and before I read another theory of it I want the full spec of what a theory has to get right. The basic puzzle: I can feel what sure seems like genuine fear, grief, or indignation about people and events I know perfectly well don't exist, but a lot of what I believe about emotion says an emotion needs its object to be real, or at least believed real. I want the desiderata list for any adequate theory of our emotional responses to fiction. Cover the standard cases (pity for Anna Karenina, dread during a monster movie) but mainly the hard ones: startle at a sudden image, crying at a movie when I already know the ending, being moved by a character I would find repugnant in real life, real-person fiction about actual dead people, the same person being wrecked by one unrealistic scene and bored by a near-identical one elsewhere, and the way a terrible movie can leave me flat while a cheesy one still gets to me. For each requirement give the case or consideration behind it, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are just local prejudices of one approach.

Then tell me which single requirement does the most to rule out the standard takes I keep hearing (the make-believe view, the thought-content view, suspension of disbelief), and whether any pair of requirements can't be jointly met. I would rather have an honest list with a contradiction flagged than a tidy list that ignores one of the cases.
