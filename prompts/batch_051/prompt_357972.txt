Okay here's the thing. A friend keeps explaining Russell's paradox with the barber: a village barber shaves exactly the men who do not shave themselves; ask who shaves the barber, and the rule blows up. His pitch is that Russell's paradox is just the barber with sets: let R be the set of all sets that are not members of themselves; ask whether R is a member of itself, and either answer forces the other. Then he concludes that naive set theory is broken because you can't just define a set by arbitrary predicates — unrestricted comprehension has to go.

Something about this buggs me, and I can't tell whether it's a real disanalogy or just me being pedantic. In the barber case the stable resolution is just that no such barber exists — the rule is incoherent, end of story, the town doesn't collapse. But in the set case unrestricted comprehension actively guarantees that R exists, so "no such set exists" isn't a clean exit; it's already a change of theory, not a resolution within one. Also shaving is a relation between a person and other people, and the barber story quietly imports a lot of town-and-person structure that has no set-theoretic counterpart, whereas self-membership is exactly the weird edge the analogy is supposed to illuminate, not something ordinary it can borrow intuitive support from.

I'd like an actual accounting: map the barber construction onto Russell's construction piece by piece, say where the analogy breaks, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that don't. Does the conclusion — that naive comprehension has to be abandoned or at least restricted to something like separation — survive the analogy, or was the analogy doing all the work? And is there a better analogy for what's actually going on in Russell's paradox, or is the barber the best we get?
