I've been defending the moral equality of combatants in arguments with friends — the idea that a soldier fighting on the unjust side of a war has the same moral permission to kill enemy soldiers as a soldier on the just side, because the rules for conduct in war (jus in bello) are symmetric and dont track the justice of the cause (jus ad bellum). Treat me like you're a professional philosopher working on just war theory. What would count against this view? I'm not just after the standard criticisms — I want a map of its exposure. Which thought experiments, hard cases (say, a well-informed volunteer in an aggressive war, or someone who could have pretty easily found out their side was in the wrong), or commitments elsewhere (ordinary self-defense law thinks aggressors and defenders have very different permissions) should a defender take seriously, and which would they dismiss as changing the subject or relying on knowledge soldiers dont usually have? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say where a committed defender can concede ground without giving up the core, and give an honest assessment of whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if it has, whether that's a real problem for a principle whose main job might be structuring laws and conventions rather than assigning individual blame. Start by brainstorming the full range of possible defeaters before organizing them.
