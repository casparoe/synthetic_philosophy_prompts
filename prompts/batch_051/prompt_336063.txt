I'm a novelist — working on a book about a woman whose grief won't resolve — and a friend who teaches undergrad philosophy sent me a summary of what she says the field has "actually established" about emotions. I can't tell how much of this I'm allowed to build a manuscript on, so I'd like a careful audit of it. Her summary, more or less verbatim, was:

"Look, the consensus in philosophy of emotion now is that emotions are judgments. Being afraid of that dog just is judging that the dog is dangerous — 'dangerous' there is an evaluative appraisal of your situation, not a bare fact. That is the only way to explain why emotions are about things, and it is also why we call them rational or irrational: the emotion inherits its rational standing from the appraisal behind it. The bodily stuff — the heartbeat, the shaking, the heat — is not the emotion itself; it is just what a judgment of importance does to a body once the appraisal is made. The old James–Lange view, where the feeling is the bodily response and the emotion is your perception of what the body is doing, has been pretty well abandoned; people with severely reduced bodily feedback still report full-strength emotional lives, so the feeling simply can't be what the emotion is. For a novelist that means: write emotion as thought. You choose the evaluation a character makes of a scene, and the emotion follows from it. Change the appraisal and you can change any feeling."

Here is my problem. On the one hand, this is exactly how I build characters: I can see that my protagonist's grief depends on how she reads her loss — as abandonment, as punishment, as release. Change the reading and the emotion shifts. On the other hand, the grief I know didn't show up as a thought about a loss. It showed up as a body first — weight on the chest, legs going unreliable, food turning to sand. I dont want to write characters who are just appraisals with a nervous system glued on; I want the reader to feel it in their own body. Also, "consensus" set off an alarm, because I'd vaguely heard there are still live disputes between judgment-type, feeling-type, and perception-type theories.

So: can you go through her summary piece by piece and tell me what a careful defender of the judgment view would actually sign off on, what they'd push back on, what a defender of a feeling-based or perception-based view would say about the parts that wave away James–Lange and bodily feedback, and at the end tell me which parts I can honestly use as craft advice and which parts are the philosophy simplified past the point where it stops being true? No citations or reading list — I need a verdict on the picture, not a syllabus.
