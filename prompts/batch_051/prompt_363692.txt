Malcolm's line on dreams was that the idea of a dream as an experience unfolding during sleep is in trouble because the only evidence anyone ever has is the waking report afterwards; there's no way, even in principle, to check the report against the alleged experience while it's going on. Dennett then gave this a different spin with the cassette theory, where the entire dream—or at least our impression of having had one—could be written into memory all at once at the instant of waking, with no actual experience during sleep at all.

Now suppose we can do real-time neural decoding: an array reads visual and auditory cortex during REM with enough fidelity to reconstruct, say, that the sleeper is looking at a face or moving through a room while it's happening, and we can compare that to the waking report afterwards. The decoder is trained on waking perception, and it's right maybe 70% of the time and occassionally confabulates, but it's way above chance and time-locked to the sleep, not to waking.

Does Malcolm's argument survive this, or does it collapse? Which premise exactly is under pressure—the verificationist bit, the first-person/third-person asymmetry, or something else? And does Dennett's cassette theory actually lose here, or can it be patched by saying the memory is written during sleep but only played back at waking, so the REM decode shows subpersonal processing rather than experience? If both are in trouble, what's the weakest claim about dream experience that the decoding actually forces us to accept?
