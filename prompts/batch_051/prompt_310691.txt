I'm working through the Jaynesian claim that probability theory is the unique logic of plausible reasoning, and I keep finding that the argument bottoms out in Cox's theorem: start from a handful of seemingly bland desiderata about how degrees of plausibility behave, and out come the sum and product rules of probability. But when I press on what the desiderata actually are, the list shifts depending on who is presenting it — sometimes the emphasis is on consistency with Boolean logic, sometimes on invariance under changes of representation, sometimes on continuity or differentiability, sometimes on the absence of exploitable non-probabilistic representations. Before I decide whether the theorem actually justifies probabilism or simply rebuilds it from assumptions that already contain it, I want the full specification that a theory in this area would have to meet. What must an adequate account of plausible reasoning deliver for it to count as showing that rational degrees of belief have to be probabilities?

So here is what I would like from you: a list of the desiderata, each with the consideration or motivating case behind it, and for each one a verdict on whether it is a genuine requirement that any account must satisfy or a preference of one camp — the strict subjectivist Bayesians, the imprecise-probability theorists, the Dempster-Shafer or possibility-theory traditions, and the logic-based approaches to belief revision. I especially want to know whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, and where the real work is being done: for example, does the theorem's force depend on assuming plausibilities are real numbers, or that equal plausibility is represented by equality of numbers, or that the rules must remain unchanged under certain transformations? Which single requirement does the most to exclude non-Kolmogorovian rivals? Keep the focus on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on a long scorecard of existing theories.

Finally, I want the answer to be explicit about its own weak points. Say what would change your verdict: if a formal result showed that your non-negotiable list can be satisfied by a class of non-additive belief functions, would you move, or would you conclude that some other tacit assumption has to give? Which argument or counterexample would make you demote one of your non-negotiables or promote one of the negotiable ones, and where in your reasoning is that correction most likely to bite?
