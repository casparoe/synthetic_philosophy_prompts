Consider the following working characterization of a methodologically individualist explanation in social science. An explanation is individualist only if it satisfies all three criteria:

(1) Causal criterion: the only things doing causal work in the explanation are individual persons—their beliefs, desires, intentions, choices, and actions—together with the interactions among those persons.

(2) Reducibility criterion: any social-level term the explanation uses, such as 'the norm,' 'the institution,' or 'group pressure,' is dispensable shorthand: it can in principle be replaced, without explanatory loss, by a description of which individuals believe, want, and do what.

(3) Microfoundation criterion: the explanation makes intelligible how the macro-level pattern is generated, maintained, or changed by the mental states and actions of the individuals involved.

Now consider this case. The members of an organization publicly enforce a dress code that, in private, almost none of them endorse. Each member obeys because each believes that most of the others genuinely approve; each privately disapproves but conceals this to avoid standing out. The organization also publishes a weekly 'adherence score' that measures only public compliance, not private approval. The score is high, everyone can see it, and employees treat it as public evidence of general endorsement, which reinforces each employee's belief that others approve. An organizational sociologist explains the persistence of the dress code as "a norm stabilized by pluralistic ignorance, with an audit metric that converts public compliance back into evidence for the norm."

Does the sociologist's explanation qualify as methodologically individualist under the three criteria? Work through the criteria one by one. In particular, address the following. Is the adherence score an individual-level aggregate or an autonomous social-level cause, and which criterion settles that? Does it matter that no single employee can produce or change the score by acting alone, or does that fact simply show that an individualist explanation must involve interactions among many individuals? Where the criteria underdetermine the answer, say explicitly whether the difficulty lies in the criteria, in the explanation as stated, or in a genuine ambiguity of the case. Finally, take a definite position: either supply the microfoundations that would make the explanation individualist, or say why, on the best version of the criteria, it should be judged holist—and state whether the case reveals that one of the three criteria is not really necessary.
