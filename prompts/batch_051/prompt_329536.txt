For most of the time I've hung around the rationalist side of the internet I've treated expected utility as the fixed point and the independence axiom as load-bearing: any gut preference that violates independence is a bug to be corrected, not reasoned around. And I'm honestly fine endorsing that in the abstract. But I recently started actually writing down my own choices on Allais-style gambles, including a bunch of variants, and I keep voting against the axiom in a suspiciously consistent way. I don't know whether I should keep correcting the gut or check whether it might be tracking something.

Here's what I get. (Amounts matter to me; wealth held constant; no ambiguity in the probabilities.)

1. Sure $1M vs $1M with 89%, $5M with 10%, nix with 1%: I take the sure million.
2. $1M with 11% (nix otherwise) vs $5M with 10% (nix otherwise): I take the $5M long shot.
Same shape, small stakes: sure $300 vs $400 with 80% (nix else): I take the sure $300; but $300 with 25% vs $400 with 20%: I take the $400.
When the same choices are laid out as 100 physical tickets I can circle, I mostly pick the independence-consistent options and it feels less like choosing than like doing arithmetic. Part of the pull toward the sure million in case 1 is clearly anticipated regret: if the 1% hits and I left a guaranteed million lying there, I'd hate myself. In a version where I'm told beforehand I'll never learn what the unchosen gamble would have paid, that pull weakens but doesn't disappear.

So I want a principle. The candidates I can see are (a) keep expected utility and independence, and treat every one of the above departures as framing noise, regret salience, or a computation error; or (b) relax independence into some rank-dependent or probability-weighting account where I'm allowed to overweight the 1% of nix and overweight moving from 99% to 100% certainty, or something in that family. State the candidates precisely enough, run each over the cases, and tell me wich of my verdicts each one has to give up (I suspect neither preserves all of them honestly). Then give me the verdict your preferred principle delivers on the case I'm actually stuck on: the same sure-million lottery pair repeated 100 times, independent draws, all paid at the end — since the sure option pays 100 million total and the expected value of the other is a lot higher, my aggregate brain flips, but I can't tell if that's rational correction or just me ignoring the feeling I'd have on a single play. Should I keep correcting the gut, or is the gut tracking something the axiom rules out by definition?
