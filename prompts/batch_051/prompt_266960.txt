I run a legal philosophy seminar of about twenty undergraduates, and we have ninety minutes. Design a classroom game that makes them feel the pull of desuetude — the phenomenon of a law remaining on the books while officials and citizens have long stopped treating it as binding — and the rule-of-law problem of reviving such a law selectively. Give the class a written code most of whose rules they use in an ongoing practice, plus three or four dead rules inherited from an earlier era; over several rounds they must decide whether to enforce, obey, repeal, or ignore a dead rule when a new case makes it relevant. Set the payoffs, costs, and voting rules so that selective enforcement is tempting and so that the class itself discovers how a dead rule is brought back to life before the facilitator names any theory. End with a debrief that maps specific moves in the game onto desuetude, repeal versus desuetude, and the rule-of-law concerns about selective revival, and that honestly states what the game cannot reproduce about real legal systems. Please give the response as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
