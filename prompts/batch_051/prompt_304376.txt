Let the rule of law be understood as a normative ideal, not merely a description of any functioning legal system, having the following six features. (1) Prospectivity: a person can learn, before acting, what consequences the state will attach to that action. (2) Publicity: the standards are accessible enough that an ordinary person can in fact inform themselves. (3) Generality: the same standards govern everyone similarly situated, rather than being tailored to particular individuals. (4) Stability: the standards change slowly enough that a person can plan over years, not merely react day to day. (5) Congruence: whatever standards are announced are the standards actually applied by officials and enforcement institutions. (6) Answerability: a person adversely affected by a decision can demand that the decision be justified by the announced standards, and some institution can review whether that justification was actually given.

Now suppose a state abolishes its general statutes and replaces them with a system of personalized administrative codes. Each resident receives a continuously updated code, generated automatically, that states exactly what the state will do in response to any course of action the resident might take; residents may query hypothetical choices and receive definitive, plain-language answers. Enforcement follows each person's code exactly, with no discretion, and officials never override the system's outputs. The codes are stable and always accessible. Two features, however, are unusual. First, no two residents have identical codes, and the principles that distinguish one resident's code from another's are not published and cannot be retrieved from the system. Second, when a resident asks why their code attaches a penalty to a course of action, the only answer the system can give is a restatement of the code itself; there is no larger body of shared rules from which the code was derived, and no human official has authority to review or revise the code.

Does this arrangement realize the ideal set out in the six features above? Work through the features one by one, marking those where the system clearly qualifies and those where it clearly fails. Then concentrate on the two or three features where the verdict is genuinely borderline, and say whether the case exposes a defect in how those features were formulated. In particular, consider whether prospectivity, publicity, and congruence can do the work that generality and answerability were meant to do, and whether the personalized codes amount to the rule of law or only to an extremely regular form of rule through law.
