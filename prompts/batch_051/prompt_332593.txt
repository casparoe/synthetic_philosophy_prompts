I cover science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and I am working on a piece about state apologies for historical wrongs — specifically, about why some official statements read as genuine apologies while others, even ones containing the word "sorry", read as evasions. I need a working definition of a *state apology*: the speech act a government performs when it properly apologises for a historical wrong. I have abstracted the cases below from real episodes, but I am asking for conceptual analysis only; I do not need citations, proper names, or examples beyond the cases themselves.

Sort these cases:

(a) A head of government tells parliament, with representatives of the harmed community present, that the state "apologises unreservedly" for a now-repealed policy under which children were forcibly removed from an ethnic minority; she says the policy was unjust, that the state accepts responsibility, and she announces a compensation fund.

(b) The national legislature passes a formal motion stating that the country apologises to a group of citizens who were imprisoned and barred from public employment under an old discriminatory law; the same motion creates a pension scheme.

(c) After a natural disaster abroad, the foreign ministry issues a statement of condolence saying that the country shares the victims' sorrow; no causal responsibility by anyone is stated or implied.

(d) A state historical commission publishes a report documenting abuses in neutral, factual language; it says what happened, who was involved, and when, but it offers no evaluative or performative language.

(e) A private railway company apologises for its own historical use of forced labour.

(f) A head of state says: "We are sorry that people were hurt during those events." Nothing in the statement identifies the state as the agent of the hurt, and no compensation follows.

(g) The government issues a statement of "deep regret for the suffering of those affected" and pays compensation, but its lawyers removed the words "apology", "wrong", and any admission of a breach because those words could affect pending litigation.

(h) Parliament passes a motion saying that it "apologises for the actions of previous generations" toward an oppressed group; the wording asserts intergenerational responsibility rather than any misdeed by sitting members.

(i) A minister says the government "apologises for any offence that may have been taken" during a controversy over how a historical episode was commemorated.

(j) A state-funded museum, acting on its own initiative, places next to an artefact a label reading: "We acknowledge that these objects were removed unjustly, and we are sorry for the harm this caused."

(k) The government pays substantial compensation, opens its archives, and says it "hopes this closes a painful chapter", but it deliberately never says that it is sorry.

To be explicit about my own priors: I take (a) and (b) to be clearly within the term, (c)–(e) clearly outside it, and (f)–(k) to be the hard cases on which I have no settled view.

I need a definition that gives a defensible verdict for each of these, including the uncomfortable ones. If no single definition captures all of them, the most useful answer is a small taxonomy of neighbouring speech acts — apology, regret, sympathy, acknowledgment, reparation — with the borderline cases placed among them. For each item, say where your definition gives the wrong answer or no answer, and then either revise it or defend the surprising verdict.

Please write the whole answer in British English. At the very end, on a separate line, give the definition you are willing to defend in the fixed form \answer{...}, with the braces containing a single-sentence statement of that definition.
