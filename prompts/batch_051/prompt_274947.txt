This prompt concerns the paradox of blackmail. The puzzle is usually set up as follows. A has come to possess a true, reputation-damaging piece of information about B. A would be legally and morally free to publish it, and A would also be legally and morally free merely to ask B for money, which B could refuse. But if A links the two acts into a conditional proposal — payment in exchange for silence — the law treats A as a blackmailer. Two separately permissible acts combine into what appears to be an impermissible transaction. Your task is to assign a credence to a specific claim about how this puzzle is best resolved.

The claim is that the correct resolution of the paradox of blackmail is coercion-based, where 'correct' means identifying the primary wrong-making feature that justifies criminalizing the conditional package while permitting each component act performed alone. A coercion-based resolution says the wrong of blackmail consists in the deliberate imposition of a forced choice between two harms — exposure and payment — and that the permissibility of each component act when performed on its own does nothing to render the imposed choice permissible.

I do not want a survey of positions or the answer that it depends on one's background theory. I want a number — your actual credence in that claim — together with a decomposition of the reasoning behind it. Assign rough probabilities to at least the following sub-questions, with a sentence or two of justification for each:

1. That the paradox arises from a false aggregation principle: the permissibility of the parts does not transmit to the conditional package. If this is false, the natural conclusion is the dissolution view, namely that the package should be lawful because its components are lawful.
2. Conditional on the aggregation principle failing, that the operative wrong-making feature is the coercive choice itself, rather than either of its main rivals: an exploitation view (A licitly holds the information but wrongfully prices B's escape from its release, turning someone else's secrets into a commodity) or a privacy-based view (the wrong lies in making the disposition of B's personal information the object of a transaction without B's consent, independent of whatever pressure B feels).
3. That a single, unified account of blackmail's wrongfulness exists at all, rather than the law of blackmail marking a disjunctive patchwork of quite different wrongs.
4. That the coercion-based account can handle the harder variants without ad hoc amendments — for example, threats to perform a permissible act other than disclosure, or demands for non-monetary benefits in exchange for silence.

Then show how these terms combine to produce your final credence, and identify which sub-question the final number is most sensitive to. Finally, state what would move that sub-question most: describe a case, an argument, or a piece of evidence that would substantially raise or lower the relevant probability, and say in which direction and by roughly how much. If you think no observation could settle it, say what that indicates about the status of the underlying question.
