I've just spent an hour arguing with a friend about Hobbes, and we came away with wildly different readings of what the state of nature argument actually gets you. His view is that once you grant Hobbes's picture of life without a common power — mutual fear, diffidence, no reliable cooperation — the argument really does deliver something close to an absolute sovereign: any reserved right of resistance or divided authority would just reintroduce the original conflict. My view is that the argument only shows we need a common judge and a reliable enforcement mechanism, and the rest of the sovereign's powers are up for grabs; Hobbes just overreaches, partly because he can't imagine coordination without submission. What's your actual credence that the logic of Hobbes's state of nature, taken on its own terms, genuinely commits you to something like his absolutist conclusion rather than to a more modest limited-state conclusion? I don't want an "it depends how you define the terms" answer — I want a number. Decompose it. Specifically: (1) how likely is it that Hobbes is right that covenants not backed by a sword are just words and cannot provide assurance; (2) how likely is it that any reserved right of resistance destabilises sovereignty in the way Hobbes claims; (3) how likely is it that Hobbes's own commitment to the right of self-preservation is consistent with the absolute sovereign conclusion; (4) how likely is it that the social contract can generate a divided or constitutional state without falling back into the state of nature. Give a rough probability to each, show how they combine to your overall credence, and tell me which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to — i.e., which one moving would shift it most. And be committed: pick the reading you think is right and defend it against the other one, don't just list weaknesses on both sides.
