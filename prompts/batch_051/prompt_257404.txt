In conversations about the mind-body problem I describe myself as a physicalist, but some years ago I settled into a fixed policy about how I defend that label. I never state what the physical includes now; I define the physical as whatever the entities, properties, and laws posited by a completed, true fundamental physics would turn out to be. When someone asks whether that makes physicalism compatible with panpsychism, epiphenomenal qualia, or even a material deity, I answer that it would be compatible if the final physics posits such things, but I do not expect it to. This policy got fixed after a colleague pressed me on panpsychism and I found that every more specific definition I offered either tied physicalism to today's physics, which I do not trust, or ruled out positions I had not actually meant to rule out.

The same colleague now argues that I have backed into something like Hempel's dilemma. He puts it this way: if the physical is characterized by current physics, then physicalism is almost certainly false, because current physics is known to be incomplete; but if it is characterized by some notional future physics, then physicalism just says that the world is whatever the completed science says it is, which is either trivially true or too indefinite to exclude anything. He thinks my policy is a maneuver for keeping the word physicalism without bearing its commitments. I think it is the only honest way to remain a physicalist while staying agnostic about which present theory will survive.

I want your evaluation of the policy itself, not of physicalism as such. Is my refusal to pin down the physical a defensible epistemic habit, a way of staying open to the history of science, or is it an unfalsifiable posture that protects the label from criticism? If it needs repair, what is the most defensible restatement, and what would I have to give up for that restatement to have content?
