A colleague in my interpretability reading group walked us through the following argument, and I have the uneasy sense that it is either the key to the whole field or a trap I haven't spotted.

(1) The evidence that a neural network really represents a feature F—rather than merely allowing F to be decoded—is decodability: we take the network's hidden activations over a set of inputs, train a simple readout such as a linear probe to predict F from those activations, and find that it succeeds at well above chance.

(2) This is the standard practice of interpretability. When a linear probe recovers sentiment, board configuration, or truth-value from hidden states, the field takes that as evidence that the network represents the feature.

(3) But decodability of this kind turns out to be cheap. Our group ran the same probes as controls on two baselines—activations from an untrained, randomly initialized copy of the model, and a fixed random nonlinear projection of the input—and the probes still recovered many of the coarse labels well above chance, occasionally within striking distance of the trained network's own accuracy.

(4) If the criterion in (1) is correct, then by (3) a network that has learned nothing, and perhaps the random projection itself, represents sentiment, board configuration, truth-value, and the rest.

(5) That conclusion is hard to swallow: representation should be something a network acquires through training, not something a random matrix or an untrained pile of weights gets for free.

(6) So decodability cannot be a sufficient condition for representation.

(7) The obvious repair is to require that the representation be causally used by the model: identify the direction, intervene on it—zero it, scramble it, edit it—and show that the model's downstream behavior changes in the predicted way. Only then is it a real representation rather than a recoverable correlate.

(8) But this repair seems too strict. Trained networks are redundant and distributed; a feature can be carried by several directions at once, and destroying any one of them may change nothing, so the causal test will deny the existence of many representations we have independent reason to accept. And interpreting the interventions themselves in a nonlinear system is exactly the problem we hoped probing would avoid.

So we end up in a dilemma: either keep the cheap decodability criterion and concede that random networks have representations—in which case "representation" stops distinguishing the trained model from a control—or reject the criterion and lose the standard evidence behind most interpretability claims, without an obvious replacement.

Where does this argument go wrong? I have two commitments I would be reluctant to give up: that a successful probe on a trained network tells us something about what the network represents, and that a randomly initialized network does not represent the same high-level features. The argument says I cannot keep both. Is it right about that? If you had to reject one step, which would it be, and what does rejecting it commit you to about what interpretability can and cannot establish?
