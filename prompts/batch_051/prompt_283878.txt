I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I want to lead a one-hour session for a group of working software engineers on a claim about mathematics that I have only half-digested myself. The claim, as I understand it, is associated with Imre Lakatos: mathematical knowledge grows something like empirical knowledge does — by conjectures, attempted proofs, and the discovery of counterexamples that force a revision of the conjecture, the proof, or the definition of the terms involved. On this view a proof is not the end of the story, because a counterexample can expose a hidden assumption or an under-specified concept, and a proof may turn out to have proved something narrower than the theorem claimed.

Don't give me the talk itself. I need the teaching plan: the order in which the pieces must be introduced and why, the one example that carries the whole session, the misconceptions to intercept and when, and a way to check what actually landed. Work through the following.

1. The order of ideas. What has to be established before the core claim becomes intelligible to an engineer, and what depends on what? My own candidates are the distinction between a deduction being valid and its premises being true, and the idea of a universal claim ranging over a class whose boundary is not fully fixed. I need the actual dependency structure, including anything I have missed.

2. The carrying example. I am inclined to build the session around Euler's formula V - E + F = 2 for polyhedra, because it is the example I keep seeing: an intuitively convincing proof, followed by counterexamples such as a cube with a cubical cavity or a picture-frame-shaped solid. Tell me whether this example does the work I think it does. What must a carrying example have for this idea, and what must it lack? Under what circumstances would this particular example mislead rather than illuminate?

3. Misconceptions and their timing. I expect my colleagues to arrive with habits: treating a proof as a passing test suite, treating a counterexample as a failing test that simply falsifies the theorem, and reading a definitional revision as moving the goalposts. Map the misconceptions that are likely to arise, in the order they are likely to arise, and say at which point in the sequence each one should be headed off rather than allowed to harden.

4. The check for understanding. By the end, how do I distinguish someone who has learned the historical anecdote about polyhedra from someone who has internalized the mechanism: proof attempt, counterexample, and definitional revision form a repeatable cycle, and mathematicians make a real choice at each step? Give me a concrete end-of-session probe that would reveal the difference, and that does not depend on anyone having done prior reading.

5. What to leave out. At this level, what should I deliberately omit — formalism, the history of foundational debates, technical metamathematics — and how should I signal briefly that I am omitting it?

I am not looking for readings or references. I am looking for the structure of the explanation.
