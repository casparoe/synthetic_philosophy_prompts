Consider Buridan's ass: a hungry ass stands exactly midway between two bales of hay identical in every respect, and because it has no reason to prefer one over the other, it cannot choose and starves. Now vary the case in one respect. The ass is subject to small involuntary motor noise: every few minutes a leg twitches nondeliberately and shifts the ass a few centimeters toward one bale or the other, with no bias toward either bale over time. The ass notices each twitch; after a twitch it can costlessly undo the displacement, but it cannot prevent the twitches from occurring. Work through this version under three standard readings of the original puzzle: (a) the sufficient-reason reading, on which a will is moved only by a reason favoring one option over the other; (b) the rational-choice reading, on which an indifference tie still permits a rational agent to take any tied best option, so starving is dominated; and (c) the picking-versus-choosing distinction, on which picking among equivalent options requires no justification even though choosing does. Does the ass's observation of its own twitches give it a reason to take whichever bale it has drifted toward, or would treating a twitch as a reason be a kind of bootstrapping? In particular, does the answer depend on its first choosing not to undo a twitch, and if so, what justifies that choice? Which reading is most affected by the variation, and does any reading now yield a different verdict than in the original case?
