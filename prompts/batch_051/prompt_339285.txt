I'm a PhD student working on decision theory, and our reading group has been going in circles for about six months over whether 'better than' is transitive. The case-first lot keep bringing up spectrum cases — a series of small changes where each step seems an improvement but the endpoints look clearly worse — and take that as a straight counterexample to transitivity. The principle-first lot reply that transitivity is partly constitutive of what it means to have a coherent preference ranking, so the case intuitions must be misfiring somewhere even if we can't see where. Both sides agree on what happens in the cases, and both agree the theory would be tidier if transitivity held. What's odd is that each side accuses the other of the same sin: treating their preferred intuition as the data and everything else as noise, and reaching for simplicity only after they've already picked a winner. My working theory is that the argument isn't really about transitivity at all — it's about what counts as evidence in this corner of philosophy, i.e. intuitions about particular cases versus intuitions about principles, with simplicity smuggled in as a tiebreaker that isn't actually independent. Is that diagnose right, and if it is, is there any move that would unstick it, or are the two sides structurally unable to talk to each other?
