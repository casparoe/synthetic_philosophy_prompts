Meta-philosophy relies on a distinction between principles and the case-by-case intuitions used to test them, but it is less clear how the two kinds of evidence combine. Consider the following argument, which I keep going back and forth on, and tell me where, if anywhere, it goes wrong.

(1) In theory choice, fit with particular-case intuitions and simplicity are both genuine grounds of justification: a theory can be more justified for capturing more cases and less justified for being more complex.

(2) If two theories are otherwise alike, but one fits one additional recalcitrant case by adding one exception clause, then, treating the existing package of exceptions as fixed, the more case-accurate theory is better justified than the less case-accurate one.

(3) This exception-adding move can be iterated: there is a finite sequence of theories beginning from a simple principle P and ending in a theory Q that assigns a distinct clause to every case, so that Q has maximal case-fit and essentially no simplicity, while each adjacent pair differs only by a single added exception.

(4) By (2), each theory in the sequence is better justified than its predecessor.

(5) The relation expressed by "better justified than" is transitive: if A is better justified than B and B is better justified than C, then A is better justified than C.

(6) From (4) and (5), Q is better justified than P.

(7) But Q is not better justified than P, all things considered. The cumulative loss of simplicity is not merely the sum of many individually tolerable losses; a theory that is just a list of clauses for each case is less justified than a simple principle.

Since (6) and (7) cannot both be true, at least one step fails. Which step is it, and what exactly is the failure? I am especially interested in whether the error lies in step (4) — an illicit drop of the fixed-package qualifier from step (2) that transitivity is not licensed to ignore — or in the application of transitivity at step (6), or whether the argument instead shows something more revisionary, such as that all-things-considered justifiedness need not be transitive.

Please give your answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion, in this order: your overall verdict; the step you reject or the inference you block; the precise distinction that dissolves the apparent contradiction; what that distinction implies about the transitivity claim in step (5); and the substantive lesson for how case intuitions and simplicity should be weighed against one another.
