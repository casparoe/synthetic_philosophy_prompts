Consider a common pair of positions in policy debate: support for a legislated minimum wage together with settled opposition to rent control. On their face, the two policies are structurally parallel. Each is a state-imposed price control: one forbids paying below a floor, the other forbids charging above a ceiling. Each is normally defended as protecting a party with weak bargaining power — the employee, the tenant — against a counterparty with greater power, the employer or the landlord. And each carries the same textbook hazards: a floor can leave willing sellers of labour without a buyer, a ceiling can leave willing renters without a unit, and either control can reshape quality, entry, turnover, and investment in the market it governs. The question is whether the difference in treatment is justified, and if so, where the justifying difference lies: in the direction of the control (floors versus ceilings), in the good being priced (labour versus housing), in the kind of relationship at stake (employment versus tenancy), or in the empirical magnitudes and side effects associated with each policy. Find the strongest candidate justification for the asymmetry, test it against close variants — a minimum wage set far above the market-clearing wage, rent control that exempts new construction or luxury units, a price floor on an ordinary consumer good, a temporary ceiling on essentials during an emergency — and then deliver a verdict. Should the asymmetry be kept, reversed, or abandoned, and does accepting either of these two policies commit one, rationally or morally, to a corresponding position on the other, or are there differences between the two cases that genuinely warrant different conclusions?
