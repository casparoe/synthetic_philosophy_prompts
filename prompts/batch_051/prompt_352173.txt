Question (10 pts): Kant famously denies that we have any direct duties to non-human animals, yet he also think cruelty to them is morally wrong. Explain his position: why can't animals be direct objects of duty, what are the "indirect" duties he does accept, and what exactly grounds those duties? Use at least one of Kant's own examples.

Student answer: Kant's criterion for being the object of a direct duty is rationality or self-consciousness: only beings that can give themselves a moral law count as ends in themselves. Animals don't meet that bar, so for Kant they're mere things, means to human ends, and there can be no direct duties to them. So far so good.

But Kant still wants to say cruelty to animals is wrong, so he introduces indirect duties. What "indirect" means here is that the duty is really owed to a person standing behind the animal — paradigmatically its owner. Kicking a stranger's dog wrongs the owner, through their property. He also brings in the sympathy point: being cruel to animals dulls our sympathetic feelings, which we need for carrying out our duties toward other human beings. So the animal is only the occasion for a duty that is ultimately owed to other persons.

His old-dog example shows the same structure. The dog served its owner faithfully, so we should put it down painlessly, but that's a duty of gratitude owed to the owner rather than to the dog, which is just the occasion for that person-directed duty.
