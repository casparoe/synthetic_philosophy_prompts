Lately, though, a friend who works in computational neuroscience has been insisting, with some technical confidence, that beliefs and desires don't exist. His claim isn't that they're hard to measure or that we lack the right instruments. It's that they are like phlogiston or the luminiferous ether: posits of a useful but false everyday theory of the mind, which a mature neuroscience will simply stop talking about, the way chemistry stopped talking about phlogiston once it had oxidation. When I objected that my therapy seemed to work precisely by identifying and modifying these supposedly nonexistent beliefs, he said that's exactly what you'd expect from a useful fiction: an instrument can be practically effective while describing the world falsely, like the sun "rising."

This has started to color how I relate to my own head. When I catch myself thinking, "I hold the belief that a mistake at work will make people conclude I'm incompetent," am I discovering something real inside me, or applying a convenient vocabulary that no more picks out real objects than talk of sunrise does? Part of what I want to know is whether my therapist's "core beliefs" and my friend's "beliefs" are even the same kind of thing, or whether the dispute partly rests on an equivocation. And I want to know what actually hangs on this: should it change how I do the CBT exercises, how I talk about my inner life with friends, or how I understand what therapy did for me? I'm not looking for a pep talk about how whatever works is fine; I want the strongest case on each side, honestly separated from what experts would actually dispute.
