I am an undergraduate currently taking a course in formal epistemology and the philosophy of probability, and we have spent several weeks on the reference class problem: a single-case probability seems relative to a choice of reference class, yet there appears to be no principled rule for selecting the class. I have been trying to devise such a rule myself, and I would welcome a candid expert assessment of it.

The rule, which I call the Interventional Anchoring Principle, goes as follows. A property is admissible for placing a token case in a reference class only if it lies on the causal pathway to the outcome, or is a measurable marker of something that does, and its connection to the outcome is one we could in principle exploit through intervention: changing the property, or changing what it marks, would change the frequency of the outcome. The probability of the outcome for the token case is then the observed frequency of the outcome in the narrowest class formed by intersecting all the admissible properties for which reliable frequency data exist; if that class is empty or its data are too thin, we fall back to the broadest intersection whose frequency remains stable. For example, in assigning a probability that a particular 58-year-old patient survives a graft, it would be inadmissible to include the referring physician's handedness or the patient's middle initial, however predictive those might be in a large dataset, because they are not causal levers. It would be admissible to include blood pressure, diabetes status, or graft type, provided there is intervention evidence that altering them, or their pathways, changes survival.

Could you act as an expert in formal epistemology and the philosophy of probability and assess this proposal? Please begin by brainstorming the strongest and most precise reconstruction of the principle; then say what it gets right and which existing distinctions or proposals it most resembles; then press it with the most serious objections. I am especially worried that the principle only relocates the original problem, since one still has to say what counts as on the causal pathway, how intervention evidence could be established without already assuming a reference class, and how to handle non-manipulable but clearly predictive attributes such as sex, ancestry, or historical facts. I am not after a reading list; I want the idea itself tested. I would like to know which objections are fixable refinements and which, if any, are fatal to the Interventional Anchoring Principle as a solution to the reference class problem.
