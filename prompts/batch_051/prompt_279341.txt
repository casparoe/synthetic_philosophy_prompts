I run a graduate seminar on causality across the special sciences — about 15 or 16 students, a mix of philosophers of biology, economists dabbling in methodology, and a couple of psychologists. Next week's session is meant to make Pearl's ladder of causation visceral before we ever see the formalism. In previous years they could all state that correlation is not causation and that observing is different from intervening, but then they'd happily go back to treating observational and interventional data as interchangeable. I want a 90-minute session where they run up against that diffence with data they've generated themselves — ideally a hidden causal structure where half the room can only watch and the other half can pay to intervene, the two groups work seperately for a while, and then they end up telling different stories about which variable does what. It shouldn't need any fancy equipment: cards, whiteboard, slips of paper, maybe a spreadsheet I can run on the projector.

Design the whole set-up for me: the hidden rule, the roles, the payment scheme (sweets or seminar points, not real money), a timeline that protects a good 20-minute debrief at the end, and a debrief that maps each moment onto rung one, rung two and rung three. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and which feature of Pearl's picture the game cannot reproduce because the students stay outside the system looking in.
