hey, in my phil of religion course we just did the salvation debate—exclusivism vs inclusivism vs pluralism—and I got kind of fixated on how much work is being done by a factual premise that people rarely name outright. The inclusivist line (Rahner-style anonymous Christians, implicit faith, that sort of thing) basically says it would be monstrous to damn someone who never had a real shot at the gospel, so God must offer grace through their own tradition or at least count their good-faith response as salvific. The pluralist line generalizes that: since people are born into a tradition by accident, no tradition should be the one and only channel of salvation. Exclusivism then has to say there's still some general revelation or extraordinary grace available even to people who never hear the message.

But here's the thing. The factual premise—lots of people never get a genuine chance to encounter the message—looks much weaker now than it did when these positions were getting worked out. Anyone with a smartphone and a data plan can pull up the central claims of any major religion in their own language in a couple of minutes. So the category of people who literally could not know what Christianity (or Islam, etc.) teaches has shrunk enormously, even if it hasn't disappeared.

What I want to know is whether the inclusivist or pluralist argument survives that change. Does the internet just kill the empirical premise, or does it force a repair where "genuine opportunity" gets redefined—e.g., needing a living community, emotional safety, trust, not just information? And does pluralism maybe get stronger, because easy access makes religious switching and mixing so common that the old bounded-tradition picture starts to break down? Don't give me a survey of possible readings; definately take a line. Say which premise the internet bears on hardest, whether the argument can be repaired or is just bypassed, and whether inclusivism is in better or worse shape after the internet than it was before.
