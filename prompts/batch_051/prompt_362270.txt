I'm giving a lunch talk next month to our decision-science group, and I need a Q&A prep sheet. My talk defends the claim that the endowment effect — the gap between what people will accept to give something up and what they'll pay to get it — is not necessarily a mistake, or at least not the kind of mistake that licenses the usual debiasing talk. My line is going to be: once you allow that a person's reference point can legitimately depend on what they already own, a WTA/WTP gap for an item you've been given is exactly what a coherent, context-sensitive valuation function produces. Being randomly assigned a mug doesn't corrupt your judgment; it just changes which indifference curve you're on, because ownership is one of the things that determines what counts as a loss. I'll try to separate this from status quo bias (which I partly conced is less defensible) and from cases where people just don't understand the auction mechanism. I'll end by saying welfare economics talks about "preferences over bundles" as if bundles floated free of history, and that's the real problem, not the subjects.

The room is mostly behavioral economists and experimental people who were trained to see loss aversion and the endowment effect as deviations from rational choice, plus a few philosophers of science who think "bias" is underexamined in the debiasing literature. They're sharp, and they will not like that last slide. Prepare me: what are the objections I'll actually get, in roughly the order they'll come, phrased more or less the way this crowd would phrase them, and from which kind of person in the room each is likeliest to come? For each, what should I concede, what should I hold, and where is my reply softest? And include the question I'm secretly hoping nobody asks — with a note on how I recover if it does come up.
