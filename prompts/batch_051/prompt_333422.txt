I am preparing for an upper-level historiography seminar, and we have been set a problem about Ranke that I want to examine more carefully than we managed in the tutorial. We read Ranke alongside Carr and Hayden White. The tutor gave us the preface to Ranke's Histories of the Latin and Teutonic Nations, in which Ranke says, in my own paraphrase because I do not have the translation here, that history has traditionally been charged with judging the past and instructing the present for the benefit of future ages, but that his work does not claim that office; it wishes only to present the past as it really occurred. That claim struck us as modest, but it produced an argument that now seems to prove too much.

Here is the argument as we reconstructed it.

1. If the historian's sole task, as Ranke says, is to present the past as it really occurred, then every feature of a historical account that does not simply record the facts as they were—selection, emphasis, plot, evaluation, metaphor—is foreign to history proper.
2. But no narrative can be assembled without selection, emphasis, and some principle of relevance, because the archive is too full and events have no intrinsic narrative shape.
3. Therefore any narrative history, judged by Ranke's own standard, must be doing something non-historical, or at best extra-historical.
4. If that is right, then when Carr says the historian is a selective interpreter and White says historical narrative is a literary artefact imposed on the past, they are not contradicting Ranke's dictum; they are only making explicit what Ranke's principle already entails once one notices that he did not actually abandon narrative.
5. But if Carr and White are merely drawing out Ranke, then the supposed objectivity dispute between Ranke and his critics dissolves: everyone agrees that narratives select, and the only remaining disagreement is about how to do it well.
6. That cannot be right, because Ranke clearly meant to be doing something different from a novelist, and Carr and White clearly meant to reject something Ranke stood for.

Where does this argument go wrong? I would prefer to keep premise 1, since it seems the fairest reading of Ranke's sentence, but I cannot see how to stop the slide to 5 without conceding that the objectivity dispute is a verbal quibble. Is there an equivocation in the argument—perhaps on what it means to present the past as it really occurred, or on what counts as narrative—and which step should I reject, and what would rejecting it cost a defender of Ranke?
