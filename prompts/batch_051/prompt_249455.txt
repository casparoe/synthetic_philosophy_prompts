I'm in a law and technology seminar right now and we've been going over the old arguments about corporate personality—the idea that a corporation is a legal person that can own property, sue and be sued, and that limited liability means shareholders aren't on the hook for what the corporate person does. It got me thinking that the current AI liability debate treats models as sort of half-persons: people say we should sue the model or make the developers pull the weights, but there's no actual legal thing there to sue. So I've been kicking around a proposal and want you to ruthlessly stress-test the mechanism, not the morality of it.

Here's the proposal. A statute says any general-purpose AI system above some compute threshold has to be registered as a seperate "artificial legal person" before it's deployed commercially. The deploying firm must set up this ALP with a mandatory liability fund, sized by a risk-based capital formula (like an insurance solvency rule, but keyed to model capabilities, deployment scale, and expected harm). The ALP can be sued in its own name, and damages come out of the fund. If the fund falls below a floor—because of payouts, because the formula gets revised upward, or because the model is reclassified as higher-risk—the ALP is automatically suspended until it's recapitalized or dissolved. The parent firm's other ALPs are not liable for this one's debts, and the human owners are only on the hook for the capital they actually put in, nothing more. Basically it's the corporate form transplanted onto AI, plus a capital buffer and an auto-suspend tripwire.

What does this scheme actually incentivize—for the deploying firm, whoever is appointed to act for the ALP, insurers, competitors, plaintiffs, and downstream users? Where does it get gamed first? Does spinning off each model into its own ALP make risk-shunting easier or harder? How does litigation change when plaintiffs know the defendant is a finite pot of money with auto-suspension as a live outcome? And can the scheme be fixed without just collapsing back into ordinary unlimited liability?
