For an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the evolution of cooperation (sixteen students, ninety minutes), design a structured classroom activity built around iterated Prisoner's Dilemma tournaments under error. The activity should make participants feel the force of a specific result before it is named: strict tit-for-tat is brittle when intended cooperation is occasionally implemented as defection, and a forgiving strategy such as generous tit-for-tat is not merely "nicer" but more robust precisely because it can repair cooperation after error.

The session should run in two phases. First, pairs play a noiseless round-robin tournament with an indefinite or randomly terminated series of rounds, so that the exact final round is never known and strict reciprocity can visibly succeed. Second, the same or similar strategies compete under an implementation error: with a fixed, salient probability (for example, one in eight), a player's chosen cooperate move is executed, scored, and observed as a defection. The error should be generated mechanically—by a marked card in a deck or a die roll—never by facilitator discretion. The rules, payoffs, and phase ordering should be arranged so that pairs playing strict tit-for-tat against themselves watch their cooperation collapse into a cycle of mutual recrimination after a single error, while forgiving strategies recover, and so that this happens in the play before the facilitator names error, forgiveness, or robustness.

Your plan should include:

(a) the concrete rules, the payoff matrix in the standard ordering (temptation > reward for mutual cooperation > punishment for mutual defection > sucker payoff), and any cards, dice, score sheets, or strategy menus;

(b) a timeline for the ninety minutes, including setup, practice rounds, both phases, and debrief;

(c) a debrief that maps specific moments in the play onto the underlying ideas: noise as a model of misperception, imperfect execution, or bad luck; forgiveness as error correction rather than mere tolerance or niceness; and the limits of strict reciprocity under realistic conditions;

(d) what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run—for example, participants not converging on tit-for-tat in the first phase or the noise being too rare or too frequent to be felt—and how a facilitator should handle each failure, if necessary by seeding scripted strategies;

(e) an honest note on which feature of the evolution of cooperation the activity cannot reproduce, such as the population-level selection of strategies over many generations rather than deliberating individuals choosing among given strategies in one session, and what the facilitator must therefore supply afterwards rather than pretend participants discovered.

Write the activity up as a runnable plan rather than as an essay about the ideas.
