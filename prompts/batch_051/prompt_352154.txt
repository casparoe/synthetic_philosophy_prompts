I have been reading David Hume's essay "Of the Standard of Taste" on my own, and next month I am leading a discussion at a members' meeting of a local arts collective. My thesis is that the essay's solution is unstable, because the very phenomenon Hume describes keeps reappearing inside his proposed standard. He writes:

"There are certain terms in every language, which import blame, and others praise; and all men, who use the same tongue, must agree in their application of them. Every voice is united in applauding elegance, propriety, simplicity, spirit in writing; and in blaming fustian, affectation, coldness, and a false brilliancy: But when critics come to particulars, this seeming unanimity vanishes; and it is found, that they had affixed a very different meaning to their expressions."

I want to argue that his later appeal to the joint verdict of true judges has the same structure: the judges may agree on the general words "delicacy," "practice," "comparison," and "freedom from prejudice" while applying them so differently that their verdict is not a real consensus. The audience is mostly practicing painters, ceramicists, and graphic designers. They have no formal philosophy background, they distrust fixed rules for art, and they are more likely to explain differences of taste by appeal to training, community, and medium than by appeal to an ideal critic. Prepare me for the question period: what will they object to, in their own terms, which objections have real force, what should I concede up front, what should I hold, and which objection am I hoping nobody raises? Please answer in American English.
