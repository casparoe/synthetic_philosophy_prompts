I have been arguing with a colleague about how much a climate adaptation plan should be punished for its worst-case scenarios, and the argument has settled into something more general that I have been calling the 'catastrophe booster.' I would like a serious assessment of it, because I cannot tell whether it is trivially refutable or quietly someone else's theorem.

Here is the rule. Take any act and assign utilities to its possible outcomes. Rank the outcomes from worst to best, and identify the bottom fifth of the probability mass in that ranking. For the purpose of evaluating the act, double the total weight assigned to that bottom fifth, and scale down the weight assigned to everything above it proportionally so that all the weights still sum to one. You then rank acts by ordinary expected utility under those modified weights. So a policy that carries a 10 percent chance of serious harm treats that chance as if it were 20 percent when compared with safer alternatives. The appeal is that you do not have to be uniformly risk-averse about money: you become more cautious only when enough probability sits in the lower tail.

I know the cutoff at one-fifth is arbitrary, and I can feel that a moving cutoff should create inconsistencies, but I have not been able to work out exactly which ones. What I want to know is whether the rule is even coherent: in particular, whether it can prefer one act over another that dominates it, and whether it leaves me open to being Dutch-booked. Have I simply reinvented something that already exists in the theory of risk-weighted expected utility under a better name and with better foundations? I would like the three strongest objections to a rule of this shape, and a judgment about whether any of them is fatal rather than just a matter of taste or calibration.
