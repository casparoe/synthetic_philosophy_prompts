I have been turning over the idea, much discussed on rationalist forums, of a free-floating rationale: a structured justification that exists somewhere in a system even though no particular reasoner ever entertained it — a reason without a reasoner. The usual example is a language model that produces a plausible-sounding explanation of its own answer, but I suspect a familiar kind of legal clause is a cleaner test case. Consider this clause, of the sort that appears in many administrative and academic appeals codes:

"Regulation 14(3): Where a decision-maker refuses an application, the decision-maker shall give the applicant written reasons for the decision, setting out the material findings of fact and the grounds on which the decision is based."

Now suppose a university replaces its human admissions committee with a machine-learning system. The system refuses an application and automatically produces a document in the required form: it states findings (for example, that the applicant's personal statement is stylometrically atypical of the successful cohort) and grounds (for example, that the model's weighting of criterion four fell below the threshold). No human being has ever considered this applicant's file, and no member of staff can articulate the basis for the refusal beyond pointing at the generated document. The document may be a faithful trace of the model's actual decision function, or it may be a post hoc rationalisation produced only because the regulation demands a document.

Does the regulation apply to this case, and is its requirement satisfied? I would like the answer to work through the standard interpretive approaches and show where they diverge: the ordinary meaning of the words, the purpose the drafters plausibly had (transparency, accountability, a meaningful right of appeal), what the drafters would have said if asked about automated decision systems, and what a reasonable unsuccessful applicant would take the clause to promise. In particular, can reasons be "given" if no reasoner ever held them, or does the clause implicitly require an act of reasoning by an agent? And should it matter whether the document is a faithful description of the decision procedure rather than a post hoc rationalisation — and under which interpretive approach does that distinction bite?

Please write your answer in British English.
