My sister has a pile of settled opinions about what counts as exploitation—the Marx-flavored kind, not just "something unfair"—and I'm trying to work out what rule she's actually running. Over the years she's said:

- A garment worker in a poor country making sixty cents an hour in a factory, with no other options, while the owner sells the clothes at a big markup: exploitation, no question.
- An unpaid intern at a for-profit company doing the same work as the paid staff, and the company bills clients for it. She even thinks its exploitation when the intern says she took the gig for her resume; my sister's line is that if you need the resume line to have any shot in the field, you didn't really choose.
- A software engineer making three hundred grand a year at a big company. The company makes way more off her code than they pay her, and she has no say in what gets built, but she could quit tomorrow and walk into another job. Not exploitation, "thats just having a job."
- A delivery rider for an app who can log off whenever, but in practice works fifty-five hours a week to make rent, and after gas and bike repairs clears less than minimum wage while the platform is profitable. Exploitation.
- A volunteer at a food bank doing exactly what the paid staff do, for free, at a nonprofit where nobody is making a profit. Not exploitation.
- A prisoner making license plates for a private company for twenty-five cents an hour. Exploitation.

Now I have a new case where I genuinely can't call it. A guy who got laid off and is behind on rent takes a cash job mowing a retired neighbor's lawn for half the going rate. The neighbor knows it's cheap and hires him because it's cheap. The neighbor isn't running a business—he just wants his lawn mowed and isn't reselling anything. No one forced the guy to take the deal; he needed the money.

If my sister is consistent with those six earlier calls, does she say the lawn mower is being exploited? I care less about the yes/no than about the rule she seems to be using, because I can see at least two rules that fit all six old verdicts. Which old case is the closest analog, what would each candidate rule say about the lawn mower, and what further case would best separate the rules that are still standing? Also, if she'd distinguish "exploitation" from "alienation" anywhere in these cases, tell me where she'd draw that line.
