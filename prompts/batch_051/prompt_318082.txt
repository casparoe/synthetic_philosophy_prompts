My colleague keeps a running list of verdicts she has issued in our reading group on the question whether death is bad for the person who dies. So far she has ruled as follows: death at 20 is worse than death at 80; it is bad to be told you will be killed in a week, even though the death itself is still a week away; it is not bad for a person that an asteroid will destroy all life on Earth a year after they die painlessly; it is bad for a child to be born with a condition that guarantees death in six months, even if the child experiences no suffering; and there is no time after death at which being dead is bad for the deceased. Her next case is this: a person is in a dreamless, reversible coma; while they are under, they are permanently and painlessly killed, so there is never a moment when they consciously experience their own death or anticipate it. She also explicitly rejects the view that the loss of possible future goods matters only if the person's present desires are frustrated.

Here is the passage from Epicurus she finds compelling, from the Letter to Menoeceus as translated by Robert Drew Hicks:

"Accustom thyself to believe that death is nothing to us, for good and evil imply sentience, and death is the privation of all sentience; therefore a right understanding that death is nothing to us makes the mortality of life enjoyable, not by adding to life a limitless time, but by taking away the yearning after immortality."

What should she say about the coma case if she wants to stay consistent with her earlier verdicts? Identify the two or three principles that fit her record equally well, state what each predicts here, and say which earlier verdict would have to be revised if she hesitates to apply the Epicurean principle to the coma case.
