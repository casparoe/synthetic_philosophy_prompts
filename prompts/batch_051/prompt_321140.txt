Take procreative beneficence — if you're going to bring one of several possible children into existence, choose the one with the best expected life — and give it the Newcomb-treatment. In the usual non-identity-flavored cases the selection info is treated as if it were perfect, and all the action comes from the fact that the candidate children don't exist yet, so there's no existing victim of the choice. Now make the test noisy: the only test the couple has is 70% accurate at picking which of two embryos would have the higher expected welfare, with both embryos equally likely beforehand, and it says embryo A is better. Run the expected-value calculation properly: does PB say take A, take B, or be indifferent, and at what test accuracy does the recommendation reverse? Then loosen the base-rate assumption: what if, before the test, A had a 70% chance of being the better one and B 30%? Finally, say what the noisy case does to the standard debate: does it weaken PB, does it show PB just is an expected-value rule over merely possible children, and does the non-identity point do different work once the information is probabilistic? Be disciplined: state which child actually ends up existing under each choice, and don't let the non-identity part dissolve into hand-waving.
