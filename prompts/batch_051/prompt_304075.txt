I am a teaching assistant for an undergraduate course in moral philosophy, and I am currently grading a batch of midterm essays on Bernard Williams's objections to utilitarianism. Last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether Williams's integrity objection is best read as an early version of the over-demandingness objection, or as something else: a point about the relation between an agent and her own actions, or about what kind of justification an impartial theory can countenance. We did not settle it, and it left me wanting these papers graded with particular care around that boundary. Below is the exam question and one student's answer. Please assign a grade, and write the point-by-point comments that justify it; I want a specific assessment, not a general impression. In your comments, separate what is settled about Williams's view from what is genuinely contested among informed readers, and do not present a contested interpretation as though it were the consensus.

Exam question. Reconstruct Williams's Jim example and explain what Williams takes it to show about utilitarian reasoning. Then explain the one thought too many example and state how its target is related to, and differs from, the target of the integrity objection. Does either example, by itself, refute utilitarianism?

Student answer. In the Jim example, Jim is on a botanical expedition in South America when he comes across a military captain who is about to have twenty Indians shot in reprisal for recent acts of protest. The captain offers Jim a guest's privilege: if Jim will shoot one of the Indians himself, the other nineteen will be released; if Jim refuses, all twenty will be shot. The utilitarian calculation says Jim should shoot, since one death is better than twenty. Williams's integrity objection is that this calculation treats Jim's own agency as merely another route through which the best total outcome can be produced, and it neglects the fact that it is Jim who must pull the trigger. A person may reasonably refuse to do something that is incompatible with the commitments that give his life meaning, even if refusing leads to a worse outcome by the utilitarian's own lights. Williams is therefore arguing that utilitarianism is too demanding: it asks us to abandon our deepest projects whenever the numbers tip the other way.

The one thought too many example makes the same point about personal relationships. A man finds himself in a situation where his wife and a stranger are both drowning, and he must choose whom to save. If he pauses to ask whether saving his wife is what an impartial principle would recommend, Williams says that pause is one thought too many; the fact that she is his wife should be enough. The example is meant to show that love and friendship supply reasons that need no vindication from impartial morality. The difference between the two cases is that the Jim example attacks act-utilitarian calculation, whereas the one thought too many example shows that utilitarianism encourages a self-indulgent attachment to one's own emotions. Neither example refutes utilitarianism on its own; Williams only means to show that utilitarianism is psychologically difficult.
