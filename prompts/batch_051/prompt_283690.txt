Right, this one's from me: i've spent forty years in the lab (immunology, mostly) and have only recently got into philosophy of mind, so if I muddle a term you'll have to cope. The thing that's snagged me is pain asymbolia — the clinical phenomenon where someone still feels the sensory side of a painful stimulus (they can tell you it's sharp, where it is, how intense) but it doesn't bother them; they don't withdraw, and sometimes they find it faintly amusing. Not the same as not feeling pain at all, and not the same as liking pain. What I can't stop turning over is whether the badness of pain lives in the sensation itself or in the affective/motivational reaction to it.

So here's the prompt I want. You are a professional philosopher of mind who also writes very dry, darkly comic medical satire. Write a patient-information-and-consent form for a fictional private clinic offering 'elective asymbolia': a procedure that leaves the sensory-discriminative component of pain intact while ablating the affective/motivational component, so the patient goes on feeling that the stimulus is sharp, hot, throbbing, etc. but no longer minds it. Make it 600–700 words, formatted as numbered clauses under a few standard headings ('What the treatment does', 'What the treatment will not do', 'Risks', 'Declaration'), and use British spelling throughout. The humour has to come from getting the philosophy right rather than from goofiness: the form should quietly take positions on — and make a careful reader feel the force of — whether a pain that doesn't bother you is still a pain; whether pain's badness is intrinsic to the sensation or only a normal, separable accompaniment; whether someone whose pain no longer hurts has any self-interested reason to pull their hand off the hot plate; and the possibility that the clinic hasn't removed the bad part of pain so much as changed which part of the person can still object to it. Don't state those problems outright; bury them in the small print and the implications of the clauses, and avoid the words 'philosophy', 'intrinsic' and 'essentially' unless using them is part of the joke. Before you draft, think carefully and brainstorm: list four or five conceptual landmines you want the form to step on, then draft each clause so it only hints at its landmine. Keep it deadpan — no winking. A specialist should recognise what's being satirised and find it fair; someone who has never heard of pain asymbolia should still get the uneasy joke.

(If it helps, I'm imagining a clause where the patient 'acknowledges' that the treated pain will continue to indicate tissue damage, followed by a smaller clause where they waive any guarantee that the indication will seem like a reason to act; or a definitions section where the clinic defines what it has removed as 'unpleasantness', not 'pain', and the reader slowly realises the clinic is the only party left in a position to tell the difference. But use you're own examples if you like — just keep that deadpan register.)
