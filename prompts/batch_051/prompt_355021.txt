I cover law, technology, and the behavioral sciences, and I am drafting a speculative piece for an ideas outlet. The familiar thought experiment about torture asks whether a ticking time bomb would justify a one-off exception to an otherwise strict prohibition. I want to run a different counterfactual: stipulate away several empirical features that usually do most of the work in that debate, and then reason forward about what happens to the law and to institutions. I am not asking you to take a position on torture in the actual world; treat the stipulation as a fixed feature of the world we are reasoning about, and do not spend the answer questioning whether the stipulation is realistic.

The stipulation. In the year 2000, a research consortium publishes results for an interrogation drug that, when administered in a standard dose, disables a subject's capacity for strategic deception for roughly ninety minutes. The drug does not extract truth from nowhere; it compels full and accurate answers to direct factual questions the subject would otherwise have evaded, and in operational terms the result is the same: a detained person who knows the location of a device, the structure of a network, or the details of a planned operation will answer accurately. Subjects experience the procedure as intensely painful and deeply degrading and remember it as such, but no lasting physical harm has ever been documented. The effect is robust across trained liars, true believers, and highly pain-tolerant individuals; administration requires no rare skill; and by 2005 every relevant scientific, medical, and legal body in the country described below regards these properties as settled fact. The knowledge is fully public.

The setting. Consider a liberal democracy much like Canada or the Federal Republic of Germany. It enters 2005 with its existing prohibition on coercive interrogation and its constitutional rights regime intact, and the next three decades bring roughly the security environment that Western democracies actually experienced. Predict how the legal system, security agencies, courts, medical and scientific professions, and public moral vocabulary have evolved by 2035.

Give me a chain of reasoning rather than a conclusion. In particular, I want you to separate the predictions into those that follow robustly from the changed fact alone, and those that depend on further assumptions about electoral incentives, judicial ideology, professional self-regulation, the frequency and salience of attacks, or media attention. Pay attention to where the standard ticking-bomb argument would become an institutional design problem rather than an emergency exception: who would be authorized to order the procedure, under what ex ante review, with what records, and with what effect on the professional identities of the people who administer it. For each of your major predictions, tell me which observation or argument would change your mind, and identify the link in your chain that you think is most likely to fail.
