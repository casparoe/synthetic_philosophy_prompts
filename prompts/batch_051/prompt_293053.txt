I've been turning over a position on the actual/potential infinity distinction, and I want a candid check. I call it "decidable-touch actualism." The core claim is this: a collection is actually infinite, in a way a finite mind is entitled to, iff there's a finite procedure that decides, for any description you hand it, whether the thing described belongs to the collection, and, for any two descriptions of members, whether they name the same thing. If you lack those procedures but still have a rule for producing more and more elements, you've only got a potential infinity.

So on my view the naturals are actual: membership is decidable (is it built from zero by successor?) and identity is decidable (two numerals name the same number iff they reduce to the same successor string). The rationals are actual too. But the reals are only potential: two decimal names can converge for a billion digits and then diverge, so no finite check settles whether they name the same real. Cantor's diagonal argument then says, on my view, not that the reals form a bigger actual infinity, but that no fixed, checkable list of real names can catch every real name. It becomes a no-closure theorem for finitely checkable lists, not a census of a completed uncountable thing. And Hilbert's Hotel is weird only because we're told to imagine an actual infinity whose identity conditions are left foggy: is the guest after a shift the "same guest" in the relevant sense? If the roster is a decidable list, something has to give.

I know this is near intuitionism, constructivism, and the old potential/actual distinction. But I'm not trying to restrict classical mathematics; I'm happy to treat it as useful shorthand. This is meant as a metaphysics of the distinction, not a revision of proof rules.

So: reconstruct my idea in its strongest form, tell me the two or three nearest existing positions (names are fine, I don't want a reading list), and give me the strongest objection. In particular, tell me wether the identity-decidability criterion makes actuality depend too much on notation or choice of description. Would I be forced to say the same set is actual under one presentation and merely potential under another, and is that fatal or just awkward? Also, please answer in British English.
