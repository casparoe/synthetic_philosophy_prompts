Im rereading Slaughterhouse-Five and want to push it through Boethius on foreknowledge and eternity, and I keep landing on a weird symmetry. The Tralfamadorians see all moments at once, and from that they conclude free will is an illusion; Billy Pilgrim internalizes that and basically stops trying to alter anything, even when he knows what's coming. Book five of the Consolation has God doing something oddly similar—not predicting the future like a super-forecaster but seeing the whole of time at once from eternity—yet Boethius bends over backwards to hang onto free will. So same starting point, roughly: a fixed sequence seen from a point outside time. Opposite conclusions.

I want a reading of the novel that keeps both in play. Where exactly does Vonnegut get from "all the moments already exist and someone sees them at once" to "therefore nothing I do is up to me"? Is there an extra premise being smuggled in, and can Boethius reject it, or is he just hoping it isnt there? And is his distinction between conditional and simple necessity actually load-bearing, or does it quietly collapse into the same fatalism the novel embraces? I'm not asking for a war-and-trauma reading. I want to know whether the novel's time philosophy actually works as an argument against free will, whether Boethius's move survives it, and where the novel pushes back on Boethius in a way the standard reconciliation tends to skip. Basically: who has the better account of what an atemporal view of your own life does to your agency, and does the book make the point better than the bare philosophy can?
