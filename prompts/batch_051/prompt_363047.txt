I'm a condensed matter physicist — comfortable with Lagrangian mechanics, collective coordinates, center-of-mass decompositions, quasi-particles, effective field theory, the whole toolkit — and a philosopher friend keeps reaching for Dennett's idea of the self as a center of narrative gravity. To me it sounds like he's borrowing physics words to make a point that is either trivially true or deeply confused, but I'd rather not be the physicist who dismisses a position without being able to state exactly what it claims.

So: restate Dennett's actual philosophical claim in the vocabulary I trust. Treat a person as a system with a huge number of internal states, behaviors, utterances, and a messy, mostly continuous stream of self-narration; the self would then be some kind of effective or collective variable defined over that mess, not a new substance, not a homunculus, not a cause over and above the underlying pieces. Be as precise as the translation allows: what plays the role of the mass distribution, what gets averaged or integrated, what the center is a center of, and what it can and cannot do causally.

Then give the residue report. Which parts map cleanly onto a center of mass or an effective description, and where do I have to fudge the physics to make it fit? For instance, a center of gravity is objective and computable once the mass distribution is fixed, but narrative gravity seems to have no agreed metric for weighting events, and arguably the center is also supposed to be the thing doing the weighing, which a real center of mass never does. And what has no counterpart at all in my vocabulary: the first-person point of view, authorship, being the audience of the narrative, possibly moral responsibility? For each bit of residue, tell me whether it is a defect in my physics vocabulary or a spot where the original analogy does more work than the physics would license. And if part of the debate between Dennett and people who say the self is more than a useful abstraction is mostly verbal, say which part and why.
