Robert Nozick presents the experience machine as a difficulty for hedonism about well-being. In the original, you may enter a machine that gives you any experiences you choose; inside you cannot tell you are in the machine. Most people, Nozick thought, would still refuse, which suggests well-being is not simply pleasant or desired experience.

Now vary the machine in exactly one respect. All the human beings you encounter inside are real, independently deciding persons who have also chosen to enter the same machine. Their words and actions are not scripted; they are the genuine products of their own deliberation under the same illusion. Only the nonhuman physical context—buildings, landscapes, food, weather, works of art—is simulated. Call this the social experience machine.

Work through how the original anti-hedonist argument fares against the social machine. Does the reluctance to plug in survive, or does it disappear once the machine contains real other subjects? If some people would refuse the original but accept the social version, what exactly explains the difference—and should we conclude that the original worry was about isolation or about reality, or that the two are not cleanly separable? Then spell out how hedonism, desire-satisfaction theory, and objective-list theory about well-being would each evaluate the two machines. In particular, explain whether the social machine shows that the original argument overreaches, or whether even the social machine lacks something the objective-list theorist can say we reasonably want.
