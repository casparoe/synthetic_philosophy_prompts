I spent last evening arguing with a friend about a rule I have been following in mathematics, and I would like a careful diagnosis of whether it is defensible or merely an aesthetic prejudice.

The rule concerns polynomials whose roots cannot be named canonically. Take x² + 1 over the real numbers: its two roots, customarily written i and −i, are interchanged by complex conjugation, and nothing in the field structure of the complex numbers as an extension of the reals distinguishes one from the other. In such cases I refuse to assert, believe, or rely on a statement about one of the labeled roots unless the statement remains true under every relabeling of the roots. I will happily assert things that are invariant under the permutation, such as that the two roots are negatives of each other or that the Galois group acts by swapping them; I will not assert anything whose truth depends on which root I happened to call i rather than −i. My rationale is that a claim that flips truth value when the labels are swapped is tracking my notation, not a determinate mathematical object, and a belief in such a claim is not really a belief about the roots.

My friend objected on two grounds. First, he argued that the rule is far stronger than the legitimate demand that definitions and functions be well-defined; mathematicians routinely have to fix a labeling to refer to a root, perform a calculation, or state a lemma, and a context-relative assertion can be perfectly contentful even if it is not invariant. Second, he argued that I do not actually obey the rule in my own work, since I use i constantly and only afterward check that my conclusions are invariant; so the rule, as I stated it, is either unworkable or insincere, and I have dressed up a temperamental dislike of non-canonical choices as a principle.

I want to know whether this rule, or some sharpened version of it, is rationally defensible as an epistemic habit. If it is not, what exactly is wrong with it—a conflation of well-definedness with truth, an overgeneralization from Galois-theoretic symmetry to ordinary mathematical assertion, or something else? If a defensible version exists, how should it be stated so that it keeps the protection against notation-induced pseudo-facts without making it impossible to work with roots at all?
