In a course on gauge theory, the instructor keeps insisting that fixing a gauge is exactly analogous to choosing a coordinate system: the vector potential is a coordinate-dependent representation of one underlying physical field, just as a single vector can be written in many bases, and the gauge symmetry is nothing but the freedom to describe the same object in different ways. From this the instructor concludes that no gauge-dependent quantity—the potential itself, its phase, the gauge-fixing term—can be physically real, and that the Aharonov-Bohm effect does not refute that conclusion. Audit this analogy. Give the explicit correspondence: what is supposed to map onto the coordinate system, the components, the invariant vector, and the change of basis. Then work through where the mapping breaks down—for instance, gauge transformations that cannot be continuously deformed to the identity, the phase a particle picks up around a closed loop, boundary conditions, anomalies, or the failure of gauge fixing to be unique—and separate the disanalogies that threaten the instructor's conclusion from those that are merely cosmetic. Does the conclusion that only gauge-invariant quantities are real survive the audit? If the coordinate analogy cannot carry that conclusion, say what a better analogy would be and what it would and would not license.
