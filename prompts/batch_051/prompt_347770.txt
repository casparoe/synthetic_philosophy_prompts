I've been keeping a running record of a colleague's judgments about whether, and when, a visitor is morally obligated to follow a place's everyday conventions, and I want a prediction about a new case I plan to put to her. Her record so far runs like this.

In a city where orderly queuing at bus stops is near-universal, a tourist walks to the front of the line and boards ahead of everyone who had been waiting: her verdict, wrong. In a country where servers are paid below a general minimum wage and depend on gratuities for most of their income, a visitor leaves no tip after a full-service meal: her verdict, wrong. In a neighborhood where the ordinary practice is to cross against the light whenever the road is clear and locals rarely wait for the signal, a visitor stands at the curb through a red while locals step past him: her verdict, not wrong. In a country where tipping is not customary and a gratuity is commonly read as implying that the recipient depends on charity, a visitor leaves a tip out of his own sense of politeness: her verdict, wrong. In a country where shoes are invariably removed at the door of a home, a visitor keeps clean, dry shoes on inside: her verdict, wrong. In a town where locals habitually open exchanges with a particular formulaic greeting but would not take a neutral hello from a visitor as rude, a visitor says hello: her verdict, not wrong.

The new case is this. In a city where pedestrians treat the crossing signal as a serious civic norm and obey it even when the street is empty, a visitor steps off the curb against the red light on a completely empty road at dawn. No car or pedestrian is anywhere near, and the crossing is observed by no one. He knows the local expectation.

I want a prediction of her verdict on that new case, made for consistency with her record. To earn it, work out which rule or rules fit all six earlier judgments. Test at least these candidates: a rule that a visitor must follow a local convention only when breaking it would concretely set back or insult an identifiable person; a rule that visitors must honor any local convention locals treat as a basic mark of respect, whether or not anyone witnesses the breach; and at least one further serious candidate, such as a strict when-in-Rome rule or a fairness-and-coordination rule. Say where the rules agree, where they come apart on the new case, which reading best fits her record, which earlier judgment puts the most pressure on that reading, what each rule would commit her to elsewhere, and what supplementary case would most cleanly settle the matter.
