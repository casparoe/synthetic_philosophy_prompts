I'm an undergrad TA for a third-year philosophy of probability course (about 20 students, 50-minute sections), and I've been asked to run a section on Bertrand's paradox. We've already lectured on the principle of indifference and on the idea that a probability shouldn't change just because you redescribe or reparamaterize the problem, so I'd rather not stand there and re-derive the three classic answers. What I want is a game where the students actually produce the three answers themselves and only realize at the end that there's no fact of the matter about which one is 'the random chord' — they should feel the paradox before I name it.

My rough idea is to split the room into three groups and give each group a different rule for 'randomly' drawing a chord: one picks two endpoints on the circumference, one picks a radius and a random point along it, one picks a random midpoint in the disk. Each group computes the probability that the chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Then we compare, and the disagreement is the moment the thing bites — not me explaining why. I'm unsure about the mechanics though: what materials make the three protocols feel equally natural rather than rigged (paper, string, rulers, printed circles?), whether small stakes like candy or a quiz point would help people commit to their answer before they see the others, and how to run the debrief so they see the principle of indifference breaking under reparameterization instead of thinking two groups just made arithmetic mistakes. Could you design this as a concrete 50-minute activity — rules, materials, a timeline, what I say at each step or deliberately don't say, a debrief that maps what happened onto the principle of indifference, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and an honest note on which feature of the paradox the exercise can't reproduce and I'd have to supply myself? Feel free to change the three protocols or the group structure, but keep it runnable in an ordinary classroom and keep it from turning into a lecture with props.
