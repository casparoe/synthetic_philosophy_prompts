A friend and I spent yesterday evening arguing about the problem of logical omniscience and whether it still has any teeth now that we actually build the agents epistemic logic once described. The problem is usually stated as a modeling flaw: standard epistemic logic treats knowledge as closed under logical consequence, so an agent who knows P and knows that P entails Q automatically knows Q, even though no finite reasoner works that way. The stock defense has long been that this is a harmless idealization—real agents merely approximate the ideal, and the idealization lets us pose clean questions about knowledge without getting lost in the mess of finite inference. The development I want to press on is the shift from describing such agents to building them. A language-model agent's practical knowledge depends on how much inference time it gets, how its premises are ordered, and what it happens to retrieve. Does the harmless-idealization defense survive that shift? Or does the shift expose a premise that was doing real work all along—that what an agent knows can be separated from how it draws conclusions—and turn the problem from a philosopher's simplification into a live constraint on bounded reasoners?
