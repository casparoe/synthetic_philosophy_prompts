Friend and I spent last night going back and forth about whether an AI could ever actually want anything, and I ended up with a distinction I want checked. I think there are two different things we call wanting. One is what I'll call scalar wanting: the system has a number it's supposed to move — reward, loss, next-token probability, whatever — and we can loosely say it "wants" to move that number, but it doesn't care about the stuff behind the number. The other is content wanting: the system's behavior genuinely organizes around some feature of the world, like a thirsty person's behavior organizes around water, and the water matters to them, not just a score that tracks water. My claim was that evolution gives us content wanting by accident — we want water because wanting water paid off ancestrally, but what we actually want is the water, not our own fitness — whereas gradient descent can only ever produce scalar wanting, because the loss signal is the whole story and nothing ever makes the content matter to the system itself. My friend says I'm just rehashing the terminal vs instrumental goal distinction, and also that under the hood humans are scalar too — dopamine, homeostasis, all that — so my "genuine vs metaphorical" line does no work. I think he's partly right and thats what bugs me. Is the scalar/content distinction coherent? Is it already in the literature under a better name, and does it track anything real about whether a system can be said to want something in the first-person rather than just metaphorically? Be candid — I half-suspect it collapses, but I can't find exactly where.
