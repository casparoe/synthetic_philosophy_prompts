So a friend and I got into this after a Sunday service. He's been reading up on the regulative principle of worship, and as he explained it, the idea is that in God's public worship whatever hasn't been commanded in Scripture is forbidden. Not just "forbidden if the Bible prohibits it," but: anything the Bible doesn't positively prescribe for gathered worship is an unauthorized human addition by default. He walked me through the argument while we were standing next to the church piano, which probably wasn't an accident:

(1) Any practice in gathered worship that God has not appointed in Scripture is forbidden.
(2) Preaching, praying, singing, and the sacraments are appointed; but the New Testament nowhere appoints musical instruments, four-part harmony, hymns written after the canon closed, or sermons delivered in English rather than the original languages.
(3) So if (1) is right, the piano in our sanctuary is forbidden, along with the choir, the hymnbook, and even our English translation of the readings.
(4) But my friend's own congregation uses all of those things, and he doesn't seem to think his church is in sin for not ripping out the piano and the choir loft.
(5) So either the principle is false, or there's some distinction he's leaning on that he hasn't stated.

He says the standard distinction is between "elements" of worship and "circumstances" of worship: piano, time of day, and room temperature are circumstances, not elements, so they fall outside the principle. But that feels suspiciously convenient to me — changing the instrumentation shapes the whole experience of worship in a way the thermostat doesn't. Which step should I reject, if any? If I give up (1), what's left of the regulative principle? And if I don't, can the element/circumstance line be drawn in a principled way, or does it just get moved so the piano stays?
