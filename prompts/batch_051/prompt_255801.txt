You are a philosopher of social science advising a government department. A minister has proposed making 'rising up the annual international happiness rankings' an explicit national objective, on two grounds: that these rankings, which rest mainly on a single self-report question about how people rate their lives, measure how happy a country's people genuinely are, and that if a country climbs the rankings it has thereby become better off, so chasing a higher rank is a sound goal. I suspect the first claim conflates several quantities such a number could be tracking, and the second does not follow even if the first were true.

Please work through the problem as follows. First, brainstorm the distinct quantities such an index might be tracking — felt affect, reflective life satisfaction, income, optimism, social desirability, mere compliance with a survey question — and the different policy goals each would justify. Second, design the strongest empirically feasible test of whether a change in a country's rank tracks a change in the population's actual happiness rather than one of those substitutes; say what data would count for and against. Third, evaluate the second claim independently: on the most favourable outcome of that test, what is still left open in the move from 'the index went up' to 'the country is better off and should aim to climb the table'? Distinguish the residue that is a measurement problem from the residue that is a conceptual or normative one.

Think carefully before answering, and be explicit about which parts cannot be settled by any data. Use British English throughout. End with a single sentence beginning 'Summary:' that states your overall verdict.
