I chair the awards committee of a professional society, and every year we award a lifetime-research medal. The same dispute breaks out each cycle: some members argue that we should give the medal partly because doing so would lift a neglected subfield, retain a researcher who might otherwise leave, or repair the society's public standing. Others reply that these are reasons to want the society to give the medal, not reasons for judging that this body of work itself merits the medal, and that only the latter should drive the evaluation. Draft a standing screening procedure the committee can run before discussion: a short checklist that sorts each proffered consideration into the appropriate category, specifies what weight each category receives at each stage, and marks the points where committee judgment or a vote cannot be eliminated. Ground the procedure in what philosophers call the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem, but write it for non-philosophers who have about two minutes per nomination, and then run it on three fictional nominations, including one where the procedure tells the committee to reject a candidate whose award would clearly benefit the field and one it cannot fully settle. Close by saying what the procedure cannot settle, separating the parts most people working on this topic would accept from the parts that are genuinely contested, and do not present a contested resolution as the settled position.
