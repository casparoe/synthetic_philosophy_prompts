Last night I had a long argument with a friend about what would make a future actually good to live in — the question people who talk about fun theory are trying to press — and about whether the right standard is total well-being or something closer to how much people enjoy being alive. At one point my friend leaned on an analogy I cannot shake. Judging a future by its total welfare, he said, is like judging a party by how many guests turn up. A jammed room where everyone is mildly and politely bored is not a better party than a smaller gathering where people are genuinely enjoying themselves; the point of a party is the quality of the experience, not the size of the guest list. From this he concluded, without pausing, that a future containing vastly more people who live flat, merely tolerable lives is not better than a future with fewer people who find their lives genuinely fun, and therefore that total welfare is the wrong currency for evaluating futures and per-person experienced quality is the right one.

I want to know how far this analogy actually reaches. Walk me through it. Set out the correspondence table: what plays the role of the guest, the party itself, the host, the length of the evening, the music, the fun? Then show where the mapping breaks. A future is not a bounded evening full of people who chose to attend; it contains everyone who will ever be born, most of whom are brought into it rather than invited. A life can be good without being enjoyable at every moment, while a party that is not enjoyable while it lasts is simply a failed party. And a party is judged almost entirely from the inside, by the felt experience of the guests, whereas a future contains childhood, institutions, achievements, and relationships that may matter even when no one is having fun at that moment. Tell me which of these disanalogies matter for my friend's argument and which do not, and whether the conclusion — that total welfare is the wrong currency for evaluating futures — survives once they are all on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it. If the analogy fails, say what a better one would be.
