I am trying to sort out what I believe about the paradox of trying not to try (wu-wei), and my verdicts on concrete cases are pulling me in different directions. Here they are.

1. A novice golfer is told by an instructor to swing naturally and smoothly. The golfer then tries hard to produce a smooth swing, and the effort makes the swing tighter and worse. My verdict: the instruction cannot be implemented by direct effort; trying to be natural is self-defeating here.

2. An expert cellist, in a high-stakes audition, tries not to think about technique. She then thinks about not thinking and plays rigidly. My verdict: the attempt to be spontaneous undermines spontaneity; yet returning to deliberate control of every note would also be wrong, and the real solution is recovering absorption in the music.

3. An insomniac strains to fall asleep, and the straining postpones sleep. The same person then gives up and reads a dull book without caring whether sleep comes, and sleep comes. My verdict: non-striving plainly works in this case.

4. A beginning tennis player practices a serve through deliberate, effortful, self-monitored repetition, breaking the motion into parts and correcting errors. My verdict: this is exactly the right method, and telling the beginner to just be natural would leave them flailing. Trying and self-monitoring are good here.

5. A person who has stopped trying in every area drifts listlessly through the day, without effort and also without absorption in any activity. My verdict: this is not wu-wei; it is a failure of agency. But I cannot yet say what this person lacks that the insomniac's successful non-striving has, and that gap bothers me.

There is at least one pair that seems hard to reconcile: the insomniac's non-striving works, the beginner's effort works, and the dropout's non-striving does not count even though it superficially resembles it. There is also a pair that may only appear conflicted: the cellist's self-monitoring fails while the tennis player's self-monitoring succeeds. My own tentative principle is that an action is wu-wei just in case the agent's attention is absorbed in the activity itself and the agent makes no second-order attempt to manage their own performance or mental state.

Please test that principle against the five cases, repair it, and tell me what principle or small set of candidate principles best captures the pattern. I would like the response as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Each item should state one candidate principle precisely; then say which of my cases motivate it, which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and what it predicts about one of these two cases I have not settled: (a) someone who spends years in deliberate training specifically to become capable of effortless performance — does the training itself count as the kind of trying that defeats the goal? (b) someone who has fully internalized a skill and performs it with conscious, deliberate care — can that be wu-wei, or does deliberateness automatically exclude it?
