My epistemology professor's rulings so far, rehearsed each week:
- A person who sees a red cube in daylight knows that it is red, and by plain inference from that knows it is not a white cube lit to look red.
- A patient who reads a normal temperature off a new, actually reliable digital thermometer he has no independent reason to trust knows his temperature.
- A person who logs that thermometer's readings for a month, each day inferring from the reading it gave that it was right again, and then concludes the thermometer is reliable, does not know that it is reliable.
- A person looking at the only real barn in fake-barn county does not know it is a barn.
- A person who checks the first thermometer against an independent reference thermometer and gets agreement does know the first thermometer is reliable.

Next week's case: a student reads a large, machine-scored study concluding that human vision is reliable under normal conditions. The study's display equipment and scoring software were built and calibrated by researchers using their own vision, and the student reads the article with his own eyes, but the study's data were generated and analyzed without appeal to any subject's visual reports. Predict the professor's verdict on whether the student knows that his own vision is reliable. Identify the closest earlier ruling, state what principle about knowledge, bootstrapping, and circular confirmation each possible verdict would commit her to, say which theory this pattern of rulings best fits, and describe the further case that would cleanly separate the remaining principles.
