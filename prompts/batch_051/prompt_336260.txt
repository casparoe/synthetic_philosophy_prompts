After a year in which I had to admit that I had for months believed something about a person I care about that I somehow also knew wasn't true, I became preoccupied with a simple question: how is self-deception even possible? If deceiving someone requires getting them to believe what you take to be false, then the self-deceiver would have to intend to make herself believe what, at the very same time, she takes to be false — which looks self-defeating. I started a small reading group about this, and its two most stubborn members now occupy the two classic poles. The intentionalist says the paradox is real but manageable: self-deception is a genuine project of the self on itself, and the only way to save its possibility is to suppose something like a partitioned mind or a temporally extended strategy of steering oneself toward evidence and wearing down one's resistance over time. The deflationist says the paradox is an artifact of the word "deception": in self-deception there is no self-deceiving intention at all; a powerful desire for p simply makes ordinary belief formation go wrong — biased attention, biased memory, biased hypothesis testing — and the person ends up with a false, motivated belief p. Once we stop importing the two-person model of lying, the impossibility problem dissolves rather than gets solved.

We have decided to stop arguing past each other and instead produce the document an adversarial collaboration between these two camps would produce, and I have been put in charge of drafting it. Please write that joint statement. It should contain: (1) a numbered agreed-claims section — precise, non-vacuous claims both parties can sign, including what paradigm cases of self-deception minimally involve, where the apparent impossibility comes from, and which tempting simplifications both reject (such as the idea that self-deception is just ordinary false belief, or that it is just static two-person lying directed at oneself); (2) a disagreements table, with each row stating the intentionalist's commitment and the deflationist's commitment, each formulated so its own side would accept the wording; (3) a short list of cruxes — sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side; and (4) for each crux, a concrete case, argument, or piece of psychological evidence both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. If any part of the dispute turns out to be verbal — for instance, about what should count as an "intention" or a "partition" — say which part and why. Keep the whole document to at most 950 words.
