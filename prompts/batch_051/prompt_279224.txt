In my high school ethics elective we have to peer-grade each other's short answers, and I got stuck on one. The question was:

"Some philosophers argue that individuals have no duty to cut their own carbon emissions, since one person's contribution to climate change is so small it makes no noticeble difference to anyone (the inefficacy objection). Explain the strongest replies to this argument, and say whether you think it defeats individual climate duties."

A classmate wrote:

"The inefficacy objection says my emissions are a drop in the bucket, so not emitting won't make any difference. But this is bad reasoning, because if a million people each emit a little, together they cause a real harm. So by the same logic, each person is responsible for one millionth of that harm, and one millionth of a death is still a harm you caused. You can't excuse it just because the harm is spread out. Also, there might be a tipping point: even if my individual action probably isn't the one that crosses the line, there is a small chance it is the extra ton that triggers some climate threshold. Since the harm of triggering it would be enormous, the expected harm of my emission is also enormous, which makes refraining clearly worth it. So the inefficacy objection is basically the old mistake of ignoring tiny effects just because they are tiny. Therefore each person has a duty to reduce their footprint, and not doing so is just as wrong as punching someone, since the total harm adds up."
