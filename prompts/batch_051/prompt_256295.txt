I have for some years defended a reconstruction of Wang Yangming's doctrine of the unity of knowledge and action, and I would like an honest assessment of how exposed the position is. On the interpretation I defend, the doctrine is neither the homiletic claim that one should practice what one preaches, nor the plainly false claim that assenting to a moral proposition automatically produces the corresponding act. It is a claim about what moral knowledge is: genuinely to know that one should, for instance, care for an aging parent is to be in a state that is partly constituted by appropriate motivational and behavioral dispositions, much as possessing a practical skill is partly constituted by a readiness to perform it. Someone who can recite the requirement while remaining unmoved and inert does not, on this view, know it defectively; they do not know it at all. They possess at most a verbal echo of the knowledge, the way a person can parrot a proof without grasping it. Apparent cases of weakness of will are accordingly redescribed as cases of incomplete or obstructed knowledge: the akratic agent is not failing to act on a truth fully known, but suffering a kind of ignorance of a practical truth.

I want a map of what would have to be true for this view to be false or untenable — and I want it to go beyond the stock objection that akrasia simply seems real. Which phenomena, cases, arguments, or results in neighboring areas, such as action theory, moral psychology, or the epistemology of know-how and understanding, would count against it? Which would be decisive and which merely awkward? Which of the standard objections would a committed defender of the unity of knowledge and action concede as counting against the view, and which would they dismiss as already ruled out by the account of practical knowledge it rests on? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say frankly whether the position, as I have stated it, has been insulated by qualification to the point of risking nothing at all — and if so, whether that insulation is a defect or the correct consequence of treating practical knowledge as a state of the whole person.

The consideration I would personally find most unsettling is not ordinary weakness but the possibility of an agent whose motivational systems are disordered in a way that seems to leave practical understanding intact — someone of whom the most natural description is that they know exactly what they are failing to do.
