I edit the science-and-ideas section of a news magazine, and a couple times a month we have the same fight: should a story about corporate wrongdoing blame the company or specific people? A reporter writes that Meridian Biotech suppressed its own safety data, and an editor changes it to three Meridian executives suppressed the data, and nobody has a rule. I know philosophers treat this as the problem of corporate moral agency — can a company literally be blamed, or is "the company did it" just shorthand for a bunch of individual acts? Half the desk treats a corporation as a legal fiction, and the other half talks as if a company can have a mind of it's own. The stakes are real: a headline can accuse tens of thousands of employees, or let a few decision-makers disappear into the corporate "we."

So I'd like a decision procedure my desk can run in about twenty minutes, before the copy goes to print, for deciding when a story should attribute blame to an organization rather than named individuals. It should be grounded in whatever philosophy has actually worked out about group agents, collective intention, and the conditions for blame. I need: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops and says "attribute only to individuals"; and where it hands the decision to a senior editor because it's a values judgment rather than a factual one. Keep the messy distinctions from getting collapsed — e.g., the difference between a company being causally mixed up in an outcome, a company being the kind of thing that can form and act on a plan, and a company being an appropriate target of moral blame rather than just a useful headline shorthand. I'd rather the procedure honestly flag what it can't settle than pretend it settles everything.

Then run it on three hypotheticals: (1) a pharma company where one executive buried a safety signal, but the company's bonus and promotion structure predictably produced that burying, and the CEO and board never knew; (2) a medical-device company where no single employee intended the bad outcome — a device shipped because five different sign-offs each looked defensible on its own; and (3) a two-founder startup where the founders jointly and explicitly decided to ship a product they knew was unsafe. Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer we won't like, and be explicit about where the philosophy runs out and the final headline language is just a newsroom call.
