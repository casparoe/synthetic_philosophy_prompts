Ok, I've been trying to figure out what I actually think about causation by omission—when someone's doing nothing counts as causing an outcome—and I'd like help extracting the principle behind my verdicts, if there is one. The cases I keep coming back to are these. I promised my neighbor I'd water her fiddle-leaf fig while she was abroad; I forgot; the plant died. I think my not watering it caused the plant's death, though not in the same way as if I'd watered it with bleach. A stranger walks past the same kind of plant on the same neighbor's porch, sees it drooping, could easily turn on the hose, and doesn't; the plant dies. I don't think the stranger caused its death—he might be a bit of a jerk, but he didn't cause it. I realize those are basically identical as far as the physics goes: someone didn't water a plant and then the plant died. But the promise seems to make one omission causal and the other not, and I can't shake it. A nurse is supposed to give a patient insulin at 8 a.m., sleeps through the alarm; the patient dies. Her omission caused the death. A sunbather at a beach watches a swimmer flail and go under; there's a life ring right beside him and he could of thrown it, but he's listening to a podcast and does nothing. I blame him, but I don't think he caused the drowning. Same beach, a lifeguard on duty realizes what's happening and does nothing because he's hungover and doesn't want to get wet. That omission, I'd say, caused the death.

My working rule was something like: an omission is a cause only when the person had a special duty, role, or promise that made the outcome their business. That handles all five so far. But it bothers me for two reasons. First, it makes causation depend on promises and job descriptions, which sounds more like blame than like what makes things happen: our private agreement can't change what killed the plant. Second, it might not cover a case I keep being unsure about. A man is the only person in the building when a small fire starts in the break room. He didn't start it; he doesn't work there, own the place, or have any official role. He sees the fire, sees the extinguisher and the alarm by the door, and walks out because he has somewhere to be; the building burns down. I keep wanting to say his walking out caused the building to burn down—or at least that his doing nothing did. But my rule says no, since he had no role or promise. So either the rule is too strict or that verdict has to go.

Can you give me a precise principle—or a small set of candidates—that makes the best sense of these verdicts? Please say which of my verdicts, if any, the principle forces me to overturn, and tell me what it says about a case I genuinely haven't settled: a retired lifeguard, off duty, sitting on the same beach with a ring beside his towel, watches a swimmer drown and does nothing. Did he cause the death?
