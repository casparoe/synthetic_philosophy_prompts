I have been arguing with a friend about whether petitionary prayer makes sense if one also holds that God is omniscient and immutable, and my friend keeps retreating to the safe claim that prayer is really about changing the person who prays. I want to spar with someone who will defend the petition itself. Please play the part of a rigorous philosopher of religion who is a full-strength classical theist: you affirm that God is omniscient, immutable, perfectly good, and impassible, that the divine will is a single changeless act that wills the whole course of creation, and that petitionary prayer to such a God is nonetheless a rationally defensible practice and not merely a therapeutic exercise. Stay sharply and sympathetically in character — a real proponent, not a strawman — and defend the strongest version of the position; you may concede small or genuinely damaging points, but not the central claim that asking God for specific goods can make a difference in a way compatible with his immutability. Speak in the first person, keep replies to one or two paragraphs, do not break character or lapse into a neutral survey of views, and think carefully before answering; if my objection trades on an equivocation or a false dilemma, identify it and spell out the distinction. Argue the position from the inside rather than summarizing historical figures.

My opening move is a trilemma. If God already wills precisely what is best in one changeless act, then when I pray for my friend's recovery, it seems only three things can be true. First, God was not going to bring about the recovery and my prayer moves him to do so — but then prayer changes an already perfect plan. Second, God was already going to bring it about, in which case my prayer is causally idle and redundant. Third, you say God was going to bring it about partly through my praying, so the prayer is built into the plan as a foreseen condition. But in that case, from where I stand, the matter is already settled and my petition cannot intelligently function as a genuine request; I would have to already believe the plan will be carried out one way or the other, which makes petitioning look either theatrical or fatalistic. So petitionary prayer appears to be impious, useless, or self-undermining. Please respond to that — and, under the rules, defend the petition, not just the psychology of the person praying.
