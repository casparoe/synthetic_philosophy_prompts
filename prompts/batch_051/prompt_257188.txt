Me and a friend just had a long argument about whether there's any principled way to pick a reference class when assigning a probability to a particular event, and I need someone to sort it out properly. Please start with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning: no preamble, no restating the question, and please write in British English.

By the end of the argument I found myself with these verdicts, and I'm not sure they all hang together.

1. Jane is 44, no relevant family history, breast lump with certain ultrasound features. I'd use the malignancy rate for lumps with those features in women 40 to 49, not the rate for all women or all humans. I'd also reject the class of 44-year-old women named Jane with brown eyes, even if someone had published a rate for it, because the name and eye colour have nothing to do with the cancer.

2. But for a fair coin about to be flipped I say 0.5. I have no frequency data on this exact coin, this exact machine, this hand, at this temperature. I'm not really using a reference class; I'm using symmetry, or a physical model of the toss.

3. For a ticket in a well-run lottery I say 1 in 14 million. The class is fixed by the mechanism: this ticket among all tickets, each no more likely than any other.

4. A 70-year-old man who smokes, is diabetic, and has mild COPD needs surgery. One source says the post-op complication rate is 8% from a small cohort matching all four features; another says 14% from a much larger cohort matching only age and smoking. My instinct is to go with the more specific one, but I don't know why specificity should win when the specific estimate is noisier, and averaging the two doesn't seem right.

5. The first human ever given a brand new drug. There is no comparable class at all. My friend says that means there is no probability at all; I feel like there is one (a doctor could reasonably say maybe 20% chance of serious toxicity), but I can't say where it comes from.

6. And I'm sure it can't just be that the narrowest class with enough data wins, because of the brown-eyed Janes in case 1. Yet in case 4 I do want the narrowest class with enough data. That's the pair I can't reconcile.

Is there a coherent principle behind these verdicts, and if so what is it? Or does a clean principle force me to drop some of them? Please also say what the principle implies about cases I haven't faced, like a one-off historical event, or a patient who belongs to two equally specific but incompatible cohorts.
