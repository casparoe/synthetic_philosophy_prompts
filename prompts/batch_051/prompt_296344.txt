I have been turning over a stubborn intuition that I suspect is widespread: that I am the protagonist of my life in a way other people are not, and that other minds, though I do not actually doubt their existence, sometimes present themselves to me as less than fully lit—as if the strangers I pass on the street were running a behavioral script rather than a lived interior. My rational side says the asymmetry is baseless. My evidence for my own experience is direct and private; my evidence for anyone else's is public and inferential; but that difference in access is itself symmetrical, since from behind your eyes I am the one whose interior is inferred. Any argument for my special reality would license every other person to draw the same conclusion about themselves, and would leave me no reason to think my copy of the conclusion is the true one. And yet the intuition survives, especially when other people act in predictable, convenient, or scripted ways.

I would like a short story that dramatizes this tension and pushes it into less familiar territory. Set it in a near-future city where a fashionable wellness-tech company sells a wearable device, a presence monitor, that claims to measure the vividness of a person's present experience from micro-expressions, pupil dynamics, voice, and skin conductance, and reports a continuous interiority score, with readings near zero labeled flat or unlit. The protagonist should not be a philosopher, but someone whose profession or temperament makes them unusually attuned to other people's inner states—perhaps a physician, a poker player, a translator, or a social worker—and who becomes increasingly preoccupied with the device's flat readings for certain people, including someone they love. Write it so that the reader feels the genuine pull of the main-character intuition before seeing it dismantled. Keep the device's readings ambiguous throughout: resist both the resolution that there really are flat, zombielike people and the resolution that the whole thing is only the protagonist's projection. The story should hold the first-person/third-person asymmetry open, and should show what it costs the protagonist to live inside it, rather than closing with a tidy metaphysical answer.
