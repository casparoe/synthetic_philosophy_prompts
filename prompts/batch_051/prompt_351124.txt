I am currently taking an early modern political philosophy course, and we have just finished the unit on Spinoza's theory of the state. For my term paper I want to extend his criteria to a political practice he never encountered: the continuous measurement of public opinion through tracking polls, and the tendency of modern governments to adjust their rhetoric and decisions in response to those numbers. My question is how Spinoza would assess this practice. Would he regard it as a new way for the multitude to make its common judgment known and to restrain rulers in the direction of the shared interest, or would he regard it as a technique that manufactures and manipulates hope, fear, and flattery, preventing the multitude from becoming genuinely united in its affects? In answering, please keep distinct and then combine two strands of his thought: what Spinoza says about the limited rational capacities of ordinary people, and what he says about the stability and power a state acquires when a multitude is led to agree in its affects rather than through coercion alone. I want a single overall prediction rather than a mere alternation between the two readings, and I would like that prediction to respect the fact that Spinoza is neither a straightforward majoritarian nor an elitist. The real test is whether his own categories can yield a determinate verdict about a technology of public opinion that he never had occasion to confront.
