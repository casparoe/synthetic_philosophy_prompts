A research funder adopts the following procedure for every project it supports. Each proposal must contain the project team's own forecast of the total cost and time to completion. Separately, the funder's analytics unit produces a reference-class forecast: using only the applicant's answers to a short, fixed questionnaire, it selects the fifty most similar past projects under a published similarity metric and reports the median cost and median time of their outcomes. The two forecasts are made under seal and then revealed together. The grant's official budget is the midpoint of the two cost forecasts, and its official deadline is the midpoint of the two time forecasts. Unspent budget is returned to the funder; overruns require a written variance. Progress reviews are measured against the midpoint figures, not against the team's own figures. After completion, the team's standing with the funder is based solely on how close the actual cost and completion time came to its inside forecast, and the analytics unit's budget in the following cycle is based solely on how close its reference-class medians came to the actual outcomes of the projects it covered.

Treat this as a mechanism rather than an epistemological position. Work through what it does to how teams write proposals and run projects, and to how the analytics unit chooses reference classes; then say how the two groups interact. Where is the scheme gamed first? Is the midpoint genuinely an exercise in epistemic modesty, or does it smuggle the inside view into the official plan? Which failure modes can be fixed without abandoning the outside-view discipline the scheme is trying to institutionalize?
