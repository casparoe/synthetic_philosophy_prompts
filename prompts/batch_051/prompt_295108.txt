Next month I'm giving a paper at a philosophy of cognitive science reading group, and I need you to prep me for the Q&A. My paper defends a reading of a specific passage from Andy Clark and David Chalmers's 'The Extended Mind' (1998). The passage is:

"Epistemic action, we suggest, demands spread of epistemic credit. If, as we confront some task, a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would have no hesitation in recognizing as part of the cognitive process, then that part of the world is (so we claim) part of the cognitive process."

My claim is that this passage is doing two jobs and the literature never separates them. 'Epistemic credit' is ambiguous between a causal reading and a normative reading. The causal reading says the artefact did part of the cognitive work; the normative reading says the artefact, or the person-plus-artefact system, is the right target of praise and blame, or at least a bearer of epistemic standing. The parity test — 'were it done in the head' — only ever supports the causal reading. But the Tetris rotation button and the navigation slide rule examples only feel decisive because they quietly lean on the normative reading. I defend the view that the only coherent version of the extended mind thesis is a deflationary causal one: coupled systems count as cognitive processes, and 'credit' is just a placeholder for causal contribution rather than normative standing. I call this 'bare crediting'. The paper argues this version still does the work Clark and Chalmers want — it lets us say the Tetris-plus-rotation-button system is a cognitive system without making the notebook a blameworthy agent — but at the price of making the thesis a lot duller than the original pitch.

The audience: mostly philosophers of mind and cognitive science PhDs, with two cognitive psychologists who study distributed cognition in real workplaces (cockpit crews, ship navigation teams) and are mildly irritated by philosophers treating their group-level findings as a theory of individual-plus-tool minds. There's also a philosopher of biology who pounces on anything that looks like a 'mark of the cognitive', and a few people sympathetic to Clark's embodied cognition work who think the extended step is overreach.

Prepare me for the objections in roughly the order they'll come, phrased as those people would actually phrase them, with what I should concede and what I should hold. Tell me which objections have real force, where my replies are weakest, the one reply that has to land for the paper to survive, and the question I'm praying nobody asks. And dont let me off easy.
