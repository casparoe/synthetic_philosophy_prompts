Im kicking around an essay idea that reads Sophocles's Oedipus Rex as basically a Frankfurt case before Frankfurt, and I want help working out whether the reading holds. The thought is this: the oracle at Delphi guarantees that Oedipus will kill his father and marry his mother, so in a strong sense he cannot do otherwise, and yet the play never lets him off the hook — he blinds himself at the end, and the language of pollution and guilt runs through the whole thing, treating him as blameworthy rather than merely unlucky. Frankfurt's whole point is that moral responsibility doesn't require alternative possibilities, that what matters is the actual sequence: the agent did it on his own, for his own reasons, with no one forcing him at the moment of action. Oedipus at the crossroads looks like exactly that — nobody makes him kill Laius; he does it out of his own temper, even if the oracle has somehow already made the outcome inevitable.

But I keep worrying that the play resists the reading. The crossroads scene, and the choices leading up to it, seem to depend on the thought that Oedipus could have just not done it — that the tragedy is built out of avoidable acts that only look, in hindsight, like them realizing the prophecy. And Sophocles never gives us anything like Black the neurosurgeon standing ready to intervene; the inevitability is much more like the gods arranging that his own choices will lead exactly where he doesn't want to go, which might be a different kind of "couldn't have done otherwise" than Frankfurt's. So which reading is more faithful to the play, and where would each be strongest? Does Sophocles actually dramatize responsibility without alternative possibilities, or does the tragedy need Oedipus to have been able to do otherwise — and what should that tell us about Frankfurt's attack on the principle of alternative possibilities? Also, if I bring in the standard Widerker-style dilemma for Frankfurt cases — the sign that triggers Black's intervention is either deterministic, in which case the example already presupposes determinism, or it isnt, in which case Jones could have done otherwise — how does that dilemma map onto the oracle? I'd like you to finish by saying what the play adds to the philosophical back-and-forth that the analytic examples miss: something about how discovering you've done the very thing you spent your life trying to avoid can still feel like guilt, even if you were trapped all along.
