Just got out of a long argument with a friend about whether novelty and variety are actually part of a good life, and I'm left with a pile of gut calls that don't obviously fit together. I want to know what principle, if any, I'm reaching for.

- If two lives are equally pleasant, but one keeps having genuinely new experiences — new places, new skills, new people — and the other is a happy, familiar loop, I want to say the first is better, and not just more stimulating but better.
- But my grandma has done roughly the same things for forty years and seems to be doing great; I don't look at her and think she's missing out on something.
- If I could take a pill that made me stop wanting novelty and left me completely content with routine, I'd still feel like taking it would be a loss — turning something off — even though I'd never regret it afterwards.
- Meanwhile the guy I know whos always chasing the next thing — new city, new partner, new project — never seems to actually enjoy any of it, and I don't think his life is better than a settled one.
- And here's the one my friend hammered me on: two people take up the same hobby. One is excited because it's new; the other is excited because it's good and would love it just as much the hundredth time. I wanted to say the second person's enjoyment counts for more... or maybe the first person's excitement is worth a little less? Something like that.

Is there a single principle about the role of novelty in well-being that gets all of these, or at least close? If not, which of my verdicts should go, and why? And what would your principle say about somone whose memories get wiped every year so they can re-experience their favorite books and places for the first time, over and over — better than a normal life, worse, or exactly the same?
