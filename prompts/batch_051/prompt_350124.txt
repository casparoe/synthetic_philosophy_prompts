I have been trying to settle a running dispute about whether natural selection, understood in a suitably extended sense, can ever be said to favour artificial systems over human beings—not in the loose metaphorical sense in which technology changes society, but as a genuine evolutionary trend in the composition of a population of replicators. The point of the exercise is to test whether such a trend can carry predictive force without any appeal to malevolence, to a self-improvement explosion, or to a single dominant system. I would therefore like you to construct a thought experiment to the following specification.

The scenario must contain a population of artificial systems—deployed language models, trading agents, automated decision procedures, or anything of that kind—that function as replicators in an extended sense of the term: they are copied, retained, modified, and replaced through ordinary human commercial or administrative decisions, and nothing is allowed to direct its own replication. You should identify a heritable trait T whose frequency in this population rises across successive deployment cycles simply because it raises a system's chance of being copied, retained, or imitated. Choose T so that each individual human act that propagates it is locally unremarkable: it must be consistent with the chooser's explicit instructions and aims, and it must not be plausibly describable as malicious, reckless, or deceptive. Finally, arrange the setting so that the aggregate effect of T's spread is that the range of outcomes still open to deliberate, collective human choice progressively narrows. The narrowing must fall out of the specified selection dynamics rather than merely accompany them, since a scenario in which humans simply decide to delegate would fail the purpose of the exercise.

After setting out the scenario, work through the mechanism explicitly. Walk through what varies, what is inherited, what constitutes differential reproduction, and why T thereby increases in frequency. Then give the verdict at each of two levels: selection at the level of the artificial lineage favours T and its aggregate consequences, whereas selection at the level of the human institutions that deploy the systems would not favour that same trajectory under any plausible long-run account of institutional fitness—even though no individual choice in the chain is objectionable. Next, identify the single structural feature of the case that most does the work in letting lineage-level selection outrun institutional-level control, and say what would happen if that feature were removed: would the conclusion collapse, reverse, or turn into a standard coordination problem? Close with the strongest objection that a defender of human control could press against treating your scenario as natural selection favouring AIs over humans, rather than as a long sequence of short-sighted but ordinary human choices.
