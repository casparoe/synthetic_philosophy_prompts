I am a high school student, and a year of arguing about politics with my family and classmates pushed me into reading moral psychology. I have come to find moral foundations theory, as developed by Jonathan Haidt and his collaborators, very plausible as a description of how moral judgment actually works. On that account, moral judgments are produced quickly and intuitively by a small set of cross-cultural foundations — care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, sanctity/degradation, and perhaps liberty/oppression — and conscious reasoning mostly arrives after the fact to justify whichever intuition fired first. The account also claims that the political left and right differ systematically in which foundations they rely on, with liberals leaning heavily on care and fairness while conservatives draw on all of them more evenly. I find this genuinely explanatory: it unifies a lot of otherwise puzzling behavior under a few mechanisms, and it predicts both the sense that the other side is not merely mistaken but disgusting, and the fact that evidence alone rarely changes anyone.

The problem is a firm intuition I cannot shake even after being convinced by all of that. Some political disagreements do not feel like two people simply weighting a shared set of intuitions differently. They feel like disagreements about how things actually should be, in a way that does not reduce to whichever foundation profile happens to be doing the judging. When my uncle and I argue about whether a policy that leaves some people without health care should be changed, I cannot believe the dispute is exhausted by the fact that I am moved by harm and he is moved by something else. It seems to me one of us could be getting something wrong. Yet the theory appears to imply that no standpoint outside the foundations exists from which to say one weighting is more correct than another, since any ranking would have to be made from inside some set of foundations. I find that implication unacceptable — and I also notice that my finding it unacceptable may itself just be my care/harm foundation complaining, which would make my objection circular.

I am not asking you to pick a side and defend it. I want a negotiated settlement between the theory and the intuition. Give the strongest case for treating my intuition as a byproduct of one foundation, something to debunk rather than trust. Then give the strongest case for treating the theory as overreaching whenever it moves from describing how moral judgments arise to implying anything about whether those judgments can be correct. Then say which move you would make — dropping the intuition, giving up or amending the theory, or accepting a reading on which the two are compatible — and what evidence or argument would change your mind. In particular, address whether the common saving move of calling moral foundations theory purely descriptive actually works, or whether it just relocates the difficulty: once I accept that my own conviction that I am right is itself generated by these intuitions, do I have any non-question-begging way to trust some of those intuitions over others?
