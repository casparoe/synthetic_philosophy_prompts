A popular article on gambling and investing lays out the Kelly criterion and then draws some large philosophical conclusions from it, and I want to know whether the summary is fair before I repeat any of it in a talk. Here is the article's summary, in its own words:

"Suppose you are offered a fair coin that pays 2-to-1 on heads and takes your stake on tails. Expected-value logic says to bet the whole bankroll every time, since each dollar wagered has positive expected value; but that guarantees eventual ruin, because a single tail loses everything. Kelly's rule is to bet, each round, the fixed fraction of wealth that maximizes expected log wealth — equivalently, the geometric mean growth rate of your bankroll — which for this coin is 25%. Kelly's analysis is said to show that this rule beats every other fixed-fraction strategy: it maximizes long-run growth, and it also maximizes your expected wealth at every finite horizon. The deeper point, the article continues, is that expected utility theory averages outcomes over parallel universes (the ensemble average), whereas a real bettor experiences one path through time, so rational choice should maximize the time-average growth rate instead. Kelly betting is therefore supposed to show that expected utility maximization is the wrong framework for risky choice, that rational agents act as if they have log utility, and that rejecting full Kelly is irrational."

I can believe the arithmetic, but the jump to "expected utility is the wrong framework" seems like a lot to load onto a gambling formula, and I cannot tell where the summary is accurate and where it overreaches.
