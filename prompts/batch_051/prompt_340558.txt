The tool-vs-agent debate in AI safety has been running for years and it's weirdly stuck. One side says we should only ever build tools — oracles, predictors, systems that emit outputs and never act in the world — because any system that plans and executes will end up optimizing for something we didn't fully specify, and by the time we catch it the damage is done. The other side says a pure tool can't do real work and just hides the agency in the humans around it, who then make exactly the mistakes the tool was supposed to prevent. Both sides seem to accept the same technical facts, so nobody really has an argument that lands. My hunch is that 'agent' is doing two jobs at once: sometimes it means having goals of its own, sometimes it just means taking a long chain of actions without a human in the loop. A system that continuously plans actions to get to an address or to track a fixed index is agentic in the second sense but not the first, and a lot of the standard arguments slide between the two senses without noticing. But maybe that's too neat and there's a real disagreement about risk underneath. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is — verbal, substantive, two different questions under one heading, or something else — and what would honestly count as progress given that diagnosis. Don't survey the positions; diagnose the stalemate.
