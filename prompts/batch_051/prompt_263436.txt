In a seminar I teach on structuralism in the philosophy of mathematics, I like to include one piece that makes its argument in verse, and this year I want that piece for the session on algebraic structure and the idea that objects are given only up to isomorphism. Please write a Petrarchan sonnet — fourteen lines, an octave, a volta at line nine, and a closing couplet — on the question whether it matters how one labels the roots of a polynomial. The octave should give the optimistic answer its due: for x² − 2 over the rationals, one can single out the positive square root of two by appeal to the ordering of the real numbers, and it can look as though the sign belongs to the root itself. The turn should complicate that picture: the ordering is extra structure supplied by an embedding into the reals. Within the field alone, an automorphism fixes the rationals and sends the root we call the positive one to its companion; in the same way, complex conjugation fixes the real numbers and sends i to −i. Any algebraic condition built from the base field that holds of one root holds of the other, so the individual roots are indiscernible and the labels record a choice of viewpoint rather than a discovery about two distinct objects. The closing couplet should state what survives once canonical labelling is given up: not the roots one by one, but the group of symmetries that permutes them — the Galois group — while the labels themselves are artifacts of a choice. The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of mathematics would not wince, and it should still stand on its own for someone meeting the material for the first time.

Please give me the response in two sections, in this order: first the sonnet itself, and then a titled section called Commentary on the choices made, roughly 250 words, explaining how the volta and the closing couplet track the shift from apparent canonical labels to well-defined symmetry, and where the poem compresses the mathematics for the sake of the form.
