Next week I present my term paper to my seminar in the physics of information, and I want to prepare for the question period. The paper defends a specific reading of Maxwell's original demon. My thesis is that the standard Landauer–Bennett resolution of the demon overcorrects by placing the entire thermodynamic burden on erasure of the demon's memory, whereas the real difficulty is already present in the way the demon acquires a selective correlation between molecular position and its own internal state. When Maxwell introduces the being in Theory of Heat, he describes it as able to follow individual molecules and to operate a frictionless shutter so as to let swifter molecules pass one way and slower ones the other, thereby making one side hotter and the other colder without any work. My reading turns on the detail that the demon does not merely observe the molecules; it sorts them according to a criterion of temperature, and it is this selective activity, rather than the later clearing of its record, that should carry the entropy bookkeeping. I argue that the standard exorcisms mistake the demon for a passive measuring device with a memory, when the original passage is better understood as a problem about the physical instantiation of a sorting rule.

The audience is the course's weekly seminar. Two physics PhD students accept Bennett's analysis and will likely press me on why erasure is not sufficient to close the thermodynamic cycle once the demon's memory is treated as a physical system. One philosophy PhD student tends to think the demon is an epistemic puzzle rather than a genuine thermodynamic challenge and will want to know whether my reading changes anything about the second law itself. The professor works on the foundations of thermodynamics and will care most about whether my argument alters the formal statement of the law or merely the informal story we tell about it. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased as those participants would phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and the weakest point in my own argument that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss.
