I am a high school student who has been tutoring younger students in algebra and basic physics, and I have developed a theory about the systematic mistakes I keep seeing. I call it the migration theory of errors. The central claim is that many persistent mistakes are not broken or partial versions of the rule the student was supposed to learn; they are intact, well-functioning rules from a neighboring domain that have been applied across an invisible boundary. For instance, a student who writes (a+b)^2 = a^2 + b^2 is not failing to understand exponents. They are preserving the distributive pattern that worked correctly for multiplication and treating the exponent as though it were the kind of operation that distributes over addition. Similarly, a student who insists that heavier objects fall faster is applying a regularity that is genuinely reliable in everyday, air-resistance-filled experience. The rule is not false; it has just been imported into the vacuum-world of the textbook.

What interests me is that these errors often come with a feeling of obviousness, and the student is not guessing—they have successfully used the imported rule many times before. If this is right, then correcting a misconception is less like replacing a wrong rule and more like doing boundary work: teaching the student which cues mark the edge of the rule's jurisdiction, rather than telling them the rule is wrong. I already know the word overgeneralization, but my claim is stronger than that. Overgeneralization suggests a rule that merely went too far. I am claiming the rule is often exactly right in some other domain, and the error consists only in not knowing where that domain ends.

I would appreciate an honest assessment. Is this already a recognized theory under another name? Does it make predictions that are wrong? Most importantly, can you give me an example of a systematic student error that cannot plausibly be reconstrued as a correct rule imported from somewhere else—and if such errors exist, do they kill the theory or just limit its scope?
