I've been arguing with friends for weeks about whether the right response to police violence is reform, defunding, or abolition, and the discussion keeps dissolving into slogans because we never pin down a concrete proposal. I want to hand you one and have you tell me what it would actually do, not whether it is right in principle.

Here is the proposal. Leave the state's legal monopoly on legitimate force untouched, but make every exercise of that force draw down an account. Each officer starts the calendar year with twenty force units. Every use of force beyond a verbal command is priced by severity — one unit for a compliance hold, three for a baton strike, five for a Taser discharge, fifteen for a firearm discharge that injures no one, and fifty for one that wounds or kills — and the initial classification is made by the officer's supervisor from the incident report and available footage. An officer whose account reaches zero is removed from field duty and may return only after retraining and a hearing before five randomly selected residents of the neighbourhood they usually patrol. A use of force that a separately drawn retrospective panel of randomly selected residents finds, by majority vote, to have been lawful and competently executed is not merely refunded: two units are credited to the account, subject to the same twenty-unit ceiling. The department as a whole also has an annual force allowance, set ex ante at three units per hundred residents in its patrol area; each officer-level debit counts against that pool, and any excess triggers a fixed monetary penalty per unit, taken from the department's overtime budget and paid as an equal cash dividend to every adult resident of the neighbourhoods where the excess occurred. Unused individual units lapse at year's end and cannot be traded, banked, or converted into leave or pay.

I want a mechanistic analysis, not a moral judgement: who is rewarded for what, who is punished, where the first gaming appears — in classification, in officer recruitment and retention, in panel behaviour, in neighbourhood assignments, in how the department handles genuinely dangerous calls — and what second-order effects emerge over five years. Then commit: tell me which single failure mode you would bet on as the largest, why the obvious fixes would not simply remove it, and whether on balance it leaves things worse than the status quo. Don't lay out a menu of possibilities and stop there; take a definite position and defend it. Please write in British English.
