Lately I've been reading environmental philosophers who talk about the "intrinsic value" of nature, and then other philosophers who say the real split is between intrinsic, instrumental, and "relational" value, or that "wilderness" is its own contested category. I genuinly can't tell whether these are distinctions my toolkit already captures—existence values sure sound like intrinsic value, and ecosystem services sure sound like instrumental value—or whether the philosophers are pointing at something my vocabulary is structurally blind to.

Here's the task. Restate the debate between intrinsic, relational, and instrumental accounts of nature's value—including the question whether "wilderness" (the idea of pristine, human-free nature) is doing independent conceptual work or whether the category that actually matters is "wildness" (not being under human management)—in the vocabulary I actually use: use values, non-use and existence values, willingness-to-pay, ecosystem services, externalities, constraints, discounting. Do it faithfully, without smuggling your own commitments into the translation. Then give me a residue report: for each strand, what translated cleanly, what had to be fudged, and what has no expression at all in my vocabulary. Give the whole answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, and no introduction or conclusion. The five points should cover: the clean translations; the distorted ones; the ones that simply don't survive; the wilderness/wildness issue; and your bottom-line verdict on whether the failure to translate indicts the economic vocabulary, the philosophical debate, or both. In each point, flag whether the underlying claim is settled among people who actually work on this or genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
