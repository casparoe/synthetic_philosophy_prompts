I am a fifth-year PhD candidate in philosophy of mind, and in three weeks I am defending a dissertation chapter on what pain asymbolia shows about the badness of pain. The chapter argues for a component view on which the badness of a pain experience is not intrinsic to its sensory-discriminative core. I take pain asymbolia as my central case: the clinical picture in which patients, typically after insular or cingulate damage, can locate and describe a noxious stimulus and classify it as intense or sharp, yet deny that it bothers them, show no aversion, fail to withdraw, and sometimes smile or laugh while reporting it. I read this as a dissociation between the sensory component of pain and a distinct affective-evaluative component, and I argue that the badness of pain rides on the latter. The chapter therefore rejects views that locate pain's badness in the painful quale itself or in the sensory-representational content that tracks tissue damage, and it proposes instead that unpleasantness is a dissociable negative-evaluative layer attached to the nociceptive signal by a motivational system. The load-bearing premise across all of this is that what the asymbolic reports is genuinely pain, not a degraded pain-like sensation from which the bad-making part has been removed by definitional fiat.

The occasion is my departmental colloquium. The audience includes two philosophers of mind who defend representationalist accounts of pain and will want to say that asymbolia is a deficit in how evaluative content is taken up rather than in what is represented; two value theorists working on well-being, one a hedonist, who will worry that I have redefined pain so that the asymbolic simply lacks pain, and who will press me on whether the contingency of the bad-making component has any bearing on whether that component is intrinsically bad whenever it is present; and one empirically oriented faculty member in affective neuroscience who will press on whether the clinical reports and lesion data license the phenomenological conclusions I draw. Several sharp graduate students will also be there.

Flag the single reply that has to land for the chapter to survive the exchange. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and say why that is the dangerous one. The deliverable is a prep sheet rather than a defense, so do not restate the chapter; give me the questions, the replies, and the vulnerabilities.
