I am a software engineer who works on formal verification and type systems, and I have no formal training in philosophy. I have been re-reading Hofstadter's discussion of the MU-puzzle in Gödel, Escher, Bach, along with the lesson he draws from it about jumping out of the system, which he abbreviates as JOOTSing, and I want to know whether that lesson still holds in light of a specific development.

The MU-puzzle is a small formal system. Its alphabet is M, I, and U. Its only axiom is MI. Its inference rules are: (1) from a string ending in I, append U; (2) from Mx, derive Mxx, that is, double whatever follows the M; (3) replace any occurrence of III with U; and (4) delete any occurrence of UU. The task is to determine whether MU is derivable. The standard solution does not proceed by churning through derivations. It counts the I's in a string. Rule 1 leaves the count unchanged, rule 2 doubles it, rule 3 reduces it by three, and rule 4 leaves it unchanged. The axiom MI has one I, and starting from a count not divisible by three, no rule can produce a count divisible by three. Since MU has no I's, it can never be derived.

Hofstadter's general lesson is that certain facts about a formal system are not visible from within it. An agent that only applies the rules of the system could derive theorems indefinitely without ever seeing why MU is unprovable. To see it, one must step outside the system and reason in a richer one, here elementary arithmetic. The richer system notices a preserved invariant, the I-count modulo three, that the rules of the object system cannot see.

The development that seems to me to put pressure on this lesson is the current generation of large language models. Suppose a capable language model is given the rules, the axiom, and the target string, and is asked whether MU can be derived. It often answers correctly, and it can frequently produce the I-count invariant or something functionally equivalent to it. But it does not appear to do what the lesson describes: it does not construct arithmetic, and it does not search for a preserved invariant within a representationally distinct meta-system. It appears to interpolate over patterns in text, much of it text that already contains human solutions to the puzzle. Yet the answer it produces is exactly the kind of fact the lesson says is invisible from inside the system.

Does the JOOTSing lesson survive this development, or does it need repair, or is it dead? Specifically, I want to know which premise is under pressure: the premise that some facts are invisible from inside the system, the premise that seeing those facts requires moving to a genuinely new representational system, or something else. If the lesson can be repaired, state the repaired claim as precisely as possible and say what it loses compared with the original.
