So I'm in a philosophy of law course and there's this pattern in the cases we keep reading that I can't put a name to, and it's bugging me. It's not the standard "did they cause it?" thing. It's cases where someone is clearly involved in a wrongdoing—they know what's going on and they do their bit—but their bit made no causal difference at all. Like a lookout for a burglary who watches an empty street all night; if he'd stayed home everything would have happened exactly the same, because no alarm ever went off and the burglars finished on their own. Or a person who votes 'yes' on a harmful scheme, but the committee already had enough yes votes before it got to them, so their vote was redundant. Or someone who shouts encouragement during an assault but nobody can hear them over the noise.

All three seem blameworthy to me, and I think most people's first instinct is to blame them, but their not but-for causes of anything and arguably they didn't even make the bad outcome more likely. I know this is somewhere near accomplice liability and overdetermination, but those don't feel like exactly the right slot. Is there an established name for this specific puzzle—being genuinely involved and blameworthy without making a difference? And if there isn't, can you name it in a way that keeps it distinct from plain overdetermination and from being an accomplice in the broad sense? I mostly want the conceptual carving here, not just a label: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it's not, and where its edges are.
