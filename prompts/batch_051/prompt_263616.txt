In my metaphysics course we have been working on material constitution for the past two weeks, and the standard case is a statue made from a single lump of clay. At any moment before the statue is squashed, the statue and the lump seem to occupy the same region, share the same parts and weight, and yet appear to differ in their persistence conditions: squashing the statue destroys the statue but leaves the lump intact. In lecture this week, the professor dismissed the view that the statue and the lump are two distinct coincident objects by appealing to the principle that two things cannot be in the same place at the same time. Since the statue and the lump would be in exactly the same place at exactly the same time, he concluded, they must be one and the same thing.

I suspect that principle is doing more argumentative work than is visible, but I cannot yet say exactly which work or quite where a mistake might enter. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that the slogan "no two things can be in the same place at the same time" might be making? Distinguish readings according to (i) what counts as a thing for the purposes of the principle, (ii) what counts as a place — a bounded region, a complete coincidence of boundaries, a complete coincidence of parts — (iii) whether the time clause is doing real work or is merely rhetorical, and (iv) whether the principle is meant to forbid partial overlap, complete overlap, or both, and whether it is offered as a logical truth, a metaphysical necessity, or merely an empirical generalization. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and then identify which reading would actually support the professor's conclusion that the statue just is the lump. Finally, assess whether on that reading the principle is a neutral premise or whether it tacitly assumes the very thing it is being used to prove against the two-object view.
