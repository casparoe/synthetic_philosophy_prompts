I run an informal study circle for adults who are working through large questions on their own, without any university connection. We have been discussing the claim that the world is becoming less violent. As an exercise, I gave one of the members this short written prompt:

"Assess the claim that the world is becoming less violent. Your answer should address the following: (a) whether the claim is true; (b) what has to be settled before the claim can even be assessed; (c) whether there is a single correct way to measure violence; and (d) whether, once a metric is chosen, there is a plain fact of the matter about whether violence is rising or falling."

He wrote the following answer:

"The claim sounds false if you follow the news, because wars and attacks are constantly reported. But the long-run data support it. In non-state societies the chance of dying by homicide was dramatically higher than it is today, and battle deaths per 100,000 people have fallen sharply over the centuries. Since 1945 there has been no direct war between major powers, which is historically unusual. So the decline is real. The standard decline-of-violence thesis gets this much right. The reason we find it surprising is availability bias: vivid recent examples make us overestimate how common violence is. There is a question about the metric, because total deaths can rise while the rate falls, since there are many more people alive now. But that is only a denominator effect; the meaningful measure is the per capita rate, because that tracks each person's chance of being victimized. Once the metric is fixed, there is a fact of the matter about whether violence is rising or falling. The best explanation of the decline, on this view, is that human nature has gradually improved: people today are, on average, more empathetic, less cruel, and more rational than people in earlier centuries. Because the change is in us rather than merely in our circumstances, it follows that the trend can be expected to continue, and that the risk of a major war is now negligible, since populations would not tolerate one. So the claim is true, provided we understand it as a claim about the per capita rate of directly inflicted physical harm."

I want the comments to be specific: quote or point to the exact sentences involved, say what he got right, say where he conflates two different things, where he misstates the explanation offered by the position he is summarizing, and where a conclusion does not follow from premises he has actually given. Assess the answer as it stands; please do not suggest further reading or use citations, since the point is to evaluate the reasoning on the page.
