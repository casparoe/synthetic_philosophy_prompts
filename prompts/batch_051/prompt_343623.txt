Here's a parallel I keep coming back to and I honestly can't tell whether it's deep or just the word "symmetry" doing all the work. Buridan's ass faces two identical bales, has no reason to prefer either, and the puzzle is how it chooses — or whether it starves. In physics, a system in a perfectly symmetric state, like a magnet cooling through it's Curie point or a ball balanced on a symmetric dome, can end up in one of several asymmetric states even though the physics has no preference among them. Which state it lands in is arbitrary from the point of view of the laws, though we usually blame tiny fluctuations.

My mapping: identical options ↔ degenerate minima of a potential; having no reason to prefer either ↔ the symmetric state being indifferent or unstable; actually choosing one ↔ the symmetry breaking; the tie-breaker (the coin, the unnoticed twitch, whatever) ↔ the fluctuation that picks a direction. If this were a real structural analogy, you'd predict that a perfectly indifferent agent can choose, and that any post hoc story about why she chose the left bale is just that — a rationalization after the fact, with no ex ante reason doing the work. That actually sounds plausible to me. But I also see a problem: the ass is supposed to act, while the magnet just undergoes a transition. "No reason to prefer" concerns reasons, and "no symmetry-breaking field" concerns causes. If those are the same kind of gap, then the analogy has already decided a substantive question — it makes arbitrary choice look fine by relabeling tie-breaking as a mechanism. So my question is whether this is a real isomorphism between choice without preference and spontaneous symmetry breaking, and whether anything actually transfers in either direction (does the physics show that an outcome can be selected by noise without being any less rational, or is that exactly what's at issue?). Or is the analogy smuggling something compatibilist-ish in by calling arbitrariness "mechanism"? Where exactly does it break?
