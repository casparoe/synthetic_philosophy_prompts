I've become aware that I follow a standing epistemic policy about arguments whose force depends on the claim that something would be an enormous coincidence. The policy is this: I don't let such an argument move me unless whoever is making it can state the competing hypotheses precisely enough that I can see what else each hypothesis would lead me to expect, and why the coincidence is more probable under one of them than under the others. I apply the same policy to statistical anomalies, conspiracy theories, and medical coincidences, not just to philosophy.

In practice, the policy has meant that I have never been moved by the fine-tuning argument in either of its usual forms. A religious friend argues that a life-permitting universe is vastly more likely if there is a designer than if the constants of nature were set by chance. A physicist friend argues that the same fact is vastly more likely if there is a vast ensemble of universes with varying constants. My policy blocks both, because neither friend can tell me much about what else the designer hypothesis or the multiverse hypothesis predicts that I could go out and check. The religious friend says this is question-begging: I am demanding a degree of formal modeling that ordinary evidential reasoning never requires, and using that demand to stay unmoved by an argument I would otherwise have to take seriously. Is my policy a defensible epistemic shortcut, or is it a bias dressed up as a rule? If the policy is partly right and partly wrong, what would a corrected version of it say, and how could I tell from the inside whether I am applying it honestly rather than protecting my prior beliefs?
