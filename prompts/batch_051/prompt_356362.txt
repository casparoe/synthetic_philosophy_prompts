My physics teacher has this ongoing thing where she rules on whether two things are "really two seperate individuals or just two counts of the same thing." So far her rulings are: two brand-new pennies from the same year and mint are distinct individuals even though they look exactly alike, because each has it's own history and you could paint a dot on one and track it. Two photons (bosons) in exactly the same mode, same momentum and polarization and everything, are not distinct individuals; there's no which-is-which, just a mode with occupation number 2. And two helium-4 atoms, also bosons, sitting in the same lowest-energy state of a trap are not distinct individuals either; she said they're just two counts of a field and asking which helium atom is which doesn't get off the ground.

Next week she said she'll ask about two electrons (fermions) in a quantum dot, one in the ground state and one in the first excited state, described by the usual antisymmetrized wavefunction, so swapping the two particle labels just flips the sign. She hasn't told us her verdict yet. I've been trying to fit her track record to three principles: (a) discernibility, where two things are distinct individuals iff something, even just a relation, distinguishes them; (b) no-labels, where same-kind quantum particles are never individuals no matter how different their states are; (c) exclusion-individuality, where fermions get a weak kind of individuality because Pauli exclusion forces them into different states, but bosons never do.

Predict what she'll say about the electrons, say which principle she's actually running (or if she's running something I haven't listed), and tell me which further case would settle between whatever principles are still left standing after her answer.
