I've been going back and forth on this ever since my friend, who runs a tiny grant program, showed me a application from someone who claimed that if he didn't get fifty dollars he couldn't finish a ritual preventing a future civilization from torturing a number of simulated people so large you'd write it with up-arrows and immediately give up. My friend didn't fund it, obviously, but I actually keep a "weird requests" line in my own budget and now I can't tell whose right. The application was basically a Pascal's mugging: someone threatens to cause an enormous amount of suffering unless you hand over your wallet, and the puzzle is that if you assign any nonzero probability to their being able to pull it off, the expected harm swamps every other consideration, so the math seems to say you should pay. Some people bite that bullet; others say the case shows that expected utility theory, at least the version where you just multiply probabilities and magnitudes without any limit, is broken.

I want to be able to give my friend a real defense of that second camp. Please argue that the correct response to Pascal's mugging is to treat it as a reductio of unbounded expected-utility reasoning: a rational decision procedure should either cap how much possible suffering can count in deliberation or treat extremely tiny probabilities differently than the plain multiply-and-sum story says. I'm less interested in the "the mugger obviously can't deliver" reply and more in what the case forces us to change in decision theory itself. As you make the argument, please separate the moves that are pretty much settled from the ones people who work on this genuinely fight about, and don't present a contested position as if it were the consensus.
