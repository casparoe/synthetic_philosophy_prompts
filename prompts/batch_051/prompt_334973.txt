I've been trying to settle for myself whether signing up for cryonics is rationally required, and I've built an argument I can't find my way out of. I trust the conclusion less than the steps, but I can't locate the mistake. Here it is, numbered so you can point at exactly where it fails.

(1) A rational choice is one that maximizes expected value: the value of each possible outcome weighted by its probability, summed together.

(2) If cryonics gives a person who is legally dead today some nonzero chance of being revived later, then signing up for it raises the probability that I have a very large amount of additional conscious life.

(3) That additional life would be enormously valuable; even a normal remaining life is worth far more to me than the yearly cost of a cryonics plan.

(4) So the expected value of signing up is a small probability multiplied by an enormous value, minus a comparatively trivial cost, and that number is much larger than the expected value of spending the same money on ordinary goods like better food, travel, or leisure.

(5) Therefore, anyone who can pay for cryonics without serious hardship is rationally required to sign up, and choosing those ordinary goods instead is irrational.

(6) But the form of this reasoning works for any option that offers a nonzero chance of an enormous payoff at a small cost. It works just as well for buying a lottery ticket with a huge jackpot, for paying a person who claims to cure aging, or for funding an unproven brain-uploading startup. If the reasoning proves cryonics required, it seems to prove all of these required at once.

(7) A person cannot be rationally required to pursue every long shot with a nonzero chance of a huge payoff. That would demand spending the same limited money many times over, and it would turn rational choice into whatever someone is willing to promise.

(8) So one of the earlier steps, or one of the moves between them, must be wrong.

Where exactly does this argument go wrong? I am looking for the specific premise or inference to reject, why it looked so plausible, and what I have to give up once I reject it. I know the end of the argument is absurd, but I can't tell which earlier commitment is doing the damage.
