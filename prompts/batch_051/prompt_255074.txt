Ok so I'm a high schooler and I've been going down a rabbit hole about if-sentences—the ordinary kind like "if it rains tomorrow the game will be cancelled," not counterfactuals. I've basically talked myself into a position I can't shake: when I'm not sure whether a sentence like that is true, my confidence in the whole thing should just equal my confidence in the second part assuming the first part. The probability of the conditional is the conditional probability. It feels almost too obviously right, and every time I think I found a counterexample I end up explaining it away.

But people keep mentioning these technical results, I think from David Lewis, the "triviality results," that apparently show: if you accept that idea and also treat if-sentences as ordinary propositions with truth values, your whole system of probabilities falls apart or collapses into something trivial. I haven't worked through the math, so I can't tell how serious that actually is.

So pretend I actaully adopt that view. What should make me drop it? I want the strongest objections, counterexamples, and formal results, sorted by how badly they hurt, with a real distinction between stuff that would be fatal and stuff that's merely awkward. Also, what does this view predict that rival accounts don't, so I'd have some way of catching it being wrong in the wild? And be honest about the patches: can I just say it only applies to simple unembedded conditionals, or that conditionals don't have truth values at all? If I do, have I turned the view into something that can't be falsified, and is that a problem for a view like this or just the plausible shape it should take? Mostly I want a map of what my position is risking, because right now Lewis's thing feels either like a death blow I'm too lazy to understand or a technicality that just means I need to be more careful.
