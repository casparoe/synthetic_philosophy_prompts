I have recently been put on a committee that must decide next month whether our firm should discontinue the use of a document-processing platform that a dozen comparable firms have already abandoned. At lunch, two colleagues fell into an argument about this kind of case, and I found that I could not tell who had the better of it. I hope you will referee their exchange move by move, since I am due to vote and do not want to be swayed by confidence alone. Their exchange ran as follows.

(1) Rachel said that when a dozen firms, each with its own engineers and consultants, all converge on the same decision, the result is a pooled sample. If a person has only one fallible private test, ordinary rationality tells them to defer to the crowd. To call that herd behaviour is name-calling for rational updating.

(2) Nikhil replied that a pooled sample requires independent observations. The later firms decided after watching the earlier ones; if the first few choices were enough for the rest, Rachel is counting a handful of original observations a dozen times. That is the structure of an information cascade: each later actor rationally puts aside their own signal and copies their predecessors, and from that point onward the public sequence contains no new information.

(3) Rachel came back that even a later firm that acted under social pressure could have tested the platform and stayed if its own evidence had been strong enough. If it switched anyway despite fixed switching costs, the action reveals that its private evidence was at least not strong enough to block switching. So the later choices do leak something rather than nothing.

(4) Nikhil answered that they leak nothing of the sort once reputational costs enter. If the cost of being the lone holdout exceeds the cost of switching, an actor with strong private evidence for the platform would still switch. The act is then compatible with almost any private signal, so one cannot read a lower bound from it; the incentives, not the evidence, set the decision.

(5) Rachel said that, by that standard, no one could ever learn from the choices of others, because one can always invent a social story to neutralise any action. The practical question is not whether the crowd is a perfect instrument, but whether following it is a better policy than treating one's own paltry private test as sovereign. As a policy, it is.

(6) Nikhil granted that the practical point had force but said it proves less than she needs. The same observable pattern — a dozen firms switching one after another — is generated both by an information cascade and by reputation-driven imitation, and an outside observer cannot distinguish which process is running. Cascades are also fragile: a small piece of contrary public evidence can reverse what looked like a robust consensus, which shows how little information has accumulated in the sequence. Unless she can separate those two processes, her policy cannot tell the difference between pooling evidence and amplifying the first few signals, which may well have been wrong.

Please referee the exchange move by move. For each of the six moves, say whether it is clearly right, plausible but overstated, or mistaken, and justify the rating briefly. Then say who is right about which specific point, what important distinction or consideration both of them are missing, and which overall position emerges in better shape.
