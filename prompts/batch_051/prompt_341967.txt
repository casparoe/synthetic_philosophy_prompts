A collegue in our department's formal epistemology reading group has a rule of thumb he swears by: when he has no information linking two variables, he treats them as independent. He calls it a presumption of independence. I tried to formalize it, and I've ended up with an argument that seems to show the whole thing is either dogmatic or vacuous. Here's the argument as it now stands:

(1) If you have no evidence bearing on the relationship between X and Y, you should treat X and Y as probabilistically independent.
(2) Treating X and Y as independent is assigning probability 1 to the proposition that they are independent — say, that their correlation is exactly zero.
(3) If you assign probability 1 to a proposition, then by Bayes's rule no future data can lower its probability; in particular, no pattern of observed co-variation between X and Y could ever lead you to think they were linked.
(4) So someone who follows (1), formalized as (2), can never come to learn a correlation between X and Y, no matter how strong the data look.
(5) But the presumption is clearly meant to be a starting point, not a trap: people who use it do sometimes discover correlations (smoking and lung cancer is the familiar case), and they take themselves to be learning those correlations from the data.
(6) But if treating X and Y as independent requires less than probability 1 — say, a prior that merely favors independence — then the rule no longer picks out a unique epistemic state: there are infinitely many such priors, each encoding a different view of how strong the dependence is and in what direction.
(7) So the presumption of independence is either dogmatic, because it forbids ever learning a dependence, or contentless, because it doesn't tell you what to believe or how to update.

The conclusion (7) seems too strong — people clearly get something out of this heuristic. I'd like to keep (1) in some form, and I don't want to give up (5), since the whole point of a default is that it can be abandoned. So I'm stuck. Give me a diagnosis: which numbered step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the real mistake is the whole set-up of assigning a prior to "X and Y are independent," then I want to know what the alternative is, and what stops it from just being the arbitrary-prior problem in (6).
