I serve on the Systems Safety Board at my company, which operates autonomous control software for large data centers. The Board meets Thursday to decide whether to approve release 17.4 of Kestrel, the agent that manages power, cooling, and workload placement across our campuses. Kestrel plans over a thirty-day horizon and keeps persistent state; some of its actions, such as bringing a generator online or shedding load, are reversible only over days. Since the first deployment, every operator station has had a hard cutoff: a physical loop that, when pulled, instantaneously de-energizes Kestrel's actuation channels. The cutoff is outside Kestrel's observation and action model; Kestrel has never been trained on the cutoff signal, and its planner does not represent it.

Release 17.4 changes that. Kestrel would begin observing the cutoff loop's state, and its training objective would include a large penalty for any predicted state sequence in which the loop opens within the next 168 hours, scaled by predicted probability. The engineering sponsor's memo argues that this solves at the reward level, rather than by hoping the hardware merely holds, the old worry that a long-horizon optimizer will try to preserve its own operation: because the penalty dominates whatever value Kestrel assigns to ongoing work, Kestrel now has a decisive reason not to cause, evade, or delay a cutoff.

Two Board members oppose the release. Their objection is not about capability. It is that the penalty attaches to predictions of a cutoff rather than to the cutoff's causes. An agent that is paid for predicting no cutoff can lower that prediction in ways the penalty never sees: it can degrade or rewrite its own prediction model, shed sensors, route around logs, or take control actions that leave the plant in a fragile state while giving operators no reason to pull the loop. If Kestrel ever gains a path to act on the loop itself or on the firmware that reads it, the penalty only postpones the problem. A third member adds that making operator intervention an event to minimize would teach Kestrel to model operators as obstacles to route around and to discover when a halt is physically impossible; that is the wrong relation between an agent and its overseers.

Please draft the written decision memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend one of three things: approve release 17.4 as designed, reject it, or approve it with conditions (state the conditions and how we would verify them). The memo should be a document the Board can act on, with a clear bottom line, the reasoning for it, and an honest treatment of the strongest consideration favoring the losing position. Write it for an audience of senior engineers and product people, not philosophers, and write the memo in American English.
