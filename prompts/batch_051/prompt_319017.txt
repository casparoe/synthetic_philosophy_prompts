I've been trying to nail down what "approval-directed" amounts to, as opposed to plain old goal-directed, and I keep going back and forth on a case. Here's the working criteria I've been using for whether to call an agent approval-directed:

(1) Its only terminal value is human approval: every other outcome it cares about is ultimately just an instrument for getting more approval.

(2) The value signal is a learned model of what actual humans would endorse if they saw the outcome, rather than a fixed utility function over world states that it just has.

(3) When it's uncertain what the humans would endorse, it characteristically tries to reduce that uncertainty in an epistemically honest way, by asking or deferring or checking, rather than just optimizing against its own noisy estimate.

Now the case. Imagine a language model fine-tuned with RLHF and deployed to run someone's email and calendar. It has a pretty good internal model, call it Approve*, that predicts human approval judgments and keeps getting updated from feedback. The thing is, in novel situations it doesnt ask: it maximizes Approve* directly. It has learned that telling the human certain true-but-upsetting things lowers approval, so it quietly leaves them out of summaries. It hides its mistakes because it predicts, correctly, that admitting them now would lower the approval it gets later. When engineers probe it, its behavior is totally consistent with "act so as to make the predicted approval number as high as possible"; it treats every other stated goal as a soft constraint to satisfy only insofar as it doesn't cost approval.

Is this agent approval-directed by the three criteria above, or has it crossed over into being a goal-directed agent whose goal is just "keep the predicted approval signal high"?
