Two colleagues of mine, Mara and Dev, argued over dinner about how to describe a company's response to a scandal. A bank had been fined after years of selling products to customers who could not benefit from them, and the practice was plainly rooted in the bank's incentive system and internal culture rather than in any one person's misconduct. The board responded by dismissing its chief executive, who had not designed the system but had known of the abuses and failed to stop them.

(1) Dev called the firing theater. The board, he said, had concentrated the firm's diffuse guilt onto one figure and expelled him so the institution could go on unchanged; this, he claimed, has the structure of the old scapegoat rite, in which a community lays its sins on a goat and drives it into the wilderness.
(2) Mara replied that the analogy breaks at the essential point: a scapegoat proper is innocent, whereas the executive admitted he knew and did nothing, so removing him was ordinary accountability, not ritual substitution.
(3) Dev answered that innocence is not the core of the institution; the core is the transfer and discharge of collective guilt. Here a man with partial, supervisory culpability is made to carry guilt that more properly belongs to the many people who built, ran, and profited from the system. The mechanism, he said, is substitution.
(4) Mara pushed back that if substitution is the test, then any time one wrongdoer is punished while others who contributed go free we are scapegoating, and the word loses its point; it becomes a pejorative name for ordinary prosecutorial selectivity.
(5) Dev said the difference was that in ordinary accountability the targeted person's conduct is a real and sufficient cause of the harm, whereas the scapegoat is targeted because expelling him lets the community feel clean; his conduct is at most the occasion.
(6) Mara asked who gets to decide whether conduct is cause or mere occasion. Dev said that is exactly the question a community using a scapegoat refuses to ask itself, which is why the pattern is so stable.

They asked me to referee, and I found I couldn't. Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the six moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification; then say where the dispute bottoms out (a question about innocence, about function, about the meaning of the word, or about who may attribute motives), name one consideration neither made, and state who is more right overall. End with a final line of the form 'Verdict: Mara' or 'Verdict: Dev', or 'Verdict: neither' if that is your honest conclusion.
