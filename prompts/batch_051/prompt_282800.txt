You are a philosopher of aesthetics specializing in the philosophy of music. Write a dialogue between two equally sophisticated philosophers who disagree about how purely instrumental music can be sad.

One holds a contour-resemblance view: music is sad because its structural features—slow tempo, low register, soft dynamics, falling pitch contours, and drooping rhythmic gestures—resemble the acoustic and bodily contours of human sadness, such as sad speech prosody, weeping, and slumped posture. On this view, sadness in music is a perceived relational property of the sound itself; hearing the music as sad does not require positing or imagining anyone who actually feels sad.

The other holds a persona view: resemblance alone is not enough, because a drooping willow or a heavy sky can resemble sadness without expressing it. Music is sad only when it is heard as the spontaneous outward form of an imagined agent's inner state—an indeterminate persona whose emotional life the music makes manifest, much as a fictional character is presented through a text.

Have them press each other with hard cases and objections, such as: the willow objection; the charge that the persona view replaces musical expression with fiction and then has to explain fiction's emotions instead; the charge that the contour view cannot distinguish depicting sadness from expressing sadness; what each view predicts about listeners who report sadness in the music without spontaneously imagining a persona; and whether the fact (assumed for the sake of argument) that listeners across cultures tend to match slow, low, falling contours with sadness—rather than with other emotions—would decide or merely describe the disagreement.

The dialogue should be a genuine back-and-forth in which each character engages the other's strongest arguments, concedes local points where appropriate, and gradually reveals where the disagreement really lives: in phenomenology, in ontology, or in what best explains the same data. Do not settle the question by authorial fiat; leave both positions as live options while making clear what evidence or considerations would count against each.

Before writing, briefly sketch each character's strongest moves and the exact point at which their views diverge; then write the dialogue, aiming for roughly 700–1,000 words.
