Right, I've been roped into something and could do with a fresh take. I'm the philosopher on our hospital's research ethics committee, and we're stuck on a randomised surgical trial that's currently paused. The trial compares a new minimally invasive repair against the long-standing open repair (say, for a shoulder problem — the details don't matter much). There's a planned interim analysis due in six months, but before that could happen one of the recruiting surgeons came to us and said he's no longer in equipoise: he's done nearly thirty of the new procedures and is now convinced the new repair is better for most patients, so he can't in good conscience keep randomising people to the open repair. At his urging the data monitoring committee did an unplanned look, and there's a modest trend favouring the new procedure on the primary endpoint — p around 0.08, with the stopping rules not formally triggered because this look wasn't in the pre-specified schedule. The trial statistician says this is exactly the sort of trend you'd expect from peeking, and stopping now would waste the data and be unfair to the patients already randomised. The surgeon says continuing means knowingly giving some patients the worse operation, which he treats as a breach of his therapeutic duty. The committee's split roughly down the middle between 'stop and report' and 'carry on'.

I've been asked to draft a short memo for the committee recommending what we should decide and why. I want it to be genuinely usable, so give a clear recommendation, set out the reasoning an ethics committee could cite, and steelman the side you're rejecting before you say why it doesn't win. Also — we keep saying 'equipoise' as if we all mean the same thing by it, and I'm fairly sure we don't. The memo needs to sort out who's uncertainty matters, uncertainty about what, and how much of it, for a decision like this. Be concrete about criteria we could apply going forward, not just to this one trial.
