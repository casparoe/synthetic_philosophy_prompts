I'm sketching an essay on self-deception and I'd like somebody to poke holes in the argument before I commit to writing it up. Here's the rough idea.

The standard puzzle is that self-deception looks impossible: if I deceive myself into believing p, I have to both know not-p (to do the deceiving) and believe p (to be deceived), and that's a flat contradiction in one head at one time. The usual moves are either to split the mind into compartments that can keep secrets from each other, or to go deflationary and say there's no intentional deception going on, just motivated bias in how I gather and weigh evidence. I think both leave something out.

My idea is to reframe self-deception as a two-stage, across-time thing instead of a simultaneous state. Stage one: I act as the architect of my own evidence stream. I skip the appointment, don't open the bank statements, avoid the friend who would tell me the truth, surround myself with people who confirm the comfortable story. Stage two: I form my belief from that now-rigged evidence stream as if it were a neutral sample. The deception, if it's anywhere, lives in the relation between those two stages.

Here's the part I want to push. The two-stage story isn't enough on it's own, because I could do both stages and still be aware the whole time that I built the stream. That would just be self-imposed ignorance or wishful thinking, not self-deception. So I want to add a condition: the rigging has to be untagged in memory. The person has to not attend to, or not retain, the fact that the evidence stream is one they rigged. It's a kind of motivated failure of source memory: I remember the evidence but lose the index that says this evidence was selected by me, for a reason.

The essay would then argue that what's distinctive about self-deception is not contradictory belief, but a failure of coordination between a designing self and a believing self over time — and in particular a memory/tagging failure that keeps the believer from seeing the design as designed. I'd contrast it with Mele-style deflationism (which I think is right that there's no single moment of knowing not-p while believing p, but too quiet about why it feels like something was done to you) and with Sartrean bad faith (which I think gets the self-aware, project-like quality right but makes it sound too voluntary).

Comments I'd especially want: (1) Is the untagged-rigging condition doing real work, or am I just pushing the contradiction from belief into memory? (2) Does the account handle the person who knew all along — the spouse who half-knew about the affair but still managed to be shocked? (3) Is the architect/occupant split just the homunculus problem again with better branding? (4) Am I actually disagreeing with the deflationary view, or just adding stage directions to it? Be blunt about which part a referee would reject first.

Also, please write the comments in American English.
