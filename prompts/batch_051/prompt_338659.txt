Ok here's one I'm trying to get straight before I teach the proof, and I'd like a case built to order rather than a literature review. Good's theorem, roughly, says a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer should never turn down costless information: the expected value of the information, assessed before seeing it, is always non-negative, because in the worst case you can ignore the information and do what you'd have done anyway. I want a thought experiment where that conclusion seems to fail for an otherwise sensible agent. Specifically: someone who would pay a small but real cost (or forgo some benefit) to avoid receiving a piece of information that is costless in the ordinary sense and that is genuinely relevant to a decision they're about to make. The point isn't to refute the theorem; it's to find the exact place the setup slips out from under it. Constraints: one agent, ordinary life (no demons, no perfect predictors, no weird physics), and the preference for ignorance has to come out of an intelligible utility function rather than being bolted on as 'knowing things just feels bad.' The cleanest version would be one where what breaks is that getting the information changes the decision problem itself—the set of acts availible, the consequences, or the states—rather than the agent updating incorrectly. So after you give the scenario, go through the theorem's assumptions and say specifically which one is false in your case, what a defender of Good's theorem would say to disqualify it, and whether the information was actually costless or only money-free. Finish by naming the feature of the case that does the work, and say what the case collapses into if you remove that feature. Write the answer in American English.
