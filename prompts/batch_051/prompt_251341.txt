At a historical linguistics reading group I attended, two members fell into a long argument about a question that had been meant only as an icebreaker: at what point did Latin stop being Latin and start being Italian? Both knew the history well, and neither gave ground easily. I wrote down the exchange as faithfully as I could.

Elena opened by saying the question should be dissolved. Lay down an unbroken chain of parents and children from the first century BCE to a modern Italian city: every child learned the local speech of the previous generation, so any answer must pick a generation across which the language changed into a different language. But those adjacent generations understood one another as well as any parent and child do; to say the child already spoke Italian while the parent still spoke Latin is to invent a rupture the speakers themselves would not have noticed. Calling the change a boundary is a retrospective projection onto a continuity. It is a heap problem, she said, and the mistake is asking when the heap ceased to be a heap.

Marco replied that the heap analogy does more work than it can bear. In the chain, the first generation and the last could not converse at all, so a real breakdown lies somewhere; continuity at each step is compatible with a chasm between the ends. Besides, language is not only a spoken continuum: Latin survived for centuries as a written and schooled standard while the ordinary spoken language drifted beneath it. The interesting question is not when one speech variety imperceptibly turned into another, but when those two things—the learned written standard and the everyday spoken language—came apart in the eyes of the people using both. That, he said, was a genuine rupture, not an arbitrary point on a gradient.

Elena answered that this only moved the problem to her side. Once you say the rupture concerns writing versus speech, or self-description, or grammar, you are choosing a criterion, and different criteria give different centuries. If the date changes with the criterion, there is no single fact of the matter about when Latin became Italian; there is a continuous tradition to which we apply the names "Latin" and "Italian" for our own purposes. Nor is Italian the only descendant of Latin: Latin branched into many vernaculars, and Italian is merely the one later elevated into a national standard. To single out the lineage leading to standard Italian and ask when it separated from Latin is to build the answer into the question.

Marco said that from the fact that several criteria can be chosen it does not follow that none is better. A language is a social fact: it exists when people treat a way of speaking as a common object—when they name it, write it for one another, teach and correct it, and use it to distinguish themselves from outsiders. That gives self-description and the rise of a written vernacular a privileged role. People did not merely drift into being Italian speakers; at some point they identified their ordinary speech as distinct from Latin and began writing the ordinary tongue instead of reaching for the learned one. That transition was uneven, but it was real and can be located in a period, just as a biological species can be real even when the exact generation of its origin cannot be pinned to a year.

Elena replied that self-description is a trailing indicator. People went on saying they spoke Latin long after their speech had left the classical language far behind; identity lags practice. And it gives a different answer depending on whose identity is surveyed: a notary in one city, a cleric in another, a poet, a merchant. If the birth of Italian depends on whose self-description is privileged, the original question has no single answer; it dissolves into a family of answers about particular communities and genres. The species analogy, she added, cuts the other way: species boundaries are drawn by biologists for specific theoretical purposes, not found lying in nature.

Marco answered that the lag only shows that self-description must be used with care, not that it is unusable. When a community keeps the old name for speech that no longer fits it, the old name is doing social work—often claiming continuity or authority—and that social work is itself evidence of a new situation, not evidence that nothing changed. As for variation by community, it means the answer will be regional and by register, which is exactly what one should expect from a social object; no one expects one date for all of Italy any more than for all populations of a species. The fact that a language is born unevenly does not make the question empty; it makes the answer plural. One can say: Italian was born when ordinary people began to own a standard—to write it, police it, and transmit it as something distinct from Latin—and that happened at different times in different centers. That is a real answer, and it is better than dissolving the question, because it preserves the one fact the dissolution loses: after a certain period, people treated the language they spoke not as decayed Latin but as an inheritance to be defended. That change in stance is not a retrospective projection; it happened, and it is visible in what people began to do.

They turned to me to break the tie, and I could not do it convincingly. Please adjudicate the argument: who is right about which specific points, where does each position overreach, is there anything important both of them are missing, and who is, on balance, more right? Present each position in its strongest form before comparing them.
