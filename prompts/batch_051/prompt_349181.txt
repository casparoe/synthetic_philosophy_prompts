So in my philosophy of cognitive science class we've been doing the imagery debate, and I keep coming back to Pylyshyn's side. His move, as I undestand it, is that the classic scanning and rotation results are perfectly compatible with the mind using something like descriptive, propositional representations plus people's tacit knowledge of what seeing or physically rotating a thing would be like. The experiments show the image being manipulated, not that there's a picture in the head. Fine. But since he was writing, there's now a bunch of neuroimaging showing that forming a visual image produces topographic activity in early visual cortex: imagining something large lights up more of V1, imagining peripheral details spreads activation in the right retinotopic way, and decoding studies can even read out some of what a person is imagining from patterns in visual areas. That feels like it ought to damage his argument, but I can't make the claim precise. Does the tacit-knowledge objection actually get refuted by this, or does it just move up a level — the brain is doing something spatial, but the representational format that matters for cognition is still descriptive? If the argument survives, say exactly which weakened version survives and which premise had to be dropped; if it dies, identify the premise the imaging evidence kills. I want the structure of the argument laid out, not a scorecard of who was right.
