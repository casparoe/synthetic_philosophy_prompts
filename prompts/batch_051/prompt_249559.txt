I am a PhD candidate in philosophy working on the informational bases of welfare economics, and I spend one day a week embedded in a small regulatory-review office in the federal government. The office screens the welfare analyses attached to proposed regulations before they reach political sign-off. The analysts are capable and overworked, none of them has formal training in social choice theory, the queue is always full, and every few weeks an analysis stalls or gets sent back because it either quietly assumes that quality-adjusted life years or monetary equivalents can be added up across people, or refuses to make a comparison that the data, properly understood, do license. The stakes are high: these figures become the figure, and they shape rules affecting everyone.

Please write a decision procedure the analysts can actually apply in no more than an afternoon, fitting on one page in checklist form, with two short pages of worked cases allowed. For each type of welfare comparison, the procedure should say what factual input and what informational assumption would be sufficient to license it; where the procedure stops with a no and hands the matter to a political authority because the decisive question is one of values rather than measurement; how to express partial comparability as a range of permissible weighting schemes rather than a single point estimate; and how to detect when an analysis has smuggled in a zero point or a common unit. I want the procedure to force a clean separation between two questions that analysts habitually run together: comparing levels of well-being across people, and comparing gains and losses across people.

Then run the procedure on three cases: a rule whose costs fall heavily on one region while its benefits are diffuse across the whole population; a comparison of present costs against benefits to people two centuries from now; and a proposal to deploy a single fixed quality-of-life index to weight years of life across patients with different conditions. At least one of these should come out as a clear no, and for the third case I expect the procedure to mark the boundary between what measurement can settle and what only a normative decision can settle.
