You're a professional epistemologist, and I'd like you to think carefully rather than give me the standard patter — but first, silently brainstorm before you commit to anything.

I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been reading a popular-science book about knowledge in which an analogy does a huge amount of quiet work. The book says knowing is tracking: a belief counts as knowledge when it tracks the truth the way a thermometer tracks the temperature. A thermometer reads 20°C because the room is 20°C; if the room were 19°C it would read 19°C; and if it read 20°C while the room were 22°C, we'd call the thing broken. The author then applies that counterfactual test to belief. You know you have two hands because the belief 'I have hands' tracks the fact: if the nearest way of being wrong is that you've lost them in an accident, you'd no longer believe you have them; and in nearby worlds where you do have them, you believe it. Fine.

But then the book draws a conclusion that genuinely threw me — and I'm a bit sceptical of it, though I can't yet see the fault in the picture. It says knowledge is not closed under deduction. You can know you have two hands while failing to know that you're not a handless brain in a vat being fed the experience of having hands, because the first belief tracks hands, but the second would still be believed in the world where you are in the vat. A friend who did philosophy says this is basically Nozick's tracking theory, and that the thermometer picture is what makes the conclusion look inevitable: a thermometer is built to track one quantity under normal operating conditions, whereas a belief has to answer to indefinately many alternatives, some of which the believer has no equipment to discriminate.

I want you to audit the analogy, not just tell me it's an analogy. Then go through the disanalogies and seperate the harmless ones from the load-bearing ones. Finally, a straight verdict: does the conclusion that closure has to go survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the thermometer analogy carrying it the whole time?
