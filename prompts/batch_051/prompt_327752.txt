I am not a lawyer or an academic; I run a small building firm, and twice in the past decade our firm has been harmed by decisions of a public body that, everyone later conceded, were careless and wrong. On legal advice, we were told there was no realistic claim because of the immunities that surround public authorities. It has left me turning over a larger question: what actually restrains the state from doing wrong, if the courts will not treat it as a wrongdoer like anyone else? I have been trying to think this through with a concrete hypothetical, and I would like you to work through it carefully rather than give me a simple verdict.

Suppose that a common-law country very like Britain or Canada passed a statute taking effect on 1 January next year. From that date the Crown and every public body lose their special immunities in civil claims. They can be sued in the ordinary civil courts for negligence, nuisance, breach of statutory duty, and similar wrongs under exactly the same substantive rules as a private person or company. There are no special defences: no immunity for discretionary or policy decisions, no need to show bad faith before a regulator, police force, or child-protection service can be liable for a careless operational mistake, no bar on suing the legislature or the courts themselves, and no special cap on damages. Individual officials remain personally liable where their own conduct would make a private person liable, but the state must indemnify them. Legal aid and the ordinary costs rules are unchanged.

I want your reasoned prediction of how the first ten to fifteen years play out. I am less interested in whether it ends in disaster than in the chain of reasoning about mechanisms and incentives. Work through, specifically: how the day-to-day behaviour of front-line officials and their managers would change; what kind of claims would flood in and which would remain rare; how the courts would cope with being asked to say what a reasonable policy or a reasonable level of inspection would have been; what happens to unavoidably risky public services such as policing, child protection, road maintenance, and licensing; and whether the state would mostly adapt by insuring, settling, and budgeting, or whether something deeper would change in how it governs. Then mark clearly which links in your prediction are robust, which are guesses, and what fact about the real world, if it turned out differently from what you assume, would most change your answer.
