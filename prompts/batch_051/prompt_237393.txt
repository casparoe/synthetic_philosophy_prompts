I've got a rule for reading dead thinkers: their last considered word gets the most authority, because that's the view they'd hold after facing the strongest objections, and earlier books are mostly stages on the way to wherever they ended up. I'm writing a term paper on Mill and the rule is backfiring. In his last years he worked on a set of unfinished drafts on socialism—published only after his death—where he is noticeably warmer to socialist proposals than in anything he chose to publish. He's drawn to cooperative ownership as a replacement for wage labor, open to public provision of some things and to serious limits on inherited wealth, and pretty caustic about the moral costs of the existing propery system. But the drafts are fragmentary, never fully revised for publication, and they sit a little awkwardly next to the books he actually put out under his own name. My friend says I've invented a "final-words fallacy": people can be mid-revision when they happen to die, and posthumous notes aren't a considered statement of anything. He thinks the late socialist drafts should get less weight, not more, than the published work.

Is my policy defensible? Can it be restated to handle unfinished posthumous material, and what would the corrected rule say about how much weight Mill's late socialism should get when I attribute a final view to him? Give me exactly six numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion.
