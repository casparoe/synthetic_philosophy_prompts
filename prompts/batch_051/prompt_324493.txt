Before I retired I worked as an ecologist, and in retirement I have been reading moral philosophy; the idea I keep returning to is consequentialist cluelessness—the worry that for interventions in complex systems, long-run consequences ramify in ways that make point-estimate cost–benefit reasoning spurious. A foundation I advise is considering the following rule, and I want a mechanistic account of how it would actually behave, not an assessment of whether it is the right response to cluelessness.

Each program proposal must carry two documents. The tractable core is a causal model that stops at the last effect whose sign the organization would be willing to take a small bet on. The speculation annex lists plausible effects beyond that point, each with a mechanism and a sign but no numerical magnitude. Funding is lexicographic: proposals are compared first on the core, and only when two fall within five percent on the core does the annex break the tie, in favor of the program with the larger proportion of positive-sign mechanisms listed. Staff budgets are renewed retrospectively. Core analysts lose budget when five-year checkpoints show they drew the boundary short of effects that were predictable from evidence available at the time; annex authors are scored only on whether their mechanisms appeared with the expected signs, never on magnitudes.

Work through what this selects for: how applicants and reviewers will draw and game the boundary between core and annex, what happens to programs whose benefit arrives late, where the design breaks first, and whether the failure modes can be fixed without reintroducing the numerical long-range forecasting it was meant to avoid. Please separate what follows from the rules themselves from what depends on contested assumptions, and do not present a contested view as settled.
