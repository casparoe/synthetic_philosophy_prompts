You are an expert in the philosophy and law of online speech, fluent enough in distributed-systems engineering to think natively in that vocabulary. I am an upper-level undergraduate taking a seminar on extraterritorial jurisdiction over online speech, and before university I worked as a network engineer; distributed systems remains the framework in which I actually reason. I want a translation exercise, and I want the residue report to matter as much as the translation itself.

The legal debate, as I currently understand it, runs like this. A state may claim authority over online speech in three modes: jurisdiction to prescribe, jurisdiction to adjudicate, and jurisdiction to enforce. When speech crosses borders these come apart. A court in one country may order a platform to remove content globally, not merely for users in that country. A data-protection authority may insist that its rules apply to a foreign company because that company processes data about its residents. Some accounts say online speech should be governed by the country of origin, others by the country of destination, and others by a targeting or effects-based criterion. I want you to restate this entire debate in the vocabulary of distributed systems: namespaces and authoritative name servers, autonomous systems and routing policy, anycast, CDN edge nodes and origin servers, cache invalidation, geofencing as access control, middleboxes, and related concepts. Do not merely decorate the legal positions with engineering words; rebuild the arguments inside the framework, so that the disagreements over origin, destination, and targeting become disagreements about where along the path from origin to reader a policy should be applied, and about which node is authoritative for which scope.

Then give me the residue report in three parts. First, which claims translate cleanly, because they are genuinely claims about coordination, authority, scope, or enforcement. Second, which claims translate only with distortion, and where you had to stretch the networking concepts to make them fit. Third, which claims have no counterpart at all in the distributed-systems vocabulary — I am especially interested in democratic legitimacy, the rights of speakers and readers, remedies for individuals, and the normative difference between a state coercing a platform and a platform choosing to filter. Finish with your honest verdict: do the untranslatable parts carry the real weight of the legal debate? If so, is my vocabulary simply missing the normative concepts, or is it usefully exposing that some of the legal debate is less precise than it looks? Please begin by brainstorming the mapping before you commit to a final version, and think carefully throughout about the distinction between authority and mere technical feasibility.
