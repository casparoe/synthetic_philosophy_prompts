I am a novelist at work on a courtroom novel, and I have stalled on a scene that has turned out to be more philosophical than legal. The judge is seating a jury, and she is unusually aggressive in voir dire: she excuses prospective jurors for holding strong views about the police, about domestic violence, about corporate defendants, and about psychiatrists, and she instructs the seated jurors that they must reach their verdict solely on the evidence presented in court, setting aside whatever they had believed before entering the room. My protagonist, one of the jurors, tries to obey this instruction and discovers that she cannot tell where her prior beliefs end and her interpretation of the evidence begins; the scene is meant to show the instruction quietly turning from a demand into an aspiration.

Before I can write the scene, I need to understand the theory of evidence and rationality that such an instruction silently presupposes. Does a legal system that screens out prior opinion presuppose something like the claim that, given a sufficiently long and careful presentation of shared evidence, rational inquirers who begin from very different starting beliefs will eventually converge on much the same conclusion, so that a juror’s starting point is merely a temporary nuisance that the evidence will wash out? If so, under what conditions is that convergence supposed to happen, and are those conditions met by the selective, adversarial, and comparatively short presentation of evidence at an actual trial? Alternatively, if evidence is always interpreted through a lens formed by what a person believed before the trial began, then what would have to change — in the questions posed during voir dire, in the wording of jury instructions, in jury deliberation, or in the standard of proof — for the system’s treatment of prior belief to be internally consistent?
