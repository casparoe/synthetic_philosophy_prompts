I am a novelist. I run a monthly craft seminar for twelve working crime and courtroom novelists; we meet for ninety minutes, and by house rule we never begin with abstract talk—everything has to come out of a scene or an exercise. I am drafting a novel in which a civil claim and then a criminal charge rest almost wholly on statistical estimates: a bus company that operated, say, four-fifths of the blue buses in town, and a stadium that counted ticket sales and knows that a large share of the crowd entered through an open gate without paying. I want a session that makes the writers feel, in their hands rather than as a proposition, why a probability can be very high and still fail to point at the person, and why readers and jurors demand evidence that singles someone out. The labels, if they come at all, should come only in the debrief.

Design that session for me as something I can run from the page: a ninety-minute exercise for the twelve of us, no opening lecture. Stage the two situations as writing-room decisions the participants must actually make—should the victim pin the accident on this company, should the stadium pursue this named spectator, and at what odds would they themselves feel the verdict had been earned. Use assigned roles (counsel, fact-finder, skeptical reader, company or stadium spokesperson), recorded forced decisions at several odds levels of your choosing—sixty, eighty-five, ninety-nine percent are fine, and adjust as needed—and a stakes scheme of points or chips rather than real money. Include a phase in which participants compare a purely statistical case with an individuated one (a witness who saw a driver, a scrape of paint, a ticket stub), and a mechanic in which each participant must write down the exact sentence about this individual that they would be willing to stand behind; the statistical cases should resist that sentence in a way the individuated cases do not. Give me a timeline in minutes, a materials list, and what I should say as facilitator at each turn.

Then give me the debrief. It should draw the distinction out of what the participants actually did: the moment their confidence detached from the percentages, the point where someone asked whether we are allowed to say it was this one, the way shifting the reference class moves the probability without adding any evidence about this defendant. Map those moments onto the contrast between naked statistical evidence and case-specific or individuating evidence, and onto the legal standards of preponderance of the evidence and proof beyond reasonable doubt. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—participants treating the high number as decisive because the arithmetic is clean, or letting a civil standard absorb the demands of the criminal one, or quietly inventing an individuating detail the scenario never supplied—and how I should steer each of those back toward the point. End with an honest note on what the exercise cannot reproduce: presumably the moral gravity, finality, and real-consequence stakes of an actual verdict, which I will need to supply in how I frame the scenes and close the debrief. No reading, no citations, no named theorists; everything has to work through what happens in the room.
