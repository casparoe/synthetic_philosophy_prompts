I've been teaching a graduate seminar on longtermism and the malleability of values, and one of the sharper students has been defending an argument that I find difficult to answer. In schematic form, the argument runs as follows. (1) The values we now hold are the product of a long and thoroughly contingent causal history — evolutionary pressures, cultural transmission, historical accidents, and personal biography — and at no point in that history were values chosen from a standpoint outside some already-contingent evaluative perspective. (2) There is no evaluatively neutral standpoint, available to us or to anyone else, from which one stage in such a causal chain could be rated more authoritative or more correct than another; any attempted rating would have to use the standards of some stage. (3) The same is true, mutatis mutandis, of every future stage in the drift of values: after our values have drifted, the values of the future will present themselves to their holders as authoritative in just the way ours present themselves to us now. (4) So the judgment that some possible future value system is bad, or worse than ours, cannot be tracking a merit or defect in the future system itself; it can only report how things look from our present, contingently formed evaluative standpoint. (5) But a judgment that can only report how things look from our contingent standpoint cannot provide a reason to spend present resources on steering or preventing value drift. (6) Therefore the project of preventing the far future from ending up with bad values is a form of temporal parochialism — no more rationally binding than a future civilization's judgment that our values were the bad ones — and it should not guide our decisions.

I cannot accept the conclusion, because the argument seems to prove too much: it would equally warrant indifference toward a future whose values had become stably cruel or self-enslaving, provided only that those values too had a contingent genealogy. Yet I cannot locate the misstep, and I do not want a reply that merely insists that some values are objectively better than others. Which premise, or which step in the reasoning, should be rejected, and what would rejecting it commit one to? In particular, what is the least costly view that lets one reject the conclusion while still taking the contingency of our own values seriously? Finally, please say how confident you are in your diagnosis and single out the point of which you are least certain.
