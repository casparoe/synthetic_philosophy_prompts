Public-health agencies, safety regulators, and popular-science writers often compress everyday dangers into micromorts, a one-in-a-million chance of dying, so that activities as different as going under general anesthesia, scuba diving, riding a motorcycle, and driving a fixed distance can be placed side by side as comparable numbers. The tables are meant to be read directly against one another, and people are invited to draw conclusions from them about whether a drive is worth the risk or whether some widely shared fear is overblown.

Yet the unit is also selective. A micromort score counts only death, and only in probabilistic form: it says nothing about nonfatal injury, chronic disability, dread, the sense of control, whether the risk was voluntary or imposed, whether the probability is spread across thousands of people or concentrated in a few, or whether the deaths would come decades later or immediately. And the same institutions that publish such comparisons often treat risk differently elsewhere—requiring far more stringent reductions for involuntary environmental exposures than for risks people choose on the road, and valuing a prevented death at different amounts depending on the context in which the risk arises.

What theory of value and of risk does the practice of presenting dangers in micromorts presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as the practice will bear, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with and from what it leaves open. In particular: does using a common mortality unit commit the practice to treating deaths as fungible, to consequentialist aggregation, or to the view that ex ante probabilities exhaust what matters about risk? Or is the practice compatible with several theories of value? Finally, suppose the implicit theory were replaced by one according to which dread, control, consent, and the distribution of harm matter intrinsically and not merely as inputs to a probability-of-death number. What, specifically, would have to change in the micromort table and in the everyday-risk advice built on it?
