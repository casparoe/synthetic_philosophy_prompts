I'm writing a paper about whether the emotions we apparently feel for fictional characters are genuinely the same kind of state as ordinary emotions about real people, and I'd like your help working out what an experiment could actually establish. The familiar conceptual case runs like this: fear that someone is in danger seems, in real life, to require believing that someone is actually in danger; yet a viewer who is tense, flushed, and covering their eyes while a character walks into a trap knows perfectly well that no one is actually endangered. This is the paradox of fiction, and philosophers have taken opposite routes out of it. Some conclude that the viewer's state cannot be literal fear because it lacks the relevant belief and the practical motivations that ordinarily belong to fear; it is at best an 'as if' analogue. Others reply that this is bad introspection: the physiology, the tears, the flinching, and the retrospective report of having been afraid are all real, and only the object of the state is unusual. I want to move past this standoff by asking what a test could show rather than what a definition can stipulate.

You are a professional philosopher of mind with a background in experimental philosophy, and you have been asked to bring evidence to bear on the claim that a fiction-induced state is a genuine emotion of the same psychological kind as fear or pity directed at a real person, rather than a merely simulated or 'as if' state that shares the surface features of emotion but has a different underlying nature. Start by brainstorming candidate operationalizations — physiological measures such as autonomic arousal or facial electromyography, behavioral measures such as willingness to pay or exert effort to prevent the fictional outcome, developmental and cultural comparisons, or anything else you judge promising. Think carefully about confounds.

To make the task concrete, consider an experiment in which the same distressing footage is presented to two groups, framed respectively as a scripted fictional scene and as a real documentary recording, while self-report, autonomic, and facial-muscle data are collected. Would a null difference in affect across the two framings, combined with a clear difference in the belief that a real person is suffering, count in favor of the same-kind reading? Or would it only show that emotional physiology can be triggered by imagined content in the way a film score can trigger shivers? Evaluate that design, propose a stronger one if you can, and then lay out exactly what pattern of results would favor each reading. Finally, be explicit about the conceptual residue no experiment could settle — for instance, whether an emotion's object must be believed real as a matter of its essence, and whether the dispute would at some point have to be closed by theory or stipulation rather than data.
