I'm a novelist, which means my working theory of knowledge is really a theory of point of view: who sees, how close the reader is to the seeing, and how much of what lands on the page has already been run through a narrator's concepts before the reader gets to it. I'm fluent in that vocabulary — free indirect discourse, psychic distance, showing versus telling, interiority, subtext, the camera eye — and I rely on it every day to decide what is going to feel "raw" in a scene. Lately I've been trying to get my head around an argument in epistemology, the one Sellars called the myth of the given. The way I understand it, the claim is that there is no bare, pre-conceptual "given" in experience that can justify what we believe: anything that could stand in a justificatory relation to a belief must already be conceptual, so the picture of experience as an unmoved first input is a myth. But this sounds suspiciously like a mistake I watch young novelists make, and also like something I secretly believe when I'm drafting. I keep reaching for the brute sensory detail, the image before the narrator has named it, the scene that "just shows" the reader what happened — as if showing could hand over something uninterpreted and let the reader form there own concepts.

So here is the task. Restate the anti-given position in my craft vocabulary — point of view, psychic distance, showing versus telling, interiority, subtext — and make the translation honest. Don't just tell me fiction is interpretation and call it a translation; do the actual work of carrying the claims across. Then tell me what didn't survive the trip. Is the novelist's dream of the unmediated image a real counterpart to the philosophical given, or is it a stylistic effect that has nothing to do with justification and foundations? And where the translation breaks down, is that because my vocabulary is too crude, or because the philosophical notion was doing more work than it had a right to?
