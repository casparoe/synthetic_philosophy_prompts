Exam question:

Consider the claim that the English past tense is formed by a single grammatical rule (add the suffix spelled -ed), and that apparent exceptions such as sang, kept, and went are not genuine exceptions but rather the frozen residue of regular historical sound changes. Write a short essay evaluating this claim. Your answer should (i) explain how the allomorphy of the regular past-tense suffix is conditioned; (ii) define suppletion and distinguish it from ordinary irregularity; (iii) explain the sense in which sound change is regular and how regular sound changes can nonetheless leave behind synchronically irregular alternations; and (iv) say what the productivity of the regular rule with novel verbs does and does not show about how irregular forms are represented.

Student answer:

The claim in the question confuses the synchronic sense of "regular" with the diachronic sense, and it is only partly right. The productive rule for the English past tense does not add a single invariant suffix; it adds a suffix whose allomorph is selected by the stem-final segment: roughly [ɪd] after alveolar stops, [t] after other voiceless segments, and [d] elsewhere. Its productivity is shown by the fact that speakers give novel and nonsense verbs this past tense, and by children's overregularizations such as breaked when retrieval of a stored form fails.

Irregular past tenses are usually taken to be stored in the lexicon as whole forms rather than generated by that rule. Suppletion is a special case of this. Go/went is the canonical example, where the past form is not phonologically derived from the stem at all. Historically went comes from a different lexical item, wend, and not from any stepwise sound change applying to go. Ordinary irregularity, by contrast, still preserves a single stem and a phonological relation between the alternants, as in sing/sang or keep/kept.

Sound change is regular in the Neogrammarian sense: it applies mechanically across the lexicon to every form containing the affected sound in the triggering environment. Regular sound change can nonetheless leave behind synchronic irregularity because the conditioning environment can later be destroyed, so that a once-predictable alternation survives only as a morphological fact. Thus the vowel alternation in sing/sang is a residue of the regular Old English sound change called i-mutation, which fronted a back vowel before a high front vowel in the following syllable; once the conditioning suffix was reduced, the alternation became opaque and had to be listed. Because the historical process is regular, the original claim is correct that apparent exceptions are frozen regular sound change.

The productivity of the regular rule shows that it is a default available when no listed form is retrieved. It does not show that regular inflected forms are never themselves stored, since a speaker could store walked and still derive it by rule. One further fact confirms the two-route story: irregular past tenses are concentrated among low-frequency verbs, because a form a speaker rarely encounters has no opportunity to be pulled into line with the default rule and is simply memorized as an exception, whereas high-frequency irregulars are the first to regularize. In sum: the regular past tense is produced by a default rule, irregulars are listed, and because the irregularities are historically derived from regular sound change, the conceptual boundary between rule-governed and listed forms is less sharp than the two-route terminology suggests.
