I have been trying to work out what, if anything, makes an original worth more than a copy that is indistinguishable to my senses, and my own verdicts on particular cases will not sit under any single rule I can state. I want you to find the principle behind them if there is one, and to tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to give up.

Consider a painting that has hung in my parents' house for my entire life. I value it partly for the image and partly because it is supposedly by a minor regional painter of the 1880s. A conservator has now established that it is a modern forgery, perhaps twenty years old, from an unknown hand, and no genuine original is known to exist. Nothing about the object itself has changed, yet my valuation has collapsed: I no longer want it on the wall, and I now think of it as nearly worthless. That verdict is not obviously consistent with my next one. A firm makes museum-grade reproductions of famous paintings, honestly sold as reproductions, with the paint texture and surface reproduced. I would happily hang one and take real pleasure in looking at it. I would not pay the price of the original, but I would treat the reproduction as a perfectly good object for my purposes. So the discovered forgery seems worthless to me as an object, while the honest reproduction seems valuable as an object, even though neither is what it purports to be. I cannot say what explains the difference.

Now a third case. A counterfeit wristwatch is made of the same alloy, with the same crystal and the same movement, and cannot be told from the genuine article without opening the case. I would wear it without discomfort and pay a good fraction of the price of the genuine watch. If I discovered I had been sold it as genuine at the genuine price I would be angry, but my anger would be about the deception, not about the watch on my wrist. Here authenticity matters to me almost not at all, in sharp contrast with the painting.

Fourth, a novelist I admire published several books under a pen name. When I learned his real identity, my valuation of those books did not change in either direction; the name on the cover was a deliberate mask, but the books were entirely his own. The masking of authorship did not matter. Fifth, a different novelist died leaving an unfinished novel, a detailed outline, and some drafted chapters. A publisher hired a competent writer to complete the book, and it appeared under the dead novelist's name with a note acknowledging the completion. I value this book much less than his wholly-authored novels, and I mentally classify it as being after him rather than by him, even though the plot is substantially his and I enjoyed reading it. Sixth, an archive announced the discovery of an unknown letter by a major scientist. Investigation showed it to be a pastiche assembled by a forger out of sentences the scientist really wrote elsewhere. Every sentence is genuinely his, and the letter contains no claim he would have rejected, but the letter itself was never written or sent by him. I regard it as worthless as a historical document and nearly worthless as an object that anyone would collect, although I would still quote the individual sentences with confidence. Seventh, a nineteenth-century sailing ship has been maintained in a museum for over a century. Every timber, plank, rope, and nail has been replaced at least once in the ordinary course of repair. I would still take my children to see the ship, and I do not think its value has collapsed in the way the forged painting's did, although no part of it was present at the original launch and no part of it was made by the original builders.

My vague working theory is that an object's distinctive value, the value over and above being useful or pleasant, depends on its being the physical product of the particular act of creation I take it to be. That would explain why the forged painting fails, why the pen-name novel passes, and why the counterfeit watch never needed to pass. But the ship seems to falsify it: not one of its parts is a product of the original act of building, yet I value it as the historical thing. And the theory does not, as far as I can see, explain why the discovered forgery is worth less to me than the honestly labeled reproduction, since neither is the product of the act I care about.

Can you state precisely the principle, or the small set of principles, that best captures my verdicts? For each element of the principle, tell me which case forces it. Then apply the principle to cases I have not listed: a perfect forgery made by the original artist but in the style of a rival and sold as the rival's work; a digital file for which no original physical object exists; a historic building restored so heavily that almost nothing original remains; and a genuine old manuscript that turns out to have been written by someone other than the person traditionally credited with it. Then say bluntly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether in those cases I should revise the verdict or revise the principle. I am less attached to the individual verdicts than to finding the most honest and economical description of what I apparently value, and I would rather you overturn a confident intuition than smuggle in a clause to save it.
