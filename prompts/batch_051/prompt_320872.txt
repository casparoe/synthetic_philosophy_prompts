I am a doctoral student in comparative moral psychology, and I find myself pulled in two directions. On one side is Wang Yangming's doctrine of the unity of knowledge and action (zhī xíng hé yī), whose economy I find compelling: it denies that there is any genuine case of knowing the right thing to do while failing to do it, and so treats moral failure as defective knowledge rather than weakness of will. On this picture a person who can recite the words but does not act knows in only a superficial, verbal way — rather as a person who has never felt pain does not know pain. On the other side is a conviction I cannot shake: weakness of will is real. I have known, with as much certainty as I know anything, that an action was wrong or imprudent, and have done it anyway, and it seems false to say that I merely had not really known. I am not asking which side should win. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that is given up, what it would take for the doctrine to be the thing that is given up, and whether there is a reading of 'know' — or of 'action' — on which the unity thesis and the reality of akratic action both survive.
