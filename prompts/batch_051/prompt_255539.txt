Last week in my seminar on the philosophy of fiction we hit a puzzle I haven't been able to shake, and I'd like to work through it properly. Readers seem to accept almost any descriptive departure from reality when a story stipulates it. If a novel tells me that in its world people pass through walls, that water is denser than lead, or that a character wakes one morning transformed into an insect, I add these facts to the fictional world and read on without resistance. But if the same novel tells me that in its world it is morally right to torment a child for amusement, or that betraying a trusting friend for one's own convenience is the honourable thing to do, something in me refuses. I cannot simply add the proposition to the story's world the way I added the physics. Instead I reinterpret the passage — the narrator must be unreliable, or the word "right" is being used in some inverted sense — or I find myself unwilling to play along at all. The same reader who effortlessly imagines impossible physics balks at a perverse morality, and the split seems to hold across people. So ordinary imaginative practice contains an asymmetry: descriptive stipulations succeed where evaluative stipulations fail.

I want to know whether anything actually justifies that asymmetry. Is there a single principle that generates both halves — that explains why "imagine that gravity points sideways" works while "imagine that cruelty is right" does not? My classmates have floated candidate explanations: maybe we cannot conceive of moral truths being otherwise; maybe we can imagine it but refuse, because we do not want to be complicit; maybe authors simply lack authority over what is morally true in a fiction; maybe the trouble is not with imagining at all but with not wanting to export a monstrous moral belief back into real life. I would like these tested rather than merely rehearsed. Test them against nearby cases. We have no trouble imagining a character who sincerely believes the monstrous thing — so why is imagining it true different? We also resist certain non-moral stipulations, such as a world where a bachelor is married or where two and two make five — does that show the real line is not between the moral and the non-moral, or only that the asymmetry needs restating? And what should we say about a reader who immerses herself in the evaluative outlook of a character whose values she rejects — is she imagining the moral proposition true, or merely imagining believing it? I want a verdict: does this asymmetry pick out something deep about morality and the imagination, or is it a local convention of how we read — and either way, should we cultivate it, resist it, or leave it alone?
