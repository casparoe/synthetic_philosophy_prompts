I'm a philosophy professor, and a midsized regional history museum has asked me to help write the policy they'll use to decide which exhibits, film series, and public programs they'll host or put their name on. The problem is that they get a steady trickle of proposals somewhere on the spectrum from what I'd call legitimately speculative-but-defensible to full ancient aliens, and right now every one of them becomes a board-level argument. They review maybe half a dozen serious proposals a year, and a wrong call either embarrasses them in the local press or loses them a donor, so the stakes aren't trivial. The staff who'd actually run the policy are curators and educators with no philosophy background, and they need something they can execute on a proposal in an afternoon: questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it stops with a yes-but-label-it, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of judgment rather than facts. The director signs the final policy, but the day-to-day triage would be done by staff, and the board only sees the cases the procedure flags as genuinely borderline.

Could you write that procedure, grounded in whatever the philosophy of science actually knows about demarcation rather than just vibes, and then run it on three proposals to show it working? I'd especially want to see it run on: (1) an exhibit arguing that Plato's Atlantis is a garbled cultural memory of the Bronze Age eruption of Thera/Santorini; (2) a donor-funded film series claiming some megaliths are too precise to have been built with the tools of their time and therefore point to a lost advanced civilization or outside contact; and (3) a partnership with a tribal historical society that wants to present its oral histories of catastrophic coastal flooding as real long-term memories of sea-level rise. That third one worries me, because I don't want the procedure to become a machine for telling indigenous communities their traditions are pseudohistory, but I also don't want to just exempt them by fiat with no rationale. Please also make one of the worked examples a case where the procedure tells the museum something it won't like. And throughout, be honest about what's settled and what's genuinely contested among people who work on this: I know falsifiability isn't the consensus silver bullet, even though the board will want a single clean test, so don't present a contested view as the consensus.
