I'm preparing a session for a group that works on the philosophy of technology, and I want to build the evening around Pixar's 2008 film WALL-E. The question I want the group to think through is the contemporary worry that epistemic dependence on AI erodes our understanding. I do not want to leave that worry vague, because several distinguishable claims are usually bundled together in it. The first is a loss of practical competence: when the system performs a task, we may lose the ability to perform it ourselves. The second is a loss of explanatory understanding: we can use the system and act on its outputs, but we cannot say why it produced them, what they presuppose, or where they are likely to fail. The third is a loss of something like intellectual character: when answers arrive without inquiry, we may stop asking follow-up questions and stop cultivating the curiosity, patience, and intellectual self-trust that make someone an understander rather than a consumer of results. Against all three stands a serious reply: human beings have always leaned on epistemic artifacts such as books, maps, and instruments, and perhaps the relevant unit of understanding is the person-plus-tool system rather than the unaided individual, so what looks like erosion may simply be reasonable cognitive offloading and division of labor.

I would like you to read WALL-E through this lens, working from the film's major scenes and images rather than quoted dialogue, and staying faithful in both directions — not flattening the film into an illustration of a thesis and not distorting the philosophical distinctions to fit the film. Concretely: which of the three strands does the film actually dramatize, and where do you find them (the hoverchair-bound passengers, the captain's growing curiosity about Earth, his confrontation with the autopilot AUTO, the screen-mediated lessons)? Where does the film run together the loss of practical competence — walking, farming, making things — with the loss of understanding, and does that conflation muddy its diagnosis or reveal something the philosophical debate misses? Where does the film resist the erosion worry altogether, given that the robots WALL-E and EVE are themselves inquisitive, interpretive, almost pedagogical figures whom the film clearly admires, and what would an honest critic be forced to concede about that? Finally, what does the film add to the philosophical debate that a paper on cognitive offloading does not, and is its implied thesis — that reclaiming understanding requires reclaiming a bodily, exploratory, first-hand relation to the world — one you would defend, or only partially accept?
