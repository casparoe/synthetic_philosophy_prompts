I'm in an epistemology seminar and I keep going in circles on something, so I'd like an actual diagnosis rather than more assigned reading. We were discussing higher-order evidence—evidence about how reliable your own reasoning is—and epistemic akrasia, which we glossed as believing some claim while also believing your total evidence doesn't support it. I've always found it obvious that akrasia is irrational. But a friend sketched an argument that the principle behind that thought eats itself if taken seriously, and now I can't find the exit.

Here's the argument, numbered the way he wrote it on the board. (1) If you have strong evidence that your reasoning on some topic is unreliable, you should lower your confidence in whatever that reasoning concludes. (2) Suppose I've just checked a long proof, it looks airtight, and then I learn from a very trustworthy doctor that the medication I took an hour ago is known to make people subtly overconfident about exactly this kind of careful checking. (3) So by (1) I should lower my confidence that the proof is correct. (4) But my belief that I'm now unreliable is itself something I reached through reasoning: I reasoned that the doctor is trustworthy, that the drug's effects apply to me right now, and that my case fits the usual profile. (5) By (1) again, since that belief is a deliverance of the same reasoning I've been told to distrust, I should lower my confidence in it too. (6) Once I lower my confidence that I'm unreliable, the reason for distrusting the proof weakens—and I seem stuck either oscillating forever between trusting and distrusting the proof, or concluding that I should never change my mind in response to evidence of my own bias. (7) That last option is absurd, so (1) must be false. But rejecting (1) seems to license ignoring evidence of bias, drunkness, self-deception, and every other comprimised state, which seems worse.

I'd most like to keep (1) and the setup in (2), but I realize that might be exactly what has to go. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Can (1) be restated in a way that doesn't regress, or is the mistake in how the argument applies it to itself? Give exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
