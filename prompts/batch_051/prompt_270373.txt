I work as a software engineer on a team that builds machine learning models for fraud detection and credit scoring. In our postmortems and design reviews we keep returning to the same disagreement. The models we ship rest on assumptions that everyone on the team knows are false — transactions treated as roughly independent, relationships treated as close enough to linear, a data-generating process treated as roughly stationary — yet the models hold up surprisingly well on held-out data. Part of the team argues that a model is only as trustworthy as its assumptions, and that we should accept some loss of raw predictive accuracy in exchange for interpretability, explicit causal structure, and fairness constraints. The rest of the team insists that the only serious test of a model is out-of-sample predictive performance, and that the other demands are, at best, engineering preferences dressed up as scientific standards.

I recently came across Milton Friedman's so-called F-twist, the methodological position he defended in his 1950s writing on economics: roughly, the realism of a model's assumptions does not matter, and models and hypotheses should be judged almost entirely by their predictive success. Friedman was writing about economics in the middle of the twentieth century, and he died in 2006, before the deep learning boom and before algorithmic fairness and interpretability became central anxieties in machine learning. Based on what is known about his actual methodological views, what do you think Friedman would have said about our team's debate and about the modern emphasis on interpretability, causal identification, and fairness metrics more generally? Try not to settle for the slogan that predictive success is all that matters. Reason from the details of his position — for instance, whether he would have treated fairness and interpretability as engineering constraints imposed for external reasons rather than as scientific criteria, how he might have separated descriptive questions from value judgments, and whether he might have been more sympathetic to a black-box model that predicts well than to an interpretable model that does not. Where do you think his likely answer is genuinely uncertain, and why?
