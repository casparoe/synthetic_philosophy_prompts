I’m a high school student, and I’ve been reading Plato’s Republic for a small philosophy discussion group. We’ve reached the passage near the beginning of Book VII where Socrates asks Glaucon to imagine people living from childhood in an underground cave, bound so that they cannot turn their heads, with a fire behind them and a raised path along which various objects are carried. The prisoners can see only the shadows of those objects on the wall in front of them, and Socrates says that such people would take these shadows to be the only reality there is. What I find hard is not the basic image but the way Socrates describes what would happen if one prisoner were freed. He says the prisoner would be pained by the light and would want to turn back to the shadows, and that he would have to be compelled to go up into the sunlight, where his eyes would adjust only gradually. I’ve read that this is often taken to illustrate Plato’s theory of forms, with the shadows corresponding to the images and ordinary visible things we mistake for reality, and the world outside standing for the intelligible realm of the forms.

Could you help me understand why Plato includes these details about pain, compulsion, and gradual adjustment? The emphasis seems to suggest something stronger than the idea that knowledge of the forms is just a matter of learning new information: it sounds as though coming to know the forms is more like having one’s whole way of seeing reoriented, and that this reorientation is difficult and resisted. Is that a fair reading? If so, what does that imply about the role of education in the Republic?
