You are a philosopher of mind and cognitive science who works on motivated cognition, and you have been asked to reason through a thought experiment rather than survey the literature.

Suppose that, starting next year, a cheap, safe, fully reversible, and voluntary neurotechnology — call it the calibration device — becomes widely available. While it is active, the device enforces exactly one change: a person's degree of belief in a proposition cannot be moved directly by what they want to be true, by social identity, or by the need to preserve a flattering self-image. Desires, incentives, and identity can still determine which questions the person asks, which evidence they seek or avoid, how long they search, and which risks they run; the device only blocks the final step in which a comforting or self-serving conclusion, rather than the information the person actually has, moves their confidence. It does not make people more intelligent, more courageous, or more transparent, and they remain free to say whatever they like.

Assume uptake is voluntary and spreads unevenly, so that within a decade roughly one adult in five uses the device at least occasionally, with much higher rates in some professions, subcultures, and age groups, while employers and insurers mostly stay neutral. Predict, with your reasoning visible, how this changes ordinary social life, close relationships, organizations, and politics over that decade. Treat the central question as mechanistic rather than narrative: which genuine mechanisms of self-deception would the device short-circuit, and which would survive or even expand because people redirect their effort into selective information seeking, biased questions, or belief-adjacent states such as hope, suspicion, and cultivated ignorance? Who would gain advantages, who would become easier to exploit, and what small adaptive behaviors would people develop to get the benefits of flattering self-assessment without triggering the device?

Before committing to a single prediction, briefly list the main mechanisms you expect to operate and say which you regard as robust and which are guesses. Throughout, make explicit what your predictions commit you to about the nature of self-deception itself — for example, whether the scenario makes sense only if self-deception is a unified last-step phenomenon, or whether your own view treats self-deception as impossible, incoherent, or reducible to earlier stages of motivated information processing, and how that changes the forecast. Finish by stating how confident you are in your three or four most load-bearing causal claims and singling out the one specific prediction or premise you are least sure of.
