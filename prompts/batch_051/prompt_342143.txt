Last week in a reading group on disgust and moral judgment, I watched someone deflect a challenge by quoting the proverb "cleanliness is next to godliness." The context was a dispute about whether feelings of purity, contamination, and sacredness deserve a place in ethics at all. One side argued that disgust is just an evolved hygiene emotion that rational moral thought should override or at least quarantine; my classmate replied that this underestimates purity and sanctity as moral values, and then produced the proverb as though it were a conclusive reply. I can tell the sentence is carrying several ideas at once, but I can't separate them, and I suspect the separation matters enormously.

I'd like you to treat "cleanliness is next to godliness" as a compressed philosophical claim and lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, from the plainly trivial to the substantive and contentious. For each reading, give me a one-sentence statement of the thesis, your verdict on whether it is true, false, or defensible-but-disputed, and whether it would actually support the move my classmate was making — the claim that purity intuitions should be treated as moral evidence rather than dismissed as mere prejudice. Be explicit about where the proverb is merely describing how human psychology happens to work, where it is smuggling in a normative ideal, and where it is making an epistemic claim about what our feelings can detect. Since I want to bring this back to the group, organize the readings as a menu I could read out, and end by saying which reading or combination her argument actually needs, and which popular readings of the proverb would give her nothing even if they were true.
