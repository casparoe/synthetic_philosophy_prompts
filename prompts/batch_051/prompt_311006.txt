I'm a novelist, and I'm outlining a near-future story built around a service — working title Second Draft — that sells people a two-week immersive simulation of the life they'd be living if they made some big, hard-to-reverse choice: having a kid, taking monastic vows, emigrating, enlisting, that sort of thing. The pitch is that some choices change what you value, so you can't just imagine your way into them; you have to try the life on. You wake up with the memories of the simulated years but your old values back, and then you decide. I don't want this company to just be a plot device; I want it to behave the way an actual institution would behave. So treat it as a mechanism and walk me through how it would play out, not whether it's a good idea in the abstract. What does it incentivize among customers, among the company's engineers, and among the people who show up inside someone else's simulation? Where does it get gamed first, and by whom? What second-order effects does it have — on customers, on their relationships, on the market for the big choices themselves? And can the failure modes be fixed without gutting the whole thing? Last thing: is there a version of the problem it can't solve even in principle, because the person who wakes up with the memories still isn't the person who would of lived the choice?
