I've been teaching myself causal discovery, and I built an argument I can't get out of. It seems to show that once you assume your Bayes net is minimal, faithfulness comes along for free, which would make the usual distinction between minimality and faithfulness look like bookkeeping. That feels too strong, but I can't locate the slip. Here is the reasoning:

(1) A graph is minimal for a distribution just in case the distribution satisfies the Markov condition for that graph, and no edge can be removed without violating the Markov condition.
(2) Suppose a minimal graph were not faithful to its distribution. Then some conditional independence would hold in the distribution even though d-separation in the graph does not imply it.
(3) Such an extra independence means the graph leaves an open route between variables that are in fact cut off from one another.
(4) An open route between variables that are actually independent is a route carrying no influence, so it is idle.
(5) Removing part of an idle route cannot create a dependence; it can only leave the pattern of independencies exactly as it was.
(6) Therefore, if unfaithfulness ever occurred, some edge could be deleted while preserving the Markov condition.
(7) So an unfaithful graph cannot be minimal; by contraposition, minimality guarantees faithfulness.
(8) But that would make unfaithful minimal graphs impossible, along with the familiar sort of exact cancellation in which one causal route offsets another. Since such cancellations seem at least physically possible, something in steps (1) through (7) must have gone wrong.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I had to choose a preference, I would rather keep the possibility of unfaithful but minimal graphs than the principle that idle routes can be pruned so cheaply.
