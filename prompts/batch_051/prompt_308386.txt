I'm a software engineer working mostly on applied machine learning, and I've ended up in a workplace reading group on AI risk even though I have no formal background in philosophy or economics. In last week's discussion about whether the field should prioritize technical alignment research, regulation, or something else, one participant kept falling back on a slogan I've now heard in several variations: "the profit motive is the root cause of AI risk." At one point he said, roughly, that trying to align systems while the same companies race each other to deployment is just moving chairs around on a ship whose engine is profit, and that this is what actually has to go. Nobody in the room managed to pin down exactly what he was claiming, and the conversation moved on before I could press him.

I want to go back next week with a clear map of the options. Please act as a professional philosopher with expertise in conceptual analysis and the philosophy of technology, think carefully, and first brainstorm a broad set of candidate readings of that slogan before narrowing them into a final organized list. For each distinct precise claim someone could be making with "the profit motive is the root cause of AI risk" (or "capitalism is the root cause of AI risk"), say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or probably false, and order the list from the weakest or most trivial reading to the strongest or most radical one. I suspect the readings might differ along a few dimensions — whether "profit motive" refers to something psychological about individual executives, to shareholder pressure, or to structural features of markets and firms; whether "root cause" means merely an important contributing factor, the deepest distal cause, or something that must be eliminated for the risk to go away; and whether "AI risk" covers accidents, deliberate misuse, or a competitive race dynamic — but I want you to surface any other dimensions you find, not just confirm mine.

At the end, say which reading or readings would actually support his use of the slogan, namely dismissing technical alignment research and ordinary regulation as at best secondary, and which readings sound radical but would not support that dismissal. The deliverable should be a menu with verdicts rather than a single defended interpretation.
