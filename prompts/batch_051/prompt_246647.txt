I am a journalist writing a long-form piece on the contemporary debate over divine foreknowledge and human freedom, and I want its centerpiece to be a rigorous argument rather than a survey of positions. Please construct and defend the following thesis: the grounding objection to Molinism cannot, on its own, decide the dispute between Molinists and their critics, because the best ways of pressing the objection either prove too much or prove too little.

Here is the background I will assume, stated so the argument is self-contained. Molinism distinguishes three logical moments in God's knowledge: natural knowledge of all necessary truths; middle knowledge, prior to any creative decree, of what every possible free creature would freely do in any set of circumstances — the counterfactuals of creaturely freedom; and free knowledge of what will actually happen in the world God chooses to create. The grounding objection charges that the contents of middle knowledge have no truthmakers: nothing about creaturely essences, possible worlds, or God's nature makes it true, before creation and independently of God's will, that a particular possible person would freely choose one thing rather than another in specified circumstances. If nothing makes such propositions true, they cannot be available for God to know; at best they are brutely and arbitrarily true.

Argue for the thesis by doing the following. First, lay out the argument with explicitly numbered premises. Second, show in detail that the alleged gap — a contingent, pre-volitional truth that is not determined by prior causes and not made true by the actual world — reappears in at least two places that the objector will not find easy to treat as benign: God's simple foreknowledge of future free actions, for those objectors who accept it; and ordinary counterfactuals about what actual or merely possible free agents would have done in non-actual circumstances, for those who accept such counterfactuals. Consider, and preempt, the reply that counterfactuals of creaturely freedom are worse off because they must be true before any creature exists, or because God allegedly needs an exhaustive array of them in order to survey possible worlds. Then state exactly what the grounding objection, in its strongest form, ends up showing: whether it merely imposes a general truthmaker requirement on all modal discourse, collapses into a different objection, or genuinely singles out something special about middle knowledge. If the strongest form of the objection survives, explain precisely how much of Molinism it damages; if it does not survive, say what the critic would have to accept in order to keep it.
