I am currently taking a metaethics course, and the Frege–Geach problem has come up in a form I found harder to pin down than the usual textbook presentations. I have reconstructed the objection as a numbered argument, and I half-suspect one of the steps is doing more work than it should. I would like a careful verdict on whether the argument goes through, and if not, exactly where it fails.

Consider the following plainly valid inference, an instance of modus ponens:

(P1) If making a false statement on an insurance claim is wrong, then corroborating a friend's false statement on the same claim is wrong.
(P2) Making a false statement on an insurance claim is wrong.
(C) Therefore, corroborating a friend's false statement on the same claim is wrong.

Now consider this argument against expressivism:

(1) Expressivism holds that the meaning of an atomic moral sentence, such as 'Making a false statement on an insurance claim is wrong', is the non-cognitive attitude it conventionally expresses: roughly, disapproval of making false statements on insurance claims.

(2) An argument is valid only if the attitudes expressed by the premises are such that anyone who holds those attitudes is rationally committed to holding the attitude expressed by the conclusion.

(3) The argument from P1 and P2 to C is valid by modus ponens.

(4) By (1), P2 expresses disapproval of making false statements on insurance claims, and C expresses disapproval of corroborating a friend's false statement on the same claim.

(5) But the antecedent of P1, although it is the very same sentence as P2, is unasserted, and a sincere utterance of P1 does not express disapproval of making false statements on insurance claims. Rather, P1 expresses a higher-order attitude: something like disapproval of a sensibility that condemns false statements on insurance claims while not condemning the corroboration of such statements.

(6) That higher-order attitude does not contain the first-order attitude of disapproving of such false statements as one of its constituents. Therefore the attitudes expressed by P1 and P2 together do not rationally commit anyone to the attitude expressed by C.

(7) So, on expressivism, the inference from P1 and P2 to C is not valid, even though it plainly is.

(8) Hence expressivism cannot account for the validity of even elementary moral reasoning, and it must be false.

I am fairly sure the conclusion is too quick, but I cannot settle whether the defect is in (2), (5), or (6), and I am unsure what turns on the exact reading of 'expresses'. Could you say whether the argument is valid as stated? If not, identify which step or steps fail. In particular, if (6) is the problem, give the strongest expressivist reading of (5) on which (6) is false, and say whether the argument can be repaired into a version that still threatens that reading.
