I'm in a philosophy of cognitive science seminar that's been stuck for two weeks on whether the things interpretability researchers find inside neural networks — features, circuits, directions, probes — should count as representations. The two camps in the room are roughly: representational realists, who think at least some of these components genuinely stand for things (a feature for curves, a direction for sentiment, whatever) in a way that licenses claims about what a network's internals mean, and instrumentalists, who think representation-talk is just a compact way of describing input-output dispositions and that saying a neuron encodes dog is more like saying a weathervane predicts rain than like saying a thought is about a dog. My professor wants us to stop doing the ususal thing where each side talks past the other and instead draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between these two camps.

Write that statement. I want the claims both sides can sign, stated non-vacuously; the precise formulations of their disagreement, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording; the cruxes — the subquestions such that settling one would actually move at least one side; and for each crux, the experiment, intervention, or argument both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table. Don't adjudicate: the deliverable is a document they could both put their names to even while disagreeing.
