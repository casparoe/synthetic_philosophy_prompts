I'm a PhD student in cognitive science and I keep tripping over the symbol grounding problem—it shows up in my reading on both the classic GOFAI debates and newer grounded-language-model work. A labmate forwarded me what he says is the clearest popular explanation he's found, from some online science magazine. He thinks it finally "gets" the problem. Here's the gist, as close as I can reproduce it:

"The symbol grounding problem is Harnad's version of the Chinese Room. A computer manipulating symbols by rules is doing pure syntax, and since syntax can never yield semantics, no purely computational system can ever mean anything by its symbols. The fix is grounding: you connect the symbols to the world through perception, by giving the system cameras, microphones and other sensors, so each symbol is causally hooked up to the thing it refers to. Once a symbol is sensorily connected to its referent, it is grounded. From there the system can combine grounded symbols into more complex meanings the way old-fashioned AI wanted, now built on a perceptual foundation."

Two things bug me. First, it collapses the symbol grounding problem into the Chinese Room, and I'd always thought they were related but distinct. Second, it makes grounding sound like it's solved by buying a camera, which can't be right. I also have this nagging memory that Harnad's actual proposal has something to do with categories and "categorical perception," which the summary doesn't mention at all—but I cant reconstruct the argument from memory and don't want to be unfair to a competent popularization.

Take your time and maybe start by breaking the summary into its individual claims and checking each one against what Harnad actually argues.
