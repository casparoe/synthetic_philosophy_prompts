Here's an asymmetry I keep going back and forth on. If I make a deal with another person and then try to back out, most of us think I should be held to it, or at least owe them something for backing out. But if I make a deal with my own future self—say, while I'm stable I sign something saying 'if I become manic and refuse treatment, treat me anyway'—the moment my later self refuses, everyone gets squeamish and starts treating the later refusal as the real me. Yet we romanticize Ulysses for having his crew tie him to the mast exactly so his later pleas would be ignored, and we praise constitutions for tying the hands of future majorities who never signed anything. So we cheer precommitment in a story, enforce it across generations in politics, but balk when an individual tries to override their own later refusal. Is there a principle that justifies this pattern, or is it just an inconsistency we like the results of? If you defend it, defend it against the case where the individual contract and the constitutional amendment look the same from the inside: someone in the later state says 'I don't consent to this.'
