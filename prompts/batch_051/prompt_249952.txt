I hold that the Church–Turing thesis, in its standard effective-computability form, is best understood as a piece of conceptual analysis rather than an empirical claim: it fixes, rather than reports, the extension of the notion of an effective procedure. I want to know what would count against that meta-level view. What would have to be true for the thesis to turn out to be an empirical hypothesis with genuine exposure to refutation? Give me the evidence and arguments that should move me, ordered by how decisive they would be — for example, a proposed procedure that working mathematicians actually recognize as effective but that resists Turing-machine encoding, the evidential weight of the convergence of independent formalisms, or the status of the distinct physical Church–Turing thesis — and say what the definitional reading predicts that an empirical reading would not. Separate what would merely embarrass the view from what would refute it, and assess whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether, if so, that is a defect in a claim of this kind or simply what a definitional reading should look like.
