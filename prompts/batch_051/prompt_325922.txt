I'm taking a course on the rise of capitalism and we spent two weeks on Weber's Protestant ethic thesis; after class two people in my study group got into an argument about it and neither would back down. Mara started by saying Weber shows you can't explain the early-modern shift toward methodical work, thrift, and reinvestment purely from material conditions — you need a change in what people actually valued. Calvinist anxiety about predestination meant believers couldn't earn salvation, so they worked systematically in a worldly calling to get some psychological evidence of election, and that spread a new economic ethos. Jonas objected that this is exactly the kind of values-explanation that sounds explanatory but isn't, because the ethic is infered from the same behavior it's supposed to explain: people saved and worked methodically, so we attribute to them an ethic of saving and methodical work. He added that the value doesn't even pick out the behavior, since a Calvinist could just as plausibly have concluded that if he was elect nothing he did mattered, or that worldly success was a dangerous vanity. Mara replied that Weber was careful here: he used ideal types and talked about an elective affinity between the Protestant ethic and the spirit of capitalism, not a mechanical one-way cause, so Jonas was attacking a cruder thesis than Weber held.

Jonas said that defense gives up the interesting claim — if it's only a mutual fit between two constructed types, then culture isn't being offered as a cause at all, just as something that coheres with the economic pattern, and you've dodged the circularity by emptying the explanation. Mara shot back that all unobservable-cause explanations have this form, including preferences in economics: you infer them from choices and then explain choices with them, and that's fine as long as you have independant evidence and predictions. Weber, she said, had that — sermons, pastoral manuals, doctrine, conduct books — not just reading the ethic off the behavior. Jonas said those texts tell you what some clergy wanted people to do, not what merchants and artisans actually valued, and the writers had an interest in preaching discipline anyway. At that point Mara said the materialist alternative is worse off: prices and technology alone don't give you a distinctive economic style, and the timing — capitalism emerging where the Reformation had taken hold — needs some cultural component even if it isn't a sufficient cause.

I have to write something for our seminar that adjudicates this move by move. Can you go through their actual exchange and say who is right about which specific points, what both of them are missing, and who comes out more right overall?
