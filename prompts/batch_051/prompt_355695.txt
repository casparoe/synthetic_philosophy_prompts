I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I've been stuck on a point of narration for weeks now. My book keeps wanting to switch between two modes. One is a close first-person present tense: the narrator's "now" sits in the middle of things, the future is undecided from inside the prose, the past exists mostly as memory that could be wrong. The other is an omniscient third-person voice that moves around in time with flashbacks and little flashes forward; there the whole timeline seems to be laid out already, and the narrator just skips across it. My critique group thinks I'm overthinking this, but it struck me that this might be exactly the presentism/eternalism split, or the A-theory/B-theory split in philosophy of time. First-person present feels presentist: there's a priviledged now, and the past and future aren't equally real for the character. Omniscient with all its time-hopping feels eternalist: every moment is equally real and the narrator just reports them out of order.

What I can't decide is whether this is a deep structural identity or just me pattern-matching. If it's real, I'd expect things to transfer. For example, readers somehow manage to hold both attitudes at once, and narrators shift tense mid-book without the novel falling apart—does that tell us anything about how people move between A-theory and B-theory talk in ordinary life? Also, an omniscient narrator can create suspense even though the whole plot is already determined on the page; is that analogous to how an eternalist can still make sense of caring about the future? And wether the parallel breaks somewhere matters to me too. I suspect it predicts that a first-person present narrator can't have genuine foreknowledge or flash-forwards, but some novels do exactly that, so maybe the "now" of narration isn't as privileged as the presentist's now after all.
