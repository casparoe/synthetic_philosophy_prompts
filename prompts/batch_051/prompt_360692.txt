I've been working on a piece about why people treat economic deal-making so different than moral deal-making, and I keep going around in circles. The cleanest way I've found to state it is this. Suppose two people have different tastes: one hates driving and gladly pays someone to drive her, the other dislikes his current job and will drive for money. They trade, both walk away better off by their own lights, and nobody thinks either one is a sellout—we call it a market. Now suppose two people have different moral views. One thinks a certain policy is deeply wrong but knows it matters enormously to the other; the other thinks a different policy is deeply wrong but knows it matters enormously to the first. They strike a deal: each backs the other's policy, so each gets something they care about intensely and concedes something they judge immoral. Both are better off by their own standards, exactly as in the driving case. But now we get squeamish: the words "sellout," "unprincipled," and "corruption" start floating around. As far as I can tell the formal structure is the same—differences in valuation create gains from trade, and each party gives up something they'd rather not give up in order to get something they want more. So why is one commerce and the other capitulation? I can think of a few candidate distinctions—third parties are usually bound by political deals but not by private trades; there's supposed to be a difference between preferences and principles; moral convictions are meant to track the truth rather than be treated as bargaining chips—but each of them seems to wobble as soon as I push. Give me the strongest principle you can find that seperates the two cases, test it against a nearby case where the moral compromise is purely personal and consensual (or where a market exchange has real moral stakes), and give an honest verdict: does the asymmetry survive, or should we give it up?
