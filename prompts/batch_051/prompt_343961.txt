I am a software engineer with no formal philosophical training, so I would be grateful for a concrete, operational answer rather than a survey of positions.

I am trying to diagnose a two-month argument between our backend team and our platform team about whether a new caching layer in the request path is "stateful." The underlying facts are not disputed. The layer retains a bounded table of responses keyed by customer ID between requests, with an eviction policy. Flushing the table at any moment can never produce an incorrect response — only additional database load and a higher p99. During failover, the layer may briefly serve an entry that is up to five seconds old. The backend team maintains that any component that retains information between requests is stateful. The platform team maintains that a component is stateful only if its output can depend on accumulated state in a way that affects correctness, and therefore this layer is not stateful. The outcome currently feeds architecture documentation, the wording of an SLO, and which team owns an on-call rotation.

I want to test the conceptual claim that this particular dispute is merely verbal: that when the contested term "stateful" is replaced with more explicit properties, the two teams will agree on the underlying facts and on what should be done in each candidate scenario, and the only remaining disagreement will be over which property deserves the label.

Please design the best empirical test I could actually run using the twelve or so engineers involved. Specify the task you would give them, the conditions you would vary, how you would score the responses, what pattern of results would count as evidence for the dispute's being merely verbal, and what pattern would count as evidence for its being substantive. Then explain what the test could not settle even in principle — in particular, whether a dispute that dissolves once the word is disambiguated shows that there was no real disagreement to begin with, and whether a dispute that survives the test shows that the parties disagree about facts rather than about a convention. Where relevant, indicate how the design should be adjusted so that practical incentives, such as avoiding on-call ownership, do not masquerade as conceptual disagreement.
