I'm giving a paper next month at our joint political theory and classics seminar, and I've managed to pick a fight with basically everyone in the room before I've even opened my mouth. The paper is about the Mytilenaean debate as a test case for whether the Athenian assembly was capable of deliberating well by its own lights. The standard line treats the second day as democracy's redeeming moment: the assembly votes one day to kill every adult male and enslave the rest, sleeps on it, reconsiders, and is talked down. I want to argue that this is almost backwards. What happened is not that the demos recovered its conscience, but that Diodotus persuaded it to adopt an explicitly instrumental self-conception: the assembly is not a lawcourt, so stop asking whether the Mytilenaeans deserve death and start asking what is useful to Athens. My Exhibit A, and the hinge of my argument, is this bit from his speech in Thucydides, *History of the Peloponnesian War* 3.44 (Crawley translation):

"his speech may have the attraction of seeming the more just in your present temper against Mitylene; but we are not in a court of justice, but in a political assembly; and the question is not justice, but how to make the Mitylenians useful to Athens."

I read this as the demos being reconstituted as a means–end calculator, not as the site of any principled reconsideration. And I push it further: the same deliberation-as-instrument logic is what later sends the fleet to Sicily and, in a different register, what lets the Athenian court treat Socrates as a public danger to be managed rather than a speaker to be answered. So the three canonical "failures" aren't lapses of democratic principle; they're successes of a certain conception of what the demos is for.

The room is mixed and none of it is on my side. Two classicists who read the speech in Greek and mostly buy the line that Diodotus is the rare rational statesman steering the mob away from Cleon's demagoguery; three deliberative-democracy people who teach this episode as proof that discussion can change a mistaken majority's mind; and one hard-nosed realist who thinks Cleon at least had the nerve to say that empire is rule over the unwilling and should be treated as such. I already know the classicists will pounce on the weakest stretch in the paper: I run straight from the ekklesia to a dikasterion when I get to Socrates, and those are not the same institution. Fine — that's the stretch, but I think the underlying conception of public speech carries over.
