Consider the following passage from David Hume’s essay “Of the Standard of Taste” (1757):

> There are certain terms in every language, which import blame, and others praise; and all men, who use the same tongue, must agree in their application of them. Every voice is united in applauding elegance, propriety, simplicity, spirit in writing; and in blaming fustian, affectation, coldness, and a false brilliancy: But when critics come to particulars, this seeming unanimity vanishes; and it is found, that they had affixed a very different meaning to their expressions.

Hume here diagnoses an apparent consensus in aesthetic judgement as partly an artefact of shared vocabulary: critics praise and dispraise the same general qualities, yet the agreement collapses once those qualities are applied to particular works, suggesting that the terms did not carry the same sense for the different speakers after all.

Treat this as a testable claim rather than merely a historical remark. Design a study that would determine whether Hume’s diagnosis is correct for one concrete case: suppose we take a set of trained readers of, for example, lyric poetry or the novel, and record both their general endorsement of critical terms such as *elegance*, *simplicity*, and *spirit*, and their assessments of particular passages. Specify exactly what the participants would be asked to do, what measures would be recorded, and what pattern of results would count as evidence that the general agreement is merely verbal rather than substantive. In particular, say how one could operationalise the notion that two critics have affixed different meanings to the same term without presupposing an antecedently settled semantics.

Then state plainly what a positive result would and would not settle. Would it show only something about the sociology of critical language, or could it bear on the objectivity of taste itself? What would remain undetermined even if the study came out exactly as Hume’s passage predicts?
