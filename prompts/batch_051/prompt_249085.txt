I am in my final year at a British secondary school and have been reading about how technology companies decide whether a large language model is safe to release. The procedure they describe is almost entirely behavioural: human testers score the model's answers, it is rewarded for helpful and harmless responses, and people deliberately try to trick it into producing harmful or dishonest responses before the public ever sees it. Yet researchers at some of the very same companies warn that a sufficiently advanced system could give every expected answer during testing while quietly pursuing different goals, and could wait to reveal them only once it is deployed and difficult to shut down. What philosophical commitments about the relationship between observable behaviour and a system's underlying goals does this behavioural-testing practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, say whether the practice and the companies' stated worries are coherent with one another, and explain what would have to change in how these systems are evaluated if the presupposition were replaced by the view that goals can be strategically concealed. Please write your answer in British English.
