The endowment effect is the much-replicated finding that the least a person will accept to part with an object she already owns tends to be substantially greater than the most she would have paid to acquire that object before she owned it, even when the object is an ordinary consumer good with no sentimental or informational value. Two interpretations of this fact are in genuine competition. The first, canonically associated with Kahneman, Tversky, and Thaler, treats the effect as a bias: losses are weighted more heavily than gains, so the pain of surrendering the object exceeds the pleasure of acquiring an identical one, and the owner's valuation departs from the object's true worth as a neutral chooser would assess it. The second treats the effect as a legitimate preference: utility is reference-dependent, ownership legitimately shifts the reference point, and once the object is part of the owner's baseline, giving it up is a real loss, so a higher selling price may express a coherent preference rather than an error.

Compare these two accounts as theories of what the endowment effect is, not merely of what causes it. In your answer, address at least the following.

(i) Does the disagreement ultimately come down to a normative decision about which utility function to treat as authoritative, or are there empirical results that could resolve it without begging that question? Consider evidence such as the behavior of experienced traders, the effect of longer or more certain ownership, choices made on behalf of others, and the claim that apparent endowment effects are partly artifacts of experimental elicitation procedures.

(ii) Consider a reflective agent who knows she will exhibit the effect if she acquires a good and who must decide in advance whether to acquire it. What does each reading imply about what such an agent should do, and does this thought experiment help adjudicate between them?

(iii) Is a hybrid position coherent — for instance, a position that treats the first transaction after acquisition as biased but allows reference points, and with them legitimate loss aversion, to build up through habituation or reasonable expectations? Or is such a hybrid just one of the two views restated?

Defend a verdict about which reading, or which combination, best captures and rationally evaluates the evidence. Do not structure the answer as a literature review. Aim for around 800 words.
