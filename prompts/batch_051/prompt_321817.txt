I keep coming back to a wrinkle in an argument I once read, and I want to know whether the argument survives it. The argument is the Twin Earth thought experiment, usually attributed to Putnam. As I understand it, you imagine a planet just like Earth except that the liquid everyone there calls "water" has a different underlying chemical structure. Now take two people who are identical in every inward respect — same brain states, same felt experiences — one on Earth and one on the twin planet. When each of them says "water," the word refers to a different chemical kind. Since the two speakers are the same on the inside, the difference in meaning has to come from their surroundings, so meaning is not settled entirely by what is inside a person.

The development is this: the language models behind today's chatbots are trained on enormous amounts of human text, but nothing out in the world — no liquid, no object, no shared environment of the sort that supposedly fixes reference — ever plays a causal role in how they come to use the word "water." They have no body and no senses, and yet they use the word about as competently as a person does in ordinary conversation.

Does the Twin Earth argument survive these systems? I want to know which specific premise the case puts pressure on, whether the case can be absorbed by saying that a language model's words have only a borrowed or secondhand semantics, and whether there is a repaired version of the argument that still says something true and important about meaning. If the argument does not survive in any form, what should replace it for systems that learn words without an environment? Keep the answer under 650 words.
