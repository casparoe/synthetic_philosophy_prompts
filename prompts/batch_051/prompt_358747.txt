I'm trying to get clear on what I actually think about guilt and whether it can be moved around, so I wrote out a bunch of cases and my gut reactions. I want to know whether there's one principle behind them, and which of my intuitions have to go.

The Leviticus scapegoat ritual: the high priest confesses the people's sins over a goat, and the goat is sent out into the wilderness. My gut says the people are genuinely released from something — it's not a sham — but I don't think the goat is now morally guilty. Whatever landed on the goat, it isn't guilt in the sense I mean when I say a murderer is guilty.

Jesus dying for other people's sins (penal substitution). My gut: I can make sense of someone voluntarily stepping in and taking the penalty, and I can even see why the wronged party might accept that. But if you described the same transaction outside a religious setting — an innocent volunteer executed so a guilty person walks — I'd call it monstrous, or at least a serious injustice. So I'm not sure the voluntariness is doing the work I keep telling myself it does.

A manager dumps his own mistake on a subordinate and fires her. Obviously wrong, obviously unjust. The subordinate isn't guilty, the manager is still guilty, and firing her doesn't discharge anything. I want to say no transfer happened there.

But then: a company pays a fine for damage caused by one of its employees. My gut says the company is genuinely liable and paying the fine settles the matter, even though the employee is the one who actually did it and the company isn't a person in the normal sense. Something moved, or at least something got settled, without anyone thinking guilt moved.

A town has a run of bad harvests and drives out an innocent outsider, blaming him. Despicable. But I also have the uncomfortable thought that scapegoating does relieve the group's tension — it works psychologically. Not morally, though. More like venting than like paying a debt.

Wergild-type blood money: someone from clan A kills someone in clan B, and clan A pays a heavy sum formally to clan B. My gut: the killer's guilt is untouched, but the payment isn't a bribe — it really settles the clan's claim and restores something. I'd call it compensation for a loss owed to the victim's side, not a transfer of the killer's guilt onto the money.

A friend takes the blame for something I did, to protect me from the consequences, and I let him. He gets punished. My gut afterward: I still feel guilty — maybe more guilty, because now two bad things happened. He isn't guilty of the original act. Yet I'd also say he took responsibility for it, voluntarily, and I now owe him a debt. So responsibility can be taken up, while guilt for the act stays with me.

Now the one I keep going back and forth on. Suppose I've wronged someone and never made it right. Later I hear that a stranger suffered a similar injury, completely unrelated. I don't believe my debt is paid or my guilt reduced — but there's a piece of me that feels a kind of dark relief, like the moral books got evened out anyway. Is that just a nasty cognitive bias, or am I tracking something real about guilt, something that wants balancing and doesn't care who balances it?

My working hunch, which I don't fully trust: guilt for an act can't be transferred, but liability, responsibility, and a kind of ritual stain can be, and a lot of practices — scapegoats, substitution, vicarious punishment, blood money — only make sense if we distinguish those and say which one the practice is actually moving. But I can already see trouble: the wergild case looks like compensation, yet it also claims to settle the wrong itself, not just the family's loss; and the penal substitution case only seems to work if guilt itself is what gets transferred, not just a penalty.

So: is there a coherent map of what can and can't be transferred — guilt, blame, responsibility, liability, debt, stain — that captures most of these verdicts? State it precisely, if there is one, and tell me what it says about further cases: a parent paying a fine for an adult child's speeding; someone doing community service in place of a friend who was sentenced; a church performing a ritual to absolve a dead person of unrepented sins; and the Yom Kippur goat again, but in a version where the goat belongs to a poor farmer who isn't compensated. If no clean map handles everything, give me the best two or three candidates, say which of my gut verdicts each one preserves, and which of my gut verdicts the cleanest candidate forces me to drop — and whether, in your view, those are the ones I should drop. Also, please write your answer in American English.
