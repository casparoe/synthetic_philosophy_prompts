A friend of mine has been going on about Locke and taxation, and he's got this argument that seems to prove way too much, but I can't figure out where it actually goes wrong. The conclusion is that basically every actually existing government taxes people unjustly, which seems too strong even if you're sympathetic to the anti-tax side.

Here's his argument:

(1) Locke says in the Second Treatise, in the chapter on the extent of the legislative power, that the legislature cannot take any part of a person's property without that person's own consent, given either by himself or by his representatives.

(2) Taxation just is the legislature taking part of a person's property.

(3) In any state larger than a small town, nobody consents to the particular taxes they pay. There is no line on your tax form where you check that you personally authorize the rate, and you can't opt out of paying while remaining a citizen.

(4) Voting doesn't fix this, because most voters never voted for the people who set the taxes, and people born into a state never consented to the constitution that gives the legislature the power to tax in the first place.

(5) So in any large existing state, taxes are imposed without the kind of consent Locke describes.

(6) Therefore, by Locke's own principle, virtually all taxation in existing states is unjust.

I don't really want to become a taxation-is-theft person, but I can't tell which premise is the weak one. Is the problem with (3) or (4), because they treat consent as if it has to be individual and explicit, when Locke also had this notion of tacit consent from living under a government and enjoying its protection? But then can tacit consent be stretched to cover taxes without turning into the absurd claim that anyone who doesn't emigrate has consented to whatever rate the government sets? Diagnose the step that should be rejected, say why it looks plausible, and explain what a repaired Lockean position would cost. Could Locke actually allow taxation without giving up his claim that property can't be taken without consent, or is there a genuine tension in his own view?
