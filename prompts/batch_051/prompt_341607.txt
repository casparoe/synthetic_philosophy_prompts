Imagine Hume had somehow hung around long enough to read the no-free-lunch theorems and, say, spend a weekend fiddling with a modern machine-learning library. Do you think he'd treat those theorems (roughly: averaged over all possible target functions, every learner generalizes equally badly, so no algorithm beats all others unless its built-in assumptions match the actual world it's used in) as a formal vindication of his problem of induction, or would he see them as a technical curiosity that misses his point? The part I really want you to chew on is wether a modern defender of induction can escape by saying that real learners don't need to beat every possible world, just the structured, non-arbitrary world we actually inhabit, and that this is just the uniformity of nature wearing a probabilistic costume. Would Hume buy that move, or would he say the prior is itself custom and habit, and we have no non-circular reason to trust it? Give your best guess, and make it lean on his actual commitments about custom, natural belief, and the limits of reason.
