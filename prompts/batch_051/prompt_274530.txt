I got into an argument in my reading group after somebody brought up Plimpton 322—the Old Babylonian clay tablet from around 1800 BCE that contains a table usually read as a list of Pythagorean triples, written in sexagesimal. He kept saying this proves "mathematics" is far older than the Greeks. I pushed back: the tablet, at least by itself, looks like a list of numbers. There's no proof, no stated general rule, no definition, no theorem. So we ended up stuck on a more basic question: what does it even take for something to count as mathematics rather than just counting, measuring, or record-keeping?

I'd like you to be a philosopher of mathematics and work this through carefully. Start by brainstorming a few candidate criteria—generality, proof, abstract objects, problem-solving, the ability to generate examples rather than just record them—and then converge on the criterion, or small set of criteria, you find most defensible. Use Plimpton 322 as your running test case: does it matter whether the Babylonians actually used the tablet to generate Pythagorean triples rather than just tabulate them? Does the sexagesimal arithmetic itself favor one side? Is "mathematics" even a stable enough category to draw the line cleanly? Finally, tell me where your line is most likely to be wrong, and what kind of evidence—historical, conceptual, ethnographic—would most likely change your mind about it.
