I am the secretary of a university debating society. Our constitution contains Rule 9, which provides that the constitution may be amended by a two-thirds vote at a general meeting, notice having been given at the previous meeting. Our standing arbiter has decided four challenges to rule-changes, and I want to know how he should decide a fifth if he is to remain consistent with his own track record.

His rulings were these. He upheld an amendment, passed by two-thirds with notice, that lowered Rule 9's own voting threshold to a simple majority. He upheld an amendment, passed by simple majority, that shortened the notice requirement for future amendments to notice at the same meeting. He upheld an amendment, passed by simple majority, that raised the threshold for future amendments back to two-thirds. He struck down an amendment, passed by the two-thirds then required, that forbade any further amendments of any kind to the constitution.

The new proposal would replace Rule 9 with a rule giving the eight-member Executive Committee, acting by majority vote, the sole power to amend the constitution; the general meeting would lose the power to amend altogether. It will be moved under Rule 9 as it now stands, and the challenge is whether Rule 9's authority can be used to transfer the amending power it confers to another body.

I can see at least two principles that fit his record: roughly, that the amendment rule may be adjusted but not alienated from the general meeting, and that it may be redirected to another body so long as amendment as such remains possible. I want a committed ruling rather than a menu. Should he uphold the proposal? Which of his four rulings is the nearest precedent? State which principle we would be adopting under each possible answer, give your reasons for preferring one, and name one further hypothetical case that would most cleanly separate whichever principles remain live. Please write in British English.
