I'm taking a seminar on language and culture, and we read Edward Sapir's *Language* (1921). I got stuck on this passage, because a freind of mine and I ended up having an argument about language death that went nowhere:

“Speech is a human activity that varies without assignable limit as we pass from social group to social group, because it is a purely historical heritage of the group, the product of long-continued social usage. It varies as all creative effort varies—not as consciously, perhaps, but none the less as truly as do the religions, the beliefs, the customs, and the arts of different peoples. Walking is an organic, an instinctive, function (not, of course, itself an instinct); speech is a non-instinctive, acquired, ‘cultural’ function.”

The friend says this passage supports the view that a language is just one more social practice, like a religion or an art style. If a community stops practicing a religion or painting in a certain style, that's a loss, but not the same kind of loss as a species going extinct. He thinks the biodiversity analogy for language death is rhetorical overdrive.

I'm not so sure. Sapir says speech "varies without assignable limit" and is "a purely historical heritage of the group," which sounds to me like each language carries a unique, irreplaceable history that no other language can encode—which starts to look a lot more like the extinction intuition.

What's your actual credence, stated as a number, that the death of a language (loss of its last fluent speakers) is morally more like the extinction of a biological species than like the death of a particular art tradition or religious practice? I don't want the "it depends on how you frame it" answer—give me a number and then decompose it. Specifically, break it into: (1) the probability that linguistic diversity has value over and above its usefulness for communication, (2) the probability that the relevant value is tied to the uniqueness of the group's accumulated history rather than just the subjective preferences of its speakers, (3) the probability that the species-extinction analogy does real moral work rather than just being a metaphor, and (4) anything else you think the number depends on. Give one line of justification for each sub-probability and say how they combine. Then tell me which single sub-question your final credence is most sensitive to and what kind of evidence or argument would move it the most. Also, if the friend and I—say he's at .2 and I'm at .7—most likely disagree on one of these sub-questions, which one is it and what does that reveal about where the argument should go?
