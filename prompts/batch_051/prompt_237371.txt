I am a teaching assistant grading an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of mind exam, and I just had a long argument with a friend about whether Daniel Dennett's "Quining Qualia" is best read as denying consciousness itself or only as denying a certain theoretical posit. I want to make sure my grading is not influenced by that argument, so I would like a careful second opinion on one student's answer.

The exam was built around the following passage from Daniel Dennett, "Quining Qualia":

"Everything real has properties, and since I don't deny the reality of conscious experience, I grant that conscious experience has properties. I grant moreover that each person's states of consciousness have properties in virtue of which those states have the experiential content that they do. That is to say, whenever someone experiences something as being one way rather than another, this is true in virtue of some property of something happening in them at the time, but these properties are so unlike the properties traditionally imputed to consciousness that it would be grossly misleading to call any of them the long-sought qualia. Qualia are supposed to be special properties, in some hard-to-define way. My claim--which can only come into focus as we proceed--is that conscious experience has no properties that are special in any of the ways qualia have been supposed to be special."

The exam question was:

Explain what Dennett means in this passage when he says that conscious experience has no properties that are special in the ways qualia have been supposed to be special. How does the Chase and Sanborn example bear on this claim? In your answer, be sure to distinguish Dennett's position from the view that conscious experience has no properties at all.

The student's answer was:

Dennett is often misread as denying consciousness, but the passage makes clear that he does not deny that conscious experiences have properties. He grants that experiences have properties in virtue of which they have their content. What he denies is that these properties are special in the way qualia are supposed to be special. His argumentative strategy is therefore not eliminativism about experience, but rather the claim that the naive notion of qualia is too confused to do any theoretical work. The Chase and Sanborn thought experiment supports this by showing that two people can taste the same coffee and disagree: Chase reports that the coffee itself has changed, while Sanborn reports that his own taste has changed. Since neither can be corrected by the other, Dennett concludes that qualia are subjective and therefore incapable of being scientifically studied. In the end, he thinks we should replace qualia talk with talk about the neural states that realize experiences.
