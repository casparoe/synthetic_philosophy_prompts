A country enacts a constitutional amendment designed to force the criminal law to live up to the ideal that law should be capable of guiding the conduct of ordinary people. Under the amendment, no person may be convicted of a criminal offense unless the trial court is satisfied of two things: first, that every element of the offense can be stated in a single sentence of at most forty words, each drawn from an official list of roughly five thousand everyday terms; second, that a reasonable person of ordinary education, reading the statute without a lawyer, could have known at the time of the conduct whether it was prohibited. Offenses that fail this test are not repealed; they remain on the books but become unenforceable, and a constitutional court maintains a public register of these dormant statutes.

Treat the amendment as an institutional design problem rather than as a question of whether law should be clear in the abstract. Walk through what it would do to how the legislature drafts new offenses and rewrites old ones; to how prosecutors choose what to charge and what they charge instead; to how judges interpret statutes and litigate the word list; and to how ordinary people adjust their behavior as enforcement shifts. Say where the system is gamed first — by defendants, by police, by drafters, by lobbyists, or by administrative agencies — and what substitutes, such as civil fines or licensing regimes, would arise for offenses that can no longer be enforced. Then identify which failure modes could be fixed by amendment and which are intrinsic to the demand that criminal statutes be self-explanatory. As you go, indicate how confident you are in each prediction and single out the point you are least sure of.
