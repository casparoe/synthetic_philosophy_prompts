Last week I sat in on a political philosophy panel where two participants—call them Miriam and Daniel—became tangled in a disagreement over a passage in section 80 of John Rawls’s A Theory of Justice. The passage is the one in which Rawls distinguishes envy in the strict sense, a hostility toward another’s greater good even when that greater good does not subtract from one’s own, from resentment, which he classes as a moral feeling because it is tied to principles of justice. He then adds a complication: some envy is excusable when inequalities are so wide that they injure the self-respect of the less advantaged, provided those inequalities cannot be justified by the principles he has earlier defended.

Miriam argued that the notion of excusable envy is unstable. If strict envy is a vice precisely because it is not a moral feeling and aims only at depriving the other, then to call a class of envy excusable must either be a concession that such envy is covertly moral or an inconsistent use of the vice category. Daniel answered that Rawls is not licensing the envy; he is diagnosing it. Excusable envy, he said, remains not a moral feeling but a symptom of a badly ordered society, the way a fever is not a virtue even if it is a symptom of an infection. Miriam replied that a fever carries no intentional content about justice, whereas excusable envy is directed at the comparative advantage itself. Consider a worker who feels no wish to harm a promoted colleague but is unable to see the promotion as anything other than an unjust gap: that seems closer to inchoate resentment than to vice. Daniel countered that this conflates the evaluation of the agent with the evaluation of the social order. A signal can be diagnostically useful while the state that carries the signal remains a defect in the person; Rawls can consistently say both that a well-ordered society should not provoke much excusable envy and that the envious person’s attitude is not morally admirable.

They later asked me to referee, and I could not. Please adjudicate the disagreement move by move: say where each is right, wrong, or partly right, and identify what both may be missing in Rawls’s own position.
