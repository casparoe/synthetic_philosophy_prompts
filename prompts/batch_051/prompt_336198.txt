So I just spent two hours arguing with a friend about whether it makes any sense to say Germany caused the First World War in a way that could justify blaming it, and I still don't know who won. My friend is a lawyer, not a historian, and he kept reaching for tort language: but-for the blank check to Austria-Hungary, Vienna doesn't risk the ultimatum, so that's your but-for cause; the alliance system and Russia's mobilization were foreseeable enough that the German invasion of Belgium wasn't a superseding cause; and the whole war-guilt question is just comparative fault wearing a tie. I rolled my eyes a lot, and we ended up arguing about whether the Fischer controversy is doing history or just doing liability with extra steps.

I want you to tell me weather the parallel is deep or superficial. Here is the cleanest version I can make of it. Debates about how to blame a nation for a war keep sliding between at least three questions: who took the first irreversible step in the July crisis; who had the prior intentions and plans that made the outcome likely; and whether a nation, as opposed to a few ministers, generals, and monarchs, can be the kind of thing you blame at all. The Fischer move pulls the blame question out of the first bucket and into the second: it makes Germany's prewar aims and planning do the main causal work, and it treats Germany itself as the proper subject of the sentence. Meanwhile the common law has spent centuries separating out but-for cause, proximate or legal cause, and comparative fault: it picks out necessary conditions but refuses to let all of them ground liability; it asks whether the harm was a foreseeable result in the normal course of things; and it apportions fault among multiple wrongdoers without pretending any one act was sufficient. Both sides seem to be doing the same basic thing: taking a mess of jointly necessary conditions, sorting them into foreground and background, keeping only the ones an agent controlled, and then marking some of those as blameworthy rather than merely explanatory. Both also hit the same wall when the agent is a collective body rather than a person, and when background conditions (a fragile alliance system, a plaintiff with a preexisting condition) look like they are doing more causal work than the supposedly responsible act.

But I can also see the obvious disanalogy: the law is allocating liability for an already-defined harm between already-identified parties, under rules that are partly policy; a historian is trying to explain an event whose parties, time-window, and even whose harm are all up for grabs. So I don't trust the comparison, and I want a referee.
