I chair my university's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Our current rules require anesthesia, analgesia, and full committee review for research on vertebrates, while work on invertebrates goes through an abbreviated approval process that effectively presumes they are insentient. A researcher has submitted a protocol involving invasive procedures, including tissue collection and terminal surgery, on octopuses. The committee is split. One side argues that octopus learning, problem-solving, and behavioral responses to injury make the current line indefensible and that we should require vertebrate-level protections at least for cephalopods. The other side replies that we lack any settled criterion for attributing consciousness across distantly related species, that nociception and defensive behavior can occur without felt experience, and that moving the line once invites an unprincipled cascade: if octopuses, why not decapod crustaceans, insects, or nematodes?

Draft the decision memo I will circulate before the meeting. It should recommend one concrete course of action, or a clearly defined variant: approve the protocol under the existing invertebrate shortcut; require full review and anesthesia for this protocol without changing the general policy; amend the policy so cephalopods receive vertebrate-level protections; or adopt a tiered scheme in which protections track specified behavioral or neural criteria. The committee needs a document it can act on, so give a defended recommendation and engage seriously with the strongest argument on the losing side. The reasoning should turn on how to manage uncertainty about the distribution of consciousness across the animal kingdom, not merely on compliance risk or public relations.
