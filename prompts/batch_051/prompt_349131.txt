Lately I have been in a recurring argument between two groups of friends. The Americans, some of them law students, treat the First Amendment's near-absolutism as obviously correct and regard European hate speech laws — a German statute criminalizing Holocaust denial, for example, or French penalties for racist public abuse — as a category mistake about what law is for. The Europeans find the American position almost perverse. The last time we discussed this, an American friend ended the debate by saying, "Sticks and stones may break my bones, but words will never hurt me." It was not offered as a folksy shrug. It was meant to carry weight: to show that the European approach confuses speech with the kind of physical injury that law properly addresses, and that the right response to bad speech is not punishment but more speech, or simply endurance. I suspect that one sentence is smuggling in several different claims, some obvious, some controversial, and some false, and that its force depends on not telling them apart.

I would like you to take that proverb apart as precisely as possible. What distinct theses could it be asserting — about whether speech can cause genuine harm, about which harms law should regulate, about the difference between being wounded and being wronged, about the distinction between offense and injury, about the role of emotion in deciding what counts as harm, and about how adults should respond to speech they find hateful? For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or something overstated or false, and identify which readings would actually do the work my friend needed: supporting something like the American approach against something like the European one. I want a menu of readings I can bring back to the group, not a single favored paraphrase. Finally, say what would change your mind about the stronger readings — which argument or consideration would lead you to reverse your assessment of them — and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
