I have tentatively come to accept epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness: experiences such as pain or seeing red are real properties with a subjective character, but they are causally inert with respect to the physical world, including my own speech and beliefs. I am aware of the paradox of phenomenal judgment this invites: my judging and asserting that I am conscious, and my judging that epiphenomenalism is true, must themselves be produced by nonphenomenal causes, which seems to strip those judgments of their warrant just where the view leans hardest on first-person experience. I want an honest map of what would count against the position, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Which arguments, intuitions, possible empirical findings, or comparisons with rivals such as interactionist dualism, type-B physicalism, illusionism, and panpsychism would genuinely damage epiphenomenalism, and which ought a committed epiphenomenalist to concede as costs while still holding the view? In particular, address whether the paradox of phenomenal judgment is best treated as a decisive reductio, a standing explanatory cost, or a pseudo-problem that dissolves under a precise statement of the view, and say what would push it from one category to another. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested verdict as the consensus.
