I've been getting dragged into a side project with a friend who works at a startup doing decision-support software for elder care, and we keep butting heads about the same thing in machine ethics. He thinks that if you get the action principles right — respect autonomy, avoid harm, maximize expected benefit — the system will implicitly know who matters, because the principles themselves refer to them. I keep saying the "who matters" part (moral patiency: whether a future self counts the same as the present self, whether a frazzled family caregiver's distress is a moral input or just noise, whether the system should ever count as a patient in its own right) has to be settled seperately and more or less explicitly. Otherwise, my worry is, you get a system that handles the textbook cases and goes quietly off the rails in the ones nobody anticipated.

So here's the proposition I want your credence on: a machine can perform ethical reasoning competently in novel, high-stakes situations — about as competently as a thoughtful human case worker — without its designers ever having explicitly specified an account of moral patiency. Please don't just give a number; decompose it. Terms I'd include: (a) the probability that competent moral reasoning has an irreducible patiency component, one that isn't just derivable from the action principles; (b) conditional on that, the probability it could still be learned implicitly from human feedback or demonstration; (c) conditional on it being real but not implicitly learnable, the probability that its absence wouldn't cause competence-losing errors in practice. Add any other terms you think belong, give each a rough number with a line of justification, and show how you combined them. Then tell me which term your final credence is most sensitive to, and what evidence would move it most. I'm currently around 0.3 on the proposition, my friend is around 0.7 — where do you think our disagreement most likely actually lives?
