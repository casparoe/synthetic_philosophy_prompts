I am a novelist rather than an economist, so I want to know whether I am being seduced by a tragic plot when I call a failed prediction “right but early.” The text I keep returning to is a sentence from Thomas Robert Malthus’s preface to An Essay on the Principle of Population (1798): “The view which he has given of human life has a melancholy hue, but he feels conscious that he has drawn these dark tints from a conviction that they are really in the picture, and not from a jaundiced eye or an inherent spleen of disposition.” A friend takes this as evidence that Malthus was asserting an impersonal constraint, not a dated forecast. His reconstruction runs:

(1) If Malthus’s claim is a structural constraint about the pressure of population against subsistence, then a period in which food supply keeps roughly level with population does not falsify that claim; it merely shows the constraint temporarily relaxed.
(2) Malthus’s claim is indeed a structural constraint, not a prediction tied to late-eighteenth-century rates of productivity.
(3) The Green Revolution is a period in which food supply kept roughly level with population, without removing the underlying biological pressure Malthus identified.
(4) Therefore the Green Revolution does not falsify Malthus.
(5) Therefore Malthus can fairly be described as right but early.

I suspect the move from a confessed dark picture to an unfalsifiable constraint is doing more than the quoted sentence can support, but I cannot tell whether that is a defect in step (2), a bad inference from (4) to (5), or my own resistance to an unwelcome conclusion. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and under what reading? If it is valid, what exactly has been shown about Malthus’s theory?
