I am a PhD student in philosophy of law, co-teaching a 75-minute session on statistical evidence for 16 upper-level undergraduates in a legal proof seminar. I want a runnable classroom activity that makes students feel the force of the naked statistical evidence problem before it is named.

The activity should be built around Judith Jarvis Thomson’s article, *Liability and Individualized Evidence* (*Law and Contemporary Problems*, 1986). In one passage, Thomson argues in substance as follows: suppose a plaintiff was injured by a bus and proves only that the defendant’s company operated 80 percent of the buses on the relevant route at the relevant time. The probability that a Blue Company bus caused the injury is .8, yet Thomson holds that this evidence is not sufficient to ground a finding of liability, because it is not individualized to the defendant. She treats the gap between the .8 probability and the legal verdict as a genuine feature of legal proof, not merely a psychological quirk.

Please design the activity so that students first encounter the .8 probability in a context where they are comfortable acting on it, then meet the same number in a legal-factfinder role and find themselves unwilling to convert it into a verdict. I would like the structure to include: a brief warm-up in which participants place small bets or make forecasts at .8 odds on an obviously statistical claim; a two-phase small-group exercise in which some groups deliberate on a civil case under a preponderance standard and others on a criminal negligence case under a beyond-reasonable-doubt standard, with the same statistical evidence underlying both; assigned roles such as foreperson and designated dissenter; a decision protocol that requires each participant to write down a probability and then vote; and a moment in which each participant predicts the group’s vote distribution before votes are revealed.

Give me one definite design, with exact odds, a payoff scheme that does not require real money, and the exact wording of the decision prompts. In the debrief, map what happened to Thomson’s idea of individualized evidence, and say which feature of her distinction the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the instructor afterward. Also tell me what will likely go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. I want a committed response: choose one design and defend it, rather than laying out options.
