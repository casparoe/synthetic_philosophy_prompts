To fix terminology: by presentism I mean the view that only present objects and events exist; by eternalism I mean the view that past, present, and future objects and events are all equally real. Here is the passage, from J. M. E. McTaggart, "The Unreality of Time" (Mind, 1908):

> It is possible, however, that this is merely subjective. It may be the case that the distinction introduced among positions in time by the A series -- the distinction of past, present and future -- is simply a constant illusion of our minds, and that the real nature of time only contains the distinction of the B series --the distinction of earlier and later. In that case we could not perceive time as it really is, but we might be able to think of it as it really is.
>
> This is not a very common view, but it has found able supporters. I believe it to be untenable, because, as I said above, it seems to me that the A series is essential to the nature of time, and that any difficulty in the way of regarding the A series as real is equally a difficulty in the way of regarding time as real.

Three connected questions turn on this passage.

First, interpret the contrast McTaggart draws between perceiving time and thinking time as it really is. Is the contrast about phenomenology, about semantics, or about something else? When he says we could not perceive time as it really is but might be able to think of it as it really is, what exactly would the thought still be missing?

Second, test the position he is describing and rejecting. A hardline eternalist might accept that the A series is a constant illusion and insist that the B series alone can do all the metaphysical work: earlier-than and later-than relations give us a perfectly real, tenseless time even if no moment is objectively present. Does McTaggart, even at this early stage of the essay, have a good reason to reject that B-series-only view? If you were steelmanning the eternalist, how would you answer him?

Third, apply the passage to presentism in particular. A presentist can agree with McTaggart that time requires an A series while denying that the A series is a constant illusion: for her, it is not that every moment has and then loses the property of presentness, but that only the present moment exists, and talk about past and future is talk about how things stand relative to that moment. Does this presentist escape McTaggart's warning that any difficulty with the A series is equally a difficulty with time itself, or does she merely relocate the difficulty?

As a concrete stress test for the third question, imagine two communities of otherwise similar beings. The first speaks and thinks in tensed, A-series terms. The second trains itself to speak and think only in tenseless, B-series terms such as earlier-than and later-than, and insists that "now" is an illusion of perspective. Could the second community actually expunge the A series from its practical reasoning — from deciding, planning, remembering, and anticipating — or would those activities force an indexical now back in? If the A series is inescapable in practice, does that vindicate McTaggart's claim that it is essential to time, or does it tell us something only about us and not about time itself?
