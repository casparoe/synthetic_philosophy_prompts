Obviously I keep circling back to Hitchcock's Rear Window, and I'd like to think through it properly before I steal from it too lazily.

Could you read Rear Window through the philosophy of privacy for me? I'm not after the usual "voyeurism is bad" line. What I'm trying to work out is which account of privacy's value the film actually embodies, if any. Here's the rough map I've picked up. One view says privacy is control: you have privacy when you get to decide what others know and see of you. Another says privacy matters because it gives you an offstage space where you can try out versions of yourself before you have to present a finished self to the world. A third says privacy isn't one thing at all; it's a set of local norms about who may look, in what setting, on what terms, and the wrong is crossing those norms, not the mere fact that some information gets out.

Watching the film, I can't decide whether Jeff's looking is wrong on any of these views, weather it becomes okay because it catches a murderer, or whether it's the viewer's looking, not Jeff's, that the movie is really testing. The neighbors never know they're being watched, and the film turns their little private dramas—the lonely woman, the dancer, the composer—into something almost lovely for an audience. Does the movie ever give a reason to think something is lost in that translation, or does it mostly just enjoy the view? I'd like a reading that's fair to both the film and the philosophy: where the accounts I described would condemn or excuse Jeff, where the film seems to agree or resist them, and what the film adds that the theories leave out.
