I'm moderating a colloquium Q&A at a philosophy of mind workshop, and the speaker defends disjunctivism about perceptual experience — the full metaphysical version, not just the epistemic one. I'd like eight to ten questions drafted that push past the basic statement of the view, since the audience already knows what disjunctivism is.

Make sure there's at least one question on each of the following, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give:

- The argument from illusion. Ask what's wrong with the inference from a hallucinated tomato being subjectively indistinguishable from a seen tomato to the claim that they must be the same kind of mental state. And have a follow-up for when she says (as she will) that indistinguishability is a relation between cases, not a shared property: if the difference doesn't show up from the inside, in what sense is it a difference in how things are for the subject at all?

- The causal challenge. Push on the fact that the same proximal activity in the visual system seems to occur whether the object is there or not, so a physicalist seems forced to say the mental state is the same either way. When she answers that in the veridical case the object itself is a constituent of the experience, follow up with: then what positively explains the conscious episode in the hallucinatory case, where there is no object to be a constituent?

- The basis problem and the negative view of hallucination. Ask why hallucination shouldn't need its own positive nature, given that a bare neural event can apparently produce a vivid, world-involving conscious episode. If she retreats to the line that a hallucination is just a state that cant be introspectively distinguished from a veridical perception, ask whether the bad case is doing any genuine explanatory work or just borrowing its profile from the good case — and what happens in a first-ever hallucination of something nobody has ever veridically perceived.

- The screening-off worry. Ask how the good case and the bad case can fail to share introspectively identical rational grounds without giving the subject a magical ability to rule out deceptively similar bad cases. Also ask why she doesnt just stay an epistemological disjunctivist and leave the metaphysics alone: what does the metaphysical version buy above and beyond having world-involving evidence in the good case?

- The transparency of experience. Disjunctivists often like the claim that introspection reveals the world, not a mental intermediary; but in a lifelike hallucination introspection seems to reveal a world too. Ask why that doesn't push her back toward a common representational kind, and write the follow-up for her likely answer that the hallucination merely introspectively seems the same without being of the same kind.

Feel free to add one or two questions of your own in the same spirit, but no citations or reading-list material, and no generic questions about how she got into philosophy. End with a question about what evidence — empirical or philosophical — would actually count against her view, with a follow-up for her likely answer. Keep the questions conversational, like something you'd actually say out loud in a Q&A.
