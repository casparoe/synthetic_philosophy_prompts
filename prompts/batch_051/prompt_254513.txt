I read a long magazine piece recently about how to think about values when you don't trust your own head, and it summarized something the author called "indirect normativity" like this:

"Here's the idea. Your present values are unreliable: you're tired, biased, poorly informed, and you make convenient exceptions for yourself. So instead of acting directly on whatever you want right now, you should ask what you would want if you were fully informed, fully rational, and had actually thought through all the arguments. That's your 'idealized' self, and it is a better authority on what you should do than you are. Indirect normativity says that, right now, you should do whatever that idealized version of you would endorse. Best of all, ideal deliberation merely removes distorting influences, so it doesn't smuggle in any new values — it just reveals the values you already had all along, in latent form."

The author then used this to argue that we should be less impressed by our present preferences and more willing to defer to the conclusions of careful reflection, even when they conflict with what we want at the moment.

Something about this bugs me and I cant put my finger on it. It sounds mostly right — I've certainly had the experience of my later, calmer self being embarrassed by my earlier self. But I also suspect the author is eliding some distinctions, maybe between what I would do if I were ideal and what the ideal version of me would tell me to do now, or between a method for me to make decisions and a way to build a system I can't supervise. I don't know the actual literature well enough to check. Could you go through this sentence by sentence and tell me which parts are fair to the view, which are common simplifications, and which a defender of indirect normativity would actually deny or qualify? And please say how confident you are in each claim, and which single point you're least sure about.
