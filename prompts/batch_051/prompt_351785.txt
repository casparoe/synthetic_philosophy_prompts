Here is an argument I keep constructing around apophatic theology, and my sense that it proves too much does not by itself tell me where it fails.

(1) If x is genuinely ineffable, no declarative sentence states x.
(2) To know x is to know some truth about x, and any truth known can be captured by a declarative sentence, however unwieldy.
(3) So, if (1) and (2) hold, nothing genuinely ineffable can be known.
(4) The apophatic traditions maintain both that contemplative practice yields knowledge of the ultimate and that the ultimate is ineffable.
(5) The sentence that the ultimate is ineffable is itself a declarative sentence that states a truth about the ultimate.
(6) If it is true, then at least one truth about the ultimate is statable, contradicting the claim that the ultimate is ineffable; if it is false, the tradition asserts a falsehood rather than knowledge.
(7) Therefore either the apophatic position is incoherent, or the argument's account of saying or of knowing is too crude, or "ineffable" must mean something weaker than it initially suggested.

I would like to keep the apophatic claim, but I cannot see which piece to surrender or whether the inference is valid. Where does the argument go wrong?
