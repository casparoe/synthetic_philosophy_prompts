I have been reading about free will on my own for a while now, and there is a question I keep coming back to that I cannot settle. In a well-known experiment, Benjamin Libet asked subjects to make a simple movement at a moment of their own choosing and recorded both their brain activity and the moment at which they reported becoming aware of the intention to move. The result was that a slow build-up of activity in the motor areas began several hundred milliseconds before the subjects reported being aware of having decided to move. Many people have taken this to show that the brain has already begun the movement before the person consciously decides, and some conclude from it that free will is an illusion.

I have also been reading David Hume, who argued that liberty and necessity are perfectly compatible, and who understood liberty not as freedom from causation but as the ability to act on one's own character and inclinations without external compulsion. My question is this: if Hume had known about Libet's result, would it have troubled him? Please reconstruct, as charitably as you can, what you think Hume would have said in response to the finding. Then identify the single point where his response would be most vulnerable, and say whether the pressure created by that weak point pushes toward a full incompatibilist conclusion or toward a different kind of compatibilism, and why.
