I keep noticing a pattern in how we argue about the data the state collects, and I can't find a word for it. A city puts up plate-readers to charge a congestion fee, and someone objects. The reply is: the cameras only log plate numbers for billing, nothing "sensitive" — and honestly that's true, a plate number by itself is boring. Then the transit card logs every tap, and the reply is the same: only for route planning, nothing sensitive. Then school cafeterias move to fingerprint readers to speed up the lunch line — only for the queue, the prints aren't even stored as images. Each of these decisions gets judged on its own, against a background that already includes the previous ones, so each one looks fine: adding one more boring record changes nothing you can point to. But after enough of them, the state has a continuously updated map of where you drive, where you ride, what time you eat, and enough to infer your health and habits — and no one was ever asked to approve that thing. The whole package is invasive, but every individual step was harmless, and the people who approved them could truthfully say "this changes nothing."

I've heard this same debate play out in three registers: German data protection seems to say each step is already a problem because the threat lives in the combination; American practice seems to say each step is fine untill someone misuses it; and Chinese social credit just does the combination in the open as a score, so there's no pretense. My question: is there an established name for the underlying phenomenon — the one where harm is a property of the combination, not of any collected item, so piecemeal review is structurally unable to see it? I'm not after a label; I want to know what the thing actually is, whether it's one concept or a family of related ones, how it differs from ordinary mission creep or a slippery slope, and whether the German/American/Chinese disagreement is really a disagreement about this phenomenon or about something else.
