I have been thinking about a structural parallel between a controversy in moral philosophy and one in evolutionary biology, and I cannot decide whether it runs deep. The first is the debate over human dignity: Kant gives dignity the role of an unconditional and incomparable worth that grounds the requirement to treat persons as ends in themselves, while a line of criticism associated with Ruth Macklin in biomedical ethics replies that appeals to dignity do no independent justificatory work, because respect for autonomy already handles what dignity was meant to handle. The second is the long-standing worry that fitness, invoked to explain differential survival and reproduction, is empty or tautological unless it is given content independent of the facts it is supposed to explain. Map these two debates onto one another: the proposed foundation or posit, the charge of emptiness, and the strategies for saving each concept. Is the resemblance deep enough that the moves used to give fitness independent content — for instance, defining fitness as a probabilistic propensity to survive and reproduce rather than as actual reproductive output — have a counterpart for dignity, or does the analogy break down early because dignity is a normative concept while fitness is descriptive-explanatory? If the mapping fails, identify the first point at which it breaks. Please answer in British English.
