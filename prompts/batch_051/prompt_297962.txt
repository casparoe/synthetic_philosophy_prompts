In my modern European history class we spent two weeks on the collapse of the Weimar Republic and the Nazi seizure of power. After class, two of my classmates, Mateo and Lena, started arguing about a counterfactual our teacher had posed — whether there would have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler — and the argument lasted through lunch. They ended up asking me to referee, and I now have to write a short paper that judges the debate, but I keep going in circles. Could you adjudicate their argument point by point?

Mateo took the "great man" side. He argued that if Hitler had died in infancy there would have been no Nazi Party as we know it: before Hitler took the party over and rebuilt it around himself, it was one splinter group among many on the radical right, and no one else in the movement combined his speaking ability, his tactical instincts, and his genuinely fanatical racial ideology. Without him, the specific program — above all the centrality of exterminatory anti-Semitism — never comes together, and the Holocaust as it actually happened does not occur. Lena answered that this stacked the deck by treating "without Hitler" as if it meant "with nothing bad happening." By 1932 the republic had lost legitimacy across much of the electorate, the Depression had thrown millions out of work, the Communist Party terrified the middle class, and the conservative elites around Hindenburg were looking for a mass right-wing movement they could use against the left; some kind of authoritarian nationalist takeover was highly overdetermined, and anti-Semitism and völkisch nationalism were long-standing currents rather than Hitler's invention. Mateo came back that "some authoritarian government" is not the same proposition as Nazi Germany: a conventional military-conservative dictatorship would probably have rearmed and fought a revisionist war, but it would not have made racial extermination the organizing purpose of the state, and the difference between the historical outcome and the most likely no-Hitler alternative is exactly the individual's contribution. Lena replied that this makes every historical event depend on one person — without Napoleon, the legal code and the wars would be different too — and that the useful question is what made an outcome of that general kind likely; at the level of function, Hitler was replaceable, because the conditions were selecting for a strongman who could fuse the nationalist right with a mass base. Mateo objected that the selection was not, in fact, succeeding without him: Hindenburg, Papen, and Schleicher repeatedly tried to run right-wing governments without Hitler and could not command a stable parliamentary majority, and Hitler's movement supplied the mass base they lacked; moreover, the purge of the SA in 1934, the remilitarization of the Rhineland in 1936 despite military misgivings, and the decision to go to war when he did were his calls rather than structurally forced. Lena said he was still choosing the outcome at too fine a grain, because the deeper structural story is what explains the rise of such a movement at all — the defeat of 1918, the Versailles settlement, the hyperinflation of 1923, the Depression after 1929, the shallow roots of Weimar democracy, and the old elites' willingness to prefer fascism to the left — and a "no Great Depression" counterfactual or a different Versailles changes much more than a "no Hitler" one; she added that the bureaucracy, the medical profession, and the army did not need to be converted by one man before carrying out mass violence. Lena's last move was to say the question was badly posed: what we should ask is how much the outcome changes when you swap the person as against when you change the conditions, and she claimed it changes much less. Mateo said that reframing begged the question, because it assumed in advance that individuals are small variables; if deleting one person makes the Holocaust not happen as it in fact happened, that one variable is doing enormous explanatory work however you do the arithmetic.
