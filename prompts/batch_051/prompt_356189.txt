I have been trying to get clear on something that struck me while preparing a course on the ethics of technology, and I keep going back and forth about whether it is a real structural insight or just a pattern I am overfitting to.

The idea is this. In software engineering, there is a principle called information hiding or encapsulation: a well-designed module exposes a narrow interface and buries its internal representation, so that other parts of a program can interact with it only through operations that the module itself sanctions. The rationale is not that the internal state is secret in any absolute sense—the source code may be open to inspection, and anyone with access to it can read the representation directly—but rather that uncontrolled access corrupts the integrity of the system over time and makes the behavior of the whole harder to reason about. What counts as a legitimate access to an object, on this view, depends on the role the caller occupies and the purpose of the access, not simply on whether the caller can technically get at the data.

I see a close parallel with the idea in privacy theory that privacy is not fundamentally about secrecy or about an individual's control over information, but about whether information flows in a way that respects context-relative norms—what Helen Nissenbaum calls contextual integrity. In both cases the problem is not that some bit of information is hidden from everyone, but that it is disclosed to someone outside the appropriate context, or through an interface that was not meant for that use. A health question answered to a doctor in a medical portal is not the same data leak as that same answer handed to an insurer or an employer, even if the content is identical.

What I want to press is whether this is a deep identity of structure—in which case work in privacy theory might be translatable into language about modules, interfaces, and callers, or vice versa—or only a loose analogy. If it is deep, what exactly is the thing on the software side that corresponds to a privacy norm, and where on the privacy side is the equivalent of a maintainability argument? If it is superficial, where precisely does the analogy break? Specifically, does the idea of preventing uncontrolled access in order to preserve system integrity really capture the normative weight of a privacy violation, or does it leave out the distinctive wrong involved when a person, rather than a program, is the one whose information is shared outside the sanctioned context? In answering, state the mapping between the two sides precisely, say what would have to be true of both domains for results to transfer from one to the other, and name something the analogy seems to predict that is actually false about one of the two domains.

Please write the answer in American English.
