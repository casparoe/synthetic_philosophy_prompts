The same person who treats the claim that someone is "such a Leo" as superstition — and who would consider an employer that screened applicants by star sign either absurd or discriminatory — will often, without embarrassment, explain a colleague's work habits by that colleague's being a Millennial, a relative's politics by their being a Boomer, or a teenager's digital habits by their being Gen Z. Both practices sort people by the calendar and then infer psychology, values, or likely fortunes from when they were born. Yet generation-talk is respectable in a way zodiac-talk is not: it fills serious journalism, political analysis, hiring commentary, and market research, and it shapes how people are actually classified and treated.

That is the asymmetry I want examined. Is there a principled difference between treating birth cohorts as real social kinds and treating astrological signs as real social kinds, or is "generation" merely astrology with institutional confidence? Give me the strongest candidate justification you can build — presumably one that appeals to shared formative conditions, real historical events, or actuarial regularities — and test it against the hard cases it invites. If the defense is that cohort categories earn their keep by supporting true statistical generalizations, say whether that standard would also rehabilitate astrology (and whether you can live with that), and whether a true cohort-level generalization can license a judgment about an individual. End with a verdict: keep ordinary generation-talk as it is, keep it only under stated constraints, or give it up.
