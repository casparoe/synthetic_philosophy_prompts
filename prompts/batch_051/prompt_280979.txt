Next week I'm giving a presentation to the research team at a global-priorities organization where I'm a visiting fellow. The team is mostly quantitative social scientists and economists, with two philosophers; they use the importance–tractability–neglectedness (ITN) framework constantly to produce quick first-pass cause evaluations and cost-effectiveness models. My thesis is that the framework as usually used double-counts, and that the only defensible reconstruction of it treats the three labels as summary statistics of a single underlying object: the expected marginal value of an additional unit of effort directed at a problem. On that reconstruction, importance is the value at stake conditional on making progress at the current margin, tractability is the derivative of expected progress with respect to effort (so it cannot be estimated independently of importance), and neglectedness is a proxy for where one stands on a diminishing-returns curve, rather than a separate feature of the world. I plan to argue that we should stop scoring the three factors separately and instead model one function from resources to expected progress, reporting ITN as descriptive statistics of that function.

My compressed argument, as I plan to present it: (i) standard ITN practice scores the three factors separately and then combines them, but tractability estimates already incorporate the size and stakes of the problem, so importance is counted twice; (ii) neglectedness does no independent work unless it is tracking the slope of the marginal-returns curve, in which case it belongs to the same function that tractability summarizes; (iii) three qualitative scores cannot constrain prioritization by themselves, because a supporter of any cause can redefine the problem narrowly enough to score high on all three.
