I am a doctoral candidate in comparative federalism, and I am writing a paper on how subsidiarity clauses behave when the regulated activity does not respect territorial boundaries. For that paper I want a careful application of a clause to a case its drafters never contemplated. The case is set in a fictional federation to keep the doctrinal issues vivid; no real jurisdiction is involved.

The Constitution of the Federation of the Nine Cantons, adopted in 1974, contains the following clause, drafted chiefly with transboundary pollution, technical product standards, and intercity transport in mind:

"Article 14. Where the Constitution has not assigned a matter to the exclusive competence of the Federation, the Federation may act only if and in so far as two conditions are jointly satisfied: (a) the objectives of the proposed action cannot be sufficiently achieved by the constituent units acting individually or in coordination; and (b) the action, by reason of its geographical reach, cost, or effects across unit boundaries, can be better achieved at Federation level. Any measure adopted on this basis shall not exceed what is necessary to attain those objectives and shall leave the units the greatest measure of autonomous decision-making consistent with their effective attainment."

The unforeseen case is this. The federal government proposes the Digital Intermediary Standards Act. The Act would impose a uniform federal code of unlawful online content—covering child sexual abuse material, incitement to imminent violence, non-consensual intimate imagery, and a narrow class of election-period disinformation—and would require very large online platforms to remove such content within twenty-four hours of notification and to publish the principal parameters governing their recommender systems. The nine constituent units currently regulate this unevenly: two have stricter hate-speech provisions, three impose broader liability for defamation, and four have no platform-specific rules at all. The largest platforms operate in every unit but keep their data centres concentrated in a single unit, with the result that content uploaded in a permissive unit circulates freely in stricter ones. Several units have proposed a concordat instead: reciprocal recognition of moderation decisions plus common interoperability, transparency, and reporting standards, but no uniform substantive code. The federal government replies that a concordat is voluntary, can be denounced on short notice, and cannot prevent platforms from routing content through permissive units; it therefore insists that only binding federal law can secure a baseline of safety and transparency across the Federation, and that the same reasoning authorizes it to act under Article 14.

I would like a disciplined application of Article 14 to this Act under four approaches: (1) the plain or grammatical meaning of the text; (2) the purpose of the clause; (3) what the drafters of the 1974 constitution would most plausibly have said if this case had been put to them; and (4) what a reasonable, informed reader of the constitution today would take the clause to require. For each approach, state whether the Federation may enact the Act, and on what reasoning. Be precise about how each approach handles the coordination option in condition (a), how it treats the federal government's definition of its own objectives, and what each approach does with the final sentence's guarantee of maximum unit autonomy. Where an approach turns on contested empirical claims—for example, whether the concordat would in fact be effective—identify the relevant factual predicate and say how the constitutional question should be resolved when that predicate is disputed rather than silently assuming either side's prediction.

Finally, tell me which of the four approaches should control here and why; flag the point at which Article 14 runs out and the decision must be made on constitutional-policy grounds rather than by interpretation; and identify the strategy by which a level of government can render subsidiarity review practically vacuous, and whether Article 14's final sentence is equipped to prevent it.
