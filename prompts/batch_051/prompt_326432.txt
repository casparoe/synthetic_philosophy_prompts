You are a political philosopher who works on the concept of terrorism and the justification of political violence. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the strongest case for each verdict before you settle on one.

In a graduate seminar on political violence, we were given a five-part working definition of terrorism: (1) the use or threat of serious violence against persons or property; (2) the targeting of non-combatants, or an indifference to the combatant/non-combatant distinction, rather than military or security targets; (3) an intention to coerce an audience beyond the immediate target, such as a government, a corporation, or a public, rather than merely to destroy the target itself; (4) a political, religious, or ideological motive; and (5) perpetration by a non-state actor. The instructor presented each element as doing exclusionary work: (1) excludes ordinary acquisitive crime, (2) excludes legitimate military targeting, (3) excludes pure sabotage aimed only at degrading an operation, (4) excludes kidnapping for ransom, and (5) sets aside state terror, which the course explicitly bracketed.

Now consider the case the instructor left unresolved. A decentralized group of environmental and anti-mining activists detonates small charges at night at an ore-processing plant while it is empty, and shortly afterward sets fire to a mining company's construction camp before workers arrive. The group plants no booby traps, selects sites and timing to avoid injury, and releases a statement documenting the company's record of pollution and land seizures, demanding that the company withdraw and that the state cancel the project's permits. No one is hurt, but the damage runs to tens of millions of dollars and the action makes other firms reassess their exposure in the region. Prosecutors charge the activists with terrorism; the defense describes the acts as political sabotage, not terrorism, because the perpetrators intended no casualties and struck only property.

Work through the five criteria one by one and say honestly whether the case satisfies each, flagging where a criterion underdetermines the verdict. Then say whether the case shows that the working definition needs revision — in particular, whether serious violence against property should be enough for the violence element, or whether the definition should require an intent to kill or injure persons; and whether property can count as the target in the non-combatant sense at all, or whether that element requires human victims. Finally, say which single criterion is doing the most contested work in this case, and which direction of revision you think is most defensible and why.
