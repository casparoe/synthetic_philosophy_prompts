Exam question: Historians often say an event like the French Revolution had many causes, and they also sometimes say it was overdetermined. Explain (a) the difference between mere causal pluralism and overdetermination, (b) why overdetermination is thought to create trouble for counterfactual theories of causation, and (c) whether that trouble should make historians give up singular causal claims such as "the fiscal crisis caused the Revolution." Use at least one standard philosophical example.

Students answer: Causal pluralism just means an event has many causes, whereas overdetermination is the stronger claim that two or more causes were each sufficient for the effect on their own. The classic example is the firing squad: if several soldiers fire at once and more than one bullet would have been fatal by itself, the death is overdetermined because any one of several bullets would have killed the prisoner. The same can happen in history: the French Revolution may have been overdetermined, since the crown's fiscal crisis and the spread of Enlightenment ideas could each have produced a revolution on their own. Overdetermination is a problem for the counterfactual theory because that theory says c caused e iff, had c not occurred, e would not have occurred. In the firing squad, if we take away one bullet the prisoner still dies, so the theory says no single bullet caused the death, which is absurd. But this is too quick, because necessitation is not the right test for causation. The right test is the INUS account: a cause is an insufficient but necessary part of an unnecessary but sufficient condition. Under that account each bullet is a cause, since it is part of a sufficient condition. Mill held that a cause is the whole sum of conditions, so on Mill's view overdetermination is no problem because each sufficient condition is itself a cause. So historians can keep singular causal claims. Saying "the fiscal crisis caused the Revolution" is shorthand for "the fiscal crisis was a causally relevant factor," and whether a factor was causally relevant is an objective matter of fact. Overdetermination therefore doesn't make causal claims subjective; it only shows we need a pragmatic rule for which cause to foreground in a narrative.
