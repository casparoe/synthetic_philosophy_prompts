I work in the manuscript cataloging department of a university library, and I've just come off a week-long argument with a colleague about whether a 14th-century Tuscan devotional text should be tagged Latin or early Italian. We have two or three of these fights a month, and the metadata actually matters — it drives what gets digitized, what language tags researchers can filter on, and whether a small grant for "vernacular manuscripts" covers something. Catalogers here have no real linguistics background, and our head of special collections has to sign off on the tagging policy. I want a decision procedure we can actually run in about 30 minutes per item: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it stops with a definite Latin tag, where with a definite early Italian tag, and where it stops and says its a values call to be made by a person. It should be grounded in whatever philosophy of language and historical linguistics actually tell us about gradual change and identity over time — there's no sharp line between Latin and Italian, people kept writing Latin-shaped prose while speaking something that wasn't Latin anymore, and the label is partly a cataloging convention, not a discovery. Then run the procedure on three hypothetical items: (1) a 9th-century property record from northern Italy whose clauses mostly still work like Latin but have a few repeated Romance features; (2) a 13th-century Florentine merchant's account book with no literary ambitions; (3) a 15th-century sermon written in a deliberately Latinized style but meant to be read aloud to laypeople. At least one should come out with an answer we won't like, and you should say so plainly. Also flag the cases your procedure can't settle and where it will most likely misfire.
