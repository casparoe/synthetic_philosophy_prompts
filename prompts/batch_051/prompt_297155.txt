Van Fraassen's Reflection principle strikes me as nearly right: a rational agent's present probability for a proposition should equal her expected future probability for it — if I am certain that tomorrow I will assign a proposition probability 0.7, I should assign it 0.7 now. It seems to fall directly out of conditionalization and the law of total probability, and it promises a single cross-temporal constraint on rationality. But I also hold a firm intuition that if I expect to arrive at a belief through exhaustion, intoxication, or a drug that reliably induces overconfidence, I should not defer to that future self now; I would rationally bind myself against that future judgment. Which should yield? If we amend the principle by deferring only to future selves that are suitably rational and at least as well informed as we are, does it collapse into a tautology, and can the restriction be stated non-circularly without surrendering the principle's content? Alternatively, should the intuition be debunked as the product of imagining an arational change in belief while applying an evidential norm to it? Present both sides at their strongest, and for each possible resolution — amend the principle, abandon the intuition, or show that the two are compatible — say what would justify it.
