The standard argument for classifying clientelist transfers—politicians distributing publicly funded jobs, building materials, or school fees to supporters, officials taking gifts to speed up permits—as corruption is the principal-agent argument. In that argument, corruption is the betrayal of a principal's interest by an agent entrusted with discretion, for the benefit of the agent or the agent's network. Public office has this structure: the officeholder is an agent of the public, whose interest lies in the impartial, rule-governed use of the office and its resources. Clientelist transfers use official discretion to benefit a personal network rather than the public. So they are corruption, whatever local participants' vocabulary of gift-giving or taking care of one's people says.

Now consider a setting where the argument's central descriptive premise is false: the formal state is a thin shell over a patronage network; holding office is widely understood, by officials and citizens alike, as a legitimate way to channel resources to one's own people; and there is no effective public principal whose interest in impartiality is being betrayed. Does the principal-agent argument survive this setting, does it need repair, or does it collapse into presupposing the Western office/public distinction it was supposed to justify as universal? Which premise is under pressure, what is the strongest repaired version—a normativized principal, an equal-treatment baseline, a harm-based criterion, or something else—and what does the repaired argument lose, especially as an account of how the practice looks from the inside? I am asking what happens to the argument itself, not what anti-corruption policy follows.
