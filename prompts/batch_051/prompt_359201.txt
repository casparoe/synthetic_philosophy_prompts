Here's a passage from early in Mill's Considerations on Representative Government, where he's characterizing a rival view of government before giving his own:

They "are not made, but grow." Our business with them, as with the other facts of the universe, is to acquaint ourselves with their natural properties, and adapt ourselves to them.

Here's the thing. Epistocrats sometimes say that if voters are ignorant, the fix is to weight votes or restrict the franchise by competence. But this Mill passage seems to get cited against them: institutions aren't just designed from scratch on the basis of who knows what; they have to grow out of a people's habits, and a competence-restricted franchise might lack the legitimacy that grown institutions have. Write an essay on whether the "not made, but grow" point actually cuts against epistocracy, or only against a strawman version of it. Reach a definite verdict and justify it.
