I'm a PhD student working on early modern French moral philosophy and I keep noticing an asymmetry in how my friends and I judge self-deception. A friend who thinks she's one serious project away from being a significant painter, and who is alive and disciplined when she works, gets called a bit deluded but brave — nobody stages an intervention. A friend who thinks her lukewarm partner will become devoted, and who is just as alive and disciplined about the relationship, gets called needy or in denial and we do intervene. Émilie du Châtelet's essay on happiness pushes on exactly this: she thought happiness requires not just clear-sighted reflection but passions, tastes, and a needed dose of illusion, which should be nourished rather than stripped away — while also insisting that some illusions, like those built on status or wealth, are predjudices to be shed. Is the painter-lover asymmetry defensible in her terms, or are we just protecting the illusion with the socially approved shape? Give me the strongest defense of treating them differently, then the strongest case for treating them the same, and an honest verdict on keep, reverse, or drop.
