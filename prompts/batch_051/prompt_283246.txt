I am the student-body treasurer at my high school. Each month I receive three or four proposals from clubs for incentive schemes — canned-food drives scored by item count, reading challenges scored by pages logged, service-hour requirements for honor cords, and similar quotas and leaderboards — and my review is what the faculty advisor relies on before signing off. I keep watching sensible-sounding targets get gamed within a month: people donate food nobody wants, log pages or hours they did not really read or serve, or find ways to inflate a number without doing the thing the number was supposed to count. By the time the gaming shows up, we are stuck with the scheme for the rest of the semester. I understand this as Goodhart's law: once a metric becomes a target, people optimize the metric and it stops tracking the underlying good. What I need is not the slogan but something I can run before we adopt a proposal.

Please write a pre-adoption stress test for incentive schemes: a reusable decision procedure I can run, with my committee of students (no statistics background), on any proposed metric or reward structure in under thirty minutes, producing a clear ruling of adopt, modify, or reject. Give me the steps in order; for each step, say what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a modify or a reject, and where it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than something a checklist can settle. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. The test should specifically catch: a metric that can be inflated without the underlying good increasing; self-reported or otherwise unfalsifiable numbers; a target that crowds out other goals rather than serving them; and any point where the reward is attached to the report rather than to the reality.

Then run the procedure on three proposals, showing exactly how each step lands and what ruling comes out:
1. A canned-food drive that gives a pizza party to the homeroom with the highest total number of items donated.
2. A reading challenge that awards points per page logged in an online form.
3. A community-service program that awards a graduation cord for a self-reported total of 40 hours.

I am asking for a committed response: make definite choices about what the procedure says and defend them, rather than laying out alternatives for me to select among. Lead with the bottom line in your first sentence — the one-sentence core rule of the test — and then give the procedure and your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the request. If the test would reject or force major changes to a proposal I might personally like, do not soften that; show where the test bites.
