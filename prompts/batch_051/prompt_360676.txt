I’ve been sitting in on a formal epistemology seminar, and our discussion of the Principal Principle has gone in circles for weeks. In David Lewis’s 1980 paper A Subjectivist’s Guide to Objective Chance, he states the Principle roughly as follows: for any reasonable initial credence function, one’s credence in a proposition A, conditional on the complete history of the world up to a time t together with a complete theory of chance for that world, should equal the chance that the theory assigns to A, provided no inadmissible information has been smuggled in. He treats this as a rational constraint on credence, not as a definition of chance.

Our group has split into three camps. One says the Principle is a substantive norm of rationality: chance is an objective feature of the world, and rational credence must defer to it. Another says it is really a conceptual constraint on chance itself: any candidate property that failed to satisfy the Principle simply would not be chance, so the Principle cannot be false but also doesn’t constrain chance from outside. A third says the original Principle is false because of undermining futures and must be replaced by something like Hall’s New Principle, but insists that the replacement is still a substantive constraint on credence. Everyone accepts the same formal equation; nobody moves.

My working theory is that the disagreement isn’t about probabilities or counterexamples at all. I suspect the camps are using “norm of rationality” and “chance” in different senses, and that Lewis’s text bundles together a metaphysical claim about what chance is with an epistemic norm about what credence to have. But maybe I’m wrong: maybe there is a real formal question lurking under the surface, or a disagreement about which cases count as admissible evidence. Diagnose this stalled debate. Is it verbal, substantive, or several different questions under one heading? And what, if anything, would count as progress given the diagnosis?
