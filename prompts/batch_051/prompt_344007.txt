You're a philosopher of science who has spent proper time on the Quine–Putnam indispensability argument and the newer literature on mathematical explanation in science, and I'd like you to think carefully here. Before you settle on a diagnosis, brainstorm the different readings of the phrases "indispensable to a good explanation", "genuinely explains", and "reason to believe in", and keep track of which reading is doing the work at each step below.

A colleague keeps pressing the following argument on me and I can't find the exit. I'll number his steps.

(1) We never directly observe electrons or quarks. Our reason for believing in them is that they earn their keep in our best scientific explanations: they are theoretical posits whose explanatory work is the entire case for their existence.

(2) So the operative rule is this: if a posit is indispensable to a good scientific explanation, that is sufficient reason to believe in it.

(3) Now take the stock cases from the mathematical explanation literature. Periodical cicadas emerge on 13- and 17-year cycles, and the fact that 13 and 17 are prime seems to do real explanatory work in accounting for why those periods evolved and why they avoid certain predator cycles. In the honeycomb case, the fact that regular hexagonal tiling minimises perimeter for a given area is doing the explaining. If (2) is right, we already have reason to believe in numbers — not just in cicadas and wax.

(4) My colleague is a nominalist, so he won't accept (3). But here's the twist: he doesn't deny that the mathematics is explanatory. He says explanatory work is cheap. Centres of mass explain the tumbling of rigid bodies; the average taxpayer explains patterns in tax statistics; infinite-population limits explain finite population dynamics; the ideal gas explains real gasses. None of these things exists, yet the explanations are perfectly good.

(5) Therefore explanatory indispensability cannot be sufficient for existence, and the platonist inference in (3) is blocked.

(6) But if explanatory indispensability is not sufficient for existence, the rule in (2) was never valid. And if that rule was the only reason we had for electrons and quarks, then that reason evaporates too.

(7) So the very move my colleague uses to kill platonism kills scientific realism about unobservables as well. You cannot be a nominalist about numbers while staying a realist about electrons.

I badly want to keep three things: scientific realism about electrons, nominalism about numbers, and the claim that the cicada and honeycomb explanations are genuinely mathematical rather than merely being described mathematically. Which step in (1)–(7) should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Once you have picked a step, tell me how whoever loses that round — my colleague or the platonist — would answer back, and whether that answer-back is any good.
