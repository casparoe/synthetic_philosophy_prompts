Okay so I just got out of a long argument with a friend about whether you should be afraid of being dead—not of dying, but of actually being dead—and he kept coming back to the move that you didn't exist before you were born and you don't lose any sleep over that, so being dead ought to be just as untroubling. That sent me looking for where the argument actually comes from, and it's in Lucretius, On the Nature of Things, book three, in the middle of Nature lecturing a mortal about mortality. The passage that got me is this:

Look back:
Nothing to us was all fore-passed eld
Of time the eternal, ere we had a birth.
And Nature holds this like a mirror up
Of time-to-be when we are dead and gone.
And what is there so horrible appears?
Now what is there so sad about it all?
Is't not serener far than any sleep?

Here's what I want to sort out. I've been reading the passage as an argument that being dead can't be bad because it's the same kind of thing as your nonexistence before birth, and you'd never call that a harm. But I'm also persuaded by the deprivation account of death's badness—the idea, usually associated with Thomas Nagel, that death is bad not because of anything you experience but because of the goods it takes away from you. So I want a real comparison of those two lines of thought. Specifically: is Lucretius making a metaphysical claim (prenatal and posthumous nonexistence are on a par, so if one isn't bad neither is the other), or is he making a more psychological/existential claim (you already manage not to be troubled by the one, so you can learn not to be troubled by the other)? And does the deprivation account actually have a good reply to the mirror? It seems like it has to either say prenatal nonexistence is also a deprivation, or find a principled asymmetry between the past and the future—like how you'd be upset to learn you were going to get ten fewer years than you'd have otherwise had, but you aren't upset that you were born ten years later than you might have been. But then is that asymmetry doing real metaphysical work, or is it just reporting on how our cares happen to point forward?

Basically im trying to figure out whether the mirror passage is an argument or just a therapeutic image, and whether the deprivation account can absorb it or has to bite a bullet. Where does each view misdescribe the other, and is there a version of either one that survives the face-off?

Wait actually I want to push on one more thing after writing that: does Lucretius even need the symmetry argument? Could he just run the Epicurean line that there's no subject left to be harmed, and leave the mirror as rhetoric? And if so, does the deprivation account's real opponent become the "no subject, no harm" point rather than the symmetry, and how does the comparison change?
