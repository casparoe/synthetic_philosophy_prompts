Last weekend I was at dinner with two friends, Dana and Marco, and I'm still not sure which of them to believe. We'd just come from a museum where a small still life that had been attributed to an old master for over a century was relabeled as a forgery from the 1940s — tests found a pigment that didn't exist in the master's day. The canvas itself hasnt changed; nobody had suspected anything for decades, and it's still just as beautiful as before. Dana said the people now flocking to see the newly relabeled picture are being snobs: the painting gave everyone the same experience before the announcement, and not one atom of it has changed, so any drop in its value is just name fetishism. Marco shot back that the object of appreciation isn't the bare painted surface — it's the artwork, and an artwork's identity includes its history. A work by the old master consists partly in his decisions, his constraints, what he could and couldn't do; the forgery is a different achievement altogether, so after the relabeling we are literally looking at a different work, not the same work with worse press.

They went a few rounds. Dana said Marco's view implies two physically identical canvases could be two different artworks and you'd need a museum label to tell which is better; so any value that survives the reveal is value placed on a story, not on the picture. Marco answered that two identical strings of words can be a confession or a poem depending on who wrote them and when; artworks are individuated by their causal history, so the story isn't an add-on but part of what makes it art in the first place. Dana came back that this turns aesthetics into detective work, and anyway the 'original' may be heavily restored or partly painted by assistants, so Marco's principle should force him to say almost no painting in a museum is really the original. Marco said all that only shows history matters, not that it doesn't; he even granted that for someone who only wanted the visual experience, the forgery genuinly was just as good before the reveal — his point was that such a person isn't appreciating art in the full sense. They both turned to me to referee, and I honestly couldn't.

Please go through their exchange point by point and say who is right about which specific claim — don't just crown a winner. I'm especially curious where each one overreaches or talks past the other, and whether there's a live position neither of them considered.
