Something I keep going back and forth on: the standard argument that genuine moral dilemnas are impossible goes something like this. If you really ought to do A and really ought to do B, but A and B can't both be done, then either 'ought implies can' gets violated or an agglomeration principle (ought A, ought B, therefore ought A-and-B) commits you to an impossible action. So any apparent dilemma must be one where one obligation actually overrides the other and you just haven't finished the calculation.

But that argument seems to assume the agent is a single unified will issuing one consistent set of oughts. A lot of cognitive science now, and a lot of rationalist-adjacent stuff about akrasia and internal conflict, treats the mind as a population of semi-autonomous subagents that negotiate, each with its own goals and its own 'oughts'. If you take that picture seriously, a moral dilemma might not be one system failing to rank two options; it might be two subagents each issuing a real demand, and the conflict is between them rather than inside a single system.

So: does the classic no-genuine-dilemmas argument survive that change of picture? Which premise does the subagent view actually put pressure on, and is there a repaired version that says something weaker but still true? Or does the argument just stop applying because it was only ever about a unified agent that maybe doesn't exist?

Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion, and in British English.
