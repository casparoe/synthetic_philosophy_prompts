I have spent years reading the rationalist forums and the embedded agency literature that grew out of them, and there is a comparison I have never managed to make precise on my own. I would like help comparing two different ways of diagnosing what goes wrong with the Cartesian picture of an agent as a cleanly bounded system that receives observations and emits actions.

The first diagnosis is representational. A real agent is built out of the same world it is trying to model: its map is drawn on, and by, a part of the territory. It therefore cannot contain a complete, fully accurate model of itself or of its own future reasoning. From this, the familiar failures follow. Logical omniscience is unavailable. There is no well-defined total prior over a hypothesis space that includes propositions about the agent's own source code. The agent cannot simply assign a probability to the output of a computation it is presently deciding how to perform. On this diagnosis, the deep problem is self-modelling and self-reference, and the boundary between agent and environment is not something the world hands to the agent; it is a cut the agent has to draw for itself, at some computational cost.

The second diagnosis is decision-theoretic. Once the agent is an object inside the world, its actions are not clean interventions made from outside that world. The agent's choices, its copies and successors, and other systems' models of it all live in the same causal network. The tidy separation between the evidence a choice gives you about the world and the causal consequences of the choice can then break down. The agent may have to reason about its own algorithm and about what its own decisions reveal about that algorithm. Standard tools—expected utility over a fixed outcome space, or causal decision theory with a well-defined intervention—lose their obvious justification. On this diagnosis, the deep problem is that decision theory has to be done from the inside, without a God's-eye view of possible acts and outcomes.

My questions are these. Are these really two different diagnoses, or is one a special case of the other? Suppose the representational problem were solved—suppose the agent had a workable way to reason about its own computations without requiring logical omniscience. Would the decision-theoretic problem remain in full force? Conversely, if the decision-theoretic problem were solved, would the representational problem still bite? Which of the two, if either, is the more fundamental obstacle, and which should be tackled first if the aim is a theory of embedded agents that amounts to more than a loose analogy?

I want a genuinely critical answer, not a diplomatic survey. If I have drawn the line between the two diagnoses in the wrong place, if one of them collapses into the other, or if asking which is more fundamental is itself a confused way of putting the matter because the two levels are not rivals in the way I have assumed, tell me that plainly and say what question I should be asking instead. If there is a third failure mode that my two-way split papers over, make the case for it.

Please write the whole answer in British English.
