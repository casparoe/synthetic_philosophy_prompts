I spent this evening in a long argument with a close friend about whether resentment is ever anything but a destructive vice. She held that bitterness always damages the person who carries it and never the person who caused it; I held that some resentments are the mind's way of registering a real wrong, and that the feeling can be a signal rather than the disease. After two hours it became clear we were not arguing about the evidence at all — we were using the word resentment for different things. Before we resume, I want a definition precise enough to let us locate the actual disagreement.

I am not after a dictionary definition or a survey of usage. I want a working definition that draws the right line between resentment, envy, moral indignation, and ordinary anger, and that yields defensible verdicts on these six cases:

1. An employee is passed over for promotion in favor of the boss's visibly less competent nephew, and for months afterward the mention of the boss's name brings a settled, cold anger.
2. A poker player who lost a big hand to a stronger hand is irritated at the winner all evening, while fully conceding that the winner did nothing wrong.
3. A person feels bitterness toward a friend who has just inherited a large fortune. She believes neither that the friend did anything wrong nor that the inheritance is unjust; what stings is simply the gap between their situations.
4. A driver suffers a medical episode, loses control of the car through no fault of his own, and damages your parked car; you feel a focused anger toward the driver.
5. A worker in a long-underpaid occupation feels lasting bitterness toward the better-paid occupation, without blaming any particular member of it — a bitterness aimed at a structure more than at persons.
6. A woman feels the same kind of cold anger toward someone who openly cheated her brother, although she herself lost nothing and took no part in the matter.

My own preliminary views, so you know where pressure should be applied: case 1 strikes me as a paradigm of resentment, and case 2 as clearly not resentment at all. I would like case 3 to come out as envy rather than resentment, but only if the definition can explain why case 4 — which involves no fault either — still counts as resentment. I have no settled view on case 5; it is the case closest to the side I was defending all evening, so I want an honest verdict rather than a flattering one. And I suspect case 6 is indignation rather than resentment because the wrong was not done to me, but I want to see whether that instinct survives a precise definition.

Please structure your response in the following sections and lengths:

1. Proposed definition (130–180 words). State the working definition, with numbered conditions if it needs them, and say in a sentence or two how it separates resentment from envy, from indignation, and from plain anger.
2. Application to the cases (180–250 words). Run the definition through all six cases and give an explicit verdict for each — resentment, not resentment, or genuinely borderline — with one sentence of reasoning per case.
3. Revisions and hard verdicts (120–180 words). Identify the case that puts the most pressure on your definition — especially any case where the definition gave the wrong answer or no answer — say whether that forces a revision or whether you will defend a surprising verdict, and state the revised or defended result explicitly.
4. Where the definition gives way (80–120 words). Say where the line between resentment and envy, or between resentment and indignation, is least stable, and tell me whether the term resentment is doing two different jobs — naming a feeling and condemning or excusing it — and whether your definition can keep those jobs apart.
