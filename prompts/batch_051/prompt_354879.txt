You are a philosopher of mind and language who specialises in the everyday concept of intentional action; treat this as a reflective-equilibrium exercise. I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I have been collecting my own reactions to cases where someone knowingly brings about a side effect while acting for a different reason. My gut verdicts are these.

1. I changed a caching rule purely to make a dashboard faster, and I knew the change would also make a colleague's flaky test pass reliably. I do not think I made the test pass reliably intentionally.

2. I changed a caching rule purely to make a dashboard faster, and I knew the change would also make a colleague's integration test fail for the rest of the day. I do think I made the test fail intentionally, even though I did not want it to fail.

3. I switched a logging library for my own convenience, and I knew the switch would also close a security hole that had been open for weeks. I was glad about the security fix. I want to say I closed the security hole intentionally, but next to case 1 I cannot say what the difference is.

4. A colleague enabled a feature flag for her own A/B test, knowing it would flood the support queue with auto-generated tickets. She says she did not intend to flood the queue; I think she flooded it intentionally.

5. A junior engineer ran a cleanup script he did not understand, knowing there was some chance it would delete production data; when it did, 'intentionally deleted the data' felt too strong. But if a senior engineer ran the same script knowing exactly what it would do, without wanting the data lost, I would say she deleted the data intentionally.

Is there one principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that captures all or nearly all of these?
