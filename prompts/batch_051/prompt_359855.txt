I'm in a philosophy of emotion class right now, and we've spent the last couple weeks on the Stoics versus the Romantics — the question of whether emotions are part of the good life or just noise that distorts judgement. The Stoic view, at least as we're reading it, isn't the caricature of having no feelings at all; it's that emotions are false value-judgements: you assent to the impression that some external thing is genuinely good or bad, when really only your own character is. The sage aims at apatheia, but Seneca is careful to allow first movements, the involuntary flinch that happens before you get a say in it. The Romantic side runs the other way: feeling is a kind of perception, it's how you know what matters, and a life that supresses emotion is maimed, not wise.

I keep wanting to test this debate against the public mourning after Diana died in 1997, and I can't settle it on my own. Quick recap, since not everyone remembers the details: she dies in a car crash in Paris on August 31st, and the public response is enormous — flowers piled outside Kensington Palace, people queuing for hours to sign condolence books — while the royal family stays away in Balmoral and the flag over Buckingham Palace isn't flown at half-mast. Everyone reads that as cold and uncaring, and the anger gets loud enough that the Queen comes back to London early and gives a live tv adress, and the flag ends up lowered. Blair went on tv the morning of the death and more or less gave the whole thing its emotional frame.

What I'd like is a careful case analysis of that episode run through the Stoic/Romantic debate in both directions — theory illuminating the episode, episode testing the theory. Please structure it like this:

- The Stoic reading (about 200 words): what Stoic theory, properly understood, says about the Queen's restraint and the public's outpouring. Was she actually being Stoic in the technical sense, or was that just protocol and coldness? Would a Stoic condemn the public's grief as assent to a false impression, or would they make room for it?
- The Romantic reading (about 200 words): what a Romantic would say the episode got right about emotion, and what they'd fault in the royals for. Be honest about the hard part for this side: whether grief for someone you never met, amplified by 24-hour media, still counts as authentic feeling on their own view.
- Where each reading breaks down (about 200 words): the features of the episode each theory struggles with — the mediated, performative quality of the mourning, the fact that restraint was experienced as an insult and emotional display as a virtue, and the pressure on the Queen to perform a grief she may not actually have felt.
- What the episode shows about the debate (about 200 words): does the case favour one side, or does it suggest both are missing something — say, that emotions have a social and communicative function that is neither private judgement nor private authenticity?
- My verdict (about 100 words): one paragraph on whether the episode should move someone off the fence, and toward which position or third option.
