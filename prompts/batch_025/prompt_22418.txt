I'm preparing a long-form interview for a science-and-ideas magazine with a philosopher who defends a sophisticated Russellian-constitutive panpsychism: the view that what physics describes in intrinsic terms is already experiential, and that higher-level consciousness arises through an appropriate organizational relation among micro-experiential properties rather than through the addition of a new non-physical ingredient. I want the conversation to move quickly past the familiar “everything has an experience” slogan and into the parts of the position where a careful critic can bite. Please draft nine interview questions, each followed by a short follow-up keyed to the answer the guest is most likely to give.

The questions should cover: why the intrinsic nature of physical properties matters if physics is silent about it; how the view avoids merely relabeling dualism or smuggling in a hidden experiential substance; the combination problem in its strongest form, not as a general worry but as a demand for an account of why organized micro-experientia yields a unified macro-experience rather than an aggregate of micro-perspectives; the access problem, i.e., why we should expect to have any introspective access to the micro-experiential properties we posit; the problem of the many, if every physical system has some experiential property, why is the relevant bearer of experience the brain, the body, or some other system; how the view handles the apparent explanatory gap between functional organization and subjective character; what role, if any, quantum mechanics or microphysical structure plays in the argument; how the view differs from illusionism, neutral monism, and property dualism; and what evidence from neuroscience, psychophysics, or physics would count against it. Please make the follow-ups press the guest to specify mechanisms, criteria, or predictions rather than restate the view.
