I'm writing a chapter on Hobbes's philosophy of geometry for a book on early modern mathematics, and I'm stuck on a question I think only Hobbes himself could answer. In *De Corpore*, Hobbes argued that geometry is a species of arithmetic — that geometric demonstrations are computations on magnitudes, and that what we call "axioms" are either definitions or measuring conventions, not independent truths. Given this, how would Hobbes have reacted to the independence of the parallel postulate from the other Euclidean axioms? And more pressingly: given that he held all reasoning to be computation, what would he have made of Gödel's second incompleteness theorem — that no consistent formal system can prove its own consistency? Would he have treated it as a computational failure, or as a genuine limitation on the computational model of reasoning that he himself proposed?
