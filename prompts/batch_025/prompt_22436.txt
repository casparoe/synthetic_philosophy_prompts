I'm a philosophy professor working in philosophy of religion and the history of philosophy, and I'm preparing to moderate the Q&A for a small colloquium on "Religion and the Novel in the Nineteenth Century." Our guest is a scholar of George Eliot who has defended, in a recent book, the view that Eliot's late novels — Middlemarch and Daniel Deronda in particular — present what she calls a "religion of sympathy": a secularized moral seriousness that functions as a substitute for orthodox faith, in which the individual finds meaning by identifying with a moral order that exceeds the self. I know the view well enough that I don't want the session to devolve into having her restate it; I'd rather press where the literature actually presses. Could you draft eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give?

I'd like at least one question each on the following. First, the 1854 essay "The Nature, Growth, and Influence of Religion upon the Happiness of the Individual": the title commits Eliot to judging religion by its influence on the individual's happiness, but the argument of the essay seems to be that a genuine religion is a recognition of an objective moral order that exceeds the individual — so is the value of religion grounded in a quasi-utilitarian criterion of individual happiness, or in the objective moral order, and how does that tension show up in the novels? Second, Eliot's "theology of the self" — her term for self-regarding religion — and the charge, made by several critics, that the "religion of sympathy" is itself just a more sophisticated version of the "theology of the self," because it still centers the moral self's expansion; does the "religion of sympathy" escape the "theology of the self," or does it just relocate it? Third, her 1860 letter to Cross, in which she describes herself as neither a Christian nor an unbeliever: what is that "neither" — a third category, or a rejection of the binary — and how does her self-description as a thinker who has learned to think independently of received forms of thought relate to the novels' actual representation of religious experience? Fourth, Daniel Deronda: is Deronda's turn toward the Jewish people a religious conversion, a moral conversion, or a historical and ethnic identification, and does Eliot's treatment of Judaism itself fall into the "theology of the self" (centering Deronda's self-discovery) or does it present Judaism as an objective moral order that exceeds the individual? And fifth, Dorothea: is her life — her "Vicosiana," her sense that her own life is woven into the whole web of human relations — a "religion of sympathy," or is it a form of moral ambition and self-realization, and how does Eliot's narrative framing in the final chapter of Middlemarch, with its image of the growth of an imperfect being toward its fit, support or undermine the "religion of sympathy" reading?

For each question, give me a follow-up that anticipates the answer she's most likely to give and presses one step further — I've found that the most productive moments in these sessions come when I can take her expected reply and turn it back on the view. And if any of the questions risks having her simply restate the "religion of sympathy" thesis rather than defend it against a specific objection, please reframe it so that it forces a choice between two live options in the literature.
