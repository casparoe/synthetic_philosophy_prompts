I'm a third year philosophy PhD student and I'm trying to wrap my head around infinite ethics for a reading group, and two of my classmates got into a heated argument over dinner last week that I still can't resolve, so I'd love a second opinion. Here's the setup they agreed on: two possible worlds, each with countably infinitely many people. In world A everyone lives a decent, pleasant life. In world B the population is exactly the same set of people, but every single one of them lives a slightly worse life than their counterpart in A — a bit more suffering, a bit less happiness, nothing dramatic, and no one is added, removed, or swapped with anyone else. Now consider three principles. (Pareto) If everyone in B is worse off than in A, then B is overall worse than A. (Dominance) If you can change the world so that every person in it is better off, then you should make that change — so if B were the actual world, the right thing would be to bring about A. (Shifting) If you take the infinitely many people in B and improve each one of them by the same fixed amount, the result is A, and merely shifting the entire population up by a constant should not change the overall value of the world, so B and A are equally good.

My roommate argued that the three are inconsistent — Pareto plus Shifting entail that A and B are equal, which contradicts Pareto's verdict that B is worse than A, and he said the lesson is that some principle has to go. His friend pushed back and said the whole thing is a category error: Pareto and Shifting only conflict if you assume "overall value" is a single number you can subtract infinities from, but you don't have to assume that. If the value relation is just a partial order, she said, you can coherently hold that B is worse than A, that shifting everyone up preserves value, and that A is not worse than B — three claims that are only inconsistent if the relation is a total order, and she argued that demanding totality in the infinite case is exactly the hidden assumption doing all the work. My roommate replied that this is just a cop-out: a value relation that can't compare every pair of worlds is useless for decision-making, because in the real world we always have to pick, and a partial order that leaves the two worlds incomparable would license doing nothing, which is absurd given that B is pointwise worse. She responded that "having to pick" is a fact about our epistemic and practical situation, not about the value relation, and that the honest answer to "which world should we bring about?" is "A, obviously, since it dominates B" — which, she said, is a practical conclusion that doesn't require B and A to be comparable in overall value at all, any more than "you should take the umbrella if it might rain" requires the two outcomes to have comparable expected utilities.

Can you adjudicate this move by move? I'm specifically interested in (a) whether the three principles really are inconsistent once you drop the assumption that overall value is a single real number, or whether the inconsistency survives in some weaker form; (b) whether my roommate's "uselessness" objection against partial value relations has real force or just begs the question against anyone who thinks the paradox reveals something wrong with the idea of a total ordering; and (c) whether his friend's escape via the dominance principle actually works, or whether it just relocates the paradox one level up — because if the right response to the conflict is "use dominance to decide," then why wouldn't the same move be available for other infinite cases where no option statewise dominates the rest, and what does that say about how much work the dominance principle can actually do? I don't need you to pick a side out of sympathy; I'd just like to know which of the two of them is more right about which specific claim, and what both of them are missing.
