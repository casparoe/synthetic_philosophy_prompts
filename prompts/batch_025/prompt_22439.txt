I'm a member of the planning commission for a mid-sized city, and I owe you a confession up front: I'm the one who talked the commission into piloting a prediction market for our current decision, largely because I've been reading Robin Hanson's work on futarchy and the rationalist blogs for years and I was convinced this would aggregate local knowledge better than a public comment period. The decision in front of us is how to rezone a large, underused industrial parcel on the east side — either as mixed residential/commercial (Option A) or as a dedicated technology park with a ten-year tax abatement for anchor tenants (Option B). The standard comment period had been leaning toward Option A, but the market, which we ran for six weeks on contracts over the five-year projected outcomes of each option (a composite index of projected tax revenue, housing units, jobs, and a traffic-congestion measure), came out in favor of Option B by a margin that looked comfortable on paper. Under the charter I drafted, the commission is supposed to adopt whichever option the market prices higher.

The problem is that when I went through the trade logs after the close, the picture got ugly. Roughly sixty percent of the volume in the Option B "success" contract was concentrated in three accounts that all opened in the same week, and the price of that contract moved in lockstep with the dates of two public lobbying events put on by the prospective anchor tenant. I've also been sitting with a conceptual worry I can't quite shake and that I think the memo has to confront head-on: a prediction market tells you what will happen, but the whole point of the futarchy is to pick the option that will do well, and once traders know the commission will follow the market, the "what will happen" and the "what we should do" start to bleed into each other in ways I don't fully understand — the market's forecast of the tech park succeeding may be partly caused by the market itself signaling to developers that the park is a done deal. I don't know how to weigh that, and I don't know whether my own pre-committed rule to follow the market should bind me in exactly the case where the market looks compromised.

Please draft the written opinion I'll circulate to the full commission before next week's vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action — follow the market and adopt Option B, set the market result aside and revert to the standard deliberative process, or adopt Option B only under specific conditions — with a clear time frame, and it should engage both the manipulation evidence and the prediction-versus-decision problem seriously rather than just asserting that markets are reliable or that they're gameable. I'd rather the memo honestly steelman the case for just following the market — I proposed this thing, after all, and there's a real argument that the concentration I found is exactly the kind of noise a liquid market is supposed to wash out, and that a committee overriding the market on a hunch is the arbitrary, capture-prone process the futarchy was meant to replace — than paper over it.
