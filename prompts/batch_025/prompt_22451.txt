I'm working on a piece for my newsletter about experimental philosophy, and I keep getting stuck on one specific debate that I think most people (myself included) just gloss over. It's the Knobe effect, or the "side-effect effect" — the finding that people are way more likely to say a chairman intentionally harmed the environment when the harm is a side effect of a new program, than to say he intentionally helped it when the help is a side effect. The standard story is that this shows our folk concept of "intention" is normatively loaded, like we're smuggling blame and praise into the word itself. But I've read enough to suspect the effect might be more fragile than the headine suggests — that it depends a lot on how the question is worded, and on the fact that "intentionally" is doing double duty (does it mean "aimed at bringing about" or just "knew it would happen"?).

Could you write me an essay that actually weighs this controversy head-on? I'd like you to consider the case that the Knobe effect is a genuine, robust feature of how people think about intention (and what that would imply for the descriptive vs. normative question about the concept), and the case that it's largely an artifact of experimental design and the ambiguity of "intentionally." Please don't end on a fence-sitting note — reach a verdict and defend it, even if the verdict is a nuanced one. I'm especially curious whether the effect, even if it's real, tells us something about the concept of intention or just about how people use the word in a moralizing register. This is the kind of thing I keep meaning to dig into properly but never have time for, so any help is appreciated.
