I've just come out of a long, somewhat heated argument with a friend over the principle of indifference, and we kept returning to two puzzles that are both habitually lumped together as "Bertrand's paradox" — the random chord (or needle) problem and Bertrand's box — and I'm not sure we resolved whether they are really the same kind of problem. In the chord problem, the difficulty is that there is no unique "random" chord: specifying the chord by two random endpoints on the circumference, by a random point on a random radius, or by a uniformly random midpoint in the disk gives three different probabilities (1/3, 1/2, and 1/4) that the chord exceeds the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and the principle of indifference has no internal way to adjudicate among them without importing extra structure. That strikes me as a genuine, deep defect in the principle — a failure of invariance under reparametrization.

In the box problem, though, I think the situation is different. There the "paradox" is that a naive application of indifference — treating the unseen ball as equally likely to be white or black — yields 1/2, whereas the correct answer, once you condition properly on the fact that a white ball was actually drawn, is 2/3. My working hypothesis, then, is that the two puzzles are different in kind: the chord exposes a real, unresolved defect in the principle of indifference, while the box is merely a misapplication of it — an error in conditioning, or in choosing the wrong reference class — that correct probability theory settles once the setup is made precise. Could you compare the two puzzles and assess whether this distinction holds up? If it does, what does it tell us about when the principle of indifference is genuinely problematic as opposed to merely being misapplied, and are there other familiar "indifference paradoxes" that you would place on one side of this line or the other?
