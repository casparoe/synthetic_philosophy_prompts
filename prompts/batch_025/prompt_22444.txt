I'm a high school junior working on a college application essay about the ethics of war, and I've been reading about just war theory on my own. This is the part of the literature I find hardest to make sense of: whether combatants on both sides of a war are morally equal, or whether the soldiers fighting for the unjust cause forfeit their moral standing. I understand the "fair fight" or "self-defense" argument for the moral equality of combatants — roughly, that an individual soldier is not personally responsible for the political decision to go to war, and that even if the war is unjust, the combatant is fighting to defend their own people, so they retain a right to self-defense against the enemy who threatens them. But I also understand the opposing view, which I've seen attributed to Jeff McMahan, that if a war is genuinely unjust, the combatants fighting to advance that cause are themselves wrongdoers, and that wrongdoers do not have a right to kill their victims in self-defense.

Could you write a dialogue between a defender of the moral equality of combatants (in the spirit of Michael Walzer) and a defender of the moral inequality of combatants (in the spirit of McMahan), in which they try to work out whether the "fair fight" argument survives the "complicity" objection? I'd especially like them to grapple with two specific cases that I think are the real crux: the conscript, who is drafted into the army of an aggressive regime and has no real choice, and the soldier who is morally ignorant of the injustice of their cause, believing in good faith that their side is the just one. And I'd like them to say something about whether the legal equality of combatants under the laws of war — the way the Geneva Conventions treat captured soldiers on both sides the same — is doing any real moral work, or whether it's just a practical concession to the difficulty of determining who is on the just side. Please make both speakers genuinely strong, with no strawmen, and make sure they actually respond to each other's points rather than just taking turns restating their own case.
