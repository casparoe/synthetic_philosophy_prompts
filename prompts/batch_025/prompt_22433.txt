I spent some thirty years as a condensed matter physicist, and now that I have retired I am working my way through the philosophy of physics with a seriousness I never brought to my own field. I find myself stuck on a point about Bell's theorem that I would like you to help me argue out, and I would be grateful for a careful, detailed treatment rather than a survey.

The standard textbook story is that Bell's theorem rules out "local hidden variable theories," and that the relevant locality assumption is the factorization condition on joint probabilities, P(A,B|x,y,λ) = P(A|x,λ)P(B|y,λ), where x and y are the distant measurement settings and λ a complete specification of the beables in the common past. But I have come to think that this bundles together two genuinely distinct conditions — parameter independence and outcome independence, in a distinction I believe goes back to Shimony — and that the moral of Bell's theorem is often misstated as a result. My view is that what quantum mechanics violates is outcome independence, not parameter independence: the marginal statistics at one wing are independent of the distant setting (the no-signaling result, which is exactly the independence that relativistic microcausality demands of observables at spacelike separation), whereas the failure shows up in the joint distribution of the two outcomes, which cannot be exploited to transmit a signal and so does not threaten relativistic locality in the operational sense a relativist actually cares about. On this reading, Bell's theorem should be taken to show that nature fails a stronger condition — local causality, i.e. factorization of the joint probabilities — rather than that it fails relativistic locality proper.

Could you please make the case for this position as rigorously as you can? I would like you to unpack the parameter-independence/outcome-independence distinction, to show concretely how quantum mechanics satisfies the former while violating the latter (perhaps with the singlet state), and then to argue why the failure of the latter does not amount to a violation of relativistic locality. And since I want to be fair to the other side, please also steelman the opposing view — that Bell's own notion of local causality is precisely the right notion of locality, and that its failure is genuinely nonlocal in a way that no-signaling cannot rescue — and say why the position I have sketched is preferable, or at least defensible.
