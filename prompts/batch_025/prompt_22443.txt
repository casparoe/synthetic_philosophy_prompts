I'm taking a graduate seminar on practical reason, and we recently worked through Kavka's toxin puzzle in detail. The setup, for anyone who needs a refresher: an offeror will pay you $1,000 if, by Thursday evening, you intend to drink a vial of toxin on Friday at noon; the intention is verified by a reliable brain scan. You know that on Friday at noon you will have no reason to drink the toxin, so you won't drink it. The question is whether it's rational to take the offer. Two of my classmates, Lena and Marcus, got into a fairly heated argument about it, and I couldn't tell who was making the stronger case.

Lena started by saying that the rational move is simply to take the offer: you form the intention, the scan confirms it, you get the $1,000, and on Friday you just don't drink the toxin, having lost nothing but a brief, harmless intention. Marcus replied that this misunderstands what an intention is—an intention is a commitment to act, not a belief or a wish, and if you know you won't drink the toxin, your "intention" is a sham that the scan would detect as such. Lena conceded that the intention has to be "real" in some sense, but argued that Kavka's point is that it can be real while being "two-level": you intend to drink it now, while also intending that on Friday, when a reason not to drink appears, you'll drop the intention and not drink; the intention's planning function is to stop you deliberating now, not on Friday. Marcus countered that if the intention is real, it should rule out deliberation on Friday too, per Bratman, which means you should drink the toxin—and if you won't drink it, the intention was never real. Lena answered that intentions are routinely updated as new reasons appear, so the fact that you drop it on Friday doesn't mean you didn't genuinely have it on Thursday. Marcus then shifted the ground, arguing that the whole puzzle depends on an under-specified detail: if the scan detects only the occurrent intention, you can just occurrently intend it briefly and let it lapse, making the puzzle trivial; but if the scan requires a stable disposition to act, you can't fake it, making the puzzle impossible—and either way, it's not the puzzle Kavka thinks it is.

They asked me to referee, and I honestly couldn't. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step—not just who wins overall?
