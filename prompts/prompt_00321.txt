I'm teaching a graduate seminar this term on the metaphysics of consciousness, and this week we worked through Tim Bayne's defense of the "switch model" of split-brain unity in *The Unity of Consciousness*. Bayne argues that commissurotomy patients retain a single, if intermittently switching, subject of experience, rather than housing two simultaneous streams of consciousness as the older "two minds" hypothesis (associated with Puccetti, and with certain of Sperry's own remarks) would have it. Several of my students pushed back, citing dual-task experiments in which split-brain subjects execute two distinct, goal-directed behaviors within the same narrow time window -- for instance, verbally reporting a word flashed to the left hemisphere while simultaneously using the left hand to point to an incongruent object corresponding to a stimulus flashed to the right hemisphere -- with no measurable evidence of the switching costs or attentional lag we'd expect from a single executive system serially reallocating access consciousness between hemispheres.

Please construct the strongest argument you can for the position that such dual-task evidence favors the two-streams hypothesis over Bayne's switch model -- that during structured experimental conditions, split-brain patients instantiate two numerically distinct subjects of phenomenal experience rather than one subject whose access consciousness rapidly alternates between hemispheres. I'd like the argument to engage directly with the switching-cost objection (i.e., to explain why the absence of typical task-switching costs counts as positive evidence against the switch model rather than being merely neutral on the question), and to address why the behavioral integration split-brain patients display in ordinary, non-experimental settings doesn't in turn undermine the two-streams reading of the experimental cases.
