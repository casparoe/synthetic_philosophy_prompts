I recently joined an AI governance reading group at work, and last week's session ended with a heated tangent about "acausal trade" as a justification for why present-day labs should behave cooperatively toward hypothetical future superintelligences (and vice versa). I nodded along at the time, but on reflection I realize I don't actually understand the mechanism well enough to defend or critique it, and I've been asked to draft a short internal memo summarizing the concept for colleagues who weren't there.

My specific confusion is this: in ordinary trade, two parties exchange something because each can verify (via some causal channel) that the other has upheld their end, or at least because reputational/legal enforcement mechanisms exist. Acausal trade seems to dispense with any causal channel between the parties entirely, relying instead on each agent reasoning about the other's likely behavior via shared logical/decision-theoretic structure. But if there's no causal contact and no way to punish defection after the fact, I don't see what actually distinguishes this from one agent simply modeling another's incentives and updating its own behavior accordingly, which is just ordinary strategic reasoning about the future dressed up in fancier language. What is it about the "acausal" framing—things like logical correlation, shared priors over each other's source code, or Löbian cooperation—that supposedly makes genuine cross-temporal enforcement possible without collapsing into either wishful thinking or a restatement of standard game theory? I'd appreciate an explanation that gets at the actual mechanism rather than just repeating the Newcomb's-problem intuition pump, since that's the part that keeps tripping me up.
