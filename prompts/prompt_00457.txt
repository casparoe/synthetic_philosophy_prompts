I'm a novelist currently drafting a speculative fiction manuscript, tentatively titled "The Plurality," in which the central conceit is that David Lewis's modal realism is literally true: every possible world exists concretely, spatiotemporally isolated from our own, and my protagonist gains the ability to perceive (though not directly interact with) her counterparts across this plurality of worlds. Before I go further into the outline, I want the underlying metaphysics to be intellectually honest rather than just a loose gesture at "parallel universes" à la comic-book multiverses, so I've been rereading Lewis's "On the Plurality of Worlds" and trying to think through what actually follows from taking his view seriously, as opposed to the more familiar ersatzist or Everettian versions of "many worlds" that show up in most genre fiction.

Could you lay out the most important implications of modal realism specifically for a story built around this premise? I'm especially interested in what it would mean for personal identity across worlds (is a counterpart really "her," or just someone qualitatively similar in a different world), for the moral weight of choices my protagonist makes once she knows that every alternative she didn't choose is nonetheless being lived out somewhere with equal ontological standing, and for how modal realism's rejection of any privileged "actual" world might undercut the dramatic stakes of a plot premised on her home world mattering more than the others. I'd also welcome any implications you think are important that a novelist might otherwise overlook—things that matter for getting the philosophy right even if they never make it onto the page explicitly.
