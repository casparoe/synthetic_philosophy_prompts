I'm a PhD student in philosophy of religion, and I'm moderating the Q&A that follows a department colloquium talk. The speaker defends a particular response to the argument from divine hiddenness — the argument that runs roughly: a loving God would want to be in a personal relationship with finite creatures; a relationship of that kind requires that the lover not be hidden from the beloved, i.e. that God be open to being known; but God is in fact hidden; so a loving God does not exist. Her response is that hiddenness is not a defect but an epistemic good. She argues that God's hiddenness is what makes genuine moral autonomy and uncoerced faith possible: a fully revealed God would produce creatures who obey on the strength of evidence rather than from free moral commitment, so revelation would actually undermine the very relationship a loving God wants.

I'm preparing a sequence of six to eight questions that press on the content of this position rather than inviting her to restate it. I want at least one question each on three points: first, whether her view requires total evidential hiddenness or allows for some degree of divine evidence — from natural theology or religious experience — without collapsing into the kind of full revelation she thinks is corrosive; second, the moral status of the creatures a revealed God would produce, i.e. whether, on her own account, God is in any sense responsible for their moral heteronomy; and third, whether her account quietly inverts the value of evidence, treating weak evidence as a mark of authenticity and strong evidence as a vice. For each of these three, please also give me a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so I can press further if she deflects or gives the standard reply. And I'd like the sequence to close with a single question about what kind of evidence — empirical or otherwise — could count against her central claim that hiddenness is what produces moral autonomy.
