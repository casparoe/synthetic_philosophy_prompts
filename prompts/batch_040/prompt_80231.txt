I just spent the better part of an hour arguing with a friend about whether objects endure or perdure, and we went in circles in a way that convinced me the stalemate is structural rather than a failure of either of us to listen. It's a debate that's been running through the metaphysics literature for decades, so I don't expect our particular fight to settle it, but I'd like a diagnosis of why it keeps stalling. Let me reconstruct where we got stuck, because I think the shape of the impasse is the point. He's a perdurantist, and his lead argument is what I'll call the problem of temporary intrinsics: if a single object is wholly present at two times, a paperclip straight at noon and bent at two, then it seems to possess incompatible intrinsic properties at once, which is impossible for one intrinsic state. My reply was that this only bites if intrinsicality is absolute; once I make the properties time-relative — the clip is intrinsically straight-at-noon and intrinsically bent-at-two — there's no incompatibility, so the objection dissolves. He countered that I've just quietly indexed every property to a time, and so my "enduring object" is really nothing but a stack of timeslices, i.e., a four-dimensional worm: I've become a perdurantist in disguise. I then turned the tables with what I'll call the tie-breaking problem: if objects are sums of their temporal parts, then the statue and the lump of clay, which co-exist over the same interval and share their matter part by part, must be identical by the principle that two sums with exactly the same parts are the same sum, yet they're plainly distinct objects. He brushed this off by saying I'm presupposing a too-rigid mereology, and that a more liberal mereology, in which overlap need not entail identity and in which parts are individuated by their full property sets rather than their matter alone, lets him keep the statue and the clay apart without surrendering temporal parts.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis to be stress-tested rather than a conclusion to be endorsed, is that this is not a disagreement about the world — not about what actually persists — but a disagreement about two auxiliary frameworks that each side is quietly building in: the logic of parthood on the perdurantist side, and the nature of intrinsicality on the endurantist side. The temporary-intrinsics objection is only an objection if intrinsicality is absolute, and the tie-breaking objection is only an objection if parthood obeys the strong same-parts-same-sum principle; so each side's flagship case against the other is really a case against a contestable auxiliary assumption, and each side's defense of its own view either imports the opponent's framework or forces a revision of those auxiliaries. Is that the right diagnosis? If it is, then is the endurantism-versus-perdurantism question partly verbal — the words "object" and "part" and "intrinsic" doing different work on each side — or is there a residual substantive disagreement that survives once you hold the mereology and the intrinsicality fixed? I want the diagnosis grounded in what each side actually says and assumes, not a survey of the literature. And given your diagnosis, what, if anything, would count as genuine progress here: a formal equivalence result showing the two views come out the same once the auxiliaries are specified, a new argument that one side's response is incoherent even on its own auxiliaries, a sharpening that shows the two camps are answering different questions (one about identity through time, one about the possibility of change) under a single label, or an honest conclusion that no progress is possible because the choice of auxiliaries is itself underdetermined by the phenomena?
