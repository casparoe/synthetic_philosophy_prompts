I am a retired toxicologist — thirty-one years in industrial hygiene and regulatory laboratory work — and I have only in the past two years begun to read philosophy, mostly epistemology. I now volunteer as a science reviewer for a small public-radio program that takes in listener letters, and roughly two to four of those letters a month are claims that some institution deliberately concealed a harm: a chemical company buried an internal study, a state agency fudged an exposure dataset, a scientific consensus was manufactured. I need a decision procedure that my co-reviewer, a working journalist with no training in philosophy, and I can actually run on one of these claims in about thirty minutes, before our editor signs off on a short weekly column. The stakes are modest — a few minutes of airtime and the credibility of the column — but they are not trivial, because listeners sometimes act on what we print, and I keep swinging between two failures I want the procedure to prevent: reflexively dismissing a claim as conspiratorial without having earned the dismissal, and taking a claim too seriously because it makes intuitive sense to me, given what I know about how institutions actually behave. The procedure must be one my co-reviewer can follow without me translating each step into plain language first.

Please lay it out as an ordered sequence of checks. For each check, say what a sufficient answer looks like, when the check fails the claim outright, and where the procedure must stop and defer to human judgment because what remains is a matter of discretion rather than of evidence. In particular, I want it to do the following.

First, sort the claim before evaluating it, because the three kinds of claim I receive require different standards of evidence: (a) a specific concealment claim, naming documents, people, or dates; (b) a systemic-incentive claim, alleging no coordinating group but asserting that the structure of funding, publication, or career incentives produces suppression; and (c) a global claim that a whole field or institution is compromised, with no specific documents. The procedure must state, with reasons, what standard of evidence applies to each type, and why a type (c) claim is almost always parked for lack of falsifiability rather than simply asserted to be false.

Second, for a specific concealment claim, I want a secrecy-capacity check: how many people would have had to know, for how long, and do people in this domain actually keep secrets of this kind — calibrated against kept secrets I can name from my own field, not against the folk intuition that "they can't keep it secret," which I know is wrong in principle but which I still find myself reaching for. I also want a failure-explanation check: whether the observed gaps, delays, and contradictions can be explained by institutional failure — negligence, self-deception, diffusion of responsibility — and whether that explanation is more parsimonious, with the requirement that the concealment explanation beat it on specific grounds rather than on mere availability. Then a records check: whether the claim generates concrete, checkable predictions (a document that should exist, a records request that should show something, a person who should be reachable), with any claim that generates no checkable prediction sent to a parking lot and told why. Finally, a silence check: the absence of a whistleblower should count as evidence only when the base rate of whistleblowing in the domain is high; where whistleblowers are rare, silence is weak evidence in either direction, and the procedure should say so plainly.

Third, for a systemic-incentive claim, the procedure should evaluate the documented incentive structure without requiring evidence of a coordinating group, and should be able to land on "this is real, but it is an incentive failure rather than a conspiracy, and here is what that means for how we talk about it" — because a naive rule that conspiracies are almost always wrong would wrongly dismiss documented systemic biases, and I do not want that failure mode built in.

Fourth, mark explicitly the points where judgment cannot be eliminated — assessing a single source's credibility, weighing motive, deciding when "unlikely" is unlikely enough — rather than pretending those steps are mechanical. And say honestly where the procedure will misfire: where it is too conservative, where it is too permissive, and what the asymmetry between the cost of a false alarm and the cost of a missed concealment does to the tilt of the whole thing.

The procedure should end with a written verdict in one of a small number of categories — something like "supported, investigate further," "plausible, needs specific evidence X," "likely false, here is why," or "parked, unfalsifiable as stated" — with a one-paragraph rationale the editor can sign off on.

Then run the procedure on the following three cases, which I have invented for illustration but which are modeled on claims I actually receive. For each, show the checks as they are run, the verdict, and the rationale.

Case 1, a specific concealment claim: A listener writes that a mid-sized chemical company buried an internal 1994 study showing that a common household chemical caused liver damage in rats, and that a state agency was aware by 1996 and delayed the warning label. The claimant cites a former employee who says "they all knew," but offers no documents, and the agency's published records show a routine, if slow, review.

Case 2, a global claim: A listener writes that the entire field of toxicology is compromised because every laboratory is funded by the industries it tests, and that no honest researcher will speak up because of careers.

Case 3, a systemic-incentive claim: A listener writes that negative results from safety testing are systematically suppressed because of how publication and funding work, and cites the general, well-known fact that negative results are under-published.

One more thing. I expect the procedure to land somewhere I do not like on Case 1, because I know from thirty years in the field that companies do bury studies, and I suspect the company in question did too. If the procedure lands against my suspicion, do not soften it: say plainly what the procedure is doing that my domain intuition is not, and specify what evidence would have to appear to move the verdict.

Please write your answer in American English.
