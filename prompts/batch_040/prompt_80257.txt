I'm working through the Lucas–Penrose argument that human mathematical understanding outruns any Turing machine, and I keep getting stuck on a point that I think most expositions quietly slide past. As I understand it, the incompleteness theorem is a *relative* result: it says that any consistent, recursively axiomatizable theory strong enough to encode arithmetic has a sentence that the theory can neither prove nor refute. That is a claim about a *fixed* theory, a fixed list of axioms. But the argument as it is usually pitched — that you can see the Gödel sentence is true and so you are not a machine — seems to assume, without flagging it, that the human reasoner's mathematical knowledge is itself a single fixed, recursively axiomatizable body of truths.

What I would like is for you to lay out, with care, the gap between the conclusion the theorem actually licenses and the stronger conclusion that is normally drawn from it. The weak conclusion is roughly that no *single fixed* formal system captures everything this reasoner can judge to be true; the strong conclusion is that the mind is *in principle* non-computational. I want you to say exactly what work the fixed-theory premise is doing, why it is not a harmless stipulation, and what extra assumptions — and how controversial they are — would be needed to bridge from the weak to the strong conclusion.

I would also like you to take up the consistency-versus-soundness point separately: the incompleteness theorem needs only that the background theory be consistent, but the move from there to the claim that the human can see the Gödel sentence is true seems to require something closer to soundness, namely that the human's judgment about the theory's own consistency is reliable. Say whether that is a genuine extra burden on the argument or whether it can be defused, and if so how. To be clear about what I do not want: no history of the debate, no catalogue of objections, no citations or reading list. I want the internal logic of the argument itself, so that I can see precisely where its force lies and at which step it depends on a premise I could reasonably reject.
