I want to work through what a particular design of universal basic income quietly presupposes, because I think one of its defining features is in tension with the way the policy is usually justified, and I can't quite pin down the philosophical commitment doing the work.

The design I mean is the canonical one, and one of its defining features is that the payment is made to the individual rather than the household: a fixed cash amount to every adult resident, unconditionally — no work requirement, no means test — and not indexed to household size or to the recipient's particular circumstances. The same per-adult amount goes to a single parent of four as to a childless adult. The payment is cash rather than in-kind, and it is funded out of general, typically progressive, taxation.

Here is the puzzle I keep coming back to. Because the payment is per adult and flat, a household's total basic income scales with the number of adults in it, not with the number of people who have needs. So the households with the most children and the fewest adults end up with the lowest basic income per person. Yet the policy is almost always justified as meeting basic needs and securing material security for everyone. I can't see how a payment that gives the least, per person, to the households with the most dependents is doing the needs-meeting work its defenders attribute to it.

So please work through the following, in order.

First, reconstruct the implicit theory of the moral and political unit that the per-adult, non-indexed design presupposes. What view of the individual, of the family, and of children's needs does it only make sense under? And which rival views — a needs-based account of entitlement, or one that treats the household rather than the adult as the relevant unit — does it quietly rule out?

Second, is the gap between the needs-based justification and the non-needs-based design a genuine inconsistency, or is the practice philosophically underdetermined here, in the sense that several theories of what the payment is for would license exactly this design? I want you to be careful to separate what the design strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with.

Third, the payment is cash, not in-kind. What view of welfare, of autonomy, or of the recipient's knowledge of their own needs does that presuppose, and what would it exclude?

And finally: suppose we replaced the presupposed view with a needs-based theory of entitlement, on which a person's share scales with their needs, including the needs of the children in their care. What specifically would have to change in the design, and would the result still be recognizably a basic income, or would it have to become something else?
