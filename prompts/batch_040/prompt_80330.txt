I'm teaching a small upper-level seminar on speech acts and epistemology this spring, and the unit on norms of assertion keeps landing on the same fork I can't seem to settle for myself: whether the norm that governs when you're permitted to say that p is a knowledge norm (you may assert p only if you know p) or a truth norm (you may assert p only if p is true, or at least you must have some warrant that p is true). I want to close the unit with a written dialogue between a committed defender of the knowledge norm and a committed defender of the truth norm, and I'd like it to actually trade blows rather than each one reciting their manifesto. The truth-norm person should press the necessary-truths objection — that the knowledge norm seems to forbid asserting mathematical or logical truths, since it's hard to see how you could *know* them in the relevant sense, yet we plainly do assert them all the time — and the knowledge-norm person should have a real answer ready rather than just conceding the point and retreating. I also want at least one moment where the truth-norm person concedes something to the other side, because I think the truth norm has a genuine problem with lucky or accidental assertions that the knowledge norm handles better, and I don't want that side to be a strawman either. Both should be sharp and good-faith, responding to what the other actually just said.

Write it as a dialogue of maybe a page or two, with the two speakers alternating and genuinely building on each other's moves. Since this is a creative piece I know there's no verdict to hand down, so instead of a conclusion I'd like a short note at the end flagging where the dialogue leans on a contested reading of either position or quietly takes a liberty with the technical details — I'd rather you tell me where you cut corners than present a smooth fake resolution. And if you think the knowledge-vs-truth framing as I've set it up is confused or that the two views aren't really in opposition the way I'm assuming, say so plainly and tell me what I'm getting wrong instead of just writing the dialogue anyway; I'd genuinely rather learn that than get a polished piece that papers over a bad premise.
