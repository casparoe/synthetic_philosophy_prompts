A popular book on the science of morality that I recently read devotes a chapter to the "evolutionary debunking" argument in metaethics and presents it in a single paragraph like this:

"Natural selection shaped our moral intuitions to help our ancestors survive and reproduce, not to track any objective moral truth. Since the content of our moral beliefs is best explained by these ancient fitness pressures rather than by any contact with moral reality, we have no reason to think those beliefs are true. This is the evolutionary debunking argument, and it is a serious challenge to moral realism — the view that there are objective moral facts. The book's conclusion is that the argument actually establishes that realism is false, and that the question is now settled against it."

I'm not a metaethicist, and I have a feeling the argument is more modest than this paragraph makes it out to be, but I can't say precisely where it overreaches. Could you audit this characterization for me? I'd like you to separate (a) what a defender of the evolutionary debunking argument would actually accept, (b) what is a subtle distortion or overstatement of the argument, and (c) what a careful defender of moral realism would object to. In particular, I'd like to know whether the move from "evolution shaped our moral beliefs, so we have no reason to trust them" to "therefore moral realism is false" is a step the argument itself takes, and whether the claim that the debate is "settled" follows from anything true in the summary. Please assess this particular characterization on its own merits rather than giving me a bibliography or a list of further reading.
