I am a novelist, and I am planning a novel built around a single metaphysical premise that I want to treat as load-bearing rather than as set dressing: the protagonist's conscious experiences are epiphenomenal. On the internal picture of the book, whatever the protagonist feels — the taste of the coffee, the weight of the grief, the hot flush of anger — plays no causal role in anything the protagonist does. Their actions, their words, and their decisions are produced entirely by sub-personal, non-experiential processes, and the experiences simply ride along as a by-product. I intend to narrate in a tight, close first person, in the present tense, so that the reader lives inside the protagonist's stream of self-report.

Before I commit to a technique, I want to understand a puzzle I keep running into — sometimes called the paradox of phenomenal judgment — and I want it stated precisely: if an experience causes nothing, then it cannot be the cause of the judgment that it is occurring. So the narrator's sentence "I am in grief" cannot be produced by the grief it is about; it must be produced by some non-experiential process that merely happens to be about the grief. I'd like you to work out, as carefully as you can, the most important implications of this situation for the craft of my narration. In particular, I want you to say: what the paradox implies about what the first-person narrator can and cannot be trusted to report about their own experience; whether it yields a systematic way in which the narration is unreliable that is genuinely different from the ordinary unreliable narrator, and if so, in what respect the unreliability is a metaphysical fact rather than a failure of the narrator's epistemic virtue; how the three items — the experience, the narrator's self-report, and the narrator's observable behavior — come to be related, or fail to be related, once neither of the latter two is caused by the former; and what all of this does to the reader's ability to use the narration to learn anything about the protagonist. And please mark, wherever you can, the points at which epiphenomenalism underdetermines the answer, so that I do not mistake a choice the theory leaves open to me for a consequence it forces.
