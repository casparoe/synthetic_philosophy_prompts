I'm developing a proposal for a one-semester term paper (roughly 6,000 words) in the epistemology of aesthetics, and I'd like you to help me turn a half-formed idea into a precise, well-scoped plan. The idea, in rough form: the standard debate over whether aesthetic judgments can be known on the basis of testimony treats aesthetic properties as either mind-independent facts (which testimony can straightforwardly report) or as purely subjective reports of the speaker's own experience (which testimony can convey only as a report about the speaker, not as a fact about the work). But if aesthetic properties are response-dependent — that is, partly constituted by the response of a competent observer under standard conditions — then the structure of aesthetic testimony is peculiar in a way the standard debate overlooks. When a critic testifies to a reader that a novel is "profound," the testimony is about a fact that is partly constituted by the reader's own response. The reader cannot verify the testimony by independent observation, because the relevant fact is the reader's own response, which the reader can only access by having the response — which is precisely what the testimony is supposed to help produce or confirm. I want to explore whether this structural feature makes aesthetic testimony epistemically different from testimony about mind-independent facts, and what consequences this has for the question of whether we can come to know aesthetic judgments on the basis of testimony.

Please turn this into a proposal that includes: (1) a precise statement of the question, phrased so that it has a determinate answer; (2) an explanation of why the existing positions — aesthetic realism, aesthetic anti-realism or subjectivism, and the standard "testimony as a source of knowledge" framework — leave the question open; (3) the approach I would take and a defense of why that approach rather than the obvious alternatives (e.g., simply applying the standard testimony framework, or arguing from the phenomenology of aesthetic experience); (4) a description of what a successful result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and (5) a realistic assessment of what could make the project too big for one semester.
