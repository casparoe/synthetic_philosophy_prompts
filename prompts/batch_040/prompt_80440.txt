I'm preparing a conceptual essay on what it means to hold a nation responsible for a war, and I want to use the outbreak of the First World War as my test case — specifically the July Crisis of 1914 and the later "Fischer controversy" over whether Imperial Germany bore a special responsibility for the war. I don't want a summary of that debate; I know the broad strokes (Fritz Fischer's 1961 book argued that Germany's leadership deliberately pushed toward a war to secure great-power status, and the postwar German historians who pushed back insisted the war was an unintended catastrophe produced by the alliance system and by a chain of mistakes). What I want is an explanation of the deeper conceptual problem the debate keeps circling without resolving.

Here is the problem as I see it. Ordinary moral and legal responsibility rests on three pillars: an agent who acts, a mental state (usually intent, or at least foresight), and voluntariness (the agent could have done otherwise). Each pillar is strained when the putative agent is a nation, and I'd like you to work through all three.

First, the agency problem. A state is not a single mind. The July Crisis was driven by many individuals — the emperor, the chief of the general staff, the chancellor, the diplomats in Berlin and Vienna — who had different information, different intentions, and different degrees of foresight. So "did Germany intend the war?" has no single answer; it depends on which individuals we count as the state and on how we aggregate their mental states. Please lay out the main options for answering that question (attributing the state's intent to its decision-making core, to its institutional incentives, or to no one in particular) and the cost of each.

Second, the mental-state problem. Even if no single leader directly wanted a general European war, the German leadership may still have acted with what German criminal law calls "conditional intent" (bedingter Vorsatz): they did not directly will the result, but they foresaw it as a serious, realistic possibility and resolved to act anyway, accepting the risk. The open-ended assurance to Austria-Hungary and the reliance on a mobilization plan that made a limited Balkan war structurally impossible look like exactly that kind of conduct. Please explain whether conditional intent — foresight plus acceptance of the risk, short of direct purpose — is enough to ground a strong, "special" responsibility for the war, or whether only direct intent does, and how the verdict would differ in each case.

Third, the voluntariness problem, which I find the most interesting knot. The same institutional structure that makes German responsibility look real — the alliance commitments and the mobilization timetable that removed the option of a limited war — is also what makes the leaders' choice look less voluntary, because those structures constrained what they could do. A state can thus be said to be responsible for a war that its own institutions made nearly unavoidable, even though responsibility ordinarily requires that the agent could have done otherwise. Please explain how, if at all, that tension can be resolved: whether designing an institution can ground responsibility even when operating under it reduces voluntariness, whether there is a meaningful distinction between the earlier voluntary choice to build the structure and the later constrained choice to act under it, and what that distinction would do to the verdict.

Finally, please keep a distinction straight that public memory of this war tends to blur: the difference between "war guilt" (moral blame, which seems to require a wrongful intent) and "war responsibility" (accountability, which can be grounded in causation, foresight, or institutional design even without a direct intent to cause the war). Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles is often remembered as a "war-guilt clause," but its text assigns "responsibility" for the damages rather than pronouncing a moral verdict. Explain why that distinction matters for my question, and whether "special responsibility" can be a coherent middle category — neither full guilt nor merely a share of causal contribution.

Please write this as a clear, well-organized conceptual explanation rather than a historiographical survey, and skip any bibliography or reading list; I'll handle the sources myself.
