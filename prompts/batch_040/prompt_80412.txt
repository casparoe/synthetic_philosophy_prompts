I'm putting together a decision tree for a philosophy seminar on the ineffective objection — the idea that your individual action (buying a steak, taking a flight, skipping an election) makes no real difference to the collective outcome, so you've got no moral reason to change it. I want the tree to sort students by what they actually believe, not by which label they've heard, so every branch node has to be a question they can answer without already knowing which position it leads to.

Here's the thing I want you to build in, because I think most treatments conflate two different moves: the objection can be read causally (your action makes literally no difference — remove it and the outcome is identical) or read as a magnitude claim (it makes some difference, but a drop in the bucket too small to count). Those come apart, and I want a branch that seperates people on which one they're actually endorsing, since you can grant you make a difference and still think it's too small to matter, or deny the difference and not care about magnitude.

Then I want a second axis: whether moral responsibility needs your action to be sufficient for the harm or whether contribution alone is enough. And a third: whether the duty attaches to the act or to the role you occupy (voter, consumer, citizen).

Use voting as a control case somewhere in the tree, because voting is designed to be non-causal (one vote almost never decides) and yet most people still think there's a duty to vote — so it should expose anyone whose position really does require causal efficacy. And use the meat case as the demand-side diagnostic: one person's purchase doesn't change what the factory produces, but the industry tracks aggregate demand, so it's a case where your action is a constituent of the total rather than a cause of any particular output.

Leaves should carry a name for the position (or 'unnamed' if nobody really defends it) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. I want roughly six to eight leaves. Flag any pair of leaves the tree can't actually separate, and note any combination of answers that no real position occupies, with a one-line diagnosis of why (incoherent, just unattractive, or unexplored). One more thing: at the leaf for the 'act as if you're the marginal person' / expected-value reply, add a note that this may be smuggling a decision-theoretic question into what started as a deontic one, and say whether removing that move collapses the case.

Keep it to about a page, no citations, no reading list.
