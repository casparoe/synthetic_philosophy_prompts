For my upper-level seminar on the logic of time and tense — 14 students, one 75-minute session, students know propositional logic but nothing more formal — design an in-class activity that makes them feel the force of the problem of future contingents (the "sea-battle" problem) rather than just hear it stated. The point I want them to run into themselves, before it is named, is this tension: to make a determinate claim about a contingent future event, or to stake a bet on one, you seem to have to treat one of the two alternatives — that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, or that there will not — as already true now; but if it is already true now, then the future is already settled, and everything we do in the meantime is mere revelation, not decision. I want the activity to make that second half, the "mere revelation" sting, something the students actually experience, not something I assert in a debrief.

Build the activity around this constraint, which is the part I do not want you to replace with a plain coin flip or die: the "sea battle" must be a self-referential future contingent. The event is not settled by any external chance device. There will be a sea battle tomorrow if and only if a strict majority of the 14 students, at the moment they commit their bets, bet that a sea battle will happen. So the truth of the future contingent is fixed by the students' own present betting. You are free to design the exact mechanics that make this clean to run — a fixed quorum, a tie-breaking rule, how the "oracle" who enforces the rule is staged, how the outcome is revealed — but you may not drop the self-referential structure: the outcome must be a function of the group's own present commitment, not of a coin, a die, or any outside chance.

The deliverable should be a runnable activity, not a description of one. Give me, as a facilitator's run-sheet:

- The rules, stated precisely enough that a teaching assistant could run them cold, including how the self-referential rule is enforced, how the betting is made simultaneous and secret, and how the outcome is revealed and settled.
- A payoff scheme using small stakes (tokens, points, or equivalent) that does not require real money, and a sentence on why the stakes matter to the point — why the activity would fail if the bets were costless.
- Any roles (bettors, the "oracle," a designated skeptic, a timekeeper) and the exact sequence of phases with a minute-by-minute timeline that fits the 75 minutes.
- A debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto specific parts of the argument: which moment corresponds to the bivalence / excluded-middle premise, which moment is where the "already settled" conclusion first bites, and where the students' own agency shows up — as the mechanism that fixes the outcome, or as the illusion that it could have.
- A frank note on what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run (collusion, refusal to commit to a binary answer, the "I think you think I think" coordination loop, or the group simply not feeling the tension) and how to handle each.
- A frank note on which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply myself. The self-referential case is a special case in which the future is fixed by the present; be honest about how much of the general sea-battle argument — the one about genuinely open future contingents, and the contrast with settled cases such as an eclipse, a future event already determined by the regularity of nature rather than by anyone's present decision or belief — the activity can and cannot carry, and exactly what I have to add in the debrief to bridge from this special case to the general one.

Do not give me a reading list, a bibliography, or citations. Write it so I can hand it to a TA and run it.
