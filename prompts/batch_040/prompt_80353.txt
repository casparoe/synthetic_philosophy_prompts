I've been working through a modal epistemology problem for a paper and I built an argument that I now think proves too much, and I cant find the exact step where it goes wrong.

The background: I want to use conceivability as a guide to possibility. The principle I'm relying on is the strong one: if it's conceivable that P, then it's possible that P. I know this is a strong principle and that it's doing a lot of work in arguments about zombies and absent qualia, which is exactly why I want to keep it if I can.

Here's the argument:
(1) If it's conceivable that P, then it's possible that P.
(2) It's conceivable that I exist but am not me. (Picture a world that is physically identical to ours, but where the individual who is my exact duplicate — same body, same memories, same psychology, occupying my exact role — is not me. That's a coherent scenario I can form.)
(3) So it's possible that I exist but am not me.
(4) But it's necessary that I am me. (Self-identity is a necessary truth: if I am me in one world, then I am me in every world in which I exist.)
(5) So (3) and (4) contradict.
(6) Therefore (1) is false — conceivability does not imply possibility.

The problem is that I don't want to give up (1), and I'm pretty confident (4) is rock solid. So the suspect step has to be (2), but I can't name why the duplicate scenario isn't a legitimate instance of conceivability. It strikes me as a perfectly coherent thing to imagine, and I keep feeling like I'm missing somthing obvious.

Can you tell me which step to reject, why (2) looked fine in the first place, and what I'm paying to reject it? And if the real fix is to restrict (1) rather than to reject (2), walk me through what the restricted principle would look like and whether it still supports the zombie argument. Please answer in American English.
