EXAM QUESTION (25 points):
"Two accounts of the morality of voting are often contrasted. The consequentialist (or prudential) account holds that a vote is worthwhile only insofar as it helps bring about better outcomes. The expressive (or duty) account holds that voting has moral value even when it cannot affect the outcome, because it is a way of expressing one's political convictions and of participating in the civic community. Evaluate both accounts. In your answer, address (i) whether the consequence of a single vote really is negligible, (ii) whether the duty to vote survives in a multi-party system where voters often choose the 'lesser evil,' and (iii) how your answer would change if the jurisdiction used a public ballot in which each voter's choice is publicly recorded and published."

STUDENT ANSWER:
Voting ethics is usually split into two camps, and I think the split is basically right.

On the consequentialist side, the argument is straightforward. A single vote almost never decides an election, so the expected effect of your ballot on the outcome is close to zero. If the probability that your vote is decisive is effectively zero, then the expected value of voting is zero, and there is no consequentialist reason to bother. So the prudential case for voting collapses, and this is why many people treat voting as optional from an outcome-focused point of view.

On the expressive side, the argument is that voting is a way of expressing your political views, and that this expression has value in its own right, independent of whether it changes anything. The duty to vote, on this view, comes from the fact that we all benefit from a functioning democracy, so we should each contribute to sustaining it. Not voting is a kind of free-riding on the efforts of those who do show up. So even if your vote cannot affect the result, you still have a duty to vote, because you are part of the community that the democratic process exists to serve.

When it comes to the lesser evil, I think the expressive account handles it better. In a multi-party system, you can usually just vote for the candidate you most prefer, so the "lesser evil" problem does not really arise. And even when you have to pick the lesser of two bad options, you are still expressing a genuine preference — you are expressing that one option is less objectionable to you than the other. So voting for the lesser evil is just a special case of expressive voting, and it does not undermine the duty.

Finally, on the public ballot: I think it actually strengthens the duty to vote. If your choice is public, then voting is an even stronger form of expression, because you are publicly declaring your view rather than hiding it. So the expressive value goes up, and the duty gets stronger, not weaker. The only cost is that you might feel pressured by others, but that is a minor practical worry and does not change the moral point.

Overall, I think the expressive account is more persuasive, because it gives you a reason to vote even when the consequentialist case fails, and it handles both the lesser-evil and the publicity cases without breaking down.
