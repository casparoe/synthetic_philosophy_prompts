I'm in a seminar on political philosophy and we're working through what exactly counts as corruption, and I keep getting stuck on a detail in anti-bribery law that I think is doing way more philosophical work then people realize. In the US, the main law against companies bribing foreign officials (the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act) has a carve-out I didn't expect: a payment to a foreign official is not a bribe if it just speeds up or expedites a routine governmental action the official was going to perform anyway — getting a permit processed, a utility connection, an inspection scheduled, cargo released through customs. But the same law treats a payment to influence a discretionary decision, say to win or keep a contract, as full-blown bribery. The UK's bribery law, by contrast, has no such carve-out at all, and a lot of big companies' internal codes ban gifts to public officials outright, even tiny ones. And meanwhile, in all of these same countries, election law criminalizes vote-buying — handing out cash or goods to voters to secure their votes — even when the goods are small and you're "buying" a single vote rather than a contract.

What I want is a reconstruction of the implicit theory of corruption that this pattern presupposes. My guess is something like this: an office has two layers, a ministerial layer of acts the official must perform no matter what, and a discretionary core, and corruption is specifically the purchase of the discretionary core, not the purchase of any official act, so lubricating the ministerial layer is fine while buying the core is a betrayal of the trust the office is supposed to embody. But then the vote-buying contrast bugs me: a vote is presumably as discretionary as a contract decision, so why is buying a vote categorically worse than expediting a permit? Does the practice really need a second principle here — something about equality of citizens or the purity of the political sphere — or can the two-layer view stretch to cover it? And is the routine/discretionary line even stable, or does the carve-out quietly depend on officials' "routine" acts being genuinely non-discretionary, which is often false (a permit officer who can sit on your file for a month has real de facto discretion)?

And finally the universality question, since my seminar keeps treating "Western standards" as a block: if the US and the UK disagree about whether the carve-out is right, what does that show about what's actually committed in the carve-out versus what's just contingent? And in a society where bringing a modest gift to a bureaucrat is a social courtesy with no expectation of a specific act, how does the facilitation-payments framework classify it, and what does that classification assume about the relationship between the social meaning of a gift and whether it corrupts? I'd like you to say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where several theories of corruption (purchase-of-discretion, integrity-of-office, appearance-of-impropriety) could all license the same rule — and what would concretely have to change in the law if the integrity view replaced the purchase view.
