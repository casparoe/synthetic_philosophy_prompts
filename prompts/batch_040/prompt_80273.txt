I spent my career in experimental physics, mostly on instrumentation and signal processing, and only in the last couple of years have I started reading philosophy and the literature on collective decision-making. The Condorcet jury theorem keeps coming up in that literature, and it is almost always introduced by the intuition that independent errors cancel out as you add more voters. From my background, that is just the sensor-array picture: a jury is like an array of detectors, majority voting is like averaging, and adding jurors is like adding channels, so the verdict should get cleaner the bigger the array. A friend of mine who works in law and economics leans on exactly this to argue that the larger the jury the more reliable its verdict, and that we should therefore favor big juries and plain majority rule in nearly every setting.

I would like you to audit that sensor-array analogy. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the detectors, the signal, the noise, the number of channels, and the averaging step — and then the places where the mapping breaks. In particular, I want you to work through the case I keep returning to: a jury in which every member has read the same single expert report, and that report is wrong. In the sensor picture this looks like common-mode interference rather than independent noise, and I suspect that is exactly where both the analogy and the theorem's independence assumption go quiet. Tell me what the theorem actually guarantees versus what the analogy quietly imports from my field, which of the disanalogies really matter for the conclusion that bigger juries are better, whether that conclusion survives once the common-error case is on the table, and — if you think the sensor picture is simply the wrong frame — what frame would carry the point better.
