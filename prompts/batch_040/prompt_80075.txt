I'm the senior editor at a weekly science magazine, and I want to turn the careful philosophical understanding of heritability into something my fact-checking desk can actually run on a daily basis. We get a steady stream of claims about heritability — from press releases, podcast transcripts, book excerpts, and policy briefs — and our readers keep absorbing the wrong lesson from them, so I need a decision procedure, not an essay.

You are a professional philosopher of science with a specialty in the epistemology of genetics and quantitative trait analysis. Before you write the procedure, do a short brainstorm (a few bullet points) of the distinct ways a heritability claim can be technically true but still misleading, so the checklist is built around real failure modes rather than a generic "be careful" list. Then write the procedure itself: an ordered set of questions a non-geneticist fact-checker can run in roughly ten minutes, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, the point at which the procedure must stop and hand the call to a human editor (me, who signs off) because the question is one of values or of study-design adequacy rather than of fact, and an honest flag on which steps are really judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

The procedure has to keep alive, even in simplified form, a few distinctions that get flattened in popular coverage. Make sure it can detect: (a) the difference between a heritability estimate for a trait within a population and a claim about the proportion of an individual's trait that is genetic; (b) the difference between the heritability of a trait and the heritability of a difference between two groups, which is a stronger and much harder claim; and (c) the fact that a high heritability estimate does not imply the trait is insensitive to the environment — a trait can be highly heritable in one environment and shift dramatically when the environment changes, so "heritable" and "unchangeable" are not the same. Also build in a check for the gene-environment correlation confound: in many human traits, the genes parents pass on are correlated with the environment they provide (sometimes called "genetic nurture," where a parent's own partly genetic traits shape the home environment), which can inflate a naive heritability estimate by attributing an environmental effect to genes. I'm giving you these distinctions so you can operationalize them; you don't need to derive them from scratch.

The procedure must end by running itself on the three test claims below, and it must be willing to give me an answer I won't like on at least one of them — i.e., a claim that sounds reasonable and is popular but that the procedure flags as misleading. For each test claim, show the questions being answered, where the procedure stops and hands off to a human, and the final verdict (publish as-is / publish with a correction note / reject the claim as stated). Reason through each claim before you commit to a verdict.

Test claim 1 (press release): "A new twin study finds that 70 percent of the variance in IQ is heritable, showing that IQ is mostly genetic and essentially fixed, so education policy is largely futile."

Test claim 2 (podcast): "Research now shows that roughly 80 percent of the height difference between two populations is genetic."

Test claim 3 (policy brief): "Because the heritability of family income is high, the current distribution of income is largely a product of nature and not something policy should try to reshape."

Don't include citations or a reading list — my fact-checkers will pull the underlying studies themselves, so the procedure should tell them what to look for in a study, not which studies to read. And when you're done, add a short note on where the procedure is most likely to misfire for someone without genetics training, and what kind of claim or study design would change your mind about the checklist being adequate — i.e., the conditions under which you'd revise it.
