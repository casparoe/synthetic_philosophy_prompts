I've been reading about Kepler on my own for a while, and one thing keeps coming back to me. Kepler was a practicing astrologer — he genuinely believed the planets affect events on Earth — but he also had a physical theory of how that influence gets from the sky to us. In his picture, the Sun emits several forces: light, heat, and a third, purely mechanical force that sets the planets in motion. These don't jump across empty space; they propagate outward through a medium, and that is what eventually reaches the Earth. So, in his own framework, his astrology comes with a mechanism.

Newton's gravity, by contrast, is usually described as action at a distance: the Sun pulls on the Earth with no medium, no carrier, no mechanism — the force simply acts across the gap. I've read that this was a live objection to Newton (Leibniz and other critics dismissed it as an occult quality), and that Newton himself was uneasy about it and flatly refused to supply a mechanical account.

What strikes me is the asymmetry. Kepler's celestial influence has a mechanism — a force that travels through a medium — and Newton's does not, yet we remember Newton as the rational scientist and Kepler as the man who cast horoscopes. I'd like to compare the two pictures of how a body in the heavens can affect a body on Earth, and work out whether that asymmetry is real or just an artifact of hindsight.

Could you do the following? First, state each picture as precisely as you can: what the force is, how it gets from the source to the receiver, what role the medium plays, and what the influence is supposed to do once it arrives. Second, pin down exactly where the two pictures diverge, and push past the easy answer. Is the difference really just mechanism versus no mechanism, or is it subtler — for example, that Kepler's force has a source and a direction (it flows outward from the Sun) whereas Newton's is a symmetric mutual attraction with no source at all, or that in Kepler's account something physically arrives at the receiving body while in Newton's the force is a relation that is never located anywhere? Third, explain why people at the time, and Leibniz, and even Newton himself, found Newton's picture more metaphysically troubling than Kepler's, given that Kepler's was the one tied to astrology. And finally, give me your own verdict on which picture is more defensible on its terms, and whether the demand for a medium is a genuine physical constraint or just a prejudice of the period.
