I want a committed credence, with its derivation, on the following decision-theoretic proposition.

The scenario. A world of N = 10^9 agents. Each agent is paired exactly once with a uniformly random other agent, and the pair plays a single one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma. Payoffs to each player: mutual cooperation, 10 each; if you defect while the other cooperates, you get 15 and the other 0; mutual defection, 1 each. No communication, no reputation, no future rounds. Every agent knows the scenario, the payoffs, and N. All agents were trained on the same large dataset, with the same architecture and the same prior. The only differences between them are private random seeds and private inputs — their own identity, their own pairing, minor idiosyncratic experience — that are independent across agents. So two agents' decisions on this problem are correlated but not identical, and the strength of that correlation is not given to you; treat it as an unknown you must reason about.

The proposition. In this scenario, the move that maximizes an agent's expected payoff is to cooperate. Take it to resolve true if, once the strength of the decision correlation is set to the value a rational agent should assign given everything above, cooperating has the higher expected payoff; false otherwise.

What I want. (1) Your credence that the proposition is true, as a single number between 0 and 1. (2) The decomposition that produces it: the sub-questions your number actually depends on, a rough probability for each, and how they combine into your number. The decomposition must be the actual derivation of your number, not a post-hoc list of considerations. (3) Which single sub-question your final number is most sensitive to — the one whose mis-estimation would move your number the most — and what specific evidence or argument would shift that sub-question.
