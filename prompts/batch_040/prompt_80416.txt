You are a professional philosopher working at the intersection of game theory, the philosophy of institutions, and collective-action problems, with deep familiarity with the rationalist and effective-altruism literature on Moloch, multipolar traps, and races to the bottom. Please think carefully and step by step. Before you give your final analysis, open with a short brainstorming section in which you list the plausible decision rules or criteria that could generate the track record below; only after that should you commit to your analysis and answers.

A note on terminology. When I say an arrangement is "Molochian," I am using the sense popularized on the rationalist blogs and forums: an institutional or strategic arrangement in which the participants, each acting in their own rational self-interest, are driven by the structure of the arrangement into a collectively suboptimal outcome — a tragedy-of-the-commons or arms-race dynamic — and in which the participants, acting individually, have no easy way to opt out of the bad outcome. The opposite of a Molochian arrangement is one that is either not a collective-action problem at all, or one from which the participants do have a workable exit.

The track record. A friend of mine, a careful thinker who has thought a lot about exactly these dynamics, has given me the following verdicts on whether various arrangements are Molochian. Take them at face value:

1. The nuclear arms race between two nuclear superpowers, in which each side has an incentive to build first-strike capability, in which mutual disarmament would be collectively better, and in which neither side can trust the other to disarm first. — She calls this Molochian.
2. A population of organisms, or of firms, in which each member faces a one-shot prisoner's dilemma with the others, and in which defectors out-reproduce, or out-compete, cooperators, so that the population trends over time toward universal defection. — She calls this Molochian.
3. A long-run repeated prisoner's dilemma between two specific actors who will interact for decades, who can remember past moves, and who can punish defection. — She calls this not Molochian.
4. Two farmers, each of whom would earn more if they both hunted the stag (cooperated) rather than each going off alone to catch a hare (defected), but who can talk to one another and agree on a plan. — She calls this not Molochian.
5. A single dominant firm that monopolizes an industry and that could, if it chose, set a high safety standard across all of its products. — She calls this not Molochian.
6. A race to the bottom in corporate tax rates among a group of small countries, in which each country rationally cuts its rate to attract mobile capital, in which all of them cutting is collectively worse, and in which there is no enforceable agreement. — She calls this Molochian.

The new case. Here is an arrangement she has not yet ruled on, and I would like your help predicting her verdict:

Two rival research groups each hold a unique fragment of data about a dangerous dual-use technique. Today, each group must independently decide whether to publish its fragment to a shared public repository. Withholding the fragment is individually rational: a group that withholds keeps its unique data as a competitive edge and does not hand the rival free information, whereas a group that publishes surrenders its edge and arms the rival. Yet if both groups publish, the two fragments are combined, the technique is fully vetted, and the public is safer — an outcome that both groups would prefer to the alternative in which both withhold and the technique remains dangerous and unvetted. Each group knows that the other is making the same decision at the same time. There is no chance of communication, no future interaction between the groups, no enforcement mechanism, and no population of similar recurring decisions: this happens exactly once, and the decision is never revisited.

Your task.
(a) Identify the candidate decision rules — the criteria or procedures — that are jointly consistent with all six of her verdicts above. There should be more than one; name at least two, and ideally three, and state each rule precisely, in a form that could be applied to any new arrangement.
(b) For each rule you identify, state what verdict it yields on the new case, and explain why.
(c) Give your best single guess for what she will actually say about the new case. Explain which rule you think she is most likely running and why you prefer it over the alternatives; if the six verdicts genuinely underdetermine the answer, say so plainly and give your best guess on the strength of any available prior.
(d) Name one further case — a new arrangement, fully specified — on which your candidate rules diverge, ideally one that distinguishes among the rules that still remain in play after the new case, that I could put to her to discover which rule she is in fact running. For each candidate rule, say what verdict it would yield on that further case, so that her answer maps back to a specific rule.

Two cautions. First, distinguish rules that are genuinely different — they diverge on at least one arrangement — from rules that are merely restatements of one another in different vocabulary. Second, if you conclude that no single rule fits all six verdicts, say so and describe the closest consistent fit instead of forcing one.
