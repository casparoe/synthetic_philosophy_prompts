So, weird new hobby: I retired from chemistry about two years ago and have been falling down the rabbit hole of philosophy of science lately, and a friend who teaches asked me to help grade a stack of exam scripts this week. Intro cognitive science class, short answer question, worth 10 marks. The question was:

"In one page or less: describe the Wason selection task, state what Cosmides and Tooby (1992) found when they presented it in a social-contract frame, and evaluate wether this evidence really supports the claim that humans have a dedicated cognitive module for detecting cheaters in social exchange, or whether it is consistent with domain-general pragmatic reasoning."

Here's one student's answer, verbatim:

"The Wason selection task shows people four cards, each with a value on one side and a value on the other, plus a rule of the form 'if a card has property P on one side, then it has property Q on the other.' The participant may turn over any cards they like, and the task is to turn over exactly those that could show the rule is false. On the original abstract versions (a vowel on one side implies an even number on the other) most people do badly: they turn over the P card and the Q card, and miss the not-Q card, which is the only other card that matters.

Cosmides and Tooby (1992) replaced the abstract content with a social one. In the version I was given, the rule was 'if you are over 20, then you may drink beer,' and the four cards showed 25, 18, beer, and soda. Here the same people who failed the abstract version reliably picked 18 and beer. Their explanation is that natural selection built us a dedicated module for detecting cheaters in social exchange, and the social-contract frame switches that module on, so the reasoning looks easy even though the logical structure is identical to the abstract case.

I would say this is a classic just-so story: you can always invent a story about why we have a cheater detector, but the story does the explaining, not the evidence. Also, the task is easy in the social version because it lets people use real-world knowledge about bars and ID checks, which is just pragmatic reasoning with familiar content, not a special module at all."

Can you give this a grade out of 10 and then walk through it point by point, saying for each claim whether it is right, wrong, or muddled, and why? I want to see exactly what would go in the margin comments, not just a verdict. And be specific about which parts of the last two paragraphs, if any, get the Cosmides-and-Tooby claim wrong rather than merely incomplete. No need to be gentle; the student is an adult.
