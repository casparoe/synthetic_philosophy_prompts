I've just had a long, rather heated argument with a friend about the frame problem in artificial intelligence — the difficulty, when you're programming a robot to reason about the world, of specifying which facts persist unchanged after the robot performs an action, without having to list them all explicitly. We were discussing it over dinner, partly because I'm taking a course on AI this term and find the frame problem more intractable than I expected, and partly because my friend is convinced that any robot which claims to have "solved" it must be doing something deeply wrong, whereas I think there are at least a few workable approaches (the closed-world assumption, non-accidental-change axioms, and so on).

To settle the argument (or at least to make it more entertaining), I'd like you to write a deadpan, in-universe product review — in the style of a tech-magazine review, roughly 400–500 words — of a fictional household robot, the "Domestic-9000," which has been marketed as the first robot to "fully solve the frame problem." The review should be written by an enthusiastic but slightly confused consumer who is trying to figure out what the robot actually does.

The humour should come from the actual content of the frame problem: the robot, having "solved" the problem by adopting a strict closed-world assumption, assumes that nothing in the world changes unless it is explicitly told otherwise, and so it never gets out of bed, never eats, never learns, and sits in a corner contemplating the persistence of its own stillness. The review should include at least one quote from a "concerned" AI researcher (an invented one) who is trying to explain, to no avail, that the robot has not so much solved the frame problem as solved the problem of being a robot at all. The piece should be funny to someone who knows the frame problem, and legible to someone who doesn't.
