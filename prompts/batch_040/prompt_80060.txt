I'm putting together a small taxonomy of recurring moves in arguments about value, and there's one configuration I keep running into that I can't pin a label on. It shows up most clearly in cases where someone has to choose between two options they've genuinely concluded they can't rank — not because they haven't thought about it, but because they're convinced there's no fact of the matter about which is better, just two real values pulling in different directions. Since the merits don't settle it, the chooser reaches for an arbitrary tie-break: a coin flip, whichever option feels a little stronger in the moment, the one that came to mind first. Up to here everyone concedes the choice was arbitrary. The interesting part is what happens next. When the choice is later called into question — by a partner, a committee, or the chooser's own doubts — the same person produces a confident, fluent argument that ranks the option they picked above the one they rejected, on a single shared scale. And the argument is tailored to whichever option actually won: had the tie-break gone the other way, they'd have produced an equally good argument for the other option. The ranking didn't exist before the flip; it was constructed to fit the result.

Two instances that keep coming back to me. A friend had to pick between a high-paying job in a city she disliked and a lower-paying one in a city she loved; she was sure it was a genuine dead heat between money and quality of life, so she flipped a coin and got the high-paying job, and two weeks later, when her partner asked why she'd traded away the city, she produced a detailed case that, once you properly discount future income and weigh career options, the high-paying job is clearly the better deal — even though a month earlier she'd called the two a tie. And on a grant committee, we had to choose between two projects everyone agreed were equally valuable but that we couldn't fund both; we broke the tie by seniority, and the member whose project was funded then spent the rest of the meeting arguing in detail that his project was the more important one all along, on grounds of long-term impact, while the member whose project was rejected had, minutes before, been making the mirror-image case for his own.

I know this lives in the neighborhood of a few things I have words for — rationalization, the idea that preferences are revealed by what you actually do rather than by what you say, and the debate over whether incommensurability is a real feature of values or just our failure to find the right common measure. But I don't think any of those captures it, because in each of them the distortion is supposed to come from somewhere already in place: a pre-existing desire, a self-image to protect, or a measure that was there all along and we just hadn't found. In the cases I'm describing, as far as I can tell, the distortion is created by the act of choosing itself. There was no ranking before the tie-break, and a ranking appears only after, and only for the option that was picked. So is there an established concept for this exact configuration? If there is, I'd want it distinguished from plain rationalization, from revealed preference, and from the "incommensurability is just ignorance" view, and I'd want the edges made clear — at what point does this stop being the thing I'm describing and become just ordinary self-deception, or just ordinary preference formation through deliberation? If there isn't, I'd be glad to have it named and defined in a way that carves out exactly this case.
