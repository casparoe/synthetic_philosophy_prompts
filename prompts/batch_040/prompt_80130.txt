I spent my career as a field entomologist and have only in the last few years begun to read philosophy seriously. I will be moderating a Q&A session, in about a month, with a guest who defends the view that sentience — and with it moral standing — is a matter of degree rather than an all-or-nothing property, and who therefore assigns different weights to the interests of different kinds of creatures, from mammals down to insects and, in principle, to still simpler organisms. I would like your help preparing a set of eight to ten questions.

Please do not write questions that merely ask her to restate the view. I am most interested in pressing on four points. The first is the distinction between what some writers call sentiment — the capacity to have experiences with positive or negative valence, such as pain and pleasure — and sentience in the broader sense of any subjective experience at all; I would like to find out whether the gradient of moral status should track the former, the latter, or some third property, and what follows if the two come apart. The second is the boundary at the bottom end of the gradient: if a sufficiently sophisticated control system can be said, in a loose sense, to care about its setpoint, what separates the thermostat's apparent concern from the most minimal real feeling, and whether her view draws that boundary in degree or in kind. The third is the practical problem of measurement: how one locates an organism on the gradient when we have almost no behavioral evidence about it, and what weight uncertainty should carry, given that the cost of underestimating a creature's sentience seems to me to be far greater than the cost of overestimating it. The fourth is the arithmetic of aggregation: if a mosquito's standing is a small fraction of a mouse's, does it follow that a sufficiently large number of mosquitoes can be traded off against a single mouse, and what the view implies for decisions that turn on very large numbers of beings with minimal standing.

For each of the four points, please give me a main question and a follow-up that anticipates the answer she is most likely to give and presses on it. Beyond those, please include a question on what the view implies for the design of experiments and for the ethics of field research — my former colleagues find the idea of doing ethical triage across species deeply uncomfortable, and I would like to see how she responds to that — and a closing question on what evidence or argument would count against the view, or would lead her to revise it.

Please write the questions in the first person, as if I am asking them, in a formal but warm register, and do not refer to any specific books, authors, or papers.
