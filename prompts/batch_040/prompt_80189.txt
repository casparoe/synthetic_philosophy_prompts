I'm auditing a graduate philosophy of mind seminar, and one topic I keep coming back to is the so-called "paradox of phenomenal judgment" as it bears on epiphenomenalism. I'll set it up the way I currently understand it, then tell you about a disagreement I overheard and ask you to referee it. The background: epiphenomenalism is the view that conscious experiences (qualia) are produced by physical processes in the brain but have no causal power of their own — they are, in the standard metaphor, the steam whistle on a train: they sound when the engine runs, but they do not move the train. The paradox of phenomenal judgment runs roughly like this. Take my judgment "I am in pain." That judgment is a physical, functional state in my brain, but it seems to be caused by my pain — I form it precisely because I am in pain, not for any other reason. If qualia are epiphenomenal, however, my pain cannot be the cause of anything. So the very judgment that is supposed to be my best evidence for having qualia appears to be caused by a thing that, on the epiphenomenalist's own view, causes nothing. Either the quale must have some causal role after all (which would contradict epiphenomenalism), or the paradox is somehow illusory, and the question is how.

Last night two friends of mine — one a neuroscience PhD student, the other a philosophy grad student — argued about this over dinner, and I genuinely couldn't tell who was more right. The neuroscience student, M, said the paradox is a live problem: if my pain cannot cause my judgment, then what does? She argued that you can't simply say the neural firing does the causal work, because then the quale is doing literally nothing and is a useless byproduct, and the only reason we ever posited qualia was to explain something — the character of experience and our first-person knowledge of it — so if the quale is absent from the causal chain that produces my pain-judgment, we have cut it off from the very evidence that was supposed to support it. The philosophy student, D, replied that M is assuming the quale has to be the cause of the judgment, which is exactly what epiphenomenalism denies: the judgment is a physical state caused by the same neural process that gives rise to the quale, so the quale and the judgment co-arise as two outputs of one process, and there is no paradox, just the quale riding along. M pushed back that co-arising doesn't answer the real question, which is why her judgments about her qualia are so reliable — why do her pain-judgments track her pains so well? — and that if the quale is not causally involved there is no mechanism that would make the tracking happen, turning phenomenal self-knowledge into a coincidence. D answered that the tracking is explained by the common cause, since the same neural state both produces the quale and constitutes the judgment, and that the deeper source of the whole paradox is a hidden premise M is smuggling in, namely the idea that the content of a phenomenal judgment must be fixed by a causal relation to the quale; deny that causal theory of phenomenal content, he said, and the paradox dissolves, because the quale never needed to be in the causal chain in order to be the subject-matter of the judgment.

As a professional philosopher working in philosophy of mind, please think this through carefully and adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the four main moves — M's claim that the paradox is a live problem, D's co-arising reply, M's reliability challenge, and D's common-cause / causal-theory-of-content reply — say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or somewhere in between, and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and, just as importantly, identify what both of them are missing, if anything. Before you commit to a verdict, it would help to first state, in a few sentences, the strongest version of each side's position, so that the adjudication is anchored to the best readings of their views rather than the weakest.
