I'm preparing a paper on the demandingness objection, and I want a thought experiment built to a precise specification. The scenario should present an agent who complies perfectly with every demand of a demanding moral theory — a Singer-style view on which you are required to redirect nearly all of your discretionary income to the most effective causes — and who is nevertheless not admirable as a moral saint, with the explanation of the non-admirability being not the standard objection that saints sacrifice their personal projects and make poor friends, but a distinct supererogation point. For background: Driver's moral-saints argument holds that a person who does everything morality requires is not fully admirable, and therefore morality cannot actually require what the saint does; I want you to press on a different seam — not that the saint gives up personal projects, but that the saint never does anything above duty. Because a fully demanding theory leaves little or no room for acts that go beyond the call of duty, an agent who does exactly what is required does only the moral minimum, and the moral minimum becomes a ceiling on moral performance rather than a floor; perfect compliance is thus the least admirable kind of moral life, even though it is the only kind the theory allows.

Constraints: the case must be set in an ordinary context (a workplace, a family, or a small community), fit in a single paragraph, and the agent's non-admirability must not come from the agent doing anything wrong, from the agent's selfishness, or from any cost to the agent's own flourishing; it must fall out of the structure of the demanding theory itself. After the scenario, walk through what the demanding theory says the agent ought to do and why, what a Driver-style saint critique says, and why the supererogation point — rather than the personal-project point — is doing the work. Then say what happens if the theory is revised to admit a zone of supererogation, so that some acts are genuinely good but not required, and how a defender of demanding theories would attack the case on the grounds that the supererogation objection is either incoherent or trivial.
