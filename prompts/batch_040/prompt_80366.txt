I've been turning this over for a while and I keep failing to find the exit, so I'd like a precise diagnosis rather than a restatement of the two sides. A friend who works on free will built what he calls a universalization objection to compatibilism, and I can't locate the hole. Here is the argument, in his claims compressed into steps:

(1) Compatibilism holds that an agent is responsible for an action only if she could have done otherwise, and compatibilists cash out "could have done otherwise" in a local, character-relative way: had she deliberated differently, given her own settled character and the situation, she would have chosen differently.
(2) But the compatibilist is also a determinist: her character, her deliberation, and her choice are all fixed outputs of prior causes, stretching back indefinitely.
(3) So the clause "had she deliberated differently, she would have chosen differently" can only be satisfied by describing a counterfactual world in which some prior causes were different, because on determinism her deliberation cannot vary while the prior causes stay fixed.
(4) But in any deterministic scheme, whatever the prior causes, the action is the inevitable output; a world with different prior causes simply produces a different action. The "could have done otherwise" condition is therefore satisfied by every action of every deterministic agent.
(5) So compatibilism makes responsibility universal: every action counts as responsible, whether it flows from her settled character or is the product of a brain tumor pressing on the right button, a drug, or a hidden manipulator wiring her to act.
(6) That is plainly false. The tumor and the manipulator are paradigmatically cases of non-responsibility.
(7) Therefore compatibilism is false, and the incompatibilist is right.

He's confident the force is somewhere in the (3) to (4) transition, and I grant that (7) is absurd. I feel like something slips in that neighborhood, but I can't name it, and I'm not sure whether the argument is actually valid and the real question is just which premise is cheapest to drop. I'd much rather keep (1) and (2) than (6). Tell me which step to reject, why it looked fine, and what I pay for rejecting it; and if there is a reading on which the argument is sound, I'd like that reading named and shown to be the one the compatibilist actually endorses, or shown not to be.
