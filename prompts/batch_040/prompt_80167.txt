I have been trying to work out what I actually think about the contrast I associate with German and American folk morality: a rule-order morality that privileges rule-following, order, and public trust, and a conscience morality that privileges individual judgment and case-by-case moral responsibility. I want a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures my gut verdicts on the following cases and then says which of the two traditions the principle gives the better of it, and in what respect.

Returning a lost wallet with identification to the police is right even if the owner is a person I dislike, because public trust depends on the rule being treated as impersonal. Not reporting a friend's minor parking violation, when the fine would cause real hardship and the rule is not safety-related, is also right, not because the rule is optional, but because conscience should not be used to make oneself a petty enforcer. Driving fifteen kilometers per hour over the limit on an empty road at night is morally permissible, though not admirable, because it injures no one's reasonable expectations. I nevertheless think it is wrong to break a workplace rule against taking snacks from the supply cupboard, even if harmless and unnoticed, because the rule is a public commitment that the workplace has asked everyone to honor. I also think it is right for a teacher to override a school rule requiring the reporting of a student's non-dangerous mistake, if reporting would shame the student and the teacher can protect the student's dignity. Finally, I think it is wrong for a citizen to refuse to pay a lawful tax they dislike, as long as there is an available legal channel for protest, because private conscience cannot veto collective decisions.

The pair I keep going back and forth on is the workplace-snack rule and the teacher-reporting rule: both seem to be harmless rule-breaking, but I treat one as a failure of order and the other as an exercise of conscience. Is there a coherent principle that gets all of these, or close? State each candidate precisely, say which case motivates each clause, what it predicts for cases I have not listed, such as a nurse following a hospital rule that delays a minor but urgent request, or a shopkeeper refusing a legal discount to a regular customer because the discount policy is meant to be uniform, and honestly say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Please also say how confident you are in your main claims and single out the point you are least sure of.
