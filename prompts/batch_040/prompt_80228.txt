I'm applying for a three-month internal research sabbatical and need a two-page proposal for it. The topic is the digger wasp (Sphex) and what its famous looping behavior actually shows about free will. I have a half-formed idea and would like it turned into a precise, fundable question.

Let me stipulate the background so you don't have to source it. The wasp paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the lip of its burrow, goes inside to inspect the burrow, then comes out and drags the cricket in. An experimenter can repeatedly move the cricket a few inches away. Each time the wasp emerges, it finds the cricket missing, relocates it, drags it back to the lip, and then goes in to inspect again — repeating the sequence, by some accounts, indefinitely. The standard philosophical use of this, under the name "sphexishness" (Hofstadter's coinage, much used by Dennett), is that it reveals a mindless machine: the wasp has no representation of the fact that the burrow has already been checked, so it is trapped in a loop by crude causal forces. This is offered as a picture of what a deterministic universe looks like from the inside, and hence as something we have reason to fear. Dennett's reply is that we shouldn't fear it, because the wasp is unrepresentatively simple, and even deterministic systems can be complex, flexible, and unpredictable. I also want to stipulate an empirical caveat: recent ethological work suggests the repeated inspection may itself be adaptive, and that the wasp may show some adaptation after many iterations, so the clean "infinite loop" story is probably overstated.

My half-formed idea: I think this exchange misdescribes what the experiment shows. The wasp is responding to the perturbation — it finds the moved cricket and drags it back. That is responsive, flexible behavior, not a failure to register the environment. What the wasp lacks is only memory of one particular fact. So the loop is a failure of state representation, not a failure of responsiveness. And the consequence I want to develop: if the real "fear" of determinism is the fear of being unresponsive to the world, the sphex doesn't even illustrate that. If the real fear is being a memoryless automaton, then adding memory and complexity doesn't get you out either, since a deterministic automaton with rich memory is still deterministic and can still loop, just in more complicated ways. I suspect, then, that the "complexity" response is answering a straw-man, and that the standard use of the example conflates at least three distinct things — behavioral stereotypy, the absence of state representation, and the absence of freedom — when the experiment evidences at most the second, and only with respect to a single fact.

Please turn this into a proposal with: a precise statement of the question (roughly: what is the free-will relevance of the wasp's state-blindness, and can a complex, flexible, unpredictable deterministic system still be "trapped" in the sense the objection targets?); why the existing positions — the mechanistic reading, the complexity response, and the empirical challenge — leave it open; the method, i.e., what I would actually do, week by week, and why this conceptual method rather than the obvious alternatives (a new behavioral experiment, or a literature survey); what a useful result would look like, and what would count as the project failing; and an honest assessment of whether this is too big for three months.
