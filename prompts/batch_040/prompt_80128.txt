The usual way people draw the line between manipulation and persuasion is a door picture: persuasion knocks and waits for you to open the door, manipulation picks the lock or goes round the back, so what's wrong with manipulation is that it bypasses your own judgement instead of going through it. That's the the standard account and it's what most of the cognitive-liberty argument leans on. But I keep getting stuck on the fact that some of the clearest cases of manipulation work entirely through a person's own reasoning — gaslighting, propaganda built on a plausible but fallacious argument, anchoring — whereas some things that do bypass conscious deliberation, like quietly changing a default option, are pretty mild. Can you audit the door picture for me: what maps onto what, where the mapping breaks, what the metaphor quietly smuggles in about the self (is there really a clean inside and outside, is consent a binary gate?), and whether the conclusion it's carrying — that the line is the bypass-versus-go-through line and that manipulation is wrong because it bypasses — survives once gaslighting and the default case are on the table. If it doesn't, what's a better way to draw the line?
