My friend is an archaeologist who works on the ancient Near East and the eastern Mediterranean, and she holds a firm view about when a legend counts as history: a legend is "historical" only if it has a real core — a real person, place, or event that the legend grew out of. I've been trying to reverse-engineer the exact rule she applies, because her verdicts on specific legends follow a pattern I can't quite pin down.

Here are her verdicts so far, each rendered on the question "does this legend have a historical core?":

1. The Trojan War — yes. There was a real war at Troy (a destruction layer at Hissarlik, plus Hittite references to a place she reads as Wilusa/Ilios), but the epic itself is fiction.
2. King Arthur — no. Geoffrey of Monmouth's 12th-century invention, at most a faint memory of some 6th-century war leader, but no identifiable core.
3. The Exodus — no. No mass exodus; at most a small band of Hebrews leaving Egypt, which is not the story as told.
4. The sack of Rome by the Gauls (390 BCE) — yes for the core, no for the details. A real sack probably happened (there's a destruction layer at Rome from that period, and Greek accounts mention it), but the specifics — Brennus, the temple bells, the famous woe-to-the-vanquished line — are legend.
5. The Nibelungenlied — no. Attila is a real historical figure who appears in it (as Etzel), but the Nibelung story itself — Siegfried, the hoard, Kriemhild — has no historical core.

The new case I want her to rule on: the Great Flood — the Genesis flood and the related Mesopotamian and Greek flood traditions (the Sumerian flood stories, the flood in the Epic of Gilgamesh, the Deucalion myth).

Do you think she'll say the flood has a historical core? I'm less interested in the bare yes/no than in the rule she seems to be running, the other rules that fit her verdicts just as well, what each of those rules predicts about the flood, and which further case would best tell those rules apart.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — your best-guess verdict on the flood, stated plainly — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
