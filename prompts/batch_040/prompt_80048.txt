I've been going in circles on why knowledge is supposed to be better than true belief, and I think the best way to make sense of it is to look at specific cases. Here's where I land, and I'd like to know if there's a principle that captures this, or if I'm just being inconsistent.

Case 1: Someone looks at a working clock and sees it's 3:00. Someone else looks at a stopped clock that happens to be stuck at 3:00. Both believe it's 3:00, and both are right. I feel like the first person is in a better epistemeic position — their belief is "fixed" or "secure" in a way the second person's isn't. If the time were to change, the first person's belief would track the truth and the second person's wouldn't.

Case 2: But now suppose both people have to make a one-shot bet right now: they each get to guess the time, and the guess is settled by the actual time. I don't see how the person with the working clock is any better off. The bet is won or lost based on the actual time, and both have the right answer. The "security" of the working-clock belief doesn't seem to buy them anything in this context.

Case 3: I also think knowledge is better than true belief when you want to use your belief as a premise for further reasoning. If I know that all humans are mortal and I know Socrates is human, I can come to know that Socrates is mortal. But if I merely truly believe that all humans are mortal (say, by a lucky guess) and I know Socrates is human, my conclusion that Socrates is mortal doesn't inherit the reliability of the premise — it's just another true belief that happens to be right.

Case 4: But then I also think that in a situation where I just need to act once — say, I need to decide whether to take an umbrella, and I truly believe (correctly) that it will rain — my true belief is just as good as knowledge for the purpose of getting me to act correctly. I take the umbrella, it rains, I stay dry. The fact that my belief was "lucky" doesn't seem to matter for the outcome.

So my verdicts are: knowledge is better in cases 1 and 3, but not in cases 2 and 4. Is there a principle that captures this pattern? I have a vague sense that it has to do with whether the belief is being used in ongoing inquiry or in a one-shot decision, but I can't articulate it precisely. And I'm not sure if this is a real distinction or if I'm just rationalizing my intuitions. What would a careful epistemologist say? And if the principle I'm reaching for is wrong, which of my four verdicts should I give up first?
