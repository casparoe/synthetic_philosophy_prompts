I want an essay on whether Bell's theorem actually forces us to give up locality, or whether we could just trade some other assumption and keep locality intact. The 'Bell's theorem proves the universe is nonlocal' slogan is doing a lot of hidden work and I want you to pull on it. Here's the setup to work from, because it's the part everyone skips: the Bell inequality is really derived from three seperable assumptions. Two of them are the locality assumptions, and they split into parameter independence (the chance of Alice getting a given outcome doesn't depend on which setting Bob chose) and outcome independence (the chance of Alice getting a given outcome doesn't depend on what outcome Bob actually got). The third is measurement independence, the idea that whatever hidden variables underlie the pairs are statistically independent of the settings the experimenters happen to pick. Now the key fact: quantum mechanics itself satisfies parameter independence — that's exactly why you can't use entanglement to send a signal faster then light — but it violates outcome independence, since the two outcomes are correlated in a way the shared state alone doesn't account for. So the real question is which of the three you're willing to drop, and whether dropping just outcome independence while keeping parameter independence is some acceptable 'soft' nonlocality or whether that's just nonlocality with the lights on. Weigh the three escapes — superdeterminism (drop measurement independence), explicitly nonlocal theories like Bohm's (drop parameter independence), and just biting the bullet on standard QM (drop outcome independence) — and reach a verdict on which assumption is the one we should actually be most scared to give up, and why. Don't end on a hedge. Also tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would flip your verdict, or where your reasoning is most likely to break down. Write it in American English.
