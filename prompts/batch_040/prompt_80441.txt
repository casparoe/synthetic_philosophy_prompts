I work on the philosophy of mechanisms and I keep hitting a wall with how anomaly detection is actually done, and I want to pin down what view of "the same mechanism" it quietly commits you to. Take statistical process control: you monitor a manufacturing process, set the control limits from it's own past output (the usual 3-sigma stuff), and flag any point outside them as a special cause — i.e. the mechanism has changed. The catch is the limits are computed from the mechanism's own history, so "same mechanism" is effectively defined as "keeps producing what it used to produce," and anything outside that envelope is an anomaly by construction. But the engineer's gut sense of "the same machine" is about the physical parts and their causal roles, not the output distribution. So what view of mechanism identity does the practice actually presuppose — functional, structural, or something behavioral/statistical? Is that circularity (baseline drawn from the mechanism itself) a real problem or just a pragmatic convention? And if the mechanism is software, where a firmware update is a planned change to the parts, does the special-cause/common-cause split even survive? Not after a reading list, just the implicit theory and where it's doing the work.
