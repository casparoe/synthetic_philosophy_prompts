I'm taking a graduate seminar on the foundations of quantum mechanics this term, and I've been trying to sort out a debate that keeps coming up in the readings but that I find harder to pin down than the textbook presentations suggest. I understand the basic setup: standard textbook QM appears to contain two incompatible evolution rules — smooth unitary Schrödinger evolution and the discontinuous collapse postulate — and both Everettian many-worlds and Bohmian (de Broglie–Bohm) mechanics try to resolve the tension by keeping only the unitary rule and reinterpreting what a measurement is. What I keep getting stuck on is a question a level below "which interpretation is right": whether the two frameworks are actually empirically equivalent, and if they are, which one is the more parsimonious and coherent theory. One claim I've been given is that Bohmian mechanics, in the special case where the distribution of particle positions matches the Born-rule distribution (the so-called "quantum equilibrium" hypothesis), reproduces exactly the same predictions as ordinary QM — which is presented to me as both a strength (it recovers everything we have observed) and a weakness (the extra particle structure is then, in some sense, unobservable and possibly redundant). I've also been told that Everettian QM faces two famous open problems, the "preferred basis" problem (why does decoherence carve the universal wave function into branches in the position basis rather than in some other basis?) and the problem of recovering the Born rule (why should the weight or probability of a branch equal the squared magnitude of its amplitude, rather than some other function of the amplitude?), and that Bohmians cite these as evidence that Everett is not yet a finished theory.

Could you write a dialogue between a well-read Everettian and a well-read Bohmian, each of whom is trying to convince the other that their own framework is the more coherent and empirically adequate account? I want both speakers to be genuinely strong advocates rather than caricatures, so each should anticipate and respond to the other's best points instead of talking past them. In particular, I'd like the exchange to engage directly with the two technical issues above: the Everettian should give their best answer to the preferred-basis objection (the decoherence / "pointer basis" answer) and to the Born-rule objection (the "typicality" or "branch-weight" answer), and the Bohmian should give their best answer to the redundancy and parsimony objection (the claim that the particle positions supply a clear, single-outcome ontology that Everett still lacks, and that the explicit nonlocality of the guiding equation is a price worth paying, given that Bell's theorem appears to rule out any local hidden-variable completion of QM).

Two more constraints. First, the dialogue should surface at least one point where the two of them genuinely agree — for instance, that both find the Copenhagen "Heisenberg cut" and its classical/quantum divide unsatisfactory — so that it reads as a real exchange rather than two monologues delivered back to back. Second, please don't end with a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations; I just want the argument itself, laid out step by step in a form that a confused student like me could actually follow and then use to think about the question on my own.
