In the decision-theory and AI-safety literature there is a debate that has been running for over a decade without either side conceding: should an agent reason about its choices causally or acausally? The causal side holds that you should pick the act that maximizes expected utility given the ordinary causal structure of the world; the fact that a predictor, or a rival agent, is running a copy of your own decision procedure is just a correlation, not a lever you can pull, and reasoning as though you could influence it is a category error that happens to pay off in a few contrived puzzles. The acausal side, the so-called timeless or functional decision theorists, holds that the right unit of decision is not the single act but the decision algorithm itself: if your algorithm is the kind that takes only the opaque box in Newcomb-like situations, then every instance of that algorithm — the copy the predictor is running, the copy inside a causally isolated rival — will do the same, so the acausal correlation is a real feature of the payoff structure that a rational agent must price in, not a superstition.

The two sides agree on every ordinary problem in which your action affects the world only through normal causal channels, and they diverge only on problems with acausal correlation. Three standard examples: Newcomb's box; the smoking-meritocracy problem, in which a single gene makes you both smoke and get lung cancer, so that your decision not to smoke cannot change the gene even though smoking is still correlated with the cancer; and the acausal tradeoff, in which you can commit now to a rule about your future behavior in a way that affects a causally isolated agent who runs the same decision algorithm, because that agent's choices are fixed by the same algorithm.

I am trying to work out what kind of disagreement this actually is, because it has the texture of a stalemate in which everyone has already heard every argument. My working hypothesis is that it is really a disagreement about the unit of decision — one side evaluating a single act, the other evaluating a policy or algorithm — and that once you make that explicit, most of the arguments either dissolve or reattach to a different question. Is that the right diagnosis, or is it a more substantive metaphysical disagreement (do acausal influences really exist, or are we just talking about correlations), or a verbal one about what "rational" even means, or a disagreement about which of the two families of problems counts as a legitimate decision problem in the first place — with each side quietly treating the other's examples as degenerate or ill-posed? And is there a hidden formal question doing the work, for instance whether there is a well-defined class of problems on which the two theories provably diverge and on which one is unambiguously better?

Diagnose it using what each side actually argues rather than the labels attached to the positions, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction that dissolves one side's central objection, a formal result that pins down the divergence, a reframing of the question — or argue that no progress should be expected, and why.
