I've been working on a derivation for a seminar on origin essentialism — the claim, usually associated with Kripke, that you could not have come from a different sperm and egg than the ones you actually came from — and I've started to suspect it quietly proves more than I intended. I can't isolate the step that's responsible.

The argument:

(1) Suppose, for the sake of argument, that I could have come from a different sperm and egg.

(2) Consider a specific case: my actual mother and father have a child a month later, from an egg and a sperm different from the ones that produced me.

(3) That later-born child has a different genetic makeup from me.

(4) If a person has a different genetic makeup from me, then that person is not me. (My personal identity is fixed by my physical, genetic constitution.)

(5) So that later-born child, produced by the very same two parents, is not me.

(6) Therefore I could not have come from a different sperm and egg of the same parents — that is, even my actual parents, at a different time, could not have produced me.

The conclusion I actually want to defend is the weaker one: that I could not have come from a different parents. But this argument delivers the stronger conclusion (6) — that I couldn't have come from a different pair of gametes even of the same parents — and I'm not comfortable committing to (6). It strikes me as too strong, and I suspect the argument is doing work I never authorized. Yet every step looks valid, and (6) is a direct consequence of (1)–(5).

So: which step should I reject? Explain why that step looked fine, and tell me whether rejecting it lets me keep the weaker claim (different parents) while giving up the stronger one (same parents, different time), or whether the two are locked together and the argument actually establishes neither on its own. I'd rather keep the weaker claim than the stronger, but I want to know what I'm paying for that preference, and whether the weaker claim has an independent route that doesn't run through the genetic-constitution principle in (4).

Open your answer with the single step you reject, stated in one sentence, before any explanation; then give the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate the argument.
