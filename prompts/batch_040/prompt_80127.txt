Write a sestina — six stanzas of six lines closed by a three-line envoi, using the standard end-word permutation — that stages the paradox at the heart of Aquinas's position on tyrannicide.

The six end-words, in the order they appear as the final words of the first stanza, must be: tyrant, crown, hand, law, body, good. Let their recurrence through the fixed permutation do real argumentative work: words that open in the tyrant's or the assassin's frame should, by the later stanzas, have migrated into the frame of public authority and the welfare of the whole community.

The poem must carry the following accurately enough that a medievalist would not wince:

The ruler is a genuine tyrant, not a merely harsh or unlucky king. His rule is a perversion of law, his regime a corruption of the office, and he governs for his own profit rather than for the common good. Recall Aquinas's distinction between the tyrant who seizes power without any title to it and the one who holds a lawful title but has corrupted the office, and let the assassin's case be strongest against the first, the outright usurper.

And yet, precisely because the ruler is so illegitimate, the intuitive verdict is that killing him is plainly justified — a rotten crown, a hand with no right to it. Build that intuition in the early stanzas, with the full moral force of it.

The turn of the argument is Aquinas's actual ruling, which the middle and later stanzas must pivot on: the right to put an evildoer to death is not a private right at all. It belongs to the one who has charge of the community's welfare, just as it belongs to a physician to cut off a decayed limb when he is entrusted with the health of the whole body. In the Summa's words, of the persons to whom the care of the common good is entrusted, "they alone, and not private individuals, can lawfully put evildoers to death."

The envoi must land the resolution: that the crown is rotten — that the assassin is right about the ruler's personal illegitimacy — does not transfer the right to kill to the private hand. The vacancy in the ruler's title is a defect in the office, not a license in the killer. The authority to strike is bound to public charge of the common good, and no private judgment, however correct, can seize it.

Hold the tension between the moral certainty of the act and the doctrinal prohibition on the actor, without collapsing into either a defense of the assassin or a pious refusal to see the tyranny. A reader who knows the doctrine should be able to tell, from the poem alone, that Aquinas's objection is not that the tyrant is legitimate, but that the right to kill belongs to the public rather than the private person.
