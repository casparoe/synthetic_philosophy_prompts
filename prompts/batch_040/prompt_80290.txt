I'm building a probability module for an on-call incident-response tool, and I want to pin down the math behind a heuristic the team relies on constantly: "two independent signals pointing at the same root cause is far stronger evidence than one." I want you to construct a concrete, realistic scenario — something an SRE team could actually face, no exotic physics, no perfect predictors, one paragraph — in which this heuristic, formalized as a naive independence assumption, badly overstates the posterior probability that a hypothesis is true.

The scenario must have exactly this structure. There is a hypothesis H with a low prior, P(H) = 0.1, that a recent deploy genuinely degraded the service for users in a particular region. There are two signals, E1 (an error-rate dashboard spike for that region) and E2 (a failing synthetic canary test that simulates a user in that region). The deploy bundled a service change with a change to the monitoring stack. Given H, the two signals are genuinely independent, each a separate noisy manifestation of the same real degradation, so P(E1 ∧ E2 | H) = P(E1|H)·P(E2|H). Given ~H, the two signals are not independent: the monitoring-stack change, shipped in the same release, can on its own make both the dashboard falsely spike and the canary falsely fail, so P(E1 ∧ E2 | ~H) > P(E1|~H)·P(E2|~H).

Use these numbers: P(E1|H) = 0.9, P(E2|H) = 0.8, P(E1|~H) = 0.1, P(E2|~H) = 0.1, and the true joint under ~H, P(E1 ∧ E2 | ~H) = 0.06.

Then deliver the following:

1. Compute P(H | E1 ∧ E2) two ways. First the naive way, assuming E1 and E2 are independent under both H and ~H. Second the correct way, using the joint 0.06 under ~H. Show the likelihood ratio and the final posterior for each.
2. State the exact conditional-independence assumption, as a formal statement, that the naive calculation smuggles in. Then identify which of the two conditionals — given H or given ~H — the "presumption of independence" is actually defensible about, and explain why.
3. Name the single structural feature of the case that does the work, and state what happens to the two posteriors if that feature is removed (i.e., if the monitoring-stack change cannot produce correlated false alarms).
4. Write a short paragraph on how a defender of the independence heuristic would attack this case — for example, on the grounds that the 0.06 joint is unjustified or unobservable, or that the presumption is a reasonable default when no correlation is known — and say which part of that attack is the strongest.
5. Generalize. State a condition, in terms of the causal structure that generates each signal (not in terms of whether the agents or instruments reporting them are independent of each other), under which it is safe to multiply the two likelihoods when combining the signals.

The point is a designed case that isolates one specific failure mode of one specific heuristic, with the arithmetic shown, not a general essay on Bayesian reasoning or on confirmation.
