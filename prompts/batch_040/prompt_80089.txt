I keep coming back to this thing about whether it's bad to bring a person into the world who would suffer, versus whether its fine to just not bring into the world a person who would have been happy. You know the asymmetry, the one Parfit and the population ethics crowd keep talking about — roughly, a life that's net bad is a reason not to create it, but a life that's net good isn't something you've failed by skipping, since nobody's actually missing out. Anyway I've been running some concrete cases through my head and I want to know if there's a principle behind my gut calls or if I'm just being inconsistent.

Here's where I land:

- Someone could have a kid who'd grow up happy and comfortable, no real suffering. They don't want kids. I think they're totally fine to just not have them. Nobody's wronged.
- Same person, but the kid would very likely have a brutal life — a severe illness, alot of pain, probably short. I think they'd be right, even doing a good thing, to not bring that kid into the world.
- Flipping it: someone who does have a kid who turns out happy and pain-free. I think that's a good thing, a real plus. No mystery there.
- Now the one that bugs me. A kid who'd have a life that's net good — happy overall, people who love them, a life worth living — but who'd also carry a chronic painful condition, like a joint disease that aches every single day, manageable but never gone. The parent is leaning toward not having the kid because of the daily pain. My gut pulls two ways. On one hand, sparing them that pain feels like a real good, so not having the kid is defensible. On the other hand, the kid's life would've been genuinely worth living, and it feels a little callous to just skip an entire worthwhile life over the pain. I can't tell which pull is the right one.
- And the mirror case: actually not creating that same net-good-but-painful kid. I feel like that's defensible too, since you're preventing real suffering. But then I notice I just said the kid's life was worth living, so how can both "bringing them is good" and "not bringing them is also good" come out true? One of those has to give, I think, but I don't know which.
- One more, to test the waters: a kid who'd have a net-good life where the hard parts are the kind that seem to make the life what it is — the grind of a demanding career, the hardship of raising kids in a tough place, the sort of struggle a person would look back on as part of why the life was worth it, not as a bug. For this one my gut is clear: bring them into the world. The pain isn't really pain in the relevant sense, or at least it's the kind that's worth it.

So: is there a principle that gets all of these, or at least most of them? I'm fine with the answer being "you have to drop one of your verdicts" — just tell me which one and why. And if the principle that saves everything needs some fiddly extra clause, that's fine too, say what the clause is and which case forces it. I'd also like to know what the principle says about a case I haven't listed: a kid whose life would be net good but includes a stretch of really bad temporary suffering they'd fully recover from, like a childhood cancer that gets cured. Does the principle say create them, skip them, or is it a wash?

And when you're done, be honest about how confident you are in each part of your answer, and single out the one point you're least sure about. I'd rather have you flag where you're guessing than sound more settled than you are.
