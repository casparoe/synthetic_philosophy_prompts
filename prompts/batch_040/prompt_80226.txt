In my AP Government class we just finished a unit on free speech, and one of my study partners sent me her summary of Mill's harm principle and what it implies for censorship. She wrote: "Mill's harm principle is the idea that the only legitimate reason for the government to override someone's will is to stop them from harming someone else. Everything that is purely self-regarding — your beliefs, your tastes, your private choices — is beyond the reach of the state, full stop. This carries over directly to free speech: since a mere opinion or argument can't physically injure anyone, no speech is ever 'harmful' in the Millian sense, so the government can never censor anything. That's why Mill is basically a libertarian, and why he's the founding father of the absolute free-speech position."

I suspect her account is inaccurate in places, but I'm not sure exactly where, because the first sentence strikes me as essentially correct and I don't want to reject the whole thing on that basis. Could you audit her summary for me and separate, as precisely as you can, (1) what is actually accurate, (2) what is a fair simplification a careful reader would accept, and (3) what Mill himself would push back on or regard as a distortion? I'm especially suspicious of two moves: the jump from the harm principle to the conclusion that no speech can ever be restricted, and the claim that Mill's defense of free speech is the same kind of argument as the harm principle. I've also read that Mill's real case for free speech is an argument about what a society needs in order to discover and preserve the truth, rather than an argument about my right to say what I want — is that the distinction her account collapses, and does her final conclusion that the government can never censor anything actually follow from the parts of her summary that are correct? If it does not follow, tell me which specific piece of Mill's argument she is leaving out, and why that piece changes the answer.
