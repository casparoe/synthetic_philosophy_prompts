I keep getting the same question in office hour from students in my philosophy-of-spacetime seminar: is Gödel's 1949 rotating-universe paper a devastating argument that the passage of time is not real, or is it a trivial restatement of what special relativity already shows about the relativity of simultaneity? I want a handout that sorts students by how they actually evaluate the argument, not by which label they reach for, and I've landed on a taxonomy that doubles as a decision tree.

Please build a taxonomy of the positions on the question "what, if anything, does Gödel's rotating-universe argument establish about the reality of time's passage?" as a decision tree: the branch nodes should be the diagnostic distinctions, and the leaves the named positions. Build the branches out of the logical space first, then locate the actual positions in it. The branch nodes must be generated from, and explicitly test, at least these three cases. (1) The modal-bridge case: suppose the Gödel solution were the only solution of the field equations admitting closed timelike curves, and it required features the actual universe plainly lacks, namely a negative cosmological constant and a globally rotating homogeneous dust; does the argument still have any force about our universe, or does it collapse to the weaker claim that general relativity is merely compatible with timelessness? (2) The SR-redundancy case: strip the Gödel universe down to its relativity-of-simultaneity content, which special relativity already has, and remove the closed timelike curves; does the conclusion that the passage of time is unreal survive, or was the closed-timelike-curve structure doing all the real work? (3) The A/B-series case: in a Gödel universe, earlier-than and later-than relations are locally available but fail to order all events globally; is that enough to show the passage of time is unreal while leaving a partial B-series intact, or does the loss of global ordering collapse temporal structure as such?

Present the result as a table with these columns, one row per leaf position: **Position** (a short name for the leaf); **Modal bridge** (does the reading take mathematical possibility, i.e. being a solution to the field equations, as sufficient for the argument's force, or does it require physical realizability?); **Work done by the CTCs** (does the reading hold that the closed timelike curves do genuine work beyond special relativity's relativity of simultaneity, or that SR already does all the work and the curves are merely illustrative?); **Target of the argument** (is it read as targeting only the passage or flow of time, the A-series, while leaving B-series relations intact, or temporal structure as such?); **Scope of the conclusion** (is the conclusion taken to be about the actual universe, or only a weaker claim about what the laws do or don't build in?); and **Most awkward commitment** (one sentence naming the hardest thing this position must swallow). At least these six positions must appear as leaves: the strong Gödelian eternalist who thinks the argument establishes the unreality of time's passage and the block universe; the SR-redundancy reading (the standard argument from relativity of simultaneity to the block universe, often called the Rietdijk–Putnam argument, with Gödel's paper as at best a dramatic illustration); the physical-possibility or modal-bridge skeptic who thinks the Gödel universe is a mathematical curiosity rather than a genuinely possible world; the A-series-only tenseless theorist who concedes the passage is unreal but insists B-series relations survive; the presentist or growing-block defender who treats the argument as at best a conditional about rotating universes; and the "the argument is a wash" position that it shows compatibility but forces no metaphysics. You may add a leaf or two if the logical space genuinely calls for one. Before the table, give the branch order in a few lines so it reads as a decision tree rather than a flat list; after the table, note any unoccupied cells in the answer space, i.e. combinations of answers no position actually takes, with a one-line diagnosis of why (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored), and flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot actually separate. Write in American English.
