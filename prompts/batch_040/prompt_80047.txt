I am a novelist, and I have been trying to pin down what I actually mean when I call a thing a work of art, because I am not sure my usage is consistent. Here is how my gut reacts to a set of cases. When I say my own novel is a work of art, I do not mean the dog-eared proof in my drawer: the same novel exists in a thousand copies and in a database, and I regard all of them as one work, so the 'work' must be something other than any particular physical object. A porcelain urinal that an artist signed and set in a gallery is, I think, a work of art — not because it is beautiful, but because someone has made a claim with it, and the artworld has accepted the claim. A wave breaking on a beach is not art, even though I would happily call it beautiful; nor is a coffee ring on a page that, by accident, resembles a face. A competent but forgettable thriller is still a work of art in the sense I mean, though I would not call it a great one; but a string of sentences assembled merely to satisfy a checklist of 'literary' devices, with nothing to say, is not art at all — it has the shape of art without the act. And an image that a machine has generated, which I find moving but which no human composed, is not art in the way I mean.

I have a tentative principle, which I am not sure I can defend: something is a work of art when it is an abstract work that can be instantiated in many tokens, and when a human has made or designated it with some interpretive claim — so that it is about something, it means something — and when the artworld, loosely, accepts it as such; beauty and craft are not required, but a genuine human 'aboutness' is. Does this principle capture all of the verdicts above? If not, state it — or a simpler rival — precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which one I should be the first to relinquish. I am most stuck on the pair that troubles me: the signed urinal, which I count as art on the strength of a bare designation, and the empty checklist text, which I do not count as art even though it, too, was published and reviewed as literature. What separates the two without simply stipulating the answer? And what does the resulting principle say about a case I have not raised — a beautiful object that a human made entirely by accident, or a novel that a human wrote but that no one has ever read?
