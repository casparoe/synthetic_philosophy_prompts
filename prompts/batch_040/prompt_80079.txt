I am a retired evolutionary ecologist, and it is only in the last couple of years that I have begun to take philosophy seriously. One idea I keep returning to is what I am calling a commitment race: a contest in which two parties each try to out-commit the other by making the most costly and most irreversible pledge, on the theory that the side that appears most committed will force the other to blink first. In my own field we study evolutionary arms races, in which each species' adaptation raises the stakes for the other, and a commitment race strikes me as the strategic cousin of that dynamic. What unsettles me is that I was trained to treat flexibility — the willingness to revise one's position in light of new evidence — as a virtue, yet in these contests the rational move often looks like binding your own hands so that you cannot take a pledge back.

The small philosophy reading group I have joined has been ruling on whether various real-world pledges are wise commitments (worth making) or self-defeating ones (ultimately harmful to the person who makes them). Its calls so far: a parent who tells a toddler "no sweets before dinner, no exceptions" and genuinely means it — wise; a government that declares it will never negotiate with terrorists under any circumstances — self-defeating; a retailer that offers a standing guarantee to match any competitor's price — wise; a negotiator who publicly fixes a walk-away price and refuses to go below it — wise; and a state that publicly vows to honor every treaty it signs, even at great expense — wise.

The new case before the group is this: a small country, hemmed in by a far larger and more aggressive neighbor, issues a public pledge that it will defend every inch of its border to the last soldier and will never accept any adjustment of its frontiers, whatever the military odds.

I would like your help working this out, since I am still learning the vocabulary. I suspect the group's five earlier calls are consistent with more than one underlying principle; it feels to me as though one principle would be that a pledge is wise only if you would still want to keep it when the moment of truth arrives, while another would be that a pledge is wise if it changes the other party's behavior enough to be worth its cost, even if you would personally regret keeping it. Can you lay out the candidate principles that actually fit all five calls, say what each one predicts for the small country's pledge, give me your best guess at which way the group will rule and why, and name one further case that would genuinely tell the principles apart?
