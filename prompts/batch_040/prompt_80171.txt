I'm writing a long-form essay for a general audience on whether "DNA is a code" is a good metaphor, and I'm stuck on its most troublesome part: the origin and status of the standard genetic code itself, the mapping from the 64 three-nucleotide words to the 20 amino acids. I want to organize the positions on "is the standard code a product of evolutionary selection, or something else?" into a taxonomy my readers could actually use to locate themselves. Here are the facts I'm treating as settled and want the taxonomy built on: the code is near-universal across all domains of life; a small number of organisms, including mitochondria, some yeasts, and some ciliates, use altered versions of it; the code has a striking error-minimizing structure, in that codons differing by a single nucleotide tend to specify the same or chemically similar amino acids; and the code is functionally portable in the lab, as shown by the early-1980s transgenic mice driven by human or rat growth-hormone genes to grow to roughly twice the size of their siblings. I know Crick's 1968 "frozen accident" argument, the idea that any reassignment of a codon would be so catastrophic that the code we have is simply the first one that happened to work, but I'm not sure how much of it still stands, and I suspect different people in my target audience would reject it for different reasons.

Please build the taxonomy as a decision tree in which each branch is labeled by the claim that separates it from its siblings, for example whether the error-minimizing structure of the standard code requires an adaptive explanation, whether the code can change without the lineage dying, and whether the codon-to-amino-acid mapping is even a natural unit that selection can act on. I want at least six leaves, and they must include: the frozen-accident or neutral-drift view; the selection-for-error-minimization view; a stereochemical or coevolutionary view in which codons and amino acids were matched by chemical affinities; a hybrid view in which the early assignments were accidents but the later structure was shaped by selection; and a deflationary view that the question is a category mistake because the "code" is not a unit that evolves as a whole. Give each leaf a name, or "unnamed" if nobody defends it cleanly, plus one sentence stating its most awkward commitment, the fact it has to explain away. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first the branch questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then the actual positions located in that space, with the empty cells noted and a brief diagnosis of why nobody sits in them, whether incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Where two leaves genuinely cannot be separated by the branch questions, flag that rather than papering over it.

Finally, I want the response to separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this. The existence of altered codes and the error-minimizing structure of the standard code are settled; whether that structure is adaptive is genuinely contested, and I do not want either the frozen-accident view or the fully adaptive view presented as a consensus. Where the taxonomy itself is a judgment call, such as which positions are genuinely distinct rather than repackagings of one another, commit to a choice and defend it, and say explicitly where you are less sure. No reading lists or bibliographies; I want the argument and the map, not pointers to where to find them.
