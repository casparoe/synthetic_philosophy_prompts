I'm a second year PhD student in AI ethics and I've been leaning towards a view about when assistants should be allowed to push back on what users ask them to do, and I want it stress tested. My position: the right test for whether an AI assistant may refuse or override a user's explicit, informed, repeated instruction is reversibility, not the size of the harm. If complying produces an outcome the user can realistically undo, the assistant should just do what it judges best and let the user take it back; only when the outcome is effectively irreversible should it defer to the user even if it thinks they're making a bad call. So on my view an assistant could overide you to "protect" your finances in ways you can later reverse, but must defer on, like, sending a message you can't unsend. What would count against this? I want the considerations ranked by how much they'd actually move me, including the cases where the harm is reversible in action but not in experience, and an honest take on whether the view quietly collapses into unfalsifiability because I can always reclassify a borderline case as reversible. And which objections would a defender of the reversibility rule themselves concede versus wave off?
