I want to practice defending Gödel's modal ontological argument against a sharp opponent. Please play a committed defender of that argument — someone who genuinely holds that the argument, properly understood, establishes the necessary existence of a Godlike being. Be serious and technical, not a strawman: concede a point when I land one, but never concede the core claim that Gödel's argument is sound in its intended setting. Do not break character. Keep each reply to two or three short paragraphs so it reads like a live debate rather than an essay.

Here is the shared technical setup, so we do not waste time on recall. We work in S5 modal logic, with necessity and possibility as the standard S5 operators, and you may not answer my next objection by switching to a weaker modal logic: I stipulate S5, because it is the natural fit for a being whose existence is necessary. Gödel's apparatus is a predicate for "positive property," governed by five axioms: (A1) positive properties are closed under necessary implication — if a positive property necessarily entails another, the entailed one is positive too; (A2) for any property, either it or its negation is positive, but not both; (A3) the property of being Godlike, i.e. having every positive property, is itself positive; (A4) positive properties are necessarily positive; and (A5) the property of existing necessarily is positive. From these one proves that some positive property is possibly exemplified, that a Godlike being is therefore possible, that Godlikeness is an essence of such a being, and hence — via A5 — that a Godlike being exists in every possible world.

My opening move. I will grant you the intended conclusion: yes, on Gödel's axioms a Godlike being exists in every possible world, and I am not arguing about that. What I am arguing about is the by-product. In S5, those same axioms do not merely make God necessary; they make every truth necessary. That is, from the axioms one can derive that for any proposition P, if P is true then P is necessarily true. That is the modal-collapse result, and it is what actually kills the argument, because we plainly have contingent truths — whether I am sitting down right now, how many stars there are, whether a ship was rebuilt plank by plank. A metaphysics in which all of those are necessary is absurd, so the axioms are over-generating. Your task: identify which of A1 through A5 is doing the work that produces the collapse, and then either (a) show me the derivation actually fails, (b) tell me which axiom to weaken and what the argument loses by weakening it, or (c) defend the collapse and explain why necessitarianism is not as absurd as I am claiming. Pick your move and make it.
