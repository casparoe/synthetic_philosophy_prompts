I've been following the rationalist and EA discussions on the philosophy of pain and welfare for a while, and I've landed on a set of views that I'd like a consistency check on before I write them up. The case that's been driving my thinking is pain asymbolia — the condition, typically from damage to the right insula or adjacent regions, where a patient still has the sensory experience of pain but has lost the affective, suffering component. They feel the "what and where" of the injury but don't suffer from it. Here's where I've landed:

(1) I think pain is best analyzed as having three separable components, not the usual two: a sensory/discriminative component (carries information about the type and location of tissue damage; has a distinctive qualitative character), an affective/evaluative component (the aversiveness, the "this is bad," the suffering), and a motivational/propensity component (the drive to act, the urgency, the behavioral response). I think these can come apart, and I think that in pain asymbolia the affective component is lost but the sensory and — at least partly, at least reflexively — the motivational components are preserved. So the asymbolia patient still feels the "what and where" and still has some drive to act, but doesn't suffer.

(2) I think the negative value of pain consists entirely in the affective component. The sensory and motivational components, by themselves, have no negative value. I'd go further and say the sensory component is a good thing — it's the body's damage-detector, and a world without it would be worse, because we'd be walking around not knowing we're being damaged. The motivational component is likewise a good thing, because it's what drives protective behavior. But neither is a bad thing to have, by itself.

(3) I think the pain asymbolia patient is, in a relevant sense, better off after the lesion than before. They retain the diagnostic information (sensory) and the protective drive (motivational) without the suffering (affective). So the lesion is, from the patient's welfare point of view, a net benefit. I think we should be open to the idea that we could, in principle, have the information and the drive without the suffering, and that this would be a genuine improvement.

(4) I think the badness of (affective) pain is intrinsic and non-derivative. Affective pain is not bad merely because of what it causes (further suffering, impaired functioning, and so on); the painful experience itself is intrinsically bad, in the way that a bad experience is intrinsically bad and a good experience intrinsically good. I'm motivated by the intuition that pain is bad even in cases where it has no bad consequences — a pain that causes nothing, or a pain in a person who doesn't act on it.

(5) I hold an experience-based, or phenomenalist, view of welfare: a person's welfare at a time is a function only of the positive and negative phenomenal character of their mental states at that time. There is no "set-piece" value to non-experiential goods or bads. Facts about one's projects, relationships, or standing that one is not currently experiencing do not add to or subtract from one's welfare at that time.

Now, here's a case I want you to stress-test the set against: an asymbolia patient, because they don't suffer, keeps using their injured limb and doesn't take it seriously — the motivational component is only reflexive, not enough to drive sustained protective behavior like going to the doctor. The injury becomes gangrenous and the patient eventually dies. I think the patient is, in the end, worse off than they would have been if they'd had normal pain and thus gone to the doctor and been treated. I don't see why this verdict conflicts with (3) — (3) is about the patient's welfare at the time of the lesion, not about the long-term outcome — but I'm not confident.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are in tension and how badly. For each pair in tension, say how severe it is and what the cheapest fix would be, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I'd also like you to flag which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection, so I don't waste time worrying about them. And please be specific about the case: does my verdict on the asymbolia patient who dies actually follow from (1)–(5), or is it a verdict my principles can't deliver?
