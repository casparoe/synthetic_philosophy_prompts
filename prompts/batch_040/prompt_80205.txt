I'm working through the ethics of panpsychism and I keep coming back to what I think is a simple arithmetic point, but I want a careful answer because I suspect I'm missing something.

Suppose we grant, for the sake of argument, that the simplest physical entities carry some minimal experience, and that this experience is a state of suffering, or at least contains suffering as a component. The observable universe contains on the order of 10^80 baryons. Even if each one's suffering is vanishingly small — say a thousandth of a thousandth of a thousandth, or 10^-1000, of the amount of suffering a human experiences in a second — the total would still be an enormous negative value, and it would dominate over all the positive experience in the universe by many orders of magnitude. So it seems to me that once you accept panpsychism, the only defensible ethical conclusion is that the universe is overwhelmingly terrible, and that any moral framework that takes panpsychism seriously must collapse into a kind of cosmic negative utilitarianism.

Two questions. First, is there any version of the math or of the ethics in which this conclusion does not follow — for instance, by denying that the per-particle experiences are commensurable with macroscopic ones, or by treating the vast number of tiny experiences as somehow not adding up? And second, if the conclusion does follow, why does the panpsychism literature keep talking about consciousness as though it were mostly a positive or neutral phenomenon, rather than just saying so?
