I work in distributed systems — I design and operate a replicated database that uses Raft for consensus — and something that happened in a design review this week has been eating at me. One of our engineers wrote that a Raft cluster "forms a shared intention to agree on a log," and our tech lead tore into him for it, saying intention is doing no work and it's just anthropomorphizing a protocol. I got into it with a friend who's doing philosophy of action, and she said that's litreally the question philosophers of collective intentionality have argued about for decades: the difference between "we intend to do A" and "I intend to do A and you intend to do A and we both expect the other to do it" is the whole ballgame, and there are at least three distinct theories that disagree about what a "we" actually is.

I'm fluent on the systems side and basically a layperson on the philosophy side, so can you restate the three main theories in the vocabulary I actually use — quorums, leader election, state machine replication, split-brain, linearizability, and epistemic-logic common knowledge — one by one, as faithfully as you can? I'll seed you so we're talking about the same things: (1) the interlocking-intentions view, where my intention that we jointly do A has to be formed on the basis of my expectation that you intend that we jointly do A, and vice versa, with the whole arrangement supposed to be common knowledge; (2) the joint-commitment view, where what binds a group is a commitment that persists until the group dissolves it, and which generates mutual obligations and a right to blame; (3) the I-mode/we-mode view, where there are two distinct modes of thought and we-mode actions like "we carried the sofa upstairs" don't reduce to a bundle of individual I-mode intentions. For each, say what translates, what has to be distorted to fit, and what has no expression at all.

Then give me the residue report, and treat it as the interesting part, not the leftover: which parts of the debate map cleanly onto my vocabulary, which you had to fudge, and whether anything the philosophers are arguing about has no counterpart in how systems actually work — and is that because my vocabulary is missing something real, or because their disagreement is less substantive than it looks? I have two test cases I'd like you to run through the theories: last month we had a network partition and the minority side kept accepting writes until someone noticed — under each theory, what's the status of the "joint action" during a partition? And when we decommission a node, the cluster keeps working fine (we even hand out fencing tokens so the old node can't write again) — is that a withdrawal that dissolves the joint commitment, or does the "we" just persist without it? If one theory handles both cases better than the others, say so. Oh, and two housekeeping things: can you write the answer in American English? I read that way better.
