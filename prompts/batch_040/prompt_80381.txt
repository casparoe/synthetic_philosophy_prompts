I'm teaching a grad seminar in the philosophy of technology and innovation this spring and I'm writing the final, and I want one essay question on path dependence that a student can't answer by just reciting "QWERTY is a case of lock-in because of network effects and switching costs." The thing that keeps coming back to me is the tension between two claims that students always blur together: (i) history is "locked in," meaning the outcome is sensitive to small, early, almost arbitrary differences and is then maintained by increasing returns and switching costs; and (ii) the same outcome, or at least a functionally equivalent one, would have arrived anyway even without that early difference. Last week a student told me "well obviously a better keyboard would have won eventually, so QWERTY isn't really lock-in" and I realized I couldn't quickly explain why that's not obviously right, which is what pushed me to write this.

Here's the case I want them to work with, and I've stated the facts so they don't need to look anything up: QWERTY is the standard keyboard layout, designed in the late 19th century partly to slow typists down so the mechanical typewriter's arms wouldn't jam, and it persisted long after that design pressure disappeared because of the cost of retraining typists and the installed base of machines. It's the textbook example of path dependence. The question should ask them to explain (a) why the claim that QWERTY is path-dependent does *not* entail that QWERTY is inefficient, and (b) what the further claim that "a better layout would have been adopted anyway" does to the case — does it refute the path-dependence claim, weaken the case's force as an example of *harmful* lock-in, or both, and under what conditions. I want them to cleanly seperate the process claim (how we got here) from the evaluative claim (whether we're here for good reasons) from the counterfactual claim (would we have gotten here anyway), and to notice that the "would have arrived anyway" argument cuts two ways: in its strong form, where the exact same layout was inevitable, it actually undermines the path dependence, whereas in its weak form, where only a functionally equivalent layout would have won, it coexists with the path dependence but shrinks the welfare loss.

Please write the question itself (something a student could answer in about 75 minutes), a model answer of roughly 600–700 words that makes all of these moves, and a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves a top answer must contain, the marks each is worth, and a one-line note on how students typically get each one wrong. And since I'm going to use your model answer as the standard, close with a note on where your own reasoning is most likely to go wrong — which of the distinctions you drew is the one a sharp student could press, or what would change your mind about how the marking scheme should weight the points.
