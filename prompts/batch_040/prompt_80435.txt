I've been stuck on this argument among Mill scholars for a while and I can't tell what's actually being disputed, so I'd rather you diagnose it than pick a side. One camp says that in his last twenty years or so Mill effectively became a socialist: he got increasingly fed up with competitive industry and started betting his hopes on workers associations taking over production, so that the means of production would be held by collectives instead of individual capitalists. The other camp says that's way to strong — that Mill never abandoned individualism, that his cooperation stuff was just a slow, voluntary, reformist tweak to capitalism (no state takeover, no expropriation), and that slapping 'socialist' on it is mostly a labeling move. My working hypothesis is that a big part of this is verbal: both sides are describing roughly the same late Mill and just disagreeing about whether the destination earns the word 'socialism.' But I have a nagging feeling there's a real knot under the label, probably around whether his collectivist turn is even consistent with the 'individuality' he spent his whole career championing. So: is this a verbal disagreement, a substantive one, a mix, or two people answering different questions under one name? And given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would actually count as progress — some distinction that splits the issue, some feature of the texts that settles it, or is it just the kind of thing that isn't going to get resolved? I don't want a bio of Mill or a list of his late essays, just the shape of the dispute.
