I want your actual credence, with the reasoning decomposed, on a question about Skolem's paradox. I don't want a survey of the debate or a history of the positions; I want a number and the terms that produce it.

So that we're working from the same setup. In first-order set theory (ZFC), the claim that a set is countable is expressed by an internal formula: roughly, "there exists a function from the natural numbers onto this set." The downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem implies that if ZFC has any infinite model at all, it has a countable one. So there is a model M of ZFC that is countable by our external, meta-theoretic lights, and yet M satisfies the statement that its own set of reals is uncountable. The reason is that the bijections M would need in order to count its own reals are not themselves elements of M; they exist only in the larger universe from which we view M. The paradox is the apparent tension between these two claims: a structure that really is countable from the outside, yet that is internally committed to its reals being uncountable.

The question I want a number on: what is your credence that Skolem's paradox is a genuine, non-trivial philosophical problem for mathematical realism—the view that the set-theoretic universe V exists as a determinate, mind-independent structure—as opposed to either (a) a presentational confusion that dissolves once one carefully distinguishes internal from external properties of a model, or (b) a difficulty that realism answers trivially, or that applies with equal force to every interpretation of mathematics rather than singling out realism?

Give me a number between 0 and 1, then decompose it. I suggest you break it into at least these terms, though you may add others: (i) the probability that the apparent tension is a real philosophical tension rather than a confusion that evaporates on inspection; (ii) the probability that the standard resolution—distinguishing the object language from the meta-language, or internal from external countability—is actually available to a realist and acceptable to him, i.e., that the realist can make the move without being charged with begging the question; (iii) the probability that, once that distinction is drawn, it fully removes the tension rather than leaving a residual problem (for instance, about which of the many countable models is "the" intended model, or about whether "countable" has a determinate referent at all); and (iv) the probability that any such residual tension is a problem specifically for realism, as opposed to a problem for mathematics generally or for anti-realist views as well. Give a number and a one-line justification for each term, then show explicitly how the terms combine into your final number.
