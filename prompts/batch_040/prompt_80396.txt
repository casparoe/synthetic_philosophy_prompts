I'm a first-year PhD student, and this term I'm teaching the discussion sections for an upper-level seminar in normative ethics; the unit we're wrapping up is on the moral philosophy of promising. My instructor has asked each of us TAs to draft one essay question for the final, together with a model answer and a marking scheme, so that we can calibrate our grading. I'd like your help drafting that question.

The topic is why promises bind, but I don't want a question that can be answered by reciting the standard map of views — the promissory-obligationist position set against reliance- or no-harm-based accounts, with the usual names attached. I want students to be forced to apply those views to a case on which they give conflicting predictions, and then to say where each view breaks down. The case I have in mind is the unreliable, or compulsive, promiser: a person with a stable, well-known trait of promising to do almost everything she is asked but keeping only a small fraction of her promises, whom everyone in her community, including the particular promisee, knows to be this way. Because of this, the promisee does not genuinely expect the promise to be kept and does not alter her plans in reliance on it, even though the promiser makes the promise sincerely and intends to be bound by it. I'd like the question to run in two parts. Part (a): in this no-reliance base case, does the promise generate a moral obligation on the promiser, and what do a promissory-obligationist account and a reliance- or no-harm-based account each predict? Part (b): a variant in which the promisee, fully aware that the promiser is generally unreliable, nonetheless takes a risk and actually relies on the promise — cancelling other plans and committing resources — so how do the two accounts' predictions now change, and does the difference between the two cases track a genuine moral difference or is it an artifact of the theory?

Please deliver three things. First, the essay question itself, worded so that a prepared student could answer it in seventy-five minutes. Second, a model answer of roughly six hundred words that works through both parts and lands on a defensible, argued verdict rather than a bare assertion. Third, a marking scheme out of twenty that separates an A from a B by naming the specific moves an A answer must make — for example, correctly applying each account to each of the two cases, identifying a genuine problem the case exposes for at least one of the accounts, and giving a reasoned verdict — as well as the specific mistakes that should cost marks, such as reciting the views without applying them to the case, smuggling in the legal doctrine of promissory estoppel as though it answered the moral question, treating the unreliable promiser's promise as void rather than as a question about the strength of the obligation, or failing to distinguish the base case from the variant.
