In modern physics, when studying phenomena at a given energy scale, physicists often work not with the most fundamental theory but with an "effective field theory" (EFT). An EFT is the most general set of terms consistent with the system's symmetries, arranged so that the leading terms dominate at low energy and higher-order terms are progressively suppressed. The coefficients of these terms are not predicted by the EFT; they are free parameters fitted to experimental data. And the low-energy EFT is largely independent of the details of any deeper high-energy theory: two very different underlying theories can give rise to the same low-energy EFT. What view of the nature of laws of nature does this practice presuppose? Does it commit us to the Humean view (laws are nothing over and above scale-relative summaries of the regularities that actually obtain, with no deeper "governance"), to the anti-Humean view (there is a fundamental law that genuinely governs the low-energy behavior, and the EFT is merely an approximation to it), or to something else? Do the different features of the practice — the scale-relativity of the laws, the fact that their form is fixed by symmetry, the fact that their coefficients are free parameters, and the decoupling from the deeper theory — all presuppose a single coherent view of laws, or do some of them pull in different directions? And what would have to change in the practice of EFT if the rival view were correct?
