I'm planning a term paper for a graduate political-philosophy seminar (one semester, roughly seven thousand words) and I'd like you to turn the following half-formed idea into a proper research-proposal document.

My working hunch is that the "boundary problem" — the question of who belongs to the demos — is usually posed as a single question with competing answers (territorial residence, civic citizenship, the "all-affected" principle, the "all-able-to-affect" principle), when in fact the field is conflating two distinct questions that have different correct answers. The first is a franchise question: who gets a vote, that is, who is on the input side of the demos? The second is a jurisdictional question: who is legitimately bound by the decision, that is, who is on the output side, and whose interests must be accounted for even if they do not vote? I think the "all-affected" principle is being misapplied when it is used as a franchise criterion; it is really a jurisdictional principle. Read that way, the standard "unbounded demos" objection dissolves: the unbounded set is the set of affected persons (a requirement on the legitimacy of the decision), not the set of voters (a requirement on the franchise), and a legitimate political community is one in which the jurisdictional boundary is a superset of the franchise boundary, the gap being handled by representation or accountability rather than by expanding the electorate.

Please write the proposal so that it: (1) states the question precisely, in a form that has a determinate answer; (2) explains why the existing positions — the territorial, the civic, the all-affected, and the all-able-to-affect — each leave this open, and in particular why the all-affected and all-able-to-affect principles look as though they conflict when, on my reading, they are answering different questions; (3) lays out the method, that is, the specific conceptual moves I would make to establish the decomposition: which standard arguments I would reconstruct and press, which puzzle cases I would test the decomposition against (for instance, non-resident workers affected by a domestic tax, transboundary environmental regulation, and the status of residents who are not citizens), and what I would do if the decomposition fails; (4) says what a successful result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and (5) gives an honest scope assessment for a one-semester paper, including what I would have to cut to keep it feasible.

Two constraints on how you write it. First, keep it answerable without any external sources: do not ask for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; refer to positions and arguments by their content, and I will supply the references myself. Second, be careful about epistemic status: where you characterize the existing positions, separate what is genuinely settled in the literature from what is itself contested among people who work on the boundary problem, and do not present a contested reading of any of these principles as though it were the consensus. Please write in American English.
