I am co-organizing a small workshop that finally sits down two camps in archaeology and the history of historiography that have been talking past each other for decades, and I would like you to draft, in the voice of a neutral facilitator who is also a professional philosopher of science, the single joint document the two sides would be expected to sign before the sessions begin. The first camp, which I'll call the collapse camp, holds that "collapse" names a real, identifiable, and recurring phenomenon in the human past — a rapid, large-scale, and largely irreversible loss of a society's population, political complexity, and material culture, driven by internal and external stresses — and that the Classic Maya lowlands, the eastern Mediterranean in the Late Bronze Age, and Rapa Nui are, in their different ways, genuine instances of it. The second camp, which I'll call the transformation camp, holds that "collapse" is not a natural kind but a value-laden, state-centric, and Eurocentric label that projects a modernist expectation of uninterrupted growth and continuity onto societies that never held that expectation; what actually occurred in those cases is better described as transformation, adaptation, or reorganization, and the word "collapse" obscures more than it reveals.

Please keep the empirical base at the level of what is broadly uncontroversial in the field — for example, that the southern Maya lowlands saw major population decline and the abandonment of many cities in the eighth to tenth centuries while Maya-speaking peoples and Maya polities continued and flourished elsewhere; that the eastern Mediterranean around 1200–1150 BCE saw widespread destructions and the disappearance of the Hittite state while neighboring cultures continued in changed form; and that Rapa Nui experienced deforestation and population decline without being depopulated — and where a particular find or interpretation is itself disputed, flag the dispute rather than asserting a specific result. You do not need to cite any sources.

The document should have four parts. First, a numbered list of claims both camps can sign, including claims about what the three cases do and do not show. Second, the precise statements of where the two sides still disagree, with each side's position phrased in wording that side would actually accept. Third, the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side — and for each crux, a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides would agree bears on it, together with each side's prediction about it recorded in advance. Fourth, a short section stating which part of the dispute, if any, is merely verbal, and why. Before you commit to these four parts, begin with a brief brainstorm of the conceptual levers that separate the camps — for instance the unit of analysis for a "society" (polity, culture, population, or region), whether "collapse" is intelligible only relative to a counterfactual baseline of expected continuation and, if so, whose expectation supplies that baseline, the degree to which the term is value-laden, and the problem of a society's identity over time — and then use that brainstorm to decide which levers actually function as cruxes. Please think carefully throughout, and make sure no claim in the signed section is one that either camp would find slanted.
