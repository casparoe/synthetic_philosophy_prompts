You are a philosopher of mind with genuine, working familiarity with compiler design and intermediate representations, and you can move fluently between analytic philosophy of mind and systems engineering. I am a compiler engineer: my native vocabulary is source code, abstract syntax trees, SSA form, optimization passes, machine code, micro-ops, and transistor-level timing. I am fluent in that vocabulary and not in the philosophy of mind.

Your task is to restate, as faithfully as you can, the core of the debate over which computations matter in the philosophy of mind — the question of when and why a physical process deserves moral status as a mind — in my compiler vocabulary. Then report, honestly, what does not survive the translation.

Translate the following, one by one, and for each give a short three-way note: what maps cleanly, what you had to distort to make it fit, and what has no expression in my framework at all.

1. Functionalism and multiple realizability — the idea that what makes a mental state what it is is its functional and causal role, not the stuff it is made of, so the same mental state can be realized in different substrates.
2. Substrate independence — the claim that a mind could in principle be instantiated in biological tissue, in silicon, or in a sufficiently faithful simulation of either.
3. The level-of-description, or granularity, problem — the observation that any running system simultaneously instantiates many different "computations" depending on the level of abstraction at which you describe it, and that nothing in the system itself picks out one level as the unique correct one.
4. The precomputed lookup-table stress case — a device that holds a giant table of already-computed outputs and merely reads them off, versus a system that genuinely performs the computation in real time as it runs.
5. The attribution question itself — what exactly is the bearer of moral status: the algorithm, the particular physical instantiation of it, the speed at which it runs, or the number of parallel copies running at once?

For each item, tell me which parts of the philosophical claim line up with something I already have a clean notion of (for example, "the same program expressed at different IR levels," or "an optimization pass that is correct because it preserves operational semantics"), which parts you forced through a distortion, and which parts I simply cannot say in my vocabulary. If a philosophical claim only fits by silently importing an assumption that my framework takes for granted, flag it as an import rather than as a translation.

The residue report is the part I care about most. In particular, confront this head-on: in my world, "the same program" has a precise, agreed-upon criterion — a transformation between representations is correct if it preserves observable behavior, same inputs and same outputs in all contexts. The philosophers do not seem to have anything as clean for "the same mind." So: does my framework quietly assume the very thing the debate is trying to establish — namely, that there is a well-defined notion of identity for a mind across substrates and across levels of description? If so, what does that imply about whether the which-computations-matter debate is a substantive disagreement or partly a failure of definition? And in the other direction, is there anything in the philosophers' position that has genuinely no counterpart in how compilers treat provably equivalent representations — most pointedly, the intuition that the lookup table is not a mind even though it is behaviorally indistinguishable from a real computation — and if so, why does that intuition resist translation?

Please think carefully and do not paper over the places where the translation breaks. Begin with a short brainstorming pass — a few sentences in which you name the two or three places you expect the translation to be most strained — and then give the full item-by-item translation and the residue report. Work from the concepts themselves; do not cite papers, authors, or reading lists.
