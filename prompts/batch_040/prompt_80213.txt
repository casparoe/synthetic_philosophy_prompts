I'm a novelist and I've got a short story stuck in my head that I keep failing to write, so take a crack at it for me. Setup: near future, the courts have quietly killed retribution. After a long run of neuroscience-and-philosophy consensus, the law no longer asks what a criminal deserves — it only asks what it takes to keep people safe and to get the offender back on their feet, so just deserts, that whole you-did-a-bad-thing-so-you-should-suffer idea, is legally dead. The protagonist is a judge who privately thinks this is a catastrophe. She believes people really do choose and therefore really do deserve punishment — she's the compatibilist in the room, the Dennett side of the Dennett-vs-Caruso fight — but she can't say so in open court because the statute won't let her. Then a case lands on her desk where the new actuarial math says the offender should walk: low recidivism risk, already rehabilitated, nothing to gain from locking them up. But the crime was brutal and the victims family is in the gallery, and their lawyer is begging her to hand down the old desert-based sentence she technically cannot give. And the the defense attorney is a hard determinist, the Caruso side, arguing my client didn't freely choose any of this, so punishing them for desert is incoherent — they'd only accept the consequentialist measures. I want the story to live in the gap between those three: the judge who believes in desert but is forbidden to use it, the family who wants the desert the law won't give, and the defense who says desert is a phantom. And here's the bit I can't crack, the in-the-weeds part: I want the judge, somewhere in the middle, to have to ask herself whether her conviction that people deserve punishment is a moral truth or just her own retributive anger wearing a philosophy costume — because that's exactly the charge the determinist is leveling, and if she can't answer it, her whole side collapses. Keep it under 2,000 words, let the philosophical clash drive the plot instead of showing up as a lecture, and don't resolve it cleanly — I want the reader finishing it not knowing which side is right.
