I run the calibration desk at a small prediction market, and I need a written procedure my junior analysts can follow when we list mechanism events — events whose outcomes are produced by a process with a genuine, in-principle knowable probability, such as lottery draws, coin flips, dice, sports games, or the weather. We list a few dozen of these a week, a bad anchor can cost us real money when the market trades against us, and the final call on each listing has to be signed off by me, so the procedure needs to run in under ten minutes and end in a one-line recommendation I can act on. We keep getting burned in two directions: junior analysts override a known probability with their own feel (refusing to list a 1-in-38 number because it "looks cold"), and senior analysts won't move a probability when the evidence that the process is not what we think is thin but persistent — a roulette table that has just run an 11-red streak, where our traders insist we leave the odds at 18/38 because each spin is independent, and our in-house statisticians insist the streak is evidence about the wheel, not a sign that red is "due."

Please write the procedure. It should take a proposed mechanism-event listing and decide, in order: (a) whether there is a well-defined objective probability to defer to at all, or whether the event is really a judgment event (an election, a court ruling, a replication result) for which no such probability exists in the same sense; (b) whether the analyst's knowledge of that probability is good enough to anchor on; (c) whether the analyst's other evidence counts as what the philosophy of probability calls a defeater — evidence that the probability is not what they think it is, or that the event is not actually generated by the process they think — and, if so, how to treat it; and (d) when the procedure stops deferring and hands the decision to human judgment, with the reason written down. Ground it in the literature on the relationship between objective chance and rational belief: I know the core idea is that your degree of belief in an event should match its objective probability, conditional on the totality of what you know, unless you have some special evidence to the contrary, but I want the procedure to be honest about which steps are real constraints and which are judgment calls dressed up as steps, and to say explicitly where it will misfire. Then run it on three cases — a 1-in-38 number on a fresh, certified wheel; the same wheel after the 11-red streak; and a coin flip we are about to list, where the flipper is a magician known for trick coins and we have no way to inspect the coin before the flip — and for each, say whether the recommendation is the one the desk would prefer.
