Our county water board votes next Tuesday on whether the reservoir operator can switch to a new herbicide for blue-green algae control. The standard 28-day fish toxicity test shows no effect, but a 10-month freshwater invertebrate study showed a small population drop, statistically marginal, mechanism unknown; the manufacturer calls it a statistical artifact, the local environmental group calls it the tip of a bioaccumulation problem. The counterfactual is not harmless: last summer's bloom put microcystin in 40,000 people's tap water for three weeks and cost the district about $2M in bottled water and boil advisories. One board member wants to apply the precautionary principle strictly — a credible threat of serious harm plus scientific uncertainty is grounds for acting, not for waiting — and use it to kill the herbicide. Another says that version is a cheat: apply it to the status quo (do nothing, risk the bloom) and it flips, and a principle that has to give the same verdict no matter which option you treat as the default is either trivial or inconsistent. Draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before the vote: recommend approve (with what conditions and what trigger thresholds for stopping), reject, or something else, and actually grapple with that same-answer-either-way point rather than waving it away. And I dont want a both-sides memo that ends with 'the board should weigh these considerations' — pick a side, defend it, and flag where your judgment could go wrong.
