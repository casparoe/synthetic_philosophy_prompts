I'm in an upper-level philosophy of language seminar this term, and we've just worked through Kripke's Naming and Identity. I've been trying to explain to a friend why "water is H2O" counts as a necessary truth when we obviously only discovered it by doing chemistry, and to do that I've been leaning on the broken-telephone analogy the way most of us in the class do: the name "water" is fixed when someone first points at the stuff and calls it water, and from then on every speaker who learns the name and passes it to someone else is just relaying the tag down a chain, like a message in a game of telephone, with nobody in the middle needing to possess any correct description of what water actually is.

My friend took that picture and argued it does double duty for us: because the tag is relayed unchanged down the chain, the name is welded to that one substance at the original baptism and can't slide over onto some different stuff in any other possible world, so the identity "water is H2O" has to hold in every world even though we could only have learned it a posteriori. He's confident the analogy carries the necessity, not just the mechanism of reference.

I'm not sure it's licensed to carry that much. Can you lay out explicitly what maps onto what between the broken-telephone picture and the causal-historical account, then name the places the mapping breaks down and the features the telephone game quietly imports that the causal-historical chain doesn't have, and finally say whether the "necessary a posteriori" conclusion survives once those disanalogies are on the table — or whether the analogy was only ever explaining how reference gets handed along and was asked to do the modal work for free?
