A friend of mine who's grinding through a set theory course walked me into a corner last week and I still cant get out. He wanted to stress-test Zorn's lemma, so he cooked up a proof that every set is countable, and it looked airtight right up until I remembered Cantor's diagonal argument. Here's the argument as he wrote it. (1) Fix any set X and let C be the collection of all countable subsets of X, ordered by inclusion. (2) C is nonempty, since the empty set is countable. (3) Every chain in C has an upper bound in C: given a chain, its union is still countable and it contains every member of the chain, so the union works as the upper bound. (4) By Zorn's lemma, C therefore has a maximal element, call it M. (5) M is a countable subset of X. (6) If any element of X sat outside M, tacking it onto M would give a strictly larger countable subset, contradicting maximality. (7) So M = X and X is countable. (8) Taking X to be the reals, the reals are countable, which is flat out false.

I keep wanting to blame step (3), because "just take the union" is the one move I'd also have written, but I cant actually articulate what's wrong with it, and my friend gave up and just said trust me, it's (3). Can you pin down exactly which step breaks, why (3) looked fine to both of us, and what the chain condition in Zorn's lemma is really demanding that the union fails to provide? And if there's a cheap way to patch the argument so it still proves something true, what's the minimal fix and what do I give up by making it?
