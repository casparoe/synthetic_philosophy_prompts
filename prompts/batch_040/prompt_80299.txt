I'm taking a course in applied ethics this term and we just finished a unit on supererogation — the idea that some acts are genuinely praiseworthy even though they aren't required, the "above and beyond" cases. And I kept having a thought that didn't quite fit the framework we were given, which is that the obligatory/supererogatory line seems to move with the person doing the act rather than with the act itself. A paramedic who stops to treat a stranger at a crash is doing what she's required to do; a passerby with the same training who stops to do the very same thing is being noble but not obligated, and nobody would blame her for walking on. A parent feeding her child is failing a duty if she doesn't; a well-off stranger doing the identical thing is merely generous. A soldier who throws himself on a grenade to save his unit is meeting his duty, while a civilian who does the same is a hero. In every case the physical act and the outcome are roughly the same, but the moral standing flips from "you must" to "how admirable" purely on the basis of who is performing it.

I keep trying to pin this down and I can't. It feels related to the talk of role obligations or special obligations — the idea that your social position, or your relationships to particular people, generate duties for you that other people don't have — but I don't think that quite captures the specific thing, which is that a single act can be obligatory for one agent and only supererogatory for another, and that the boundary between the two isn't fixed by the act's content. If there's an established term for this configuration, what is it, and how exactly does it differ from role obligations, from special obligations, and from the ordinary supererogation/obligation distinction? And if there isn't one, I'd rather you coin a clear one than force-fit me onto the nearest existing label: define it, and tell me where its edges are. Does the phenomenon still hold when the role was freely chosen, as with the paramedic, versus something you're born into, as with the parent? Does it survive when the "obligation" is only a professional or contractual one rather than a genuinely moral one?

Be honest about how confident you are in whatever identification you give me. If you think this is a real, established concept, say so and name it; if you think it's something people describe but never actually bothered to name, say that plainly rather than dressing it up as a term. And tell me where your reasoning is most likely to go wrong, or what consideration would make you revise your answer.
