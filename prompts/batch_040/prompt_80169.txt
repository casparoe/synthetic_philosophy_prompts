I'm teaching a small graduate seminar (about eleven students) on the economics of growth and development, and this week we're working through the 2001 American Economic Review paper by Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson that uses European settler mortality as an instrumental variable for the quality of colonial-era institutions. I've asked students to submit short critical essays, and I'd like you to grade the one below the way I would: assign a letter grade (A–F), then give a point-by-point commentary in which you mark each substantive claim as correct, imprecise, or wrong and briefly justify each marking. I want you to be specific about which of the student's moves are genuinely problematic versus merely loose phrasing, and to say whether the essay as a whole shows real understanding of what the instrument is supposed to do.

Essay question: "Explain the identification strategy in the 2001 Acemoglu–Johnson–Robinson paper and assess whether settler mortality is a defensible instrument for colonial institutions. Your response must (i) state what the instrument is measuring and why it is correlated with institutions; (ii) explain the role of the first-stage regression and what the first-stage F-statistic tells you; and (iii) discuss at least one threat to the exclusion restriction and how the authors address it."

Student's essay: "The paper's strategy is to exploit the fact that European settler mortality was high in tropical, disease-prone regions and low in temperate ones. Where mortality was high, Europeans did not settle in large numbers, and the colonial powers set up extractive institutions to harvest resources quickly and ship them home. Where mortality was low, Europeans settled in large numbers, and the colonial powers set up institutions closer to those of the metropole, including property rights and the rule of law, because settlers had long-term stakes in the land. The authors then use settler mortality as an instrumental variable for the quality of colonial institutions. The first-stage regression is a regression of institutions on settler mortality, and the first-stage F-statistic tells you how strong the instrument is; a large F-statistic means the instrument is not weak. The main threat to validity is the exclusion restriction: settler mortality might affect today's economic performance through channels other than institutions—for example, by directly shaping today's disease environment and hence health and productivity, or by affecting the demographic and human-capital endowments of the population—in which case the IV estimate would be biased. The authors address this by showing that the instrument is uncorrelated with the error term in the reduced form, and by arguing that the colonial powers' choice of institutions was driven by settler mortality rather than by other factors. Overall, I think the strategy is convincing, because the instrument is exogenous and strongly correlated with institutions."

For the grade itself, treat (i)–(iii) as the rubric: a strong essay handles all three accurately, a weaker one fumbles at least one. If you conclude the essay is stronger or weaker than that rubric implies, or that my criteria are miscalibrated, say so plainly instead of just conforming to them.
