I keep running into a configuration in bidding wars and standoffs that I don't have a word for, and I'd like to know whether the field does. Two parties escalate — outbidding each other, raising demands, refusing to step back — and the escalation keeps going well past the point where the money or resources actually at stake are trivial. The tell is that the usual sunk-cost explanation doesn't fit: either party could have walked away at almost no material cost, and the amount already committed is small. What is actually doing the work, in the cases I've seen, is the relative or reputational stake — the refusal to let that particular opponent come out on top, or the refusal to be the one who blinked — so that conceding to the specific other party is the real loss, not the money spent. One instance: a property bidding war in which both bidders pushed far above the asset's value and the winner later said, plainly, that it was about not letting the other buyer have it. Another: a labor standoff in which both sides kept raising the stakes after the original grievance had already been settled, because backing down in front of the other side was the move neither could make. I know this sits somewhere near escalation of commitment, the sunk-cost fallacy, and war of attrition, but none of those seems to capture a motive that is relational rather than a loss of one's own. Is there an established term for escalation driven by the relative or reputational stake rather than by sunk cost? If so, what is it, and how exactly is it carved off from escalation of commitment, the sunk-cost fallacy, and war of attrition? If not, coin a term and define it, including its boundaries — at what point does it collapse into ordinary spite, ordinary face-saving, or a plain war of attrition?
