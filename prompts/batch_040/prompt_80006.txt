I run a small cause-prioritization consultancy and we take on a new cause proposal roughly every two weeks. Each one gets a two-hour workshop where my team — three people, none of us with a stats background — is supposed to score it on importance, tractability, and neglctedness and come out with a fund / fund-with-reservations / pass call that I and one of my partners have to be able to defend in a client meeting. Right now the process is basically vibes plus a spreadsheet, and I keep hitting the same failure modes. We score a cause high on neglectedness because almost nobody's working on it, but then it turns out the reason nobody's working on it is that the people who could work on it already tried and found it intractable, so "neglected" ended up being a proxy for "we don't know how to do this." And we score importance in whatever units the proposal comes in — QALYs, dollars of economic output, "units of alignment progress" — and then try to add them across causes, which is nonsense.

Can you write me a decision procedure my team can actually run in that two-hour slot? I want the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and the exact point where the procedure stops and hands the call to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Three things it has to handle. First, the neglectedness score has to seperate "few people are working on this because it's hard to even understand what the problem is" from "few people are working on this because the people who could work on it are already working on it and hitting a wall" — I think those are different and I want the procedure to catch the difference, not just count heads. Second, the importance score has to be doable without converting everything to one unit, because we're not going to fake a QALY number for a cause that's about something we can't quantify. Third, tractability has to tell the difference between a research bet, where the next step is "do more theory" and the payoff is option value, and an action, where the next step is a concrete intervention with a payoff curve we can at least sketch.

Then run it on two proposals. One is a two-year research program on a high-tail-risk AI safety problem where the importance is potentially enormous but the only "next step" is more theory and nobody knows if the theory will pan out — I think the procedure should be honest that this is a research bet with a tail-risk importance estimate, not an expected-value action, and score it that way even if that means a lower number than the importance alone would suggest. The other is a cause that's neglected only because it's embarrassing to talk about publicly, so the neglectedness is real but it's going to evaporate the moment we fund it and put our name on it — I think the procedure should flag that as a decaying score rather than a stable property.
