The most common way of defending majority decision — in a criminal jury, in a referendum, or in the newer practice of drawing several answers from a single AI model and taking the majority — is the wisdom-of-crowds picture. The idea is that any one judge is fallible, but if you pool enough of them the errors wash out, so the group lands on the truth more often than the best individual would. This is the intuition the Condorcet jury theorem formalizes, and it is the move people make when they argue that a large deliberative body is more reliable than any of its members, or that we should simply defer to the majority on questions of fact.

I'd like you to take that analogy — the crowd as a wiser version of the individual — seriously and audit how far it holds. Start with the correspondence table: what in the real institution plays the role of the independent voter, the competence threshold (each judge being more likely than not to be right), the assumption that there is a fact of the matter to be hit, and the majority rule. Then lay out where the mapping breaks, and tell me which of those breaks matter for the conclusion the analogy is being used to support and which are only cosmetic. I am most interested in the breaks that land in exactly the settings where the analogy gets deployed: a jury that actually deliberates, since the jurors talk to one another and so their errors may no longer be independent; a referendum on a policy question where it is not obvious there is a correct answer to be more or less likely to hit; and a crowd whose members draw on the same sources or share the same blind spots. On that basis, say whether the conclusion the analogy is carrying — the bigger and more independent the body, the more likely it is to be right — survives, or whether it only holds on a knife-edge of assumptions that real institutions do not meet.

Two final stress tests. First, what does the theorem actually say when the median judge is more likely than not to be wrong rather than right, and does the assumption that simply adding voters improves accuracy quietly reverse in that regime? Second, in the AI version the voters are repeated draws from a single model — same weights, same training data, same systematic biases — so they are far from independent. How much of the theorem's guarantee, if any, transfers to that setup, and is majority voting over the samples doing real epistemic work or mostly supplying the appearance of it? If the crowd picture turns out to be doing more load-bearing work than it can bear, end by sketching what a better account of when aggregating judgments actually improves accuracy would look like.
