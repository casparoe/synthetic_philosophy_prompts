I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy of language, working on deflationary theories of truth, and I've built an argument for a seminar that I now can't get past, because it ends in a conclusion I can't accept. Let me fix the vocabulary first, since the argument turns on three claims I want to keep distinct.

- The disquotational schema (DS): for every sentence S of the language, the biconditional "S is true" ↔ S holds.
- The uniformity condition (UC): DS applies to every sentence without exception, including sentences that themselves contain the word "true"; there is no carve-out for self-application, because DS is a schema rather than a single biconditional.
- The redundancy claim (RC): the predicate "true" contributes no property to the content of any sentence in which it occurs; equivalently, every occurrence of "true" can be eliminated by DS without altering the content expressed.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) Deflationism is the thesis that DS, UC, and RC jointly hold.
(2) By DS, for any fully spelled-out sentence S, "S is true" is equivalent to S.
(3) By UC, DS applies even when S contains "true," so each occurrence of "true" in a fully spelled-out sentence can be eliminated one at a time.
(4) So for any particular, fully spelled-out sentence, however many occurrences of "true" it contains, all of them can be eliminated without changing the content. I'm comfortable with everything up to here; this is just deflationism working as intended.
(5) Now let G be the single sentence: Every sentence I have ever asserted is true.
(6) G is a sentence of the language, and it contains an occurrence of "true."
(7) By RC, that occurrence of "true" in G must be eliminable — replaceable, via DS, by something that expresses the same content.
(8) But to eliminate "true" in G we would have to replace G with a single sentence that asserts, one by one, every sentence I have ever asserted — an infinite conjunction, which is not a sentence of the language.
(9) The case is worse if G is read as Every sentence I will ever assert is true, since then the list of conjuncts is not even fixed in advance, so no completed sentence, however long, could do the replacing.
(10) So the occurrence of "true" in G cannot be eliminated without either changing the content or failing to express it at all.
(11) So RC is false: in G, "true" is doing genuine, non-eliminable work.
(12) So deflationism — DS together with UC together with RC — is false, or at the very least must be abandoned in favor of the view that "true" picks out a real property.

The conclusion (12) is one I can't accept, because DS and UC are, for me, the heart of the position, and I don't want to throw them out over a single tricky sentence. I take the argument to be valid, and I can't tell whether the problem is that one of the premises is false or that one of the steps simply fails to follow. I'm most reluctant to give up the intuition that drives (8) through (10) — that in G, "true" really does seem to be doing work that nothing else in the sentence is doing — because that intuition is what gives the whole argument its bite. But I'm not sure that intuition is the right thing to protect, since I also want to keep DS and UC.

So: which step do you reject, and what does it cost you to reject it? In particular, I'd like to know whether the argument is valid with one false premise, or whether a step fails to follow — and if the latter, exactly where the reasoning slides.
