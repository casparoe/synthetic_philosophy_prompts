I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, so I'll describe our company's practices concretely and ask you to reconstruct what they implicitly assume.

Incident post-mortems at our company are "blameless" by policy: the writeup must attribute the failure to systems, processes, and incentives, and naming an individual as the cause is a process violation. Every action item in the post-mortem, however, must have a named individual owner, and owners are tracked to completion on a public dashboard. Our code tooling also computes a per-author defect rate from line-level blame history, our on-call system records a per-engineer "reliability score" from SLO burns, and both feed individual performance reviews and compensation. Our stated value is "ownership," and taking ownership of a failure is an explicit review criterion, even though the failure is officially not anyone's fault. The same incident is thus simultaneously a system failure with no individual cause and an individual performance datum, and the person who "owns" the failure is not the person who caused it.

I think there's a standing debate in social science about whether social phenomena should be explained in terms of individual agents or in terms of institutions and wholes — I've seen the terms "methodological individualism" and "holism" — but I'm not sure that's the right frame for what we're doing.

Please answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. Across the five points, cover: what view of social causation the blameless-post-mortem practice presupposes; what view the individual-scoring and review practices presuppose; whether the two can coexist coherently, or whether the org is quietly making a different split (for example, between explanation and responsibility); whether the practice is underdetermined in the sense that several theories could license it; and what would have to change in our processes if the company followed through on the holistic view rather than the individualist one.
