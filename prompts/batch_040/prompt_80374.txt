The Jain doctrine of many-sidedness has, in my experience, calcified into a slogan people deploy to end arguments: "Every standpoint is valid in its own respect, so nobody can really be shown to be wrong." I heard it again last week in a seminar on moral disagreement, where a colleague used it to refuse to adjudicate between two competing positions, treating the slogan as a settled fact about how truth works rather than a claim to be examined. I believe he has the actual doctrine badly distorted, but I can't say precisely where the distortion enters.

Taking that slogan as the object, lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making, from the trivial to the radical. For each reading, classify it as a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or an outright falsehood, and say whether that reading would actually license the refusal to adjudicate that your colleague was making.

I want the analysis to run on the technical structure of the doctrine rather than just the popular summary: the qualified predication (the "syād" qualification, often rendered "in some respect"), the requirement that a proposition carry conditions or limitations in order to be true, the range of qualified responses (affirmation, denial, both, and the inexpressible), and the distinction between the different standpoints (nayas) and the partial expressions (paryayas). Using that apparatus, say which of the qualified responses the slogan quietly collapses into one, and whether the doctrine's own machinery — the fact that each side is conditioned and limited — supports or undercuts the "all views are equally valid" reading.

Close by telling me which reading your colleague almost certainly means, whether the doctrine, properly understood, would let him use it the way he does, and what a genuinely many-sided response to the two positions in the seminar would have looked like.
