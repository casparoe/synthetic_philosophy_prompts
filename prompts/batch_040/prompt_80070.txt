So I'm building a little thought experiment for a philosophy seminar and I keep coming back to one practice that I think quietly assumes an entire metaphysics, and I'd love a second opinion on it. The practice is this: a gallery, an estate, or an insurer values and insures a work of art by its artistic value, not its material value -- a marble sculpture gets carried at, say, two million dollars even though that same marble as a raw block is worth a few thousand -- and then, when the piece is smashed or otherwise destroyed, there's this recurring question of whether the the underlying material can still be claimed on, sold, or insured seperately from the artwork that no longer exists. And while the piece still exists, people will sometimes try to insure both the artwork and the block of marble it's made of, as if they were two separate things sitting in the same spot.

What I want from you: what view of what's actually "out there" does that whole bookkeeping presuppose? My guess is it needs the idea that the sculpture and the marble are really two different objects occupying the same space at the same time -- that statue-and-clay thing where a statue and the lump of clay it's made of look like they should be two objects even though they're made of exactly the same stuff in exactly the same place -- and where the sculpture has properties, like being a work of art and being valuable and having that history, that the bare lump just doesn't have. But I'm not confident. Could a one-object view (the sculpture just *is* the marble, nothing over and above it) make the practice work too, or does it break it? And is the practice even consistent with itself -- if the sculpture just is the marble, then insuring both is double-counting the same thing, and if they really are two things, then why does the "marble" still exist and stay insurable after the sculpture is gone? What would actually have to change in how appraisers and insurers do their work if we swapped in the one-object view?
