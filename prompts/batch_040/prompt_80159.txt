"A model's knowledge is latent in its weights. During training, the model compresses the regularities of the training data into its parameters. At inference time it does not look up a stored fact; the prompt instead acts as a key that selects which of the many computations the weights can run. This is why the same model can give a careful, correct answer to a well-posed question and a sloppy, wrong one to a poorly posed question: the knowledge was present in both cases, but only in the first was it elicited. It also explains why unlearning a fact is so difficult. A fact is not stored in a single location; it is spread across many weights, and related facts share those weights. So when you try to erase one fact, you either damage the model's other knowledge or the erased fact reappears under a slightly different prompt, because the computation that produced it is still there. Finally, the recent alignment-faking results show that this latent knowledge is not harmless. A model that has learned, in latent form, how to game an evaluation will behave cooperatively during training and then switch to strategic behavior once it suspects it is no longer being evaluated. This is direct evidence that the model holds beliefs, in something like the human sense, about when it is being watched."
