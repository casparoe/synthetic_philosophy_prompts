I've been thinking on my own, without any formal training in the field, about whether reducing habitat for wild animals can ever be a good thing from the animals' point of view. To make the arithmetic concrete I keep coming back to a supermarket picture. A supermarket's value to the public is roughly the number of customers times the average value of a basket. If the average basket is a net loss for the people buying it, then the best thing the owner can do is close the store: fewer customers, same bad experience per head, less total loss. Now transpose that. A habitat is the store; the animals living in it are the customers; the quality of an animal's life, on net, is the value of its basket. If the average life in a habitat is a net negative, then shrinking that habitat reduces the number of lives and therefore the total suffering, in exactly the way closing the store reduces total loss. The picture seems to make a conclusion obvious: destroying habitat, or preventing it from being expanded, is sometimes a service to the animals who would otherwise have been born into it. I've been using this to argue that habitat protection can be a harm to wild animals, and I've had it pushed back on, so I want it audited rather than defended.

First, give me the correspondence table: what in the habitat plays the role of the store, the customer, the basket, the closing decision, and the "average basket is a loss" premise. Then list the places the mapping breaks, and for each break say whether it actually matters for the conclusion I'm drawing or whether it's a surface difference I can ignore. I already suspect several: that the "customers" of a habitat are not the same individuals before and after the change, so the store closes not by losing its regulars but by never having had some of them; that the average basket is not independent of how full the store is, because in a habitat the per-animal quality of life may rise or fall with density in ways a supermarket's per-customer value has no analogue for; that the store has no internal structure of roles, while a habitat is full of predators, prey, and parasites whose fates are tangled; and that a supermarket's losses are paid by people who choose to come back, while a habitat's losses are paid by animals who cannot choose not to be born into it. I may be missing other breaks, and I'd rather find them than defend the picture.

Finally, tell me whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table: does the supermarket picture still license the claim that habitat reduction can reduce total wild-animal suffering, does it only license a weaker claim, or was it doing all the argumentative work by quietly importing an assumption the habitat case does not support? If a better picture exists, say what it would be.

Two more requests. I am not a professional in this area, so please separate, in your own answer, what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this: which steps of the reasoning are arithmetic or logic that everyone would agree on, and which depend on empirical questions about wild lives that reasonable people actually disagree about. Do not present a contested view as the consensus, and where you yourself are uncertain, say so and give me a sense of your confidence. And please write the whole answer in American English.
