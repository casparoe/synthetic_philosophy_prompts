I'm on the advisory panel for a small grant that will fund one postdoctoral researcher for two years to work on the conceptual foundations of cognitive penetration — that is, the question of whether non-perceptual mental states (beliefs, expectations, desires) can directly alter the content or character of perceptual experience, as opposed to merely modulating it at a later, post-perceptual stage. The postdoc is a philosopher of perception with a strong background in epistemology and the philosophy of mind but limited familiarity with the empirical literature, so I want the project to be primarily conceptual rather than experimental. I've been bouncing the agenda around with a couple of colleagues and we keep landing on different questions, which is why I'd rather have a defended prioritization than a survey of the field.

There are two complications I think the project has to engage with up front. First, there's a live dispute over what even counts as penetration: one line of argument holds that mere causal influence of cognition on perception (attention, adaptation, perceptual learning) is not genuine penetration, while a competing line holds that if a cognitive state is the reason a perceptual state has the content it does, that is penetration even when the causal route is "early" and the experience is non-inferential. Second, the rise of predictive-processing and predictive-coding models of perception threatens to dissolve the question altogether: if every percept is partly constructed by top-down predictions, then "cognition affects perception" becomes trivially true, and the real issue gets reframed as one about the granularity of the boundary between "perceptual" and "cognitive" processing — a reframe that some think makes the traditional question a pseudoproblem and others think merely relocates it.

Given that, which open questions in this area matter most for a two-year conceptual project, and in what order? For each question you prioritize, I want to know why it is upstream of the others (how many downstream questions depend on its answer), how tractable it is for a conceptual rather than an empirical researcher, and what kind of evidence over the next couple of years would move it up or down the list. I'd also like you to say which of the usual claimed implications of a positive answer — for the science of perception itself, for the epistemology of testimony, for theories of perceptual content — you think are overrated or don't follow at all. Please structure your answer in the following sections, in this order: (1) "The shortlist" — the three or four questions you'd prioritize, each in one or two sentences (about 150 words); (2) "The ordering" — your ranking of those questions with a short justification for each (about 300 words); (3) "Why this order" — the explicit criteria you used (upstream-ness, tractability, neglectedness, downstream leverage) and the trade-offs between them (about 200 words); (4) "What would change it" — the specific evidence or argument that would move the ranking, and which of the standard implications you'd deprioritize and why (about 200 words).
