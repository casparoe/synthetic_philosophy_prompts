I'm a software engineer and I've recently gone down a rabbit hole on Zeno's paradoxes and supertasks, mostly because the question of whether a physical machine could actually "finish" infinitely many steps in a bounded amount of time keeps popping up in weird corners of how I think about computation. I know the standard Thomson's lamp setup: on at t=0, off at 1/2, on at 3/4, off at 7/8, and so on forever, and then you ask what state it's in at t=1. The answer I keep getting handed is "undefined, because the on/off sequence has no limit." The problem is I feel like that single sentence is quietly doing two different jobs and I can't tell them apart.

On one reading it's a purely logical point: even granting the lamp works perfectly, the infinite history just never fixes a unique state at the endpoint, so the supertask leaves a gap rather than a contradiction. On another reading it's a physcial one: the switch has to move faster and faster, needing unbounded acceleration (and probably unbounded energy) as t approaches 1, so no real device could actually complete it. Could you explain the difference between these two objections precisely? Specifically I want (a) why "no limit" by itself only buys you underdetermination and not impossibility, (b) whether the physical/energy objection is genuinely independent of the limit objection or just the same thing in disguise, and (c) how this lines up with Zeno's dichotomy — walking to the midpoint, then to the next midpoint, and so on — where the position does converge to the wall. Does having a limit there actually rescue the walk, or is the walk just as physically suspect as the lamp? I keep conflating "the math doesn't converge" with "the physics forbids it" and I really want to untangle that.
