Two people in my logic reading group got into it about Skolem's paradox and I genuinely couldn't referee it. Roughly what was said:

Dana: the paradox is that ZFC proves, if it's consistent, that it has a countable model, but it also proves there's no countable model of ZFC — so it proves a thing and its negation at once, which means the whole enterprise is incoherent.

Ravi: that's a misstatement. The real content is that a countable model M of ZFC contains a set — take its own power set of ω — that M believes is uncountable, while from the outside M (and that set) is countable. No contradiction, just the internal vs. external perspective.

Dana: ok, but then M is just wrong about its own sets, and I don't accept that as a resolution. 'Countable' should be a fact that doesn't depend on which model you're peeking from. If the answer flips with the vantage point, the word isnt doing any determinate work.

Ravi: countability is determinate — 'there's a bijection with ω' — it's just that that existential quantifier ranges over the functions that are in M, and the bijection that witnesses the external countability simply isn't one of them. We're each right relative to our own domain of quantification.

Dana: so is the upshot just that M is a smaller, impoverished copy of reality that's missing some functions, and the 'paradox' is really an artifact of first-order logic not being able to pin down the intended model? And what's the difference between this and properties that do transfer cleanly, like being finite or being an ordinal? Is the relativity a deep feature of the subject matter, or just a limitation of the formalism?

Can you go through it move by move and say who's right about which specific point — not just who wins overall — and be honest about how confident you are in your verdict and which single step you're least sure of?
