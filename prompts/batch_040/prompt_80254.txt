The etiological account of biological function — the "selected-for" or proper-function view associated with Millikan — is commonly introduced through an analogy to engineering: natural selection is a designer, a population is a factory, fitness effects are the specification, and an organ's function is what it was designed for. The heart's function is to pump blood because that is what selection built it to do, just as a watch's function is to tell time because that is what the watchmaker built it to do. The analogy is then pressed into service to license two conclusions: first, that function is a historical matter, fixed by what a trait was selected for and independent of what the trait currently does; and second, that this historical notion is the correct one, beating the rival "current causal role" or mechanistic account (the view associated with Bechtel and Neander), according to which a trait's function is the job it actually performs in the organism's present economy.

I want an audit of how far the designer analogy holds. Start with the correspondence table: what plays the role of the designer, the blueprint or specification, the intended function, the factory, the quality-control step, and the moment of "design time." Then list the disanalogies, and for each one say whether it matters for the two conclusions above or is just noise that doesn't bear on them.

Then test the analogy against three specific cases, and for each say what the designer picture would predict, what the etiological account actually says, and whether the gap between them is a real break in the analogy or a superficial one: (a) the giraffe's left recurrent laryngeal nerve, which loops under the aortic arch and travels down the neck and back up rather than taking a direct route — is the long path a design mistake, and what does that tell us about whether selection can really be a designer? (b) the mammalian middle-ear bones (malleus, incus, stapes), which are homologous to reptilian jaw bones and were co-opted from chewing to hearing — did the "designer" repurpose them, and can the etiological account even give a clean answer about what their function is? (c) a hemoglobin variant such as the sickle-cell trait, which was favored because carriers resist severe malaria but causes disease in those who inherit two copies — what is its function, and does the designer picture make that answer look right or wrong?

Finally, say whether the two conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table: does the etiological account still need the designer analogy to be plausible, or was the analogy doing all the argumentative work? And if there is a better analogy for what the etiological account is actually claiming — selection as something other than a designer — give it and show where it still breaks.
