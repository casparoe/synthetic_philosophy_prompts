For the final exam of my upper-level free will seminar, I need a single essay question, answerable in about 90 minutes, on Frankfurt-style cases and the principle of alternative possibilities (PAP). The question must be unanswerable by reciting a summary of the literature, and it must separate students who genuinely understand the issue from students who have memorized the labels.

Please ground the question in the following setup. Frankfurt-style cases are intended to refute PAP (the thesis that an agent is morally responsible for an action only if she could have done otherwise). The "flicker of freedom" strategy responds to Frankfurt's original case by pointing out that the agent retains a momentary power to do otherwise — a "flicker" — and argues that this flicker is enough for moral responsibility, so the case does not refute a weak form of PAP. Stump's "no-flicker" variant modifies the case so that a counterfactual intervener is triggered by the slightest sign of the agent's inclination toward the action, before the decision has crystallized, so that at no point in the actual sequence does the agent have the power to do otherwise.

Please produce three things:

1. The exam question itself, in two parts. Part (a) should ask the student to explain the role of Stump's no-flicker case in the debate: why it is supposed to be a counterexample to the flicker of freedom strategy, and what it is supposed to show about the weak form of PAP (the thesis that moral responsibility requires at least a flicker of freedom). Part (b) should ask the student to analyze what happens to the no-flicker construction for an "atomic decider" — an agent who decides all-or-nothing, with no detectable intermediate stages or precursors to the final decision — and to say whether this undermines the claim that the no-flicker case supports the weak form of PAP.

2. A model answer of about 650 words.

3. A marking scheme totaling 20 points, built around five named must-hit moves, with the points for each move and a one-line note on how students typically get each move wrong.

Because this deliverable has no single extractable answer, end your response with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that states, in one sentence, the specific move that separates a top answer from a memorizing one.
