I have recently retired from a career in experimental physics, and in the past few years I have taken to reading philosophy with some seriousness. The debate on peer disagreement has struck me as both important and oddly detached from how we actually handle conflicting evidence in the sciences. I would be grateful if you would draft the document that a committed conciliationist and a committed steadfastist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

Let me first say how I understand the two positions, so that you can correct me if I have got it wrong. The conciliationist holds that, when one learns that an epistemic peer disagrees with one about some proposition, one should, as a matter of principle, lower one's credence in that proposition, typically by splitting the difference between one's own credence and the peer's. The steadfastist holds that one need not do so; the fact of the disagreement is not, in itself, evidence that one's own view is less likely to be true.

Now, my physicist's instinct suggests a point that I suspect is relevant. In my own field, when two experimenters report conflicting measurements, we do not average the two numbers and consider the matter settled. We look for the systematic error: a calibration problem, a hidden confound, a difference in methodology. The disagreement is a signal to investigate, not a number to be averaged. The conciliationist's 'split the difference' prescription strikes me as the philosophical equivalent of averaging two conflicting measurements, which is something we would never do in the laboratory. I am not sure whether this intuition is a reductio of conciliationism, or whether it simply shows that I am thinking of disagreement in the wrong way. I would be grateful if the document could address this point directly.

With that in mind, please produce the following:

1. A numbered list of claims that both the conciliationist and the steadfastist can sign. These should include their shared commitments about what an epistemic peer is, about the nature of the evidence one has when one learns of a disagreement, and about what is at stake in the dispute.

2. A table of their precise disagreements. For each disagreement, give the claim as the conciliationist would state it and as the steadfastist would state it, phrased in each case so that the relevant side would accept the wording.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I suspect that at least one crux concerns whether the disagreement is first-order evidence about the proposition in question or higher-order evidence about the reliability of the reasoning that produced one's credence, and that another concerns what, exactly, counts as an epistemic peer, given that the concept seems to require both parties to regard one another as equally reliable, a condition that is not always stably satisfiable: if I regard my peer as less reliable than myself, then we are not peers and the framework does not apply, yet my peer may regard me as a peer, yielding an asymmetric situation that the standard literature does not seem to handle well. I would be grateful if you would identify the cruxes for yourself and tell me whether the two I have guessed at are the right ones or whether I have missed something more fundamental.

4. For each crux, a concrete case, argument, or thought experiment that both sides would accept as bearing on it, together with what each side would predict or conclude from it, written down in advance.

Please write this as a document that both camps could put their names to, and do not take a side in the dispute.
