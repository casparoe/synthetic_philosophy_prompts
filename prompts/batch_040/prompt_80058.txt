I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been corresponding with a behavioral decision theorist, Marta, about ambiguity for a piece im doing, and I'm trying to figure out which account of the aversion her data actually support, because I keep seeing people conflate a few different ones. Here's the track record of what her lab finds, scenario by scenario. Baseline is the classic Ellsberg urn: 100 balls, 30 black and 70 red-or-amber in some unknown split, and subjects reliably prefer the bets on the known proportions (black, and not-black) over the bets on the unknown ones (red, and not-red). When she runs that, she gets robust ambiguity aversion. Now the variations. One: she constructs a version where the ambiguous bet has the better worst-case, so the ambiguity can only work in your favor, and the aversion drops a lot, with some subjects now preferring the ambiguous bet. Two: she keeps the baseline exactly but has a machine fill the urn, randomized and its process verified, so the red-amber split is still genuinely unknown but nobody's pulling strings, and the aversion drops to about half. Three: she lets subjects, for a small fee, peek at the actual split before they bet, and most of them pay to look, and once they've seen it they bet like its a known risk.

Here's the new case I want to predict for the piece. Same baseline urn, but instead of a stranger or a machine, the person filling it is a friend of the subject's who says, before they start, that they want the subject to win, and the ambiguous bet is the one that could hurt. So: which account does her track record actually point to, what would that account predict her subjects do in the friend case, and what one further scenario would best tell the accounts apart? And be candid about how confident you are and what would change your mind, like what result in the friend case, or what argument, would make you switch to a different account, and where is your reasoning most likely to go wrong?
