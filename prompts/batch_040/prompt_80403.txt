I'm working a piece for the ideas section on how expected value quietly stops working, and I'd rather build a small game to the exact specs than just rehash the Pasadena paradox the way half the blog posts out there do. Here's what I need.

Design a coin-tossing gamble: keep flipping a fair coin until it comes up heads, and let the payoff depend on which flip that the first heads lands on. Calibrate it so that every term in the expected-value sum has the same size — the payoff on flip n should grow exactly as fast as the chance of reaching flip n shrinks. Then make the sign of the payoff alternate with n: win on the odd-numbered first-heads flips, lose on the even-numbered ones.

Once you've got it, run the checks and show the arithmetic:
- confirm the expected value is genuinely undefined and not merely infinite — the winning terms should blow up to plus infinitiy and the losing terms to minus infinity, so it's an infinity-minus-infinity mess rather than the all-positive case;
- show the truncated expected value as a function of the cap N, and say whether it converges as N heads to infinity;
- compute the median payoff of the full uncapped game and say which flip the median lands on;
- and here's the bit most writeups skip: take the Cesaro mean of the sequence of truncated expected values and tell me what number it settles on.

Then line up three candidates for the fair price of a ticket — the ordinary expected value, the Cesaro value, and the median — and tell me what each says you should pay and why they disagree.

Last: which single feature of the construction is doing all the work, and what happens if you pull it out (both if the signs stop alternating and if you slow the payoff growth so the terms no longer match in size)? And how would a defender of plain expected-value maximization push back on using this game as a counterexample at all?
