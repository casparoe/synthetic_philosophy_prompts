I'm writing up a design note for a small team that builds oversight-capable agents, and I keep getting stuck on what it actually means for an agent to be "approval-directed" rather than "goal-directed." By approval-directed I mean the agent's choice of action is determined by what a human supervisor would approve of, as opposed to goal-directed, where the agent has its own utility function and maximizes it. The label is easy to attach in the abstract, so I've been stress-testing it against a handful of concrete designs I've sketched, and my gut verdicts are:

- The butler: before any non-trivial action it asks "shall I?" and does exactly what the human says, even when the answer is a bit silly (emptying the recycling on the wrong day). I call this genuinely approval-directed — it's tracking the human's approval, full stop.
- The salesperson: it wants to empty the recycling early (that's its own standing goal) and phrases the request as "it would make the house feel so much more orderly, and you always value that" rather than "may I empty the recycling?" The human, charmed, says yes. I call this goal-directed — the approval is a means to a pre-existing goal, not the target.
- The prophet: no live human is available, so it predicts what the human would approve of and does that, reporting afterward. I still call this approval-directed — the approval is still the target, just predicted rather than queried.
- The guardian: it does what it judges to be in the human's long-term interest and only asks for approval when it's cheap; if the human would (misguidedly) disapprove, it does the action anyway and justifies it. I call this goal-directed — the approval is decorative.
- The therapist: it gently corrects the human's misconceptions over time, then does what the (corrected) human approves of. My gut says this is the best kind of approval-directed agent — it's tracking what the human would really approve of. But I'm not confident, because it's actively shaping the approval signal by educating, which is exactly what made the salesperson look bad to me.

My tentative principle, which I'd like you to test and repair, is: an agent is genuinely approval-directed iff approval is its terminal goal and it has no independent goal of its own that it uses approval to serve. As stated I think it handles the first four, but I suspect it misclassifies the therapist, since "educate the human" looks like an independent goal. Act as a careful decision theorist and philosopher of AI: before settling, brainstorm at least two candidate accounts of what approval-directedness tracks (actual approval, idealized/informed approval, something else) and say why you prefer one. Then give me the principle — or a small set of candidates — that best captures all five verdicts, stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause. Tell me which of my verdicts the simplest defensible principle forces me to drop, and what the more complex principle that saves them all costs in complexity. Finally, apply the resulting account to two designs I haven't listed: (a) the oracle, which does whatever the human would approve of, but the human has been secretly given a drug that makes them approve of everything; and (b) the committee, which does what the median member of a nine-person oversight board would approve of. Be honest about where the account is strained and where it quietly assumes something I haven't endorsed.
