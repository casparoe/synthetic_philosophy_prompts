I am a retired theoretical physicist. For the last part of my career I worked on the formal side of things — the logic and information-theoretic structure of physical theories — so I am comfortable with formal systems, but I have only recently turned to philosophy proper, and I will confess that my philosophical terminology may be a little loose. I would like you to act as a professional philosopher working at the intersection of mathematical logic, formal epistemology, and decision theory, and to give me a careful consistency check on a set of commitments I have been formulating.

The subject is this: what would a fully rational, self-trusting agent have to be like? I have been reading about the limits that self-reference imposes on a reasoning system's ability to vouch for itself, and I have been trying to pin down exactly where the trouble lies. To make the question concrete, I am imagining an agent whose reasoning is captured by a fixed formal theory T. Think of T as a consistent, recursively axiomatizable extension of a sufficiently strong arithmetic, so that T can represent its own provability relation; I will write Prov_T(φ) for "T proves φ." The agent is, in effect, its own theory: it must make decisions about what to do, and it does so by reasoning within T. I have written down five commitments about what such an agent ought to satisfy. Please treat them as my own views, not as a standard position in the literature.

(1) Proof-gating. The agent should never act except on the strength of a proof. It performs an action a only if T proves that a is the optimal action. No acting on bare evidence, high probability, or intuition.

(2) Internal self-trust. The agent should be able to vouch for its own reliability from the inside. Concretely, T should be able to prove the self-trust principle: for any proposition φ that matters to the agent's decisions, "if T proves φ, then φ is true." The agent should be able to cite this principle as a theorem of T and use it, from within, to justify that its actions are sound.

(3) Consistency. T is consistent — it never proves a contradiction. I take this as non-negotiable: an inconsistent theory proves everything, so its "proofs" carry no weight at all.

(4) No ungrounded self-confidence. The agent's confidence in its own reasoning must be grounded entirely in its own proofs. It must not rest on any external, non-provable premise — no "I was built correctly," no "my axioms are true" taken on faith, no appeal to anything outside T.

(5) Decisiveness. The agent should be decisive: for every well-posed decision that comes before it, it should be able to prove either that the action is optimal or that it is not, so that it always knows, on the strength of a proof, what to do.

Here is what I would like from you. Please think very carefully, and do not rush to a verdict. I would like you to begin with some explicit brainstorming: walk through the possible ways these five commitments might interact with one another, and note any places where one commitment seems to threaten another. Only after that brainstorming should you settle on your final assessment.

In your final assessment, please do the following. First, identify which pairs (or triples) of commitments are genuinely in tension — that is, where the usual motivations for the two commitments are actually incompatible, so that no single agent can satisfy both. Second, identify which apparent tensions dissolve on closer inspection — places where two commitments look as though they conflict but in fact do not. Third, for each genuine tension, say specifically what would have to give: which commitment should be relaxed or dropped, and at what cost. Please rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad.

Two further notes. First, I have a feeling that something in this set is off, but I genuinely cannot say which of my own commitments I would be willing to let go of; so please treat all five as equally sacred until your analysis shows otherwise, and do not assume I already know where the trouble is. Second, I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations to particular papers. I want you to reason from the logic of the situation itself — the theorems of the relevant logic and the structure of the commitments — and to explain your reasoning so that a careful reader could follow every step. If a point turns on a subtle distinction (for example, between a statement's being true and its being provable, or between a principle's being usable by the agent and its being provable within T), please flag that distinction explicitly, because I suspect that is where my error lies.
