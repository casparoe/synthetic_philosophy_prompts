I have recently been retained to advise a family whose trust instrument contains a no-contest clause, and a beneficiary is now threatening to contest the trust on the ground that the settlor lacked mental capacity when it was executed. The clause provides, in substance, that any beneficiary who contests the trust, or any of its provisions, or joins any proceeding challenging its validity, shall be treated as having predeceased the settlor, with the share that would have gone to the challenger reallocated to the remaining beneficiaries. In my jurisdiction, however, courts will decline to enforce such a clause where the challenger acted in good faith and had probable cause for the challenge — that is, where the challenger possessed evidence that, if credited, would have supported the challenge had it been pursued. I have been trying to understand what this structure presupposes, and I am not sure I have succeeded. The clause uses the legal system to make a legal act — invoking the legal system to question the clause's own applicability — the very act that triggers the clause's penalty, and the probable-cause exception requires the court to adjudicate the merits of the challenge before deciding whether the clause may be enforced at all, so that the clause's enforceability turns on the very claim it purports to foreclose. What conception of legal rules does this practice presuppose: that a rule is a threat whose deterrent effect is what matters, that it is a constitutive norm that defines the legal status of acts, or something else? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say which rival conceptions of rules the doctrine quietly rules out, and tell me whether the clause plus exception is internally coherent or whether it silently relies on two different accounts of what a rule is — one for the clause's operation and another for the court's evaluation of it. Finally, explain what would have to change in the doctrine, and in how a drafter could use such clauses, if the presupposed conception were replaced by the rival one.
