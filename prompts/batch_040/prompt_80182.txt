I'm a novelist, and I've been wrestling with a problem that I think I've now turned into an argument, and I can't find the flaw in it. I'd like you to approach this as a professional philosopher working in the philosophy of literature and the metaphysics of mental states — someone who knows the standard treatments of the paradox of fiction cold. Please think carefully and work through the argument step by step rather than reacting to the first objection that occurs to you. And before you give a final verdict, start with a short brainstorming pass: in a few bullets, list the distinct moves a defender of the standard resolution could make in response, so you consider the full space of replies before committing to one.

The background, as I understand it. The paradox of fiction runs roughly like this: (a) I feel real grief when a character I've come to care about dies in a novel; (b) grief is, by its nature, a response to what one believes to be an actual loss; (c) I do not believe the character exists, and I do not believe any actual loss has occurred. (a)–(c) cannot all be true, yet (a) and (c) are plainly true. The resolution I keep meeting — the "quasi-emotion" or "pretend-emotion" move — says that what I feel is not genuine grief but a quasi-grief: a real psychological state, produced by my engagement with the fiction, that is caused by pretend beliefs rather than real beliefs. It is a different kind of state from genuine grief, so there is no contradiction: I do not have genuine grief (which would require belief in an actual loss); I have quasi-grief (which requires only pretend belief).

Now the argument I built, which I think shows that this resolution proves its own failure:

(1) The quasi-emotion resolution locates the difference between quasi-grief and genuine grief in the cause of the state: quasi-grief is produced by pretend beliefs, genuine grief by real ones.
(2) The resolution never claims that quasi-grief feels different from genuine grief. The felt quality is the same — the tears are the same tears, the ache is the same ache. The only difference the resolution identifies is where the feeling comes from.
(3) If two mental states are identical in every intrinsic respect — same felt quality, same bodily and behavioral effects, same value to the person having them — then they are the same kind of state. What individuates a kind of mental state is its intrinsic character, not its cause.
(4) So quasi-grief is not a different kind of state from genuine grief. It just is genuine grief.
(5) But if my reaction just is genuine grief, then by (b) above it is a response to what I believe to be an actual loss — which means I must believe the character exists and was lost.
(6) I do not believe the character exists. So the quasi-emotion resolution, in collapsing quasi-grief into genuine grief, re-derives the very inconsistency it was designed to dissolve.
(7) Therefore the standard quasi-emotion resolution of the paradox of fiction proves its own failure.

I cannot accept (7). I know the resolution is the best available account, and I want to keep both of the things that matter to me: that I do feel something real when I read, and that I do not believe my characters exist. I anticipate one reply, and I want to put it to you now: you may say the resolution can claim that quasi-grief is intrinsically different — that it comes with an awareness of its fictional status, a kind of lightness. But I don't know what that amounts to. When a character dies in a book I've loved, I don't feel light; I feel the same ache I would feel for a real loss. The lightness seems to be a feature of my situation — I know it's a book — not a feature of the feeling itself. So I'm not sure the intrinsic-difference move has any real content, and if it doesn't, I'm not sure what saves the resolution.

So: which premise or step of (1)–(7) should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? Explain why the step you reject looked fine to begin with. Then say specifically what the quasi-emotion account, the make-believe or pretense account, and the fictional-realist account each have to say in response — what each one has to pay to defuse this argument, and whether any of them actually can. Please don't just tell me the argument is invalid; walk me through exactly where the reasoning turns. And tell me, of the two things I'd most like to keep, which one I'm actually in danger of losing.
