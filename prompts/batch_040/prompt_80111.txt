I'm writing a take-home for an upper-level philosophy of language / philosophy of science seminar and I want one essay question on Otto Neurath's project of a universal pictorial language — a visual language of pictograms meant to communicate facts (especially statistical ones) without depending on any one written script. I don't want it answerable by summarizing what Neurath did or why; I want students to confront a specific technical problem. Pictorial languages handle positive states of affairs well (a row of house-icons shows 'N houses exist') but run into trouble with negation and quantification: how do you depict 'there are no houses here' or 'not all workers are employed'? A blank is ambiguous ('none' vs 'unspecified'?), and a 'no' mark over an icon is a convention, not a picture, which seems to betray the whole point of a *pictorial* language. Write the question, a model answer of about 600 words that works through the negation and quantification problems and says whether they're fatal or solvable (at what cost), and a marking scheme that seperates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each must contain.
