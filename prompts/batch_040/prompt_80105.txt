I keep seeing Quine's 'gavagai' thing presented as if the punchline is obvious — 'gavagai' might mean 'rabbit' or 'rabbit-stage' and we can never tell which, done. But I think that papers over the real conceptual problem, which is a gap between two claims. One: the evidence underdetermines the translation — several incompatible manuals fit all the observations equally well — a claim about our epistemic access. The other: the translation is genuinely indeterminate — there's no fact of the matter as to which manual is right — a claim about reality. Why does Quine think the first gives us the second? What has to be true about the nature of meaning for that leap to work, and is it defensible or an overreach from an epistemic to a metaphisical claim? And the part I really want: if the native's actual mental states — the meanings they really have — are the fact of the matter, then underdetermination is just us being in the dark and indeterminacy is false. So why does Quine think he can't just appeal to those mental states? Please answer in British English.
