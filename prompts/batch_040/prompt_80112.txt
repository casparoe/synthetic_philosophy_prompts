I was listening to a philosophy podcast the other day and the host spent like ten minutes on Putnam's Twin Earth thing, and honestly I'm not sure how much of it I can trust. He basically said:

"Putnam's Twin Earth argument is supposed to show that meaning isn't in the head. Imagine a planet that's exactly like ours except the stuff in the lakes and the rain is a different chemical compound, XYZ, that just looks and behaves exactly like our water. A person over there who's had the same experiences we have and says 'water is wet' has a thought that's identical to ours from the inside, but it's really about XYZ, not H2O. So two people can be internally exactly alike and yet be thinking different thoughts, which means the content of a thought can't be fixed by anything inside the skull. That's the point behind Putnam's idea that meaning isn't in the head. And the takeaway is that what a word means is partly fixed by the stuff it actually picks out in the world, and this is true of every single thought you have, not just words like water. It's basically the same idea as Wittgenstein's claim that a word's meaning is its use in a language."

Then he tacked on two conclusions. First, that if meaning is out in the world and not in you, then you can't really be held responsible for what you say, because the meaning wasn't really yours in any real sense. Second, that this is why two people on different planets could never really understand each other, because they'd be referring to totally different things.

I get the water part, but I dont trust the Wittgenstein bit or either of the conclusions. Can you go through his summary and tell me which pieces are actually right, which are a fair simplification, and which are places a philosopher working on this would push back on?
