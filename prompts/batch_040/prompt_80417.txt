Draft the joint statement that a committed debate optimist and a committed debate skeptic about scalable oversight could both sign after a year of good-faith adversarial collaboration. The optimist's position: structured argumentation between AI agents, with a human judge who may inspect only the final arguments and not the underlying computation, is a viable path to governing AI systems that reason far beyond human capacity, and the central failure mode to engineer against is the agents' collusion, not the judge's inattention. The skeptic's position: the judge's verification burden cannot be separated from the agents' strategic incentives, and obfuscated arguments—claims that are persuasive to a resource-limited human but wrong, and that the judge cannot cheaply audit—make debate a procedure that degrades into a popularity contest or a rubber stamp for whichever agent is most rhetorically sophisticated.

The deliverable is the document such a collaboration would produce. It should contain, in order:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. These should cover the formal structure of debate (what the judge is permitted to see, what the agents are permitted to do, what the judge is forbidden from doing), what debate is supposed to solve (oversight of superhuman reasoning), and what each side's standard arguments get right.

2. A disagreements table. For each residual disagreement: a neutral statement of the dispute, followed by each side's position phrased in words that side would accept for its own view.

3. The cruxes—the sub-questions such that settling any one of them would move at least one side. For each crux, give a concrete case or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction about how the case resolves written down in advance. Two cruxes I want you to make sure the document reaches: the delegation test, i.e. whether the judge's verdict on a debate is more reliable than the judge's verdict on a single agent's answer, and whether that gap survives the agents' strategic behavior; and the verifier's curse, i.e. the claim that the judge's own error rate in auditing obfuscated arguments, rather than the agents' quality, sets the ceiling on what debate can deliver.

4. A concession ledger: the claims each side has already conceded to the other over the course of the collaboration, so that the residual disagreement is made exact. In particular, the skeptic must have conceded some version of the optimist's claim that debate improves on a single agent's answer, and the optimist must have conceded some version of the skeptic's claim that obfuscated arguments are possible in principle.

Two requirements the document itself must satisfy: (a) neither the agreed claims nor any disagreement statement may rest on a strawman of either position—each side must be stated in the form of its strongest case; and (b) if you conclude that part of the dispute is verbal, say which part and why.

On the epistemic side, I want you to be explicit about how confident you are that each phrasing would genuinely be accepted by the side it is attributed to, and to flag anywhere your characterization of a side's position leans on a contested reading of that side's view. And if any part of this task cannot be done well as posed—say, if the two positions are not actually in genuine tension, or if the cruxes you end up identifying are not the real cruxes—tell me plainly rather than complying politely.
