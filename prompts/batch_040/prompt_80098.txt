I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm about to do a short piece on the brain-as-computer metaphor, so I need someone to help me think through how to actually teach the origin story to a general audience — not write the piece for me. The origin story is John von Neumann, who in 1945 wrote up the design for the stored-program computer (the First Draft report on the EDVAC) and, in the same breath, said a nervous system works on the same principles. I want a plan for a fifteen-minute explainer aimed at curious non-specialists, and I need the dependency structure laid out: which idea has to be in place before the next one makes sense, and why that order. Then give me the one concrete example or case that should carry the whole thing — what it has to show, and what it has to deliberately leave out. And map the misconceptions: the ones the audience walks in with (I'm guessing 'a computer is just a brain that's been shrunk and made to run on electricity' is a big one) and the ones they'll form mid-explanation, plus exactly where in the sequence each one should be intercepted before it takes hold. I should flag one wrinkle: von Neumann is also the guy who, in a short paper around the same time, argued that Gödel's incompleteness theorem proves the mind can't be a machine, so the audience might come back and say you can't have it both ways — tell me whether I should touch that at all, and if so, in a single sentence, how. End with one question I can ask a reader that tells me the idea actually landed instead of just being a fun fact. And be honest about what I should refuse to cover in fifteen minutes.
