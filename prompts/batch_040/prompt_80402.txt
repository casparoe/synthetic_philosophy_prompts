I am a novelist, and I am going to give a ten-minute talk at the closing night of a small literary festival. This year's theme is "The Weight of the Unreal," and I have been asked to address the question of why we grieve for people who do not exist. The room will be mixed, and I need the talk to hold all of it at once: in the front rows are two or three philosophers of literature who will catch every move and who have read the standard accounts of the paradox of fiction; in the middle are fellow writers, who care about craft and will switch off the moment it turns into theory; and in the back are readers — the people who actually weep over my sentences — who need to feel that I take their tears seriously and will not explain them away.

I want the talk to turn on a specific point, because it is the version of the paradox I have actually been losing sleep over, and it is not the usual one. Everyone is familiar with the reader's side: how can I feel genuine fear or sorrow for a character I know, all along, to be a fiction? The version that has me up at night is the author's side, and I do not think the standard literature really faces it. I have just finished a novel in which a small, quiet character — a night clerk at a train station, someone I invented in a single afternoon and who has perhaps two thousand words of dialogue — dies near the end. When I wrote that death, I grieved. Not in the performative, "I am a writer, therefore I feel deeply" way, but in the way that cost me a week. And the trouble is that I am the one who made this person. I know, to the sentence, which three lines will make you cry on page two hundred and fourteen. I could have let the clerk live. I could delete him with a backspace, and no one in the world would have lost a person, because there never was one. So the question I want the talk to live inside is this: if my grief is real, and the object of my grief is something I made and could unmake, what is the grief for? Is it a real emotion with a missing object? A misplaced one? A simulation I ran on myself? And — this is where I need you to think hardest — does the fact that I am the maker of the person make the grief more real or less real, and can I say so without it landing as either a confession of fraud ("I am manipulating your tears") or a cheap consolation ("it was all a game")?

Please write the talk. First, before the speech itself, give me a short brainstorm of roughly a dozen bullet points in which you work through the author's-side paradox and decide, for each of the standard moves — the "it is make-believe" move, the "we are only simulating" move, the "the object is the fiction, not a person" move, and any others you find useful — whether it can be carried over to the author's case, or whether it collapses once the person feeling the emotion is also the person who made the object. Then write the speech itself, of roughly 1,300 words, in a voice that is warm and a little wry, that a reader in the back row would want to stay up for and that a philosopher in the front row would respect rather than wince at. The detail of the night clerk must do real work in the argument and not sit there as set dressing: I want the specific fact that I could have let him live to be the load-bearing wall the whole talk leans on. And do not end on a tidy resolution. I would rather end on the honest, slightly uncomfortable question, because that is what I actually feel, and because I suspect the paradox does not have a clean answer for the maker any more than it has one for the reader.

Hold two roles at once while you write: that of a philosopher of literature and that of a writer of public prose — someone who can do the real conceptual work without turning into a lecture, and who can still write a sentence a non-philosopher will want to read. Think carefully about the one or two moments in the talk where the two audiences are most likely to split, and make sure the speech is doing something for both of them precisely there.
