Question: Thucydides and the sources on Socrates give us three episodes that are usually cited as evidence that Athenian democracy was unstable and prone to collective error: the Mytilenean debate, the decision to launch the Sicilian Expedition, and the trial of Socrates. In about 400 words, identify a single explanatory factor that links all three, and say what that factor reveals about the relationship between direct democracy and collective decision-making. Use at least two specific details from the episodes themselves.

Student's answer:

The common factor in all three episodes is that the Athenian assembly was making consequential decisions under emotional pressure, on the basis of rhetoric rather than evidence, and then having to live with the consequences of those decisions.

At Mytilene, the assembly, in a fit of anger at the revolt, voted to execute all the men in the city and enslaving the women and children. Cleon pushed for the harsh sentence and Diodotus spoke against it, and the next day the assembly changed its mind and sent a second ship to recall the order on the first one. The second ship caught up with the first before any executions had taken place, so the city was spared as a whole. The episode is famous because it shows the assembly reversing itself within a day — but the reversal came only because a second assembly was convened, and it cost time and a sea voyage.

The Sicilian Expedition was decided in much the same way. Nicias, who had led the first debate by opposing the expedition, then tried to scare the assembly by talking about the impiety of ignoring the eclipse and other divine signs, and that backfired: the assembly took his ominous speech as a reason to send the expedition anyway, and with more men. At the second debate, the assembly told the generals they could do whatever they thought best. Alcibiades's charisma in the debate was the other side of the same coin: the crowd was moved by the force of character of the speakers, not by the evidence.

The trial of Socrates was decided by a popular jury of 501 citizens chosen by lot, which convicted him of impiety and corruption of the youth by a vote of 280 to 221. A barely-majority decision that the city had to live with for the rest of its history.

What this reveals about direct democracy is that it concentrates the cost of a bad decision in a single vote: there is no institution between the assembly and the outcome, so the same body that gets excited is also the body that has to clean up the mess. Mytilene is actually the best case, because the assembly did correct itself — the second ship — which shows that the system's failure mode isn't the absence of correction, but the delay and expense of correction. The other two episodes show what happens when no correction comes at all: Sicily is lost, and Socrates is dead.
