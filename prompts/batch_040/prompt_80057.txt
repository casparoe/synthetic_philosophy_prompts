I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a weekly paper, and I'm working on a long read about restorative justice — the practice where, instead of (or before) a criminal trial, the victim, the offender and a facilitator sit down together and the offender is asked to face the victim, acknowledge what they did, and agree on some form of repair (an apology, compensation, community work). I've been reading Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" to work out what the practice is actually doing to the idea of blame, and I keep getting stuck on one specific thing that I can't seem to articulate. My editor thinks I'm overthinking it, but I don't think I am.

Here's the bit that's bugging me. Restorative justice only works if the offender is willing to engage — to accept responsibility and offer repair. But in practice, if the offender refuses to take part, or shows up and flatly denies responsibilty, the conference collapses and the case just reverts to the ordinary punitive track: the judge sentences them and they get punished whther they accept it or not. So the same legal system has two branches, and they seem to presuppose different things about what blame is. The restorative branch treats blame like something interpersonal — a demand the victim has on the offender that can be 'met' by the offender's own acknowledgement and repair (which is exactly the kind of thing Strawson's reactive attitudes and his 'reserve attitude' talk about). But the punitive branch treats blame like something you can impose on someone whether or not they accept it — more like a debt or a desert that gets paid regardless of their attitude.

So what I want you to do is work out what view of moral responsibility the restorative practice actually presupposes, and whether the punitive fallback it relies on is consistent with that view or quietly betrays it. Is the fallback a genuine contradiction (showing the practice secretly presupposes retributivism after all), or is it just a pragmatic safety valve that doesn't commit the system to any view of what blame is? And if the presupposed view were wrong — if blame really is just a debt to be imposed, not an interpersonal demand — what would actually have to change in how these conferences work?

Please write it in British English. And when you're done with the prose, end with a JSON object with exactly these fields: "implicit_theory" (the view of blame the restorative practice strictly presupposes), "compatible_alternatives" (views it fits with but doesn't strictly require), "the_fallback_verdict" (one sentence on whether the punitive fallback is a contradiction or a safety valve), "consistency" (whether the two branches can coexist under a single theory of responsibility), and "changes_required" (what would have to change in the practice if the presupposed view were replaced).
