You are a philosopher of practical reason who also designs seminar activities for upper-level undergraduates. I teach a 75-minute seminar on practical reason (about 18 students) and I need an in-class activity on the sunk cost fallacy, but not the standard "people irrationally keep spending" version, because a recent personal episode made me doubt my own teaching. I'd been renewing a failing research direction on the grounds that "the cost is sunk, ignore it," and only realized afterward that the real reason I couldn't let go was that the investment had changed who I was and what colleagues expected of me; separately, I'd been writing off a non-refundable deposit as pure waste when it was actually a live option I had bought. So the point I want the activity to make, which I don't think is stated cleanly in the usual treatments: the slogan "sunk costs are irrelevant" only holds if the value of your options is independent of how you got here, but a past expenditure can be (a) pure waste, (b) a live asset you purchased, or (c) a change in your identity or in others' expectations, and in (b) and (c) the cost is not really sunk, so the slogan misleads in both directions.

Design the activity so students run into this themselves before it is named: give them a portfolio of ongoing projects to triage (continue or kill), where the prior costs are secretly of these three kinds, with rules, roles, a scoring scheme that needs no real money, a timeline for the 75 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments onto the three-way distinction and then opens the harder question of whether it is actually irrational to let history reshape your preferences, or whether that is just what preferences are. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be frank about which feature of that harder question the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply myself. Before the full design, start with a short brainstorm of the specific project scenarios you would use and why each one isolates exactly one of the three cost kinds.
