I'm constructing a specification for what an adequate theory of the bootstrapping problem — the puzzle of whether an agent can come to know the reliability of her own information sources by using those very sources — must deliver. Before I evaluate any particular account, I want the full desiderata list: the cases any such theory must get right, the distinctions it must respect, the structural constraints it must be consistent with, and the results it must not contradict. For each desideratum, give the motivating case or consideration behind it, and flag which are non-negotiable (failure on them disqualifies the theory outright) and which are preferences of particular camps.

The cases to cover should include, at minimum:

1. The canonical perceptual bootstrapping case: a subject who sees a zebra in a field and reasons that her visual system must be reliable, since it is producing coherent, stable, and successful perceptual output. The theory must say whether she has come to know that her visual system is reliable, and if not, exactly which step in the reasoning fails.

2. The instrument-checking case: a subject who reads a thermometer, gets a plausible value, and concludes the thermometer is functioning. The theory must say what, if anything, makes this case different from the perceptual case.

3. The no-lose bootstrapping case: a procedure that is guaranteed, by design or by the structure of the situation, to produce a correct output about the reliability of the source in question (for example, a test that can only pass if the source is reliable, or a situation in which the source's output is trivially entailed by its own reliability). The theory must say whether such a procedure confers knowledge of reliability, and if so, whether this is a genuine exception to the anti-bootstrapping principle or a sign that the principle is too strong.

4. The rollback case: a subject who uses a belief formed by a process to justify the reliability of the process, and then uses that reliability judgment to justify the original belief. The theory must say whether the circularity is vicious or benign, and what makes it so.

5. The verification-versus-ratification distinction: the theory must distinguish between using a source to verify a specific claim (which may be legitimate) and using a source to ratify its own general reliability (which is the bootstrapping move). It must say what the line is, and whether it is sharp or gradient.

6. The first-order-versus-second-order knowledge distinction: the theory must say whether knowing p is different from knowing that one knows p, and whether the bootstrapping problem is a problem about the latter.

7. The closure principle: the theory must say whether it accepts, denies, or restricts the principle that knowledge is closed under known entailment, and if it restricts it, say what the restriction is and why it is needed.

8. The trilemma structure: the theory must say which horn of the trilemma (easy knowledge, closure failure, or skepticism) it lands on, and whether it thinks the trilemma is genuine or dissolvable.

9. The anti-circularity constraint: the theory must say whether it accepts a general anti-circularity constraint on justification (no belief may be justified by a chain of reasons that loops back to itself), and if so, what the constraint is and how it applies to the bootstrapping cases.

10. The safety and sensitivity conditions: the theory must say whether the bootstrapped belief about reliability is safe (would be true in nearby possible worlds where the subject forms the belief) and sensitive (would not be formed in nearby possible worlds where the process is unreliable), and whether either condition is necessary for knowledge.

11. The disjunctive response: the theory must address the move whereby the subject is said to know the disjunction "either the process is reliable or p is false" without knowing which disjunct is true, and say whether this is a legitimate response to the bootstrapping problem.

12. The legitimate self-correction and calibration cases: the theory must distinguish between bootstrapping and legitimate self-correction (using a source to detect and correct a specific error, such as a broken thermometer) and legitimate calibration (using a source to adjust its own output against a known standard). It must say what makes the latter legitimate and the former illegitimate, if it thinks the former is illegitimate.

For each desideratum, give the case or consideration behind it, and flag any pair of desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfied. Then, briefly, score the main existing contenders (reliabilist bootstrapping embracers, closure-deniers, anti-circularity theorists, easy-knowledge deniers, and the skeptical horn) against the list, and say which single desideratum does the most to exclude candidates. A table is fine for the scorecard.
