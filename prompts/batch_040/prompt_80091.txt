I'm building a framework for evaluating theories about whether virtual objects and virtual harms are real, and I want the specification before I take sides: the full list of what any adequate theory of virtual objects and virtual harms has to deliver — which cases it must get right, which intuitions it must explain, which distinctions it must not collapse. I keep watching people swing between two poles, "it's just pixels, so nothing virtual is real and no virtual wrong is a real wrong" and "virtual objects are as real as physical ones, so stealing a rare in-game sword is exactly as bad as stealing a physical one," and I don't think either pole survives the actual cases. The purpose, in case adequacy is relative to it: a theory good enough to (i) tell a court or a platform's terms of service whether virtual-property theft and griefing are genuine wrongs that warrant a remedy, and (ii) tell a parent or a clinician whether a child's grief over a deleted virtual pet or a lost virtual friendship is a real loss. A theory that can't do both of those isn't adequate for my purposes.

Here are the cases I think any adequate theory has to handle, and I'd like you to tell me whether I've missed any that are load-bearing and whether any of the ones I've listed is actually a red herring: (a) a player in an MMO has a rare, one-of-a-kind in-game item stolen by another player and feels genuinely robbed, even though the "item" is a row in a database; (b) a player spends weeks building an elaborate base in a block-building game and a stranger logs in and destroys it (griefing); (c) the same kind of virtual item can be converted into real money — gold-selling, or a platform currency such as V-Bucks or Robux that is bought with real currency — so the virtual object has a real-world monetary value; (d) the game company can, at any time, delete every virtual object in the game, through a server wipe or a platform shutdown, even though players treat those objects as theirs and have paid for them; (e) a user is sexually assaulted or "murdered" by another user in a VR social space and suffers real, lasting psychological harm, though no physical contact occurred; (f) two virtual objects that are, as far as the database is concerned, the same item — same type, same stats — feel different in value to players if one has a history, being the "original" of a limited series or having been traded from a well-known player, and the other was freshly generated.

For each requirement, say why it is a requirement (what it rules out, or what it forces on any theory), and mark which you think are non-negotiable for any adequate theory versus which are preferences of a particular camp — the physicalists, the social constructionists, the value-based or functionalists. I especially want you to flag any pair or subset of the requirements you suspect can't be jointly satisfied, and if you find one, say which requirement gives: in particular, whether the demand that virtual objects be treated as socially constituted (real only insofar as a community sustains the practice) is compatible with the demand that they carry genuine, non-arbitrary weight (real monetary value, real grief), or whether those two pull in opposite directions. And tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding the popular candidates. Finally, score the main contenders against the list, briefly — the naive physicalist ("it's all real, it's on a server"), the naive reductionist ("it's just pixels, nothing matters"), the social constructionist (real insofar as a community sustains the pretense), and a value-based or functionalist view (what matters is the attachment, function, and loss, whatever the ontology of the object) — but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than the scorecard; I can do the scoring myself, it's the specification I want.
