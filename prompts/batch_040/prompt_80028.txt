I'm a stratigrapher by training and I keep running into this thing where philosophers of history and historians argue about whether "the Middle Ages," "the Renaissance," and "the Scientific Revolution" are real periods or just later inventions — labels that later people, sometimes centuries later, stapled onto the past to make a story, as opposed to genuine, datable breaks in the record. I've been thinking about how weird it is that we geologists have a whole formal apparatus for exactly this question and historians basically don't, and I want you to run the comparison for me.

Here's the machinery, so you don't have to reconstruct it from scratch: a boundary between, say, one stage and the next on the geologic time scale gets pinned down by what we call a GSSP, a "golden spike" — one specific rock outcrop at one specific horizon where we drive an actual (usually not golden, usually not spike-shaped) marker in, and the rest of the world's rocks of that age are supposed to corelate to it. The boundary is normally defined by a first appearance datum, the first occurrence of some diagnostic fossil, and it has to be sharp, radiometrically datable, and traceable across regions; where no good fossil marker exists (most of the deep time before ~630 million years ago) we just give up on a physical horizon and define the boundary by a fixed radiomtric date instead. The base of the Cambrian, for instance, is pegged to the first appearance of a particular trace-fossil burrow at a cliff in Newfoundland.

So: restate the whole periodization debate — the realist side (these names track real, if gradual, discontinuities, and the lateness of the label doesn't make the period fake) versus the constructivist side (the periods are retrospective narratives, the boundaries are where we decide to put them, and there's no "first appearance of the Renaissance" you could drive a spike into) — in the vocabulary of stratigraphy: type sections, first appearance datums, facies, correlation, radiometric ages, the fiat of ratifying a boundary at one location. Then give me a residue report: which parts of the historical debate translate cleanly, which only translate if you distort them (I suspect the "real but gradual" realist position is the one that breaks, since a GSSP is a sharp horizon by definition), and which have no expression at all in the stratigraphic framework. And end with the question I actually care about: does the fact that geologists can run a perfectly functional, convention-laden periodization system show that the historians' dispute is mostly a dispute about conventions (less substantive than it looks), or does it show that history has a genuine problem geology never faces, because there's no physical substrate to anchor the conventions to?

Please write in American English.
