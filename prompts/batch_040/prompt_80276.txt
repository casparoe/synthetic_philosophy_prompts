I just finished a popular book on the ethics of procreation, and it devotes a chapter to David Benatar's asymmetry argument. Here is how it presents the argument:

"Benatar's asymmetry comes down to four claims. The absence of pain is a good thing: when a person who will never exist does not suffer, that is a good thing, even though there is no one there to benefit from the relief. The absence of pleasure is a bad thing: a person who will never exist misses out on all the enjoyment of life, which is a loss for them. The asymmetry is that we count the first of these as a genuine benefit to the person who does not exist, but not the second — because pleasure requires a subject to be enjoyed, whereas the badness of pain does not. From this Benatar concludes that procreation is always wrong. We would be bringing into existence someone who will inevitably experience pain, and since the pleasures they would have enjoyed do not offset that (there is no one to be harmed by their never being born, but there is someone to be harmed by their suffering), the balance comes out against creation. In practice, though, the argument only bites when the life we would create is on balance not worth living — when its suffering outweighs its happiness. For a person whose life is on balance positive, the asymmetry does no work, and bringing them into existence is unproblematic. So Benatar's view is really just an empirical claim that most lives involve more suffering than joy; if you judge your own life to be worth living, you can simply reject the premise and have children with a clear conscience."

I've been inclined to agree with the book's last two sentences. I do think that if a child's life would be on balance great, there is no good reason not to have them, so maybe the argument really does only apply to bad lives. But I've also heard people say the asymmetry is supposed to condemn procreation even when the life would be wonderful, which would make the book wrong on that point. I don't know enough to tell which is right.

Could you go through that summary clause by clause and tell me which parts are accurate, which are a common simplification, and which are things a careful defender of Benatar's view would object to? In particular, I'd like to know: (1) does the asymmetry really depend on the created life being on balance negative, or does Benatar's argument work even for a life that is on balance positive? (2) Is it right to describe the absence of pleasure as a "bad thing" or a "loss" for the person who does not exist? (3) Given your corrections, does the book's conclusion — that the argument is really just an empirical claim about suffering, and that you can reject it if your own life is worth living — survive?
