I'm a retired field ecologist — I spent thirty-some years counting and classifying birds in wetlands — and I've only in the past couple of years started reading philosophy of science seriously. One topic has been eating at me: Hempel's raven paradox, the observation that, since "all ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "all non-black things are non-ravens," a white shoe (a non-black object that is not a raven) seems to count as evidence for the raven hypothesis. As someone who has built a career on the evidential weight of observations, I find that conclusion deeply counterintuitive; I mentioned it at a dinner with former graduate students and we couldn't agree on whether it's a real paradox or a fake one, so I sat down and wrote a short essay trying to resolve it, and I'd value your critical comments.

My essay's central claim is that the paradox is really a disguised base-rate problem, and that it dissolves once you treat confirmation as a matter of degree rather than all-or-nothing. The white shoe does confirm "all ravens are black," but only by a factor equal to the ratio of how likely a non-black object is to be a non-raven given that all ravens are black versus given that some are not; because ravens are a vanishingly small fraction of non-black things, that ratio is barely above one, so the confirmation is real but negligible. I argue that our gut reaction — that the shoe "shouldn't count" — is right in spirit (it counts for almost nothing) but wrong in letter (it does count, a tiny bit), and I contrast this with the "entrenchment" response, the idea that predicates like "raven" and "black" are simply more entrenched in our language than "non-black" and "non-raven," which I find less satisfying because it feels like it explains the intuition without doing the probabilistic work.

Could you comment specifically on whether the base-rate framing genuinely dissolves the paradox or merely relocates it — for instance, whether it can handle the Goodman-style "grue" objection, in which you replace "black" with a contrived predicate like "grue," defined as black if the object is observed before some fixed date and white if observed after it, so that "all ravens are grue" fits every raven we have ever seen yet predicts future ravens will be white, and the same base-rate logic seems to let a white shoe confirm that hypothesis just as readily, which I think is a real threat to my account? Please give me exactly five points of critique, as a numbered list, each point one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
