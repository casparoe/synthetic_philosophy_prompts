I'm writing a paper on how people actually learn moral character from reading about other people's lives — not the abstract virtue-ethics stuff but the concrete mechanism — and I keep coming back to Plutarch's Parallel Lives, where he says the point isn't to write history but to hold up a mirror so you can see your own life in someone else's. That framing is basically the whole tradition of edifying biography: you read a life and it's supposed to do something to your character.

So I want you to read The Imitation of Christ (Thomas à Kempis) through that lens. I know it's usually filed under devotional / hagiographic writing and treated as a model of Christian self-denial, but to me it reads like the most radical version of the Plutarchian idea: it's not a life so much as instructions for becoming a life, and the "mirror" gets inverted — instead of reflecting your own faults back at you, it's telling you your whole self has to be erased and replaced with someone else's (Christ's).

The thing I'm stuck on is whether moral learning from a life actually works, and whether it works differently in these two cases. In Plutarch you're a spectator: you watch Alexander or Caesar make a choice, and you're supposed to let the example rearrange your own judgment — the model stays the model and you stay you. In The Imitation you're not supposed to be a spectator at all; the whole point is that you stop being "you" and become the subject of the narrative. So is imitation even a form of learning, or is it a different animal — more like a kind of self-overwriting than a lesson absorbed? And does the Plutarchian mechanism (recognize the example, compare it to yourself, imitate the good parts) quietly assume the very stable self that the Imitation is trying to dissolve?

One more wrinkle: Plutarch pairs his lives — Alexander against Caesar, Lycurgus against Numa — so the moral isn't in one life but in the contrast, and the reader has to do the comparing. The Imitation has no such pairing, it's a single fixed model. What does the pairing do for moral learning that a single exemplar can't, and does the Imitation lose anything, or gain something, by collapsing the comparison into identification?

I dont want a summary of the book or a defense of either work. I want you to take Plutarch's mirror idea seriously as a theory of how biography transmits character, test it against The Imitation, and tell me where the theory holds, where it breaks, and what the book shows about moral learning that the argument in the Lives never has to face.
