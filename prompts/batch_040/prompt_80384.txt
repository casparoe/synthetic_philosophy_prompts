I'm a microbiologist doing a short-term consulting engagement for a company that has sold a probiotic supplement for about twenty years under a specific bacterial strain name. We just finished re-sequencing the strain that actually runs on our production line, and I'm now stuck on something the company's compliance team asked me to settle, which turns out to be harder than I expected.

Here's the situation. The production strain is being compared to the reference strain that the label name is built on. On the 16S rRNA gene, the two are about 99.6% identical, which is comfortably above the old cutoff that microbiologists used to treat two bacteria as the same species. But on a whole-genome comparison, the average nucleotide identity comes out to about 94.6%, which is just below the 95% threshold that most taxonomists now use. So the older standard says "same species, the label is fine," and the newer standard says "different species, the label misnames the product."

What I can't do is pick between them, because neither threshold was chosen for a reason that would make my number fall on one side. Populations sit on both sides of both lines, and I can't point to any biological fact that makes 95.0% more "the same" than 94.6%, or that entitles the rRNA gene to decide over the whole genome. The company wants a yes-or-no on the label, and I keep feeling like I'm being asked to answer a question that might not have an answer in the form they want it.

Can you help me think through what's actually going on? Specifically: is there a fact of the matter about whether my production strain is the same species as the reference, or is "same species" only well-defined relative to some chosen criterion, and if it's the latter, what does that do to the question the company actually asked me? And is it coherent for the strain to be "the same species" on one measure and "a different species" on another without that just being a contradiction? I'd rather have a clear way of reasoning about it than a verdict I can't defend.
