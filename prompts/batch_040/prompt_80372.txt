I've been working through the St. Petersburg paradox for a seminar on decision theory, and I keep getting stuck on a point that I think receives less attention than the headline puzzle. The setup is familiar: a fair coin is flipped until the first head, and if that head lands on the nth flip the player is paid 2^n dollars; the naive expected value of the payoff diverges, yet nearly everyone would pay only a modest entry fee to play. Rather than just re-explaining the paradox, I want you to compare two quite different ways of dissolving it. The first is the expected-utility route: attach a concave — say, logarithmic — utility to wealth, and show that the expected utility of the gamble is a small finite number, so an agent with such preferences rationally sets a low price. The second is the "the expectation simply isn't a fair price" route: the claim that because the expected payoff does not exist as a finite number, the expected-value criterion is just the wrong yardstick for this particular game, and the utility transformation is doing less of the real work than its advocates assume.

The part I most want to press is what changes if the casino caps the maximum payout at some very large number M, so the game is effectively truncated at the flip where 2^n would first exceed M. With a cap in place the "fair price" becomes a well-defined finite quantity that depends on M, and I'm curious whether the two routes above give the same or different qualitative answers to how that fair price behaves as M is raised — and whether the fact that the median outcome of a single play is just the smallest payout, $2, no matter how high M goes, should be doing any work in the comparison at all. Please compare the two routes on this truncated-game question, and be explicit throughout about which parts of your answer are settled among people who work on this versus genuinely contested, rather than presenting one camp's view as the consensus.
