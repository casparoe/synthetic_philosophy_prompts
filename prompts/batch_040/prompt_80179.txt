Much of my graduate seminar in the philosophy of psychiatry (14 students) is spent on Hacking's looping effect, and I keep feeling that students hear about it without feeling it. In a 90-minute session, design an in-class activity — a game or staged role-play with real roles, rules, and small real stakes (points or a shared resource are fine) — that makes the looping effect something they run into themselves. Use a fictional diagnostic category rather than a real disorder, so that no one is pathologized, but the structure of the loop has to survive the substitution. The crucial requirement is that the loop show up in what happens, not in what I say afterwards, and that it be distinguishable from a plain self-fulfilling prophecy, where the label simply comes true and the category stays put. In the looping case the category itself has to be revised and becomes unstable even when everyone complies, so the rules should include a mechanism in which a rotating committee role revises the category mid-game on the basis of observed behavior, and the revision is forced even in the round where everyone is successfully conforming to the label. Give me the full design in continuous prose, like a letter, with no headings, bullets, bold, or tables: the fictional category, the roles, the rules and payoff scheme, a timeline across the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game to parts of the looping effect, says what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run and how to handle it, and is frank about which feature of the looping effect the activity cannot reproduce and I have to supply.
