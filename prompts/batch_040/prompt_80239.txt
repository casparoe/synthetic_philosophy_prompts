I'm a third-year student and I TA a fifty-minute problem section for an introductory game theory course, and next week is the first time we get to the strategy-stealing argument. My students have a year of discrete mathematics and propositional logic but no game theory, and last year's post-section feedback makes it clear that this is the one result they leave the room feeling like they understand while actually missing the parts that make it worth teaching. So please don't explain the argument to me; plan how I should explain it. I need the order in which the pieces have to go in and why — in particular, which of the standing assumptions (finiteness, perfect information, two players, zero-sum, no draws possible) are actually load-bearing for the argument and which are just hygiene, because I don't think the students will see the difference on their own. I need the single game that should carry the whole thing and what it must have and lack: I keep reaching for tic-tac-toe, but I'm increasingly sure it's a poor anchor, partly because it is a draw under perfect play and I don't want to open with a game whose honest conclusion is that nobody can force a win, and partly because it is small enough that students can just inspect the whole position and the argument stops feeling like it is doing any work. I need the misconceptions the students will form and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off — specifically, I'm worried they will (i) take the argument to hand them the actual winning strategy rather than merely guarantee that one exists, (ii) read it as proving the first player wins when all it establishes is that the first player cannot lose, and (iii) treat the step where the first player makes an arbitrary extra move as a triviality, when that is the one place where the whole proof lives and can quietly break. And I need one question I can ask at the end that would tell me whether they have actually absorbed the non-constructiveness rather than just being able to recite the proof, plus what I should openly refuse to cover in fifty minutes and say so.
