I've been going back and forth on how medicine treats consciousness, and I keep wanting to seperate the philosophical question from the practical one but I can't. I want to think about it from the access-vs-phenomenal angle. Quick setup since the distinction does the work: "access" consciousness is a mental state being available for report, reasoning, and the control of action; phenomenal consciousness is raw experience, what-it's-like-ness. Some people think the two come as a package, others think you can have one without the other.

Now the practice. In most hospitals, a patient who is awake -- eyes open, normal sleep-wake cycles -- but shows no behavioral sign of awareness (no eye tracking, no response to voice, no command-following) gets diagnosed with a "vegetative state," a label the medical literature is increasingly replacing with "unresponsive wakefulness syndrome." The diagnosis is defined purely by what the patient can and can't do behaviorally. And it carries real weight: in a lot of jurisdictions it's one of the conditions under which a court will let nutrition and hydration be withdrawn, and it's been the the diagnosis at the center of some of the most famous end-of-life litigation. Meanwhile, fMRI work over the past couple of decades has found that a non-trivial minority of patients diagnosed as vegetative can, when asked, deliberately modulate their brain activity -- the standard task is to imagine playing tennis or navigating your house, and the relevant cortex lights up -- so the behavioral diagnosis misses people who are, at some level, aware. And here's what puzzles me: the main published diagnostic criteria are still purely behavioral; neuroimaging is at best an add-on for equivocal cases, not part of the diagnosis. The field knows its criterion misses people, it has a better tool, and it keeps the criterion anyway.

So what view of consciousness does this practice presuppose? When the law and the hospital treat a purely behavioral diagnosis as a fact about the patient's inner life, are they presupposing that phenomenal consciousness requires access -- or even behavioral visibility, the kind of thing you'd expect from a view that ties consciousness to a state being globally available to the rest of the brain, global workspace theories being the canonical example? Or are they just assuming that if there's hidden experience, it doesn't matter morally? Or is the behavioral criterion just an epistemic proxy that the practice could in principle do without? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, say where it's underdetermined, and tell me what would have to change in the diagnosis or the legal practice if we accepted that phenomenal consciousness can exist without any access or behavioral trace.
