I've been working through a family of variants of the St. Petersburg game, writing down how much I would actually pay to play each one, and I can't get any single rule to fit all my numbers. Here are the games and my willingness to pay for each.

Game 1 (the classic). A fair coin is flipped over and over until it first lands heads; if the first heads appears on the n-th flip, you win 2^n dollars. I would pay about $20 to play. I take it the expected value of this game is infinite, and I find the gap between that and my $20 genuinely puzzling. I regard this as the core paradox.

Game 2 (the capped game). Identical to the classic, except the casino has a fixed bankroll of one million dollars and cannot pay out more than that in total, so any win that would exceed one million is paid at exactly one million. I would still pay only about $20. I had assumed that capping the payout at a million dollars would push the expected value up to something large, and I have been told that in fact it is only about $21. Please check that figure and explain, if it is right, why a million-dollar cap leaves the expected value so small.

Game 3 (the truncated game). Identical to the classic, except the coin is flipped at most one hundred times: if it lands tails all one hundred times you win nothing, and the largest possible win is 2^100 dollars. The expected value here is exactly $100 — a perfectly ordinary finite number — yet I would still pay only about $20. I find this at least as paradoxical as the classic, because the infinity has been removed and there is a real, modest hundred dollars of expected value on the table that I decline to pay for.

Game 4 (the simple bet). A single flip of a fair coin: heads you win $200, tails you win nothing. The expected value is also $100. For this one I would pay close to $100 — call it $90. I do not find this paradoxical at all.

The tension I keep coming back to is between games 3 and 4: they have the same expected value, yet I pay $20 for one and $90 for the other. So expected value plainly does not determine my willingness to pay. I suspect the honest answer is a trade-off: either you need a fairly sophisticated principle about how I value risky prospects rather than their average, and that principle saves all four of my verdicts, or you keep a simpler principle — pay up to the expected value — and then you have to tell me which of my verdicts is the one that's wrong. Please lay out that trade-off. State the principle or principles precisely, say which of my four verdicts motivates each part, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts, if any, the simplest adequate principle forces me to give up.

Finally, apply the principle you settle on to a game I have not listed. Suppose you already hold a sure $10,000, and you are then offered a ticket that pays an additional $10,000 with probability one half and nothing with probability one half; the ticket's expected value is $5,000. A stranger with no sure money at all is offered the identical ticket. I believe I would pay less for the ticket than the stranger would, even though its expected value is the same for both of us. Does your principle capture this difference, and if so, how does it do so?
