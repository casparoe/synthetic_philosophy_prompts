I'm on the curatorial committee of the Harwick Historical Society, and we're building a permanent exhibit on the 1907 shipyard lockout. The problem is our sources: the town paper at the time was owned by the shipyard and covered the lockout as a riot stirred up by outside aggitators, the union put out a commemorative history forty years later that turns it all into a heroic victory, and the town council minutes and incident logs nail down the sequence of events but say nothing about what it was really about. Half the committee wants one definitive reconstructed account — the old ideal of letting the sources speak for themselves — and the other half wants the two partisan narratives side by side with a note that we cant adjudicate between them. I keep coming back to the thought that neither option is innocent: which events we pick and the shape we give them, tragedy or rise-and-fall, is itself an interpretive act, and visitors walk out with a story either way. Can you draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting? It should recommend a concrete exhibit structure, not just "present both sides," and it should engage the definitive-account position seriously rather than just calling it naive.
