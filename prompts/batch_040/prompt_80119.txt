I'm in a philosophy of biology seminar and the etiological account of biological function keeps coming up — the Millikan-style view that a trait's function is just whatever it was selected for — and my group-mate built this argument that the etiological account proves way too much, and I can't get out of it. Here's what he wrote down:

(1) A trait's function is just what it was selected for.
(2) A trait was selected for F-ing just in case F-ing was a causal contribution to its fitness.
(3) So a trait's function is just what it currently does causally to boost its fitness.
(4) But that's exactly the causal-role account, the one the etiological account was supposed to be a rival to.
(5) And on that account malfunction is impossible, since if a trait stops playing that causal role it stops having that function.
(6) But malfunction is obviously real — a heart that's had an infarction still "has the function" of pumping blood, we'd all agree.
(7) So the etiological account either collapses into the causal-role account and loses malfunction, or it's just incoherent.

I feel like something slid between (2) and (3) but I can't name it. I'd rather keep (1) and (6) than give up either, and I honeslty don't want to conclude Millikan is wrong, but I cant point to the exact move that's doing the work. Can you tell me which step to reject, why it looked fine on the page, and what I'm paying by rejecting it? And if the real problem is that (2) is only true under one reading of "selected for" and false under another, say which reading the etiological account actually needs to hold.

End your answer with a single line that begins "Verdict:" followed by the number of the step I should reject.
