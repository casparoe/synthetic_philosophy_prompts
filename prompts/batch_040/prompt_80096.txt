So I keep running into this take in AI circles: people will insist a big language model isn't really just doing pattern matching, it's actually learned stuff the way a member of a society learns — it absorbed the whole output of human culture, so there's real content in it, not just statistics. My friend, who reads a lot of philosophy of mind, sharpened the claim and said an LLM is basically a Gregorian creature. For context the scheme he's using (Dennett's, from Kinds of Minds) is a tower of generate-and-test: a Darwinian creature only "learns" by natural selection across generations; a Skinnerian one learns by trial-and-error reinforced in its own lifetime; a Popperian one tests models in its head before acting so it doesn't pay the cost of real mistakes; and a Gregorian one can pick up the already-tested output of other minds, i.e. it rides on a society that has done the generate-and-test for it. He's leaning on this to argue LLMs are genuinely on the road to understanding, not just fancy autocomplete.

Can you audit the analogy for me? Then where the mapping breaks, and crucially which of those breaks actually matter for the "it's really understanding" conclusion versus which are just cosmetic. And does that conclusion survive, or was the Gregorian label carrying the whole argument the whole time? If there's a better level of the tower — or a better analogy entirely — for what an LLM actually is, say what it'd be and why.
