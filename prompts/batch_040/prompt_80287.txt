I'm taking a seminar on the political economy of development, and we've been going over the institutions-versus-geography-versus-culture debate for a month now, and I'm frustrated by how little it has moved. The camps are the institutions side, the geography side, and the culture side, and everyone is extremely smart, everyone has access to the same data, and yet nobody has converted anyone in the last couple of decades. My working theory is that the impasse isn't about the facts or about what counts as good evidence, but about the structure of the available case studies. Here's what I mean. The institutions side's cleanest cases are the "twin" pairs — North and South Korea, the US and Mexico — where geography and culture are roughly held constant and only the institutions differ, so institutions look like the cause. But those are exactly the cases where you can't test whether geography or culture might also be doing the work, because you've already selected for the case where they're the same. The geography side's cleanest cases are the tropical and landlocked countries, where institutions are also bad, so you can't tell whether it's the geography or the bad institutions. The culture side has no clean cases at all, because culture correlates with everything. So my hypothesis is that the debate has stalled because the cases each side cites are structurally incapable of discriminating between the three explanations: each side's evidence is untestable against the others, not because the data is bad, but because of which cases count as the "clean" ones. If that's right, the disagreement isn't really about institutions versus geography versus culture, but about which variable you're allowed to treat as the independent one when you can't actually hold anything constant.

I want a diagnosis, not a verdict: is this a substantive disagreement, a disagreement about standards of evidence, a disagreement about which cases count as data, or two groups answering different questions under one heading? And given your diagnosis, what — if anything — would actually move it? A new dataset, a better identification strategy, a conceptual distinction?
