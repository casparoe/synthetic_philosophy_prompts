I'm a novelist, currently deep in a historical novel set in the final decades of the Western Roman Empire, and I've been locked in a long-running argument with a friend who is a professional historian about what historical explanation actually amounts to. He insists that the covering-law model of explanation — the idea, carried over from the philosophy of the natural sciences, that to explain an event is to subsume it under one or more general laws, the way a physicist accounts for a projectile's path by invoking the laws of motion — is simply the wrong instrument for history, because historical events are singular, unrepeatable, and shot through with the choices of particular agents, so that the best historians do not deduce from laws but construct a narrative of causes, and that the narrative just is the explanation. I've come to agree with him, and in doing so I keep leaning on an analogy drawn from my own craft: a novel explains its characters' actions without any laws at all. When my protagonist — a mid-ranking general — refuses to march on the capital, I do not appeal to a universal law of human conduct; I give him a character, a motive, a web of circumstances and consequences, and the reader finishes the chapter understanding why he did what he did. A historian explaining the collapse of the western provinces is doing exactly this, I tell my friend: assembling a story of causes so that we see why things went the way they did. The analogy does two jobs for me. First, it supports my conclusion that narrative is a complete and self-sufficient form of historical explanation, and that the covering-law model is a category mistake. Second, it defuses the standard objection — that if a historical explanation is "just a story," then it is not an explanation at all but mere storytelling — by pointing out that a novel is, in the ordinary sense, genuinely explanatory, so a historical narrative can be too.

I'd like you to audit that analogy — "a historical explanation is like a novel" — as rigorously as you can, because I suspect it may be carrying more weight than it can bear. Begin by laying out the correspondence explicitly: what in the novel plays the role of the historical agent, the causal chain, the body of evidence, the constraint of what actually happened, and the audience's feeling of "now I see why it happened"? Then separate the disanalogies that matter for my two conclusions from the ones that are irrelevant to them, and be precise about which features of the novel the analogy quietly imports that a genuine historical explanation either lacks or is not supposed to have. I especially want you to press two points. One: a novel's coherence is manufactured by an author who already knows the entire story and can bend events toward a pattern, whereas a historian is reconstructing a pattern she did not choose, from evidence that may never fully close — does that gap change what "coherence" means in each case? Two: a novel can be causally sloppy — a character can act "because the plot demanded it" — and still function as a novel, so a novel's explanatory force is not identical to genuine causal explanation; if the analogy imports that slack, what happens to the claim that a historical narrative is an explanation rather than a story? Finally, tell me plainly whether my two conclusions survive once those disanalogies are on the table, and, if there is a better analogy for what a historian is actually doing, name it and say specifically what it adds that the novel cannot.
