You are a professional political philosopher with deep expertise in the history of political philosophy and in the conceptual foundations of comparative political analysis. Please think carefully and work through this in stages: begin with a short brainstorm of the different senses in which a polity can be called "liberal," then develop a structured analysis. Keep the analysis conceptual rather than empirical: do not rely on specific statistics, policy details, or published indices about the two countries in question, and do not cite sources. Treat Sweden and Norway purely as a case study, so that the answer depends on conceptual reasoning rather than on any particular up-to-date data.

The question to address: which of two neighboring countries — Sweden or Norway — is "more liberal," and, more fundamentally, what would it take to settle that question in a defensible way?

Work through the following, in order:

1. The ambiguity of "liberal." Distinguish at least three senses in which a country can be called liberal, and explain how each would be measured: (a) the classical, normative political-philosophical sense (negative liberty, limited government, strong property rights, rule of law); (b) the justice-oriented, Rawlsian sense (fair basic institutions, equality of opportunity, protection of basic liberties, a social minimum); and (c) the descriptive, colloquial political sense (a society whose dominant parties and public culture are progressive or left-of-center on social issues). Explain why these senses can come apart, and give one example of a polity (real or hypothetical) that scores high on one sense and low on another.

2. The exit/voice decomposition. Now introduce a sharper lens. A citizen's real liberty in a country can be decomposed into two kinds of option: "exit" options (the realistic, low-cost ability to leave the jurisdiction, to opt out of a policy, to move, or to choose an alternative provider) and "voice" options (the realistic ability to change the rules from within, through voting, protest, litigation, and public discourse). A polity can be strong on exit but weak on voice (people can leave, but those who stay can do little to influence things), or strong on voice but weak on exit (people can influence policy but cannot easily leave or opt out). Explain how this decomposition reframes the question "which country is more liberal," and explain why a verdict that ignores the exit/voice distinction is likely to be misleading.

3. Dimensionality and aggregation. Even within a single sense of "liberal," there are multiple dimensions (economic freedom, personal and social freedom, political and civil liberties, tolerance, equality of opportunity, and so on). Explain the conceptual problem of aggregating these incommensurable dimensions into a single "liberalness" value, and explain why the statement "Sweden is more liberal than Norway" is not well-defined unless you first fix (i) which sense of "liberal" is intended, (ii) which dimensions are included, and (iii) how they are weighted.

4. The de jure / de facto distinction. Explain the difference between a country being liberal in its formal institutions and laws versus in its actual practice and social norms, and explain why this distinction matters for the comparison and for how any verdict should be qualified.

5. The negative / positive liberty tension. Explain why a polity with a strong welfare state (high taxation, extensive public provision of healthcare and education) is at once "more liberal" in the sense of securing positive liberty and "less liberal" in the classical sense of negative liberty, and explain why this internal tension makes a single scalar verdict unstable.

6. What would settle it. Given the above, explain what a defensible answer to "which is more liberal" would have to look like. In particular: is a single scalar verdict ever justified, or is the most honest answer a multi-dimensional profile (for example, "X is more liberal on dimension a and less liberal on dimension b")? Under what conditions, if any, would a single verdict be acceptable, and what would a careful analyst have to show in order to earn it?
