I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine, and as part of our anniversary issue on the foundations of mathematics I'm running a one-off reader salon in a bookshop: about a dozen people, educated but with no formal mathematics, and I have roughly forty-five minutes for the segment on the axiom of choice. I keep coming back to the axiom because it's the one idea in the foundations where the dispute is not about the calculations but about what counts as a legitimate move, and I can't get an audience to feel that from a paragraph of prose. I think the way in is a game, and I've been circling a puzzle that I believe sits at the exact center of it. The puzzle: countably many people, each wearing a hat that is either black or white, each able to see every hat except their own, all of them simultaneously announcing a guess for the color of their own hat; the claim is that there is a strategy whose existence is guaranteed by the axiom of choice and that ensures only finitely many players guess wrong, even though no player can ever compute the guess the strategy assigns them. I want the activity to build toward precisely this, but I don't want to hand them the puzzle cold, and I don't want to say the words "axiom of choice" until the debrief.

So please design the whole thing for me. First a finite warm-up: a small number of players, say five or six, who can sit in the room with actual cards, where the group collectively tries to keep the number of wrong guesses down and can find a rule and state it out loud — something they can actually play and feel working. Then the step up to the infinite version, staged so that the group experiences the winning strategy as something that exists but that no individual player can carry out, with roles, a turn structure, and a scoring scheme that gives the group a shared, visible score and a small non-monetary prize so the collective outcome genuinely matters to them. I want them to hit the wall themselves — to feel the difference between "every box has something in it" and "I can point to one thing in each box" — before the idea is named. Then a debrief that maps particular moments in the game onto the actual mathematics: why the finite version is harmless and the infinite one is not, how the same move gives you well-ordering of the reals, non-measurable sets, and Banach–Tarski, and why a mathematician might reasonably refuse the axiom on those grounds. Give me the materials list and a rough minute-by-minute pacing across the forty-five minutes, and tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this with non-mathematicians and how I should handle it. And be frank about which part of the idea the game simply cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in words.
