I'm working through a short essay on a value theory I keep calling "tranquilism" and I'd like a second pair of eyes on one section before I keep going. The view, roughly: the only thing with intrinsic value is the absence of craving and suffering. Positive pleasure, the good feeling of getting what you want, enjoyment for its own sake — none of that counts. It's more austere than plain negative utilitarianism, because it doesn't just care that pain is absent, it cares that the want, the thirst, the low-grade agitation of not having what you're after, is absent too. I know it sounds weird, but I want to steelman it for a bit.

The section I want feedback on. I'm trying to show tranquilism has a problem with cravings that actually get satisfied:

The trouble starts with a craving that gets its wish. I'm thirsty, I want a glass of water, I get the water, the craving is gone. On tranquilism the good thing that happened is the *absence* of the craving — it stopped being present. But notice what it took to get that absence: I first had to have the craving. If I'd never been thirsty I'd never have had the craving, and so I'd never have gotten the relief of its absence. So compare two lives. In life A I am never thirsty, so I never crave water, so there's never any absence-of-craving to be had — just a flat, craving-free background. In life B I get thirsty, I crave, then I get the water and the craving stops. On the face of it, life B contains a stretch where craving is present (bad, or at least not-good, on tranquilism) and life A doesn't. So life A is at least as good as life B, and probably better. But that's absurd: it makes it better never to want water than to want it and get it. The only way out is to say the craving in life B wasn't actually bad — that a craving is not intrinsically bad but only bad insofar as it leads to suffering, and a satisfied craving leads to relief, not suffering. But if that's right, then the claim that the *absence of craving* is intrinsically good is doing less work than it looks like. It turns out the only thing that matters is the absence of suffering, and the whole craving bit was just a fancy way of talking about a kind of suffering all along.

So my question is narrow: does the "satisfiable craving" move actually generate a live problem for tranquilism, or am I smuggling in the assumption that craving is only instrumentally bad? And if tranquilism can defuse it, does it have to give up the "only the absence of craving and suffering matters" part to do it?
