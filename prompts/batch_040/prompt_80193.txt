I've been trying to pin down what a genuinely fair way of deciding as a group actually has to be able to do, and I keep getting the impression that the honest answer is "nothing can," so before I put my trust in any rule I want the full specification first. My real situation is a small co-operative of about a dozen people who has to choose a single outcome that lies on a line — where along a shared strip of land to plant the community garden, or how to divide a fixed budget along a one-dimensional scale. Each of us privately has one favorite point and gets progressively less happy the farther you move away from it: one peak, with no second-best island somewhere else on the line.

Write me the list of requirements that any acceptable decision procedure for a group like mine must meet in order to count as fair, in the form of a specification rather than a description of any particular rule. For each requirement, state what it is, the concrete case or failure mode that makes it a requirement (what actually goes wrong if the procedure lacks it), and whether you regard it as non-negotiable or as a preference that belongs to one particular camp of people who care about group decisions. Then mark which of the requirements you suspect cannot all be satisfied at once, and say which single requirement does the most work to rule out the obvious candidates.

The part I care about most is whether my specific structure changes the verdict. I have a hunch that simply taking the median of everyone's favorite point would be both fair and impossible for any one of us to game by misreporting where our favorite really is, and I want to know whether that hunch survives your list — or whether some requirement I haven't thought to ask for yet quietly kills it. I would much rather learn now that my case is special than discover later that I had been handed a generic impossibility that was never meant to cover a line of options with one peak per person.
