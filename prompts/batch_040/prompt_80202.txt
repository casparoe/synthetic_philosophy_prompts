I've been going back and forth on deflationary theories of truth for a while now — the idea that "true" isn't some deep property that sentences hook onto, just a handy device. The disquotational schema ("S is true" is just "S" with the quotes dropped) is the semantic version, and I keep coming back to the pragmatic one, where the whole point of the truth predicate is indirect discourse, like when you want to agree with what someone said without repeating it word for word. A friend at a meetup walked me through an argument last week that I now think shows deflationism just collapses into "truth is assertion," which is obviously garbage, but I can't find the step thats actually doing the work.

Here's it as I remember it: (1) Deflationism says the predicate "is true" has no substantive job — "S is true" is just a way of endorsing or asserting S, which is why we have it at all. (2) So the reason "is true" exists is to let you assert/endorse S. (3) Therefore "S is true" is true just in case S is asserted or endorsed. (4) So truth just is assertion. (5) So whatever gets asserted is true, and whatever is true gets asserted. (6) But I can assert "2+2=5" and it's plainly false, and "2+2=4" is true whether or not anyone ever says it. (7) So truth is not assertion. (8) So deflationism is false. I'd rather keep deflationism than (5), and I'm fairly sure (1) is atleast roughly right, so I figure the break is somewhere in the middle but I can't name it. Which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what does it actually cost me to give it up?
