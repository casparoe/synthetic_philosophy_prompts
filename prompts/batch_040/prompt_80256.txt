I keep running into this debate in my ethics reading group and it just won't move, and I'm not sure why. The question: is it wrong for me to do a thing (buy a product, burn carbon, buy meat) when my single act makes no difference — the harm would of happened anyway because of everyone else doing the same? One side says no: your act makes no difference, so you didn't cause anything, so it's not wrong. The other side says yes: your act is a part of, contributes to, the collective harm even though it makes no difference.

My working hypothesis is that most of this is verbal, hiding in the word 'contribution.' I think the 'no difference' side is using a counterfactual, but-for sense — you only contributed if the harm wouldn't have happened without you — while the 'contribution' side is using a part-based, proportional sense — you contributed because your act was one term in the sum that produced the harm, even though pulling it out changes nothing. And real collective harms like climate are overdetermined, so those two senses genuinely diverge, which is why each camp thinks they're just using the ordinary word and nobody budges.

Is that actually right, or is there a real substantive disagreement underneath — e.g. even granting the proportional notion, whether being a proportional part of a cause with no counterfactual difference is enough to make your act wrong? I want a diagnosis with reasons, grounded in what each side actually says, not a survey of the literature, and an honest answer about what would count as progress — a distinction, a rephrasing of the question, a formal point — or why no progress should be expected. And say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you're least sure of.
