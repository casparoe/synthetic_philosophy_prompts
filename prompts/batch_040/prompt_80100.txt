I'm trying to figure out what I actually believe about moral worth — specifically, what it takes for an action to be performed "for the right reason" — by going on my gut reactions to a set of cases. Here are my verdicts, and I want to know whether a single principle, or a small set of them, captures the pattern, what that principle says about cases I haven't yet considered, and which of my verdicts it forces me to give up.

1. A hospice nurse quietly raises a dying man's morphine because she judges that easing his suffering is what he needs and what a good nurse ought to do; nothing else is moving her. I assign this full moral worth.

2. The same nurse, the same patient, the same dose — but this time she raises it because it is the last task before her break and she wants to be finished. The act is still the right one. I assign this little or no moral worth.

3. A person catches a stranger who is stumbling, without any deliberation and without any thought of duty, simply because they are a warm, helpful kind of person. I am torn, but I lean toward full moral worth, even though the agent never acted on a moral principle.

4. A person returns a lost wallet stuffed with cash because they are a devout believer acting on the conviction that it is a commandment of their faith, and the commandment happens to line up with what morality requires. I am torn again, but I lean toward full moral worth, because the believer genuinely takes the right thing to be right.

5. A thief breaks into a corrupt billionaire's vault and donates the entire take to a children's hospital, fully aware that the theft is wrong but convinced that the moral arithmetic justifies it. I assign this no moral worth — the act is wrong, full stop — though I grant that the motive is genuinely good.

6. A person keeps a promise to meet a friend for dinner because they know that keeping promises is right, and also because they genuinely look forward to the evening; both motives are present and both are doing real work. I assign this full moral worth.

7. A person does not cheat on their taxes because they are terrified of the social disgrace of being found out. The act is right; the motive is pure fear of shame. I assign this little or no moral worth.

8. A person who was raised from infancy to always do the right thing now does the right thing automatically, the way a musician plays a well-practiced piece, without ever reflecting on whether it is right. I assign this only partial moral worth, and I am not sure why it differs from case 3.

The pairs that bother me most: I give case 3 (the instinctive helper) full worth but case 8 (the trained automatic person) only partial worth, even though neither ever deliberates about duty and both act rightly; and I give case 4 (the believer acting on a commandment) full worth, which seems to clash with any rule that says the operative reason must be the moral reason itself rather than something that merely coincides with it.

Two cases I have not listed, and I would like your principle to say something definite about: (a) a person who forms the full intention to do the right thing for the right reason but is killed in a car accident before they can act; and (b) a person who does the right act for the right reason, but the act, through sheer bad luck, produces a bad outcome that no one could have foreseen.

Please give me a principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely; for each, the clause that does the work in each of my cases; its verdicts on (a) and (b); and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which I should be willing to drop.
