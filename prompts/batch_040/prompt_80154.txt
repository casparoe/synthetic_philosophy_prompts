ok so I've been thinking about the liberalism vs communitarianism thing on and off for about two years now, mostly just in my head and in arguments with friends, and I finally wrote down where I've actually landed. I'm not a philosopher (I've read a bit of Sandel and followed the online debates, so forgive the flattening), but I'd like a real stress test on whether this package hangs together before I go and defend it to people.

Here's the list:

(1) You can't step outside your own commitments and judge them neutrally. Every evaluation of what's worth living for is done from inside a set of attachments you didn't choose, so the liberal picture of a self that can hold all its commitments at arm's length and pick the best package is a fiction.

(2) But rights still trump. If a community demands something that violates a member's rights — forced religious practice, the community deciding a member can't marry outside, can't speak a different language at home — the member's rights win, full stop. The community's claims are real but not unlimited.

(3) The good life is partly made by your community. You can't understand someone's values by stripping away their community; identity is socially formed. A person who's had their community taken from them hasn't just lost an option, they've lost part of the self that would have made the choice.

(4) My exit test: a communal demand is legitimate only if a member could realistically leave the community without losing their livelihood or social standing. If the only way out is catastrophe, you don't have consent, you have captivity — so a community that makes leaving ruinous has forfeited the right to make strong demands of its members.

(5) Group rights are a cover story for individual rights. The only legitimate reason to protect a minority culture is that its members need the cultural context to make meaningful choices; the group itself has no interests beyond its members. So when someone argues "the community has a right to X," I only accept it if I can translate it into "the members have a right to X."

(6) But here's a case where I think the community's claim is real: a tight-knit religious community in a small town that's worried that if outsiders move in, the school will have to stop being parochial, the kids will assimilate, and within two generations the culture just dissolves. I think they have a legitimate claim to limit new arrivals in there area. Otherwise the majority just grinds them down and nobody's individual rights are being violated but the culture dies anyway.

You're a professional political philosopher who's worked on the liberalism-communitarianism debate for decades, so treat this like a case you're being asked to audit, not a debate you're being asked to join. Before you commit to a verdict, do a quick brainstorm pass: go through my list pair by pair, list every place that might be a tension, and seperate the ones you're confident about from the ones you're still unsure about. Then give me the final audit: which pairs are in real tension, which are only apparently in tension (and why they dissolve on inspection), and for each real tension what the cheapest fix is and what giving it up would cost me. Order the real tensions by severity, worst first, rather than treating them as a flat list, and end with your one recommendation: if I could only change one item on the list, which should I change and why. Don't just tell me to become a pure liberal or a pure communitarian — I want to keep as much of this package as I can — and please explain in plain language, I'm not a philosopher.
