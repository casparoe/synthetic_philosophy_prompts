I'm writing a think piece about the Allais paradox and want to do a fun counterfactual: how would Daniel Bernoulli react to it?

Quick context: Bernoulli is the guy who, in a 1738 paper, basically invented expected utility theory to defuse the St. Petersburg problem — the coin-flip game where you win $2, $4, $8, ... for each tail before the first head, so the expected dollar payout is infinite, and his answer was that you should take the expected value of your utility instead, with utility growing (logarithmically, roughly) with wealth.

Now the Allais setup. You're shown two seprate choices. First: (A) 11% chance of $1M and 89% chance of nothing, vs (B) 10% chance of $5M and 90% chance of nothing — most people pick B. Second: (C) the exact same 11%/$1M/89%/$0 gamble as A, vs (D) 10% chance of $5M, 1% chance of $1M, 89% chance of nothing — most people pick C. The independence axiom says the 89% chance of nothing is common to both options in the second pair and should be irrelevant, and the same cancellation runs through the first pair, so picking B and C together amounts to asserting at once that a 10% shot at $5M beats an 11% shot at $1M and that an 11% shot at $1M beats a 10% shot at $5M.

My question: what do you think Bernoulli would say? Would he treat the B-and-C pattern as evidence that his whole utility machinery is broken, or does he have some move available to him — leaning on diminishing marginal utility, on the claim that the test-takers just aren't maximizing anything coherent, or something else? And specifically, wether his diminishing-marginal-utility story is enough to explain the pattern, or whether he'd be forced to admit the problem is on the probability side rather than the utility side. I'm curious whether you'd put him in the "people are irrational" camp or the "the axiom was too strong" camp, and what his actual argument would look like, given that he didn't have the von Neumann–Morgenstern axiomatization to defend. No citations or anything, just reason from what he argued in 1738.
