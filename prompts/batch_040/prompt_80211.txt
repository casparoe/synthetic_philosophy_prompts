I'm a hospital chaplain at a regional hospital, and like once a week I get a version of the same call: a family asking wether we can tell a dying patient the tests came back all clear, a patient asking us to lie to his spouse, a nurse asking if it's OK to tell a confused elderly patient that her son is coming tomorrow when he's not. The ethics committee is mostly Catholic and I keep getting asked to speak for that tradition, so I want a decision procedure my team can actually run in the room, grounded in Augustine's actual view of lying rather than the popular summary that he thought lying was always wrong. I want the specific machinery to do the work: the difference between lying and merely speaking falsely (he requires the intent to communicate falsehood, not just saying something untrue), his taxonomy of lies by purpose (to injure, to benefit someone, or just to please or amuse) and by who is affected (the liar, the hearer, a third party), and the hierarchy of alternatives he endorses — truth is best, silence or changing the subject beats a lie, and deceiving without asserting a falsehood (his example of the madman who asks where his friend is and is deliberately led astray) is permitted where lying is not. Then the procedure itself: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. My team is trained laypeople, not philosophers, so it has to be runnable in under ten minutes, and the final call in the room is made by me or the committee, not by the procedure — mark which steps are pure judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run it on three cases: (1) the family asking us to tell a terminal patient the tests were all clear; (2) the confused patient asking whether her son is coming, when he isn't; (3) a case where you think the procedure will give an answer I won't like — pick it and say why it misfires. Be honest about what the procedure can't do, including where Augustine's own framework is in tension with itself (if deception is permitted, where exactly is the line between permitted misdirection and a lie in the moment, when you're talking to a confused person?).

At the very end, on a single line, give: Verdict: <label for case 1> | <label for case 2> | <label for case 3>, where each label is one of: truth, withhold, misdirect, lie. If the procedure says lie anywhere, I want to know why, because I don't think it should.
