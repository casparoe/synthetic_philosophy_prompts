I write country diagnostics for a mid-sized development fund -- the kind of shop that decides whether to back a particular country's reform program, and the diagnostic is the main document the investment commitee reads. Roughly every two weeks I get a new country to cover and I've got about a week to turn in causal section. The thing that keeps biting us is that the committee keeps asking "so is it the institutions, the geography, or the culture that's doing the work here?" and I keep writing three-paragraph surveys of the whole debate, which is not what they need. They need me to actually commit to a story for this country, or to say honestly that I can't, with reasons. And the diagnostic has to be signed off by a senior economist before it goes out, so if my causal section is hand-wavy, it bounces.

I want you to act like a professional philosopher of science who also knows the causal-inference-from-history literature, and help me build a decision procedure I can actually run on a country in a day or two. Not a literature review -- a procedure: the questions to ask in a fixed order, what counts as a good enough answer to each one, where the procedure tells me to stop and say "not identifiable, here's what I can and can't claim," and where it has to hand the call to a person because the question is really about which story is more useful to tell, not which one is true. I'm a political scientist, not an econometrician, so please don't make the procedure depend on me running a regression or parsing an IV paper; I need it to be something I can do with a desk of secondary sources and a whiteboard.

Before you write the procedure, take a beat and brainstorm the failure modes it has to catch -- the places where the three standard explanations co-vary so badly you can't tell which is doing the work, the places where the "good institutions" story and the "good culture" story are the same story told twice, the places where a single natural experiment looks clean but is secretly selected on unobservables, and the places where the honest answer is just "we can't tell." Then write the procedure, and then run it on two countries: one where you expect it to land cleanly on a single explanation, and one where you expect it to come back "not identifiable." For the second one, pick a country where the committee has a strong prior that it already knows the answer -- a country they've been funding for years with a favorite story -- and where I would personally really not like that answer; explain why the procedure is right to give me that answer even though I won't like it.

Please write the whole thing in American English and keep the language plain -- the committee is smart but they're not philosophers, and if the procedure sounds like a paper they'll just ignore it.
