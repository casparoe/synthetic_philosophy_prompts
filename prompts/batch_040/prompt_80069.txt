I write about technology and the far future for a science-and-ideas magazine, and next month I'm giving the opening talk at a small workshop on longtermism. I intend to argue a contrarian line: that the simulation argument, taken seriously, is a genuine reason to dial back what I'll call "far-future fanaticism" — the practice of assigning overwhelming moral weight to the welfare of generations thousands of years hence. My argument, in brief, is this. If there is a real chance we are living inside a computation, then the far future that longtermists multiply out — trillions of minds across an expanding civilisation — may not be out there at all. A simulation is a computation, and a physical computation is bounded: the run can be paused, truncated, or discarded, which caps the number of future minds and therefore caps the total value at stake. Before I go up, I want to be ready for the questions. The room will be mostly longtermist researchers from effective-altruism organisations, with two or three philosophers of mind who are sympathetic to the simulation hypothesis but think its ethical upshot is a non-sequitur. Draft me a prep sheet: the four or five objections I'll actually get from this audience, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way this room would put it, with a short reply I can deliver in under two minutes and a note on where my reply is softest. Close with the one question I'm hoping nobody asks. Keep the whole thing to no more than 600 words, and write it in American English.
