Quick one, sorry if it's a bit rambling, I typed this up between office hours. I keep reaching for an analogy in my intro ethics course to motivate expressivism: moral judgments are a bit like 'Bless you' or 'Thank you' — they don't report a moral fact the way 'it's raining' reports weather, they just express the speaker's attitude, the way 'Bless you' expresses sympathy at a sneeze. It lands well for the flat cases ('Killing is wrong' ≈ 'Boo to killing'), and a colleague of mine recently used the same picture in a talk to argue that moral disagreement is just a clash of attitudes, with no fact of the matter to be discovered.

Can you audit how far this analogy actually holds? I want the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the sneeze, the blessing, the speaker's feeling, and so on — and then the places where the mapping breaks, especially when moral sentences get embeded in conditionals, negations, and questions: 'If it's wrong to break promises, then it's wrong to break a promise even to save a friend', 'It's not the case that lying is wrong', 'Is it wrong to...?'. You might recognize those as the Frege–Geach problem, but I want the audit of the analogy, not a survey of the problem. I suspect the analogy is quietly doing all the work for the conclusion about moral disagreement, but I want to know precisely where it fails, which features of the greeting side it imports that moral sentences don't have, and whether an expressivist has any real way to patch it (I've heard of 'prejudgments' and 'higher-order attitudes' but don't trust my memory on what they actually accomplsh). And please say whether my colleague's conclusion about moral disagreement survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time. If there's a better analogy for the same job, I'd love to hear it.
