I got into a really long argument with my friend about the Liar paradox last night and I couldnt sleep about it, so I wrote this down. Can you read it and tell me where I'm going wrong? I especially want your eyes on the middle move, the "false" vs "not true" thing, because that's where the whole argument stands or falls.

So. Everyone knows the basic Liar: "this sentence is false." Call it true and it's false, call it false and it's true, broken. The usual fixes are all some version of "the sentence just doesnt get a truth value." Tarski says the language isn't allowed to contain its own truth predicate, so the Liar isn't even a well-formed sentence. Kripke lets the language contain its own truth predicate but builds the interpretation up in stages, and the Liar ends up in a gap, neither true nor false, just undefined.

Here's the part I keep turning over. The gap fix works for the Liar as it's usually stated, but only because the Liar says it is false. Now write a sentence that says "this sentence is not true" instead. In the gap theory it's the same kind of self-referential mess, so it's undefined too. But if it's undefined, then it is not true, which is exactly what it says, so its content is correct, so it's true. And now the gap theory is forced to call a sentence true that's the same shape as the Liar. The gap just got eaten by a one-word change.

So my claim is that the real lesson of the Liar was never "some sentences lack truth values." The point is that "false" and "not true" come apart, and any theory that treats them as interchangeable, bivalent or gap, hands the paradox back to itself the moment it says "the Liar is not true," because that's the exact wording a revenge sentence needs.

The only account I can see that survives is one where truth isn't a fixed point but a process, the revision-theory approach (Gupta and Belnap, if my memory's right) where you keep re-evaluating a sentence against its current value and the Liar just oscillates, true, false, true, false, never settling. The revenge sentence oscillates the same way, so it's not a counterexample, just another unstable sentence. The "paradox" is really the non-stabilization, and that's a coherent thing to say even though it's not a truth value.

So: (1) is the "false" vs "not true" distinction actually doing the work I think it is, or am I strawmanning the gap theories? (2) does revision theory really defuse the revenge sentence the way I'm describing, or am I papering over something? And (3) is there a cleaner way to state the overall point that I'm missing?
