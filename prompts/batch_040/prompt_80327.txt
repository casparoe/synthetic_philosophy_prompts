I'm a novelist writing a book whose philosophical spine is the distinction between knowing how and knowing that. The story follows a master luthier — a maker of violins and cellos — and her young apprentice, and the central tension is whether the master's skill (the "knowing how" to make an instrument that sings) is a genuine form of knowledge or something else: a knack, a habit, a bodily feel, or a body of knowing-that she simply cannot put into words. I want to make sure I'm not smuggling in a single, unexamined epistemology, so I'd like a careful classification instrument that sorts the main philosophical positions on the knowing-how / knowing-that distinction. That way I can see exactly where my novel's default assumptions sit and where they might be vulnerable.

Please take on the role of a professional philosopher working in epistemology and the philosophy of action, with deep familiarity with Ryle's original distinction and its twentieth- and twenty-first-century descendants. Think carefully before you write, and do not rush to a list of names.

First, do a short brainstorming pass — a few bullet points, no more than about two hundred words — in which you map the logical space of the question. Identify the genuinely independent axes on which positions about "knowing how to φ" can differ, without yet assigning any of them to a named view. Candidate axes (feel free to revise or add): whether knowing how is propositional at all, or a non-propositional state; if it is propositional, what the proposition is (a general rule, a claim about one's own ability, or a case-by-case "which action here"); if it is non-propositional, what kind of state it is (an ability, a familiarity or acquaintance, or a first-personal phenomenal state); whether a "what-it-is-like" component is essential; and whether the knowledge is in principle articulable and teachable, or locked in the body.

Then produce the deliverable, which I'd like to be a decision tree (if you judge a scoring quiz would serve me better, you may propose that instead, but I lean toward a tree). Requirements:

1. The tree begins from one neutral starting commitment that a reasonable person could accept without already knowing which position they hold — for instance, "I accept that the luthier's skill is a real, non-accidental competence, and that there is something it is for her to know how to make a good instrument." Every branch node after that must be a concrete question or case on which the positions genuinely come apart, phrased so a reader can answer it without knowing the label it leads to. No node may simply ask "do you think X?" where X is a position name.

2. Build the tree from the logical space first, then locate the actual positions as leaves. For each leaf give: a short label; a one-sentence statement of its core commitment; and, only where you are confident, a well-known defender (Ryle, Williamson, Russell, Polanyi, and so on). If a leaf corresponds to a combination that nobody, as far as you can tell, actually defends, mark it "unnamed" and say so.

3. Note the unoccupied combinations — cells of the logical space that appear empty — and give a one-line diagnosis of why (incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored).

4. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot actually separate, i.e., two views that differ in name but not in any answer a reader could give. Name them and say why the tree fails to tell them apart; do not paper over the failure.

5. Drive the branches with at least three concrete test scenarios, one of which must be the luthier-and-apprentice case. The others should be cases where the distinction bites in a less obvious way — for example, someone who knows every relevant proposition and still cannot do the thing; someone who can do the thing fluently but cannot state a single governing proposition; or a case where the competence is wholly procedural and embodied. Make each scenario specific and vivid enough that I could lift it into a scene.

6. My audience is me (a novelist, not a philosopher) and, secondarily, the philosophy-minded readers of the book. Keep the prose clear and gloss any technical term in a passing clause rather than assuming it.

7. On size: aim for roughly five to seven branch nodes and six to nine leaves. Go over only if the logical space genuinely demands it; do not pad.

Before finalizing, check each branch node and confirm it actually separates its children — that a reader could plausibly answer toward one child and away from the other without contradicting themselves. If a node fails that test, replace it.
