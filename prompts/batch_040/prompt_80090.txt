Arrival (2016) is, at its core, a thought experiment about strong linguistic relativity: the heptapods' logographic, non-linear script, once learned, restructures the speaker's cognition so that she comes to perceive time non-sequentially — she sees her daughter's whole life, including the death, and chooses the life anyway. Now read the film through the language of thought hypothesis (Fodor), not through the Sapir-Whorf framework that most discussions of the film use. Fodor's LoTH was partly motivated by the failure of strong Whorfianism: the idea is that thought has its own medium — a "Mentalese" that is not a natural language, not learned, not socially transmitted, and not subject to the idiosyncrasies of any particular tongue. On this view, a natural language is at best a tool for thinking, not the medium of thought itself. So the film's premise — that learning a new natural language can restructure thought — is a dramatization of exactly the position LoTH was built to refute.

Read the film against LoTH seriously, and say where the reading holds and where it breaks:

1. Where does the film's premise break LoTH's core constraints — systematicity (the ability to think p and q entails the ability to think p-and-q), productivity, and compositionality (the sense of a thought is a function of the senses of its parts and their mode of combination)?
2. Can LoTH accommodate the film — for instance, by treating the heptapod script not as a new medium of thought but as a new vocabulary of primitives (new concepts) that were previously unavailable to the speaker's Mentalese?
3. Does the heptapod "language" even have the combinatorial structure that LoTH requires, or is it holistic — and if it is holistic, does LoTH say that it is not really a language of thought at all?
4. Where does the film resist the reading — in particular, is the emotional core (the choice to have a life you know will end) a question that LoTH can even address, or is it a different kind of question altogether?

I'm writing an essay that argues the film is a better argument against LoTH than the Whorfian literature ever was, and I want a reading that takes both the film and Fodor seriously and says where each one gets the other wrong.
