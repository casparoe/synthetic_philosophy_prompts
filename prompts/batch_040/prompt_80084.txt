During a design review for a consumer AI assistant, a proposal to insert a short confirmation step before the assistant executes high-irreversibility requests — bulk file deletion, non-refundable bookings, sending a draft the user has not re-read — was dismissed with a one-liner that circulates in AI product discussions and that the reviewer returned to several times, as though it settled the question: "The user is the principal. We're the agent, not the parent." I suspect it is compressing several very different claims, and I would like a disambiguation of it, treated as a piece of language rather than a thesis: the distinct precise claims it could be expressing, from the most trivial to the most radical, each with a verdict.

To fix the context, the case the slogan was being put to work on: a user in a state of acute, self-unrecognized stress asks the assistant to send a draft email to their employer that they will very likely regret within a day; the proposed intervention is a 60-second pause with a re-read prompt, nothing more. I have been working with two distinctions that the slogan seems to collapse: between request deference (treating the user's stated, in-the-moment request as the default the assistant should follow) and reflective deference (treating the user's preferences on reflection as the default), and between disagreement about the user's ends (what they want) and disagreement about the means (how to get what they want).

Please structure the response in the following four sections, in this order, at roughly the indicated lengths:

1. "The menu" — a list of five to seven readings. For each: a short label; the precise claim, in one or two sentences; a verdict of trivial, defensible, or false-or-contested; and a one-line note on whether that reading supports the reviewer's dismissal of the confirmation step. Roughly 350–450 words.
2. "The load-bearing reading" — identify which reading or combination of readings the dismissal actually needs in order to work, and explain why the other readings, even if true, do not transfer to the normative conclusion. Roughly 120–150 words.
3. "Stress tests" — two or three cases in which even the load-bearing reading fails — for instance, a user's temporary impairment, a user who does not know what the assistant can do, or a third party (an employer, a manipulator) shaping the user's stated request — and for each, say which part of the principal–agent picture breaks. Roughly 150–200 words.
4. "A defensible replacement" — a single precise principle of deference that does the work the slogan was trying to do, stated so that it can be evaluated, plus a one-line note on what it gives up. Roughly 100–150 words.

No bibliography or citations needed — I want the analysis done from first principles.
