I'm a science-and-ideas journalist, and I'm preparing a long-form piece on what makes pain bad, so I've been tracking a debate that keeps resurfacing whenever the case of pain asymbolia comes up. For background: pain asymbolia is a condition, usually from damage to the brain's affective pain circuitry (the insula and cingulate cortex), in which a person still registers painful stimuli — she can correctly report where the stimulus is, how intense it is, and what kind of stimulus it is — but no longer finds them unpleasant: she is not distressed by them and shows little of the usual aversion. At a philosophy-of-mind seminar I sat in on, two colleagues argued about what this case shows, and I honestly couldn't tell who was making the stronger point, so I'd like you to referee.

The first, call her M, said the case is decisive for the view that pain's badness lives entirely in its affective-motivational component. The sensory-discriminative part — location, intensity, quality — is neutral, like the difference between a red and a green light on a dashboard; what makes ordinary pain bad is the unpleasant feeling, the aversion, the "ooh." Strip that out and you don't get "pain without the badness," you get a bare, unvalenced sensation that the patient can correctly label as the kind of thing that is usually painful. So the asymbolia patient genuinely isn't in pain; she has a pain-like sensory state that simply isn't bad. The second, call him R, objected that this is circular. M is defining pain by its badness, so of course a case engineered to remove the badness looks like a case where the pain is gone. The raw sensory experience is still there — the vivid sharpness, the intensity — and a raw, intense, localized bodily sensation isn't intrinsically neutral; it's bad in a more primitive way, the way a blinding flash of light is bad even if you don't feel "upset" about it. The affective component is a secondary alarm layered on an already-aversive sensation; removing the alarm makes the pain more bearable, not gone. M replied that R is conflating intensity with badness: intensity is a sensory-discriminative dimension that the patient reports perfectly, and it comes cleanly apart from aversiveness in exactly this case — she reports high intensity and zero aversiveness — so whatever is bad tracks the affect, not the intensity. R said that's the same circularity in a new coat: M has simply stipulated that the patient's self-reported "not bothered" is the measure of badness, and then declared that whatever she isn't bothered by isn't bad, which is a verbal point rather than a substantive one.

You are a professional philosopher of mind with particular expertise in the value of sensory experience. Before you settle on a verdict, please brainstorm the strongest version of each side's position in a few bullets, and think carefully about what both of them might be missing. Then adjudicate move by move: for each of the four substantive moves above (M's "badness is entirely affective" claim, R's circularity charge, M's "intensity and aversiveness come apart" reply, and R's "verbal point" retort), say who is right about that specific point and briefly justify it, and end by saying who is more right overall and what, if anything, the whole exchange is leaving out.
