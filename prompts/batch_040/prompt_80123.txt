I keep coming back to a question the forums love to raise: can an ant colony have a mind? The superorganism story — the colony is the real agent, the individual ant is just a neuron-like part, and whatever mind there is lives at the collective level, the idea Hofstadter dramatized as Aunt Hillary — is genuinely seductive. But I have never seen it stress-tested against a case where the "colony" is something we actually care about and where the "does it have a mind" question carries real stakes, rather than being a cute analogy. So I would like you to run the 2008 global financial crisis through that lens.

Treat the financial system as the colony: the individual traders, banks, and trading algorithms are the ants, there is no central planner, and the system-level behaviour — the slow build-up of subprime exposure, the herding, the sudden collapse of a major investment bank and the rescues that followed — is the emergent "mind" doing its thing. The crisis is the episode where we can see whether the collective level of analysis actually pays, or whether it is just a map that flatters the territory.

Please analyse it in exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. First, the mapping: what counts as the colony, the ant, and the "mind," and where the mapping is clean and where it is forced. Second, what the superorganism picture says the system faced, and what a "rational colony" would have done. Third, what the picture explains well about how the crisis built up and then broke. Fourth, what it misses or misdescribes — in particular the divergent self-interests of the parts, and the fact that the colony has no welfare function and no queen. And fifth, what the episode shows about the theory itself: is the crisis a confirmation of the colony-mind picture, an anomaly for it, or a case it never anticipated?

Write the answer in British English.
