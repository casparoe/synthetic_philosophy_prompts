Last night I had a long, fairly heated argument with a friend about whether "cancel culture" — or, more precisely, the practice of social sanction (ostracism, loss of employment, deplatforming) in response to a perceived moral wrong — should be understood as a form of punishment. He closed the argument by stating his position as a compact summary, which I've written down below. I can't tell where he's being careful and where he's being sloppy, and I'd like a claim-by-claim audit from the standpoint of someone working in the philosophy of punishment and the legal philosophy of speech.

His summary: "Cancel culture isn't punishment, because punishment requires an authority — a court, a state, or an institution with legitimate power to impose a sanction. What's actually happening online is just people updating their beliefs in light of new information and adjusting their behavior accordingly, which is a normal and healthy part of moral life. And when a company deplatforms someone, that's just the private market exercising its right to choose who it does business with, no different from a landlord refusing to rent to a tenant; the person's speech isn't censored, since they can still make it on other platforms or in person. Even where people feel anger toward the person who was 'canceled,' that anger is a rational response to a genuine moral wrong, so describing the whole practice as 'punishment' just smuggles in the assumption that the person who was targeted actually did something wrong. On the whole, then, calling it 'cancel culture' or 'punishment' is a category error: it's really just rational belief-revision and ordinary market choice."

Please separate, for each claim, what a careful philosopher would accept, what is a common simplification that distorts the underlying idea, and what is simply wrong. My own inclination is that he's wrong on the authority point, but I'm genuinely unsure about the deplatforming point, so I'd be especially glad of help there. In particular, I'd like you to (a) address whether the concept of punishment really does require a legitimate authority, or whether that conflates two different questions; (b) examine the deplatforming point and the landlord analogy, including whether removing a venue or an audience is the same kind of thing as refusing a private transaction, and what that difference (if any) does for the claim that no sanction is involved; and (c) say whether his conclusion — that the whole practice is "just" belief-revision and market choice — follows from the parts of his summary that survive correction. I'm not after a bibliography or a list of papers; I want the conceptual work done on the merits.
