I've been thinking about what happens when a political leader does something terrible but genuinely necessary — ordering a strike, say, that kills a few civilians to stop an attack that would kill thousands. I've landed on a view I want stress-tested: the leader's hands get "dirty" even though they did the right thing, and the guilt that doesn't wash off afterward is not a malfunction but a required part of moral fitness — a leader who feels no guilt after such an act has failed to register that something truly awful happened, and is morally worse, not cleaner. That lingering guilt is the point, not a side effect. What would count against this? I want the arguments and intuitions that should actually move me, ranked from most to least decisive — including the case that the office of the statesman genuinely licenses the act, so no real wrong is done and the guilt is a category error; the case that guilt is just a false-positive alarm that should switch off once the act is correctly judged; the case that the guilt really tracks luck (two leaders make the identical call, one's bomb hits a hospital, and the one who feels more guilty is just the unlucky one, not the one who did something worse); and the practical case that demanding this guilt paralyzes the people who most need to be able to act. I also want an honest read on whether the view has been quietly qualified into unfalsifiability — if "too much guilt is paralysis, too little is moral blindness" lets it absorb any outcome, does it actually risk anything? And which of these would a committed defender concede as counting against it, versus dismiss?
