I'm a mid-career cognitive scientist who has spent about a decade on cross-modal perception, and I'm trying to decide where to aim a small new lab—five or six people, mostly behavioral work with some fMRI, no computational-modeling group—for the next four or five years. I keep circling back to Molyneux's problem, but not in the way the philosophy literature usually discusses it. I have no interest in the Locke-versus-Leibniz framing or in the sphere-and-cube case as a standalone curiosity. What I find genuinely unresolved is a boundary that most of the work treats as if it were a single issue: the claim that a person born blind, on first seeing, cannot immediately recognize a shape they know only by touch, and the claim that there is no deep, modality-independent conceptual structure shared between vision and touch. I've been calling these the recognition claim and the concept claim, and my working suspicion is that the empirical results—cataract-restoration studies, the delayed-recognition findings—mostly speak to the first, while the philosophical debate has mostly been about the second, and that everyone keeps assuming the two come and go together when they may not.

Given that, I'd like you to help me set an agenda. Of the open questions in this area—which, as I see them, include whether the cross-modal gap is a matter of missing concepts versus missing learned mappings, whether there is any innate cross-modal common currency of shape and texture, whether the direction of transfer (touch-to-sight versus sight-to-touch) is asymmetric, and what the delayed-recognition results actually do and do not establish—tell me which two or three are most worth a lab with my skill set investing in, and in what order. I want the ordering defended rather than just listed: for each, why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is with the methods I actually have, and what evidence over the next couple of years would move it up or down. And be candid about which of these are overrated—where the philosophical prestige is high but the payoff for a working cognitive scientist is low. Please answer in American English.
