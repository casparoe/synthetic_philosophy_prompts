I'm a novelist and I've waded into a rabbit hole I can't get out of. I'm writing a book where the protagonist is, in a real sense, scattered — the draft has them existing in pieces across several locations, and my editor keeps asking wether that's one character or a bunch of them, and I realized the only honest way to answer is the special composition question, i.e., when do some things make up something. So I've been reading the debate between the Lewis-style universalists, who think that any collection of things, no matter how scattered or absurd, composes an object, the nihilists, who think composite objects basically don't exist, and the van Inwagen-style moderates in between, who say composition is real but constrained, like only for animals and persons.

Here's what's bugging me: as far as I can tell this has been going on for decades without moving an inch, and both sides agree about everything you can actually point at — the table, the cat, the person. The only cases they disagree about are the gerrymandered ones, like the cat plus the moon. And what I keep noticing is that neither side actually stakes out the weird cases in earnest: the universalist concedes that asking about cat-plus-moon isn't a serious question, and the nihilist concedes the gerrymandered cases and treats talk of tables and cats as convenient shorthand for arrangements of simples. So my working hypothesis is that this isn't really one debate but but two questions under one name — the universalists are answering "how many objects are there?" while the nihilists are answering "is that question worth asking at all?" — and they're just talking past each other, with the real disagreement being about which questions count as metaphysically serious, which is a starting commitment neither side can argue for.

Can you diagnose it for me? I want to know whether this is a verbal dispute about the word "object," a substantive disagreement in disguise, two questions under one name, or maybe a hidden formal question doing the work (like whether "the sum of" is a total function or a partial one) — with reasons from what each side actually argues, not a survey of the history. And given your diagnosis, be honest about what would count as progress: a new distinction, a formal result, a rephrasing of the question, or should we just expect this to keep going forever? It matters to me because my editor wants to know if the scattered character is one person, and I'd like to know whether the philosophers' impasse means the question is genuinely open (in which case I pick an answer and own it) or means the question is malformed (in which case I need to rethink the premise of the book).
