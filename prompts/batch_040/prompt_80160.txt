I'm doing a third-year seminar on the metaphysics of reasons and we've been going round and round about what I'd call "free-floating rationales" — the idea that there might be normative reasons that don't attach to anyone at all. No existing person is in a position to act on them, no one's interests or well-being are at stake, no one could even care about them — and yet they're supposed to be genuine reasons, not just a feature of how we talk. My lecturer thinks the whole idea is basicly incoherent: a reason has to be a reason *for* someone, so a reason with no reasoner is a contradiction in terms. My friend thinks it's obviously true, e.g. there are facts about what's good or bad that hold whether or not anyone is around to notice.

Here's the concrete case I keep coming back to because I think it's the cleanest test: you promised Alice to do X. Alice is now dead. No one else knows about the promise, no one will ever be affected by whether you do X, and you yourself have no desire to do it. Is there still a genuine normative reason for you to do X? If the reason survives in a case like this, it really isn't anchored to any living person, which is exactly what free-floating is supposed to be.
