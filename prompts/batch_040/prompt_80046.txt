I'm in a philosophy of mind seminar and we've been stuck on the Dennett–Searle dispute about original versus derived intentionality for like three weeks now, and I cant tell if it's actually resolvable. Everyone in the group knows the Chinese Room by heart, so I keep circling back to the one example nobody really engages with, which is Dennett's two-bitser: a dead simple two-state machine where the exact same internal state can be read as meaning one thing in one setup and a completely different thing in another, with nothing about the machine's inner nature changing at all. The point, as I understand it, is that if a state's content can just flip like that, then content isn't intrinsic to the state, so the original/derived line is doing a lot less work than Searle thinks. Searle, as far as I can tell, never really answers the two-bitser directly — he just keeps asserting that the brain has this intrinsic causal power to produce intentionality that machines lack, kind of like a heart's power to pump blood.

My working hypothesis is that the two sides are answering different questions under one name: Dennett is making a conceptual point about whether "intrinsic content" is even a well-defined property, while Searle is making a quasi-empirical claim about what brains are made of, and they just keep passing each other. Is that the right diagnosis, or is it a genuine substantive metaphysical disagreement that could in principle be settled? And what would actually count as progress here — some principled line that separates the two-bitser from a real brain, a demonstration that the two-bitser's trick applies to neural states too, or is there just no fact of the matter and the impasse is the point? I don't want a survey of the arguments, I want the structure of the disagreement diagnosed and an honest take on whether anything could end it. Keep the whole thing under 450 words.
