The Sleeping Beauty problem has been debated for over twenty years, and the two main camps have not converged. Let me lay out the setup as I understand it and then give you my working theory about why the debate is stuck; what I want is a diagnosis of the disagreement, not a verdict on who is right and not a survey of the arguments.

The setup: a fair coin is tossed. If it lands heads, a subject named Beauty is awakened once, on Monday, and then returned to sleep with her memory erased. If it lands tails, she is awakened on Monday, her memory is erased, and she is awakened again on Tuesday. When she is awakened she has no way of knowing which day it is or what the coin landed. The question is what credence she should assign to the coin having landed heads.

The two camps: the "halfer" says her credence should be 1/2, on the ground that the coin is fair and the fact of her being awakened is not new information about the coin, since she was going to be awakened in either case. The "thirder" says her credence should be 1/3, on the ground that there are three equally possible centered awakenings — heads-Monday, tails-Monday, tails-Tuesday — and a self-locating principle of indifference gives each probability 1/3, so the heads-worlds come to 1/3 in total. Both camps agree on the setup, on the coin being fair, and on the question being about her credence at the awakening; they disagree only about the answer.

The debate has generated a large literature — betting arguments, Dutch book arguments, a "long" version in which the coin is tossed many times and the fraction of awakenings that occur in heads-worlds converges to 1/3 — but the camps have not moved.

My working theory is that this is not a substantive disagreement about probability. I think the two sides are reasoning over different sample spaces and, in effect, answering two different questions under one name: the halfer is asking "what is the probability the coin landed heads?" while the thirder is asking "what is the probability that this particular awakening occurs in a heads-world?" And I suspect the betting and Dutch book arguments, which are usually taken to settle the question in the thirder's favor, are really decision-theoretic coherence arguments — they show which credence assignment avoids being Dutch-booked, which is a constraint on betting behavior, not a constraint on what the probability "really" is. If that's right, then the position that the problem is simply ill-posed, because it is unclear what "probability" is even supposed to be in a self-locating situation, is the real diagnosis, and the impasse is a sign that the question is misframed rather than merely hard.

Using what each side actually argues, tell me which of these is correct: a substantive disagreement about how to apply a fixed notion of probability to a self-locating case; a verbal disagreement about the meaning of "probability"; or two camps answering different questions (a probability over coin-toss worlds versus a probability over centered awakenings) under a single heading. Is there a hidden formal or conceptual question doing the work that neither side has isolated? And given your diagnosis, what, if anything, would count as progress — a distinction that reframes the question, a formal result, a change of question — or is there a good argument that no progress should be expected, and if so why?
