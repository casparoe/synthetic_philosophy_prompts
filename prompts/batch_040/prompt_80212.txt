I've come to hold what I'll call the "thin" reading of Davidson's principle of charity in radical interpretation: that the principle is a methodological constraint on the interpreter — a rule for fixing the meaning of the interpreter's own vocabulary by requiring that the subject be interpreted as coming out mostly rational — rather than a substantive empirical claim that the subject is in fact mostly rational or that we mostly agree with them. On this reading, when a radical interpreter and the subject appear to disagree, the interpreter's first obligation is to doubt their own semantic assumptions, not to attribute irrationality to the subject. What would count against this reading? I want the arguments and cases that should actually move me, ranked by how decisive they'd be — not just the standard objections — including the case of a subject who is systematically wrong about an entire domain in a way that is behaviorally manifest, so that their actions and predictions in that domain track their false beliefs, and reinterpreting their terms to make them come out right would cost the interpreter predictive power over the subject. Which of these would a defender of the thin reading concede as counting against it, and which would they dismiss? And give me an honest assessment of whether the reading has been qualified into unfalsifiability — whether any disagreement can always be explained by retreating to "the interpreter's semantic theory was wrong" — and if so, whether that insulation is a defect or a feature for a methodological principle of this kind.
