Hey, you're a philosopher of biology who also actually reads the collective-behavior and animal-cognition literature, not just the philosophy side. I'm writing a paper section on whether an ant colony can have a mind, and I need a thought experiment built to a very specific spec. First, the two camps, stated the way I want them treated:

- Camp F, the superorganists: a colony has a mind if it shows the functional hallmarks of a mind at the colony level — a coherent goal hierarchy, integrated and adaptive behavior, something like memory, and a self/other distinction. The fact that it's built out of individuals is no disqualifier, any more than a brain being built out of neurons is. Minds can be distributed across a system.
- Camp I, the organizationalists: a colony is an organization, not a mind. It's more like a company or a city than like a brain. It can coordinate and even pull off complex collective behavior, but it's a network of individuals each following local rules (pheromone trails, mouth-to-mouth food sharing, local cues), and the "colony-level" behavior is an emergent pattern, not a mental state. A swarm of non-mindful individuals doesn't add up to a mind the way a crowd of people doesn't add up to a person.

Now the spec. The deficit has to be focal — one specific function lost, the rest working — and it has to track the severed channel. Hold everything else fixed; this is a single-variable lesion, the colony behaving like a fugue (many voices weaving one pattern, no conductor) right up until you cut one thread. Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph.
