I've recently started a project reconstructing the history of a small community from its administrative records — census rolls, land deeds, court dockets, tax ledgers — and in the last few weeks a colleague has kept pushing back on my caution by saying the archive is "a window onto the past": the records are simply there, you look through the glass and report what you see, and any residual distortion is a matter of the researcher's interpretation rather than of the source itself. I think the metaphor is quietly doing a lot of the argumentative work, and I'd like it audited rather than dismissed.

Could you first lay out the correspondence table — what plays the role of the window, the glass, the frame, the light, the view, and the thing outside — and then show where the mapping fails. Two features of my material are especially important. First, the people I actually want to study appear in these records almost exclusively as objects of administration: named in a deed, tallied in a roll, listed as a party in a docket, but almost never in their own voice or as the authors of anything. Second, the gaps are not random smudges on the glass; they track the categories and purposes of the office that produced the record, so that what is missing is missing in a pattern that reflects the recorder's interests rather than the community's life. Given those two features, which of the resulting disanalogies actually bear on the conclusion my colleague is trying to license — that the archive affords neutral, objective access to the past — and which are merely surface mismatches that don't threaten it? Does that "neutral access" conclusion hold up once the disanalogies are on the table, and if it doesn't, what would be a better analogy for what an archive actually is?
