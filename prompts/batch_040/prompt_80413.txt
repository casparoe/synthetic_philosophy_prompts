I'm preparing a book-club discussion of Ian McEwan's Atonement and want to read it through the debate over objectivity in history. The novel's central device is that the young Briony's misreading of a single scene produces a false fact that ruins two lives, and it ends by revealing that the narrator has fabricated the reconciliation ending the characters never actually get. Read the novel against two theses: Carr's claim that historical facts are not simply given but are selected by the historian from the raw material of events, and White's claim that a narrative's choice of emplotment — tragedy, comedy, romance, or satire — is not determined by the facts it describes. Does Briony's error track Carr's point, or does it show something Carr would reject? Does the fabricated ending illustrate White's claim that emplotment is a free, quasi-literary choice, or does the novel resist that reading by making the fabrication an act of guilt and atonement rather than a neutral structural decision? And where, if anywhere, does the novel smuggle back in the Rankean assumption that there is a 'how it actually was' that the narration has betrayed?
