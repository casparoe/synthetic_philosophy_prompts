I've been trying to pin down what I actually believe about the difference between killing someone and letting them die, and I keep getting tangled up because I suspect it is not the same thing as the difference between doing something and merely allowing something to happen. So I have written down my gut verdicts on a handful of cases. I would like you to recover the principle, or the small set of principles, that sits behind them, apply it to a couple of cases I have not listed, and tell me honestly where it fails to capture one of my verdicts.

Here are my verdicts.

1. The harvest. A surgeon has a healthy, anesthetized patient on the operating table whose five organs would each save a different dying patient. Harvesting them would kill the healthy patient. I think it would be a grave wrong to go ahead, and the surgeon is right to decline.

2. The cable. A runaway vehicle is barreling down a track toward five workers. I can yank a cable that redirects it onto a side track, where it will kill one worker. I think yanking the cable is permissible, though I am not sure it is obligatory.

3. The pond. A jogger sees a small child who has wandered into a shallow, clear pond and is drowning. She could wade in and pull the child out in a few seconds, at the cost of ruining an expensive pair of shoes. I think she is seriously wrong to keep walking.

4. The registry. A person ticks the box declining to be an organ donor when renewing a driver's license. Years later, a compatible patient dies in part because of the ongoing shortage of donors. I do not think this person is really blameworthy, or at most that it was a mild lapse.

5. The ventilator. At the request of a terminally ill patient, a doctor disconnects the ventilator, and the patient dies within the hour of the underlying cancer. I think this is permissible, and I would describe it as letting the patient die rather than killing him.

6. The injection. A doctor, with a terminally ill patient in severe pain who has not asked to die, administers a dose of a drug large enough to stop the patient's breathing, in order to end the suffering, and the patient dies. I think this is not permissible, and I would describe it as killing the patient.

The pairs that trouble me. The harvest and the cable both involve killing one person in order to save five, yet I call one a grave wrong and the other permissible. The pond and the registry are both omissions that help bring about a death, yet I call one seriously wrong and the other barely blameworthy. And the ventilator and the injection are both acts by a doctor that bring a patient's death forward, yet I call one letting die and the other killing. So the doing/allowing line and the killing/letting-die line clearly do not track each other, and neither one, by itself, explains my verdicts.

What I want from you. First, a principle, or a small set of principles, stated precisely enough that it could be applied to a case I have not seen, with a note on which of my cases motivates each clause. Second, its verdicts on two cases I have left out: (a) my pushing a large, otherwise healthy stranger off a footbridge onto the track so that his body stops the runaway vehicle and saves the five workers, killing him; and (b) a doctor who, to ease a dying patient's anxiety in the final hours, gives a dose of a sedative that she knows will probably hasten the patient's death by a few hours, but whose aim is comfort rather than death, and the patient dies. Third, an honest statement of which of my six verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether you think I should drop that verdict, or whether saving it is worth the extra complexity the principle must take on.
