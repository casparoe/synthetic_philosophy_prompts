I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm writing a profile of a historian who sorts historical works into "Whig" history — writing the past as a progressive path toward the present — and "contextual" history. I've tracked her calls on recent works: Whig, a history of the English Reformation cast as the essential first step toward modern religious liberty and parliamentary sovereignty, scolding the medieval clergy for their superstition; Whig, a history of 19th-century cholera that presents the outbreaks as the catalyst that inevitably produced modern public health, dismissing the contemporary folk remedies as ignorant; Whig, a history of the printing press treated as the cause of the Reformation and the Enlightenment, mocking the scribes as backward. Contextual, a history of the 1918 influenza that records the mortality and the varied local responses without ranking them by their contribution to later medicine or judging the responders by our standards; and contextual, a history of an 18th-century textile mill that stays with the workers' daily experience, does not treat the mill as a stepping stone to the factory system, and does not grade the workers' choices by modern efficiency. A new book has just come out: a history of 20th-century architecture that devotes most of its space to the buildings still standing and celebrated today, describing each in its own time and in the terms of its own period, without claiming that architecture "progressed" toward them and without judging them by today's aesthetic. Please give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the rule she is most plausibly running and why; (2) the strongest alternative rule that fits her track record just as well; (3) her predicted verdict on the new book under your best-guess rule; (4) the verdict the new book would receive under the alternative rule; and (5) a further historical narrative that would cleanly separate the two rules.
