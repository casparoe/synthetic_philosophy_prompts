In his account of "real patterns," Daniel Dennett holds that a pattern is genuinely objective — a feature of the world itself, rather than a projection of the observer's interpretation — if and only if it is compression-optimal: it is the most efficient description of the data it captures, in the sense that no shorter description predicts that data at least as well. On this view, the three stances — the physical stance (predicting a system's behavior from its physical structure and the laws of nature), the design stance (predicting its behavior from its purpose or function), and the intentional stance (predicting its behavior by attributing beliefs and desires to it and treating it as rational) — are simply competing descriptions of the same system, and the correct stance to take toward a system is the one that yields the best compression of its behavior.

I would like you to argue the following position: that this compression-optimality criterion does not deliver the ontological objectivity Dennett intends for "real patterns," and that "real patterns" are therefore not mind-independent features of the world but projections of our predictive practices.

Please build the argument around the following point, which I take to be the crux. Deciding which description is the best compression of a body of data requires a rule for trading off two features of a candidate description: its predictive accuracy and its brevity. There is no interest-independent fact about how to weight these two features against one another; the weighting is fixed only by a choice of scoring (or loss) function, and different admissible choices select different best descriptions. Argue, on this basis, that the set of "real patterns" a system has is relative to our choice of scoring function, and that the intentional stance is accordingly not "real" in any mind-independent sense but a useful projection whose "reality" is contingent on the values we bring to the task of modeling.

To make the point concrete, work through a contrast between a thermostatically controlled room and a human agent, and explain how, on the argument, the "reality" of the intentional description of each is a matter of our modeling interests rather than of the structure of the world.
