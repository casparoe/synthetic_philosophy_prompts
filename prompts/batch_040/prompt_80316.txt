I've been going in circles with a friend who works in an intensive care unit over a configuration that keeps coming up whenever we argue about when a person has actually died, and I can't tell whether it has a name or whether I'm just reinventing a distinction the field already has a label for. The shape of it is this. Picture a cardiac arrest: the heart and the breathing stop, and by the circulation-based way of marking death the person is plainly no longer alive — a bystander at the scene would say they are dead. But after twenty minutes they are resuscitated and wake up with no neurological damage whatsoever. Now the family is adamant that the person was dead for those twenty minutes, "dead and brought back," while the intensivist insists the person was never dead at all, only in a kind of suspended animation, because nothing that could not be undone had happened to the brain. The circulation-based criterion and the brain-based criterion are not merely giving different timestamps here; they are giving opposite answers to whether death occurred, and there is no moment at which the two converge, because the heart stopped and then restarted before the brain was ever in question.

I've seen a second, cleaner version of the same thing. A patient is on a ventilator and the heart is still beating, but the entire brain, including the part that keeps the body integrated, has flatlined and will not recover. By the brain-based criterion the person is dead, full stop, even though the heart is going; by a strict reading of the circulation-based criterion the person is alive, because the circulation is intact. Again the two criteria are not disagreeing about when — they are disagreeing about whether.

So here is my question. Is there an established term for this specific configuration — the case where the two standard criteria for death do not merely disagree about the time of death, but disagree about whether death happened at all? My working hypothesis is that there is no single clean name, and that what I'm noticing is actually a cluster of related but distinct ideas: the idea that death only counts if it cannot be reversed (so a reversible arrest never was a death), the waiting period that some organ-donation protocols impose after the heart stops in order to make sure it really will not start again, and the more general point that the two criteria simply carve the space of possible cases in different ways. But I want to be sure I'm not missing an established label, and I won't be satisfied with a bare term. What I actually want is the carve: what exactly is this phenomenon (disagreement about whether, as opposed to disagreement about when), how does it differ from each of the nearby ideas I have listed, which of those, if any, is the best fit, and where are its edges — for instance, does it bite just as hard in the ventilator case as in the arrest case, and does the answer depend on how "cannot be reversed" is understood?
