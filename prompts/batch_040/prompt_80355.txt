I will be moderating the audience discussion that follows a conference talk, and the speaker is a political theorist who defends a specific account of what corruption is. Her view, in brief, is that corruption is not simply the violation of a rule or the pursuit of private gain in public office, but the betrayal of a fiduciary relationship of trust between an office-holder and the public. On this account, an act can be illegal without being corrupt — if it breaks a rule but betrays no trust — and corrupt without being illegal — if it betrays the trust the office is supposed to embody while no rule is formally broken. She extends the account to two contested cases. In the first, clientelism — the exchange of public goods or favors for votes — she holds to be genuinely corrupt in high-capacity states, where the office-holder is trusted to allocate goods according to impersonal public criteria, but not the same kind of wrong in low-capacity states, where the state has no credible commitment to providing public goods at all, so that the exchange functions as a distorted form of governance rather than as a betrayal of a trust that already exists. In the second, gift-giving to officials, she treats as a boundary case: in a number of societies it is a norm of social integration rather than a quid pro quo, and she argues that labeling it corruption is a form of conceptual imperialism, exporting the assumptions of a particular, Western and high-capacity, political context.

Please draft nine to eleven questions for the discussion. I want them to do real work on the substance of her position rather than simply have her restate it. In particular, I want at least one question each on: why the fiduciary-trust account is to be preferred over the simpler rule-violation account, and what it buys us; the asymmetry between high- and low-capacity states in the clientelism case, and whether that asymmetry merely tracks the power of the states that happen to set the standard; the gift-giving boundary case, and where, on her account, the line between a culturally normative gift and a bribe is to be drawn; and what, if anything, survives as a minimal cross-cultural core of corruption once the trust-based and context-dependent elements are allowed to vary. For each of these four, please also give me a follow-up question keyed to the answer she is most likely to offer, so that I can press her at the point where the standard reply leaves her most exposed.
