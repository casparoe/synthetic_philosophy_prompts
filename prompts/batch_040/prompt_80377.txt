I work in epidemiology and do most of my thinking in causal diagrams — directed acyclic graphs, the do-operator, direct versus indirect effects, mediation, potential outcomes, and counterfactuals. I've been trying to make sense of the doctrine of double effect, which I understand roughly as the claim that an act producing a good effect and a foreseen but unwanted bad effect can still be permissible, on the conditions that the act itself is not intrinsically bad, that the agent intends only the good effect, that the bad effect is not the means by which the good is brought about, and that there is a proportionate reason for tolerating the bad effect. I'd like you to restate that doctrine, condition by condition, in the vocabulary I actually use: what the intervention is, which outcomes sit on the causal path from the intervention to the good outcome and which fork off as side branches, and so on. Keep the translation honest — where my vocabulary forces you to stretch something or to leave it out, say so rather than papering over it.

Then work through the two cases that are supposed to do the doctrine's real work: diverting a runaway trolley from five people onto a spur where it will kill one, versus pushing a heavy man off a bridge so that his body stops the trolley and saves the five. In my language I'd expect the first to be a case in which the one person's death is a forked-off side effect — not on the mediating path to the five being saved — and the second to be a case in which the man's death is itself the mediator through which the five are saved. Confirm or correct that reading, and tell me precisely where the causal picture stops being adequate.

Finally, give me the residue report: which parts of the doctrine translate cleanly into causal terms, which only translate with distortion, and which have no expression in my vocabulary at all. I suspect the intention condition is the one that doesn't survive — that two acts with identical causal structure can differ in moral status purely because of what the agent is aiming at, as when a physician gives a dying patient a dose of morphine that will hasten his death, which the doctrine may permit if she intends only pain relief but not if she intends the death, even though in my vocabulary the two acts are the same intervention with the same downstream effects. Tell me whether that residue is a genuine limitation of the causal framework, or a sign that the doctrine was less precise than its defenders think.
