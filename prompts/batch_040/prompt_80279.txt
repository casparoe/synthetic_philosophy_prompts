I'm a PhD student working on the philosophy of social norms, and I've been working with a colleague who has been classifying everyday social practices as either conventions or not. By a convention she means, roughly, a regular, recurrent way of doing things that holds because each participant expects the others to do it, rather than because the practice is intrinsically the best available option. She has given me a set of verdicts, and I'm trying to reverse-engineer the exact test she is applying, because I suspect she is quietly running one of a couple of slightly different principles, and I want to pin down which one before I put my own cases in front of her.

Her verdicts so far: she counts driving on the right-hand side of the road in a right-driving country as a convention, and she counts greeting an acquaintance with a handshake as a convention. She does not count the local habit of eating dinner around seven o'clock as a convention, on the ground that there is no problem to coordinate on — each person simply chooses for herself when to eat, and the fact that most people happen to eat around then is incidental. She also does not count following the official rules of chess as a convention, on the ground that the rules are fixed and define the game rather than being one option among several equally good ones. Now here is the case I am unsure about. In a newly founded startup, the founder has decreed that all daily stand-up meetings will begin at 9:00 a.m. sharp, and she has made clear that chronic lateness will cost you your job. There is no substantive reason to prefer 9:00 over 9:15 or 9:30 — any of them would serve the purpose equally well — but everyone now shows up at 9:00 because of the founder's rule, not because they independently expect one another to.

Given her track record, do you think she will classify the startup's 9:00 stand-up rule as a convention or not? I am less interested in a bare yes or no than in the rule she appears to be running. Two candidate principles fit all four of her earlier verdicts equally well: one says a practice is a convention just in case it solves a coordination problem for which there is no uniquely best solution, and the other says a practice is a convention just in case it is sustained by common expectation of convergence. Could you say what each of these predicts for the startup case, give me your best guess as to which one she is actually running and why, and name one further case that would cleanly separate the two? And since what we are ultimately after is how norms come to be, not just how they are sustained, I'd also like your view on whether a practice that is currently held up only by outside enforcement can, over time, mature into a genuine convention once the enforcement is dropped and people keep doing it out of mutual expectation — and whether that answer should change how she rules on the startup case.
