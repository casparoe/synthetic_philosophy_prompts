You're a cognitive scientist who's spent about a decade working on concept learning and Bongard problems, and you're advising a grad student who wants to build a theory of what it takes to solve a Bongard problem -- not just a system that can solve them, but an account of the requirements any adequate solver, human or machine, has to meet.

Quick refresher so we're on the same page, because the details matter: a Bongard problem shows two boxes, six figures in each. The task is to name a concept that's true of all six figures in one box and false of all six in the other. There's a solution key, so there's one "intended" concept. But here's the wrinkle that makes these interesting for concept induction rather than plain pattern matching: the twelve figures are usually consistent with more then one concept. The intended one is the setter's, and it's not recoverable from the twelve examples by logic alone -- it's the concept that's "natural" or "robust," the one that would correctly classify a new thirteenth figure and that a human could state out loud. There's also a known tension: the intended concept is usually the simplest one that fits, but some puzzles are constructed so that a naive "pick the shortest description that fits" strategy picks the wrong answer. And the six examples in each box are deliberately built to share a bunch of surface features that are NOT the concept -- size, orientation, position, extra shapes -- so a good solver has to ignore a lot of whats on the page.

Given all that, write the desiderata: the requirements any adequate theory of Bongard problem solving has to satisfy, with the motivating case or consideration behind each one. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable (drop them and you don't have a theory of Bongard solving) and which are just preferences of particular camps -- e.g. "the solver must be fast" or "the concept must be human-legible": are those genuine requirements or just what Bongard happened to care about? Then: is there a subset of these desiderata that's jointly unsatisfiable, and if so which, and why. I suspect the simplicity tiebreaker and the anti-simplicity puzzles are in tension, but I want you to check whether there's a deeper conflict -- maybe between "recover the intended concept" and "the intended concept is external to the examples." And finally, which single requirement knocks out the most candidate theories you can think of (a pure minimum-description-length solver, a black-box few-shot classifier, a symbolic search over a fixed feature vocabulary, whatever else is out there). A short scorecard at the end is fine, but keep the weight on the criteria and their cases, that's where the thinking is.

Think it through carefully before you write; a short brainstorm of candidate requirements at the top is fine, then the final list.
