I'm in a seminar on the ethics of climate change, and a fellow student has been pressing an argument on me that I think contains a genuine move but that I can't get past, because the conclusion is one I can't accept and I can't find the step that breaks. He's trying to show that we have no moral reason to cut emissions, and he's confident the argument is airtight. Here is his argument, as close to his own reconstruction as I can get it:

(1) Which particular people will be alive in 2100 depends on our emissions policy, because policy affects the timing of births, and the timing of a birth determines which sperm meets which egg, so a different policy yields a different set of people.
(2) So the people who will live in 2100 under a high-emissions policy are not the same people who would have lived in 2100 under a low-emissions policy.
(3) A person is harmed by a choice only if the choice makes that person worse off than they would otherwise have been.
(4) The 2100 people who exist under high emissions would not have existed at all under low emissions.
(5) For a person whose life is worth living, not existing is not worse for them than existing with that life.
(6) So high emissions does not make any 2100 person worse off than they would otherwise have been.
(7) So high emissions harms no one.
(8) So we have no moral reason to cut emissions.

His whole point, as he sees it, is that the people we would save by cutting emissions are not the same people who would be harmed by not cutting them, so there is no one to wrong. I think there is a real insight buried in the chain, but I cannot name where it fails. Which step should I reject, and what do I give up if I reject it? If I have to choose, I would rather keep the idea that we do have reasons to cut emissions than give up any single premise, but I want to understand what each option costs.
