I don't have a philosophy background — I'm a software engineer — but I keep running into one slogan and I can't tell what it's actually claiming, so I'd like help disambiguating it. The slogan is "nobody is made worse off."

I hit it last week at a dinner, where a friend of mine who works in energy policy was defending a proposal to put a fee on carbon emissions and hand the revenue back to households as a flat per-person dividend. When someone objected that the fee would raise energy prices and hurt low-income households, she waved it off: "It's a Pareto improvement — nobody is made worse off. Most households come out ahead and the climate is better off, so what's the objection?" The objector pushed back, and she retreated to, "Well, in expectation, everyone is better off." I wasn't in a position to weigh in, and it's been nagging at me since.

What makes it feel slippery to me is the contrast with how the phrase works in my day job. Over there, "nobody is made worse off" is crisp: we ship a change, we run the benchmark suite, and if every metric goes up or holds and nothing regresses, we call it a win — the claim is checked against a specific model, the suite. In the policy conversation I'm pretty sure the slogan is doing a lot of quiet work that the engineering version doesn't. I've also since come across a distinction I'll just call the "safe" version: not "nobody is made worse off under our best model of what will happen," but "nobody is made worse off under any plausible model" — in my terms, the change is a win no matter which plausible parameterization of the world you run it against, the way a good test passes under every plausible config, not just the one you tuned it on. I don't know whether that's a standard term or just my phrasing; I'm interested in the idea either way.

So here's what I'd like. Take "nobody is made worse off" as it was being used in that exchange — to shut down the objection that low-income households lose — and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be standing in for. I can see at least these axes; add any I'm missing:

- Ex ante vs. ex post: is the claim that each person's expected welfare is higher before we know how things turn out, or that in every actual or possible outcome everyone is actually better off?
- Whose welfare, and by what measure: is "worse off" about income, about utility, about subjective well-being — and does a flat dividend that's the same dollar amount for everyone count as "not worse off" for a person for whom that dollar is worth more?
- The policy alone vs. the package: is the claim about the fee by itself, or about the fee-plus-dividend package, where the dividend is doing the compensating for the people the fee hurts?
- Actual vs. potential: does "nobody is made worse off" require that everyone actually ends up better off, or is it enough that the winners could in principle compensate the losers, even if they don't?
- Under which model: is the claim relative to one specific forecast of how the economy and the climate will respond, and how does that compare to the "safe" version that has to hold across every plausible model?
- Who counts: does "nobody" include people not yet born, who presumably get the climate benefit but don't pay the fee?

For each, tell me which are trivial (true on basically any reading), which are substantive and defensible, and which are false or incoherent. Then — and this is the part I care about most — I don't just want a menu I have to pick from; I want you to commit. Tell me which single reading your friend most likely meant when she used the slogan to end the objection, and tell me whether the dismissive move — "nobody is made worse off, so what's the objection?" — is actually supported by any of the readings, or whether it only works if you quietly slide between them. Give me your reasons. And if you think the "safe" version is the one that should have been on the table, say so and say why the plain version was doing more work than it could bear.
