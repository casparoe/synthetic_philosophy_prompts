I've adopted the interventionist account of causation à la Woodward: a variable C is a cause of E just in case there could be an intervention on C — an exogenous manipulation that sets C to a chosen value while holding all other direct causes of C fixed — such that intervening on C would change the distribution of E. The theory's selling point is that it replaces the bare counterfactual "if C had not occurred, E would not have occurred" with a counterfactual about interventions, thereby (it is claimed) breaking the circularity and anthropocentrism that plague older manipulationist accounts. I want to be pressed on a specific seam of this view rather than the familiar objections: the status of causal claims in domains where no intervention is even physically possible — the causal structure of the very early universe, cosmological inflation, or the deep macroeconomic mechanisms that no agent could actually perturb. The question I keep returning to is whether the theory can genuinely ground such claims by appealing to merely *potential* interventions (interventions that no physical system could perform but that are "conceivable" in the right counterfactual sense), or whether at that point "potential intervention" quietly collapses back into an ordinary counterfactual and the interventionist apparatus is doing no real work — i.e., whether the theory is counterfactualism in disguise once you strip away actually or physically possible manipulations.

Given that framing, map out what would count against the interventionist account. I want the arguments and considerations that should actually move me, sorted by how decisive they would be, and I want you to separate what is genuinely fatal from what is merely awkward. Specifically: (1) which observations, thought-experiments, or formal results about causal structure would be predicted by the interventionist theory and not by a pure counterfactual account, and vice versa — so I can tell whether the two theories are empirically or extensionally distinguishable at all, or whether the interventionist machinery is a notational convenience over the same set of causal judgments; (2) which features of the early-universe and macroeconomics cases the theory can and cannot describe without either (a) smuggling in an unprincipled notion of "conceivability" of an impossible intervention, or (b) simply conceding those cases and thereby narrowing its scope; (3) what a committed interventionist would themselves concede as counting against them versus what they would dismiss as a misreading, and where the line between those two is honestly drawn; and (4) whether the view has been qualified — via the potential-intervention move, via restricting the domain to physically realizable interventions, via a pluralist stance — into a position that risks nothing, and whether that insulation, if present, is a defect for a theory of this kind or an acceptable cost.

Be committed. Take a definite position on whether the interventionist account survives the no-intervention-possible challenge, or whether it is counterfactualism in disguise, and give your reasons rather than laying out the options and leaving the choice to me. Where the theory is genuinely silent about a case, say so plainly instead of forcing it to speak, and flag where your own judgment rests on a contested reading of what counts as an intervention.
