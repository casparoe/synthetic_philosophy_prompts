Next month I'll be running the audience questions at a public lecture series on the design of public policy, and the speaker is a committed defender of libertarian paternalism — the view that the state is permitted to shape how choices are presented (defaults, opt-out structures, the salience of certain options) without that amounting to paternalism, precisely because the person remains free to choose differently. I have read enough of this debate that the standard objections — the autonomy objection, the slippery-slope worry, the charge that a nudge is just manipulation wearing a nicer label — feel well worn, and I would rather not spend the slot rehashing them.

I want a sequence of six to eight questions that start from the defender's own framing and then turn on a point I find genuinely underexplored, the baseline problem. The defender's case is that a nudge is non-paternalistic because it leaves the option set untouched, but the status quo default was itself put in place by someone, so there seems to be no neutral, non-paternalistic baseline to measure against: every default is already a paternalistic choice by whoever holds the pen, and the only difference between a nudge and plain paternalism is who made the last change. Please build the questions so that this baseline point gets progressively harder to answer, and end with a question about what would have to be true for a non-paternalistic nudge to be a coherent category rather than a relabeling of ordinary paternalism. For at least three of the questions, also give me a follow-up keyed to the reply the defender is most likely to offer — for instance a reply to the effect that the state is merely making the existing status quo easier to stick with rather than altering anyone's options, or that the real test is whether the person would regret the decision after a proper cooling-off period — so that I can press the point without derailing the discussion. I am not after questions that merely invite the speaker to restate the view; I want questions that force a choice between commitments.
