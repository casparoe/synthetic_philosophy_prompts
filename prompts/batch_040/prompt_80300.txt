Two friends of mine, Sam and Dana, got into a real argument at dinner last week over a news story, and I honestly couldn't tell who was right. The story goes like this: about five years ago a city council approved building a highway through a wetland that had been acting as a natural flood buffer for a low-lying neighborhood downstream. The council had a flood-risk assessment in front of them that flagged exactly this, but they signed it off anyway because the traffic and economic benefits were judged to outweigh the risk. Last month a big flood come through, the neighborhood got inundated, and three people died.

Sam says the council *did* the harm, not just allowed it: they deliberately destroyed the flood buffer, so but-for their decision the wetland would have soaked up the water and those three people would still be alive. Destroying a protection that was already in place is a doing, he argues, and the doing/allowing distinction is supposed to track real causal contribution, and this was one. Dana pushes back that the flood was an independent natural cause — the council didn't make it rain — so at most they failed to keep a buffer in place, which is a letting-die, not a killing. And she stresses the distinction is a moral one, not a causal one: we don't hold people to the same standard for allowing as for doing (nobody's obligated to throw themselves into a burning building the way they're obligated not to set it on fire), and the council's actual duty was to deliver the public benefit of the highway, not to preserve a wetland as infrastructure. Sam fires back that "independent cause" is a dodge, because the council's act is what created the vulnerablity in the first place, and they *knew* about it — they had the risk assessment — so knowingly creating the vulnerability is closer to a doing than to mere negligence. Dana's last move was that the but-for test is just too loose to do this work: but-for the council's vote, a million other things wouldn't have happened either, and which act you single out as "the" cause (the vote, the engineer's plans, the mayor's signature, the drivers who now need the road) is basically arbitrary, so the but-for test can't be what makes it a doing.
