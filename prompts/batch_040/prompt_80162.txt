I'm a program officer at a research foundation that funds multi-year projects, and each year I decide whether to continue or stop funding every project in our portfolio, most of which have already spent a large share of their budget. The usual advice is to ignore the sunk costs, but that feels too crude: some projects have genuinely learned things from the money already spent, and some of our continue-versus-stop calls are partly about what we signal to donors and partner labs. Write me a decision procedure my team could actually run on a project at review time—the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a stop-funding verdict, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of judgment or values rather than facts. It has to separate the money already spent, which should not enter the decision directly, from the evidence that spending produced, which should update our estimate of the project's prospects, and it has to give real weight to the strategic reason to continue, the value of signaling commitment, without letting that excuse a decision that is really just regret or embarrassment. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run it on three projects—one that has produced strong intermediate evidence but is now expensive, one that has produced nothing but is cheap to keep alive, and one where stopping would publicly undermine a partnership we depend on—and for at least one, tell me what the procedure concludes even if I won't like it.
