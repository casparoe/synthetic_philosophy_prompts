Here is the background I am working from. In his Autobiography, Benjamin Franklin describes the project he began in his twenties to attain what he calls moral perfection. He enumerates thirteen virtues—Temperance, Silence, Order, Resolution, Frugality, Industry, Sincerity, Justice, Moderation, Cleanliness, Tranquility, Chastity, and Humility—and he builds a small paper apparatus for training himself in them: he devotes one virtue to each week, keeps a ruled page with a column for each day of the week, and enters a small black spot wherever he catches himself violating the virtue of the week, intending to run through the full set of thirteen on a repeating cycle. He also reports, with unusual candor, that the project never worked the way he had hoped, and that Humility—added last, after a friend pointed out his pride—is the one virtue he can least claim to have mastered, because the very success at it would make him proud of it. Near the end of his life, in a letter written in his eighties, Franklin reports that he has effectively stopped the active pursuit of moral perfection and has settled for something like contentment with a favorable opinion of his own virtue.

The position I hold: that late abandonment was not a failure of Franklin's method, nor a surrender born of age or a decline of will, but the correct terminal state of the project. On this reading, the ledger was always a training regimen rather than a destination. It was a device for forming stable dispositions, and the moment a person can honestly stop logging faults and simply trust the dispositions the training has produced, the apparatus has done its job, and the honest thing to do is to set it aside. The late letter should therefore be read as a graduation, not a capitulation.

Your task is to tell me what would count against this reading. I want the evidence, arguments, and considerations that should move me, sorted by how decisive each would be. In particular, address the following:

1. Which facts about the project's design—its fault-only logging, its weekly rotation, its self-assessment loop, and the humility paradox—would, if anything, undercut the graduation reading and instead support the rival reading that the method was broken or self-defeating?
