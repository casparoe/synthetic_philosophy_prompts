I spent thirty-five years as a field ecologist, mostly working on predator–prey dynamics and wildlife management in temperate forests, and I retired three years ago. I have only recently started reading philosophy seriously, and I keep circling one question that I think my old training has actually prepared me to press: whether the naturalness objection to wild animal suffering can even be stated coherently. I have written a short essay and would like your comments on it.

The Baseline That Was Never There

The naturalness objection to wild animal suffering runs roughly like this: animals in the wild suffer as a result of the ordinary workings of nature — predation, starvation, disease, competition — and we have no obligation to intervene in those workings. The objection is usually defended by drawing a line between human-caused suffering and natural suffering, and granting that the former is a moral problem while the latter is not.

The standard rebuttal is that this line is morally arbitrary: we do not let the fact that a harm is "natural" excuse it, and the appeal to nature is a fallacy. But I think this rebuttal concedes too much. It treats "natural" as a well-defined category and merely denies its moral significance. My claim is stronger: at the population level, the category is not well-defined at all, so the objection cannot function as a comparison.

Consider what a "natural" baseline of wild suffering would have to be. It would have to be the suffering profile of a wild population in the absence of human influence. But every extant wild population's suffering profile is already the product of human-driven change. The overabundance of deer in a Central European forest — and the starvation, disease, and parasite load that overabundance entails — is the residue of the human-driven decline of wolves and lynx. The suffering of seabirds on a remote island is the product of rats that humans shipped there. Even in landscapes we call pristine, the current predator–prey dynamics are the residue of human-driven extinctions elsewhere in the food web. There is no "natural" baseline to which the current state can be compared, because the current state is not a deviation from a natural state; it is the natural state, as far as any current population is concerned.

The upshot is that the naturalness objection cannot be stated as a comparison to a natural baseline. At best it collapses into one of two different claims. The first is an epistemic-humility claim: we do not know the consequences of intervention, so we should not intervene. The second is a practical claim: we cannot intervene at the scale at which wild suffering occurs. Both are true, and both matter. But neither is a claim about the moral status of wild suffering, and neither addresses the question the objection was supposed to answer.

What this leaves is a different and, I think, harder question: which interventions reduce net wild suffering without creating new suffering? This is a question about ecology and population dynamics, not about the metaphysics of nature. And the hard cases are not the obvious ones — rabies vaccination, wildlife crossings, the removal of an invasive species — but the ones where reducing the per-capita suffering of one species changes the size of that population or of another. If you reduce the suffering of a prey population, its population grows, and total suffering may rise. If you reduce the suffering of a predator population by supplementing its food, it may overconsume and drive its prey into a worse state. The welfare of one species is coupled to the welfare of another, and the central problem of wild animal welfare is therefore not "is wild suffering a moral problem?" but "how do we do population-level welfare arithmetic in systems where the welfare of one species is coupled to the welfare of another?"

I should concede one point. One could argue that "natural" means the counterfactual absence of any human influence, which is a coherent (if empirically inaccessible) concept. I grant that the concept is coherent. But I maintain that it cannot ground an obligation, because the relevant comparison for any actual decision is between available interventions, not between the status quo and an unattainable counterfactual.

I would like your comments on three things. First, whether my central claim — that the naturalness objection cannot be stated because the baseline is incoherent at the population level — is too strong, and what the strongest version of the objection looks like given the concession I make in the final paragraph. Second, whether the move to population-level welfare arithmetic is well-motivated, and whether the coupling claim (that reducing the per-capita suffering of one species can raise total suffering) is as general as I suggest, or whether it holds only in special cases. Third, the empirical accuracy of the examples: please flag anything you think is wrong or overstated.

Two further requests. Please mark, as you go, which of these claims you take to be settled among people who actually work on this — ecologists, wildlife managers, animal ethicists — and which are genuinely contested, and please do not present a contested view as if it were consensus. And where you are unsure of an empirical or philosophical point, say so explicitly, and say what would change your mind. I am a novice in philosophy, so I would rather have a careful, appropriately hedged answer than a confident but overreaching one.
