I'm teaching a philosophy of science seminar (upper-level undergrad, about 14 people, one 75-minute session) and I want a game that makes the F-twist land in the gut instead of staying a thing you've read about. In case it's not familiar: the F-twist is the claim from Friedman's famous methodology essay that how realistic a model's assumptions are is irrelevant to how well it predicts — a model with absurdly false assumptions (billiard players who calculate like supercomputers) can outpredict one that faithfully captures the mess. I've read it like a dozen times and I still dont feel it.

Design a game where two teams model the same live system in the room and the team forced to be realistic — required to bake true details about the system into its model — loses to the team forced to be idealized, capped at maybe two variables. I'm fine being the system myself if that helps. Give me exact rules, what the realistic team's dossier of true facts should contain, constraints that actually force the two teams into different modeling behavior (I'm worried the realistic team will just list true facts and never let them do any work — close that loophole), a scoring scheme, and a debrief mapping specific moments in the game to parts of the F-twist. I suspect the realistic team will overfit to one-off quirks; I want that to be visible in the play, not just asserted afterwards. Also tell me what will go wrong the first time I run this, and be honest about what the game can't show — I don't want to oversell it as a refutation of realism in modeling.
