I've been trying to build a simulation exercise around the four "kinds of minds" learning modes — the Darwinian/Skinnerian/Popperian/Gregorian stack, in case you don't know it: Darwinian creatures only adapt by natural selection across generations, no within-lifetime learning at all; Skinnerian creatures adapt within their lifetime by trial and error, paying real costs for every mistake; Popperian creatures can run hypotheses or internal models that fail without the creature itself dying or losing anything, so the mistakes are cheap and internal; and Gregorian creatures learn socially, by imitating or copying others, so a behavior can spread through a group without any individual ever discovering it. I want a thought experiment that actually separates these in principle, because in practice people just wave their hands and say "oh, that's just learning" and I can't tell which mode is doing the work.

Design me a small foraging scenario — a population of identical agents on a grid, a food source that moves to a new location on a fixed schedule, and that's about it, no exotic physics, no telepathy, no perfect predictors — that would let me run the same population in four configurations where exactly one mode is active and the other three are disabled. The deliverable is: the scenario spec, then for each of the four runs the observable signature that would let me tell which mode produced it from the trace alone (foraging paths, number of real-world failures before the food is found, whether the population converges on the same strategy or not, speed of spread, whatever is actually discriminating), and then an honest accounting: which pair of modes is genuinely hardest to keep apart in this setup and why, which feature of my design is doing the work, and how a defender of the mode that comes out looking weakest would attack the case. One thing I specifically want to nail: Popperian and Gregorian learning can look identical from the outside — both get you to the food fast without many real-world failures — so the signatures have to exploit something structural, like whether the discovery is independent per agent or cascades through the population, not just a speed difference. I'm taking a course on the evolution of cognition and the professor keeps using this vocabulary without ever pinning down what would count as evidence for one mode over another, so I'd like the explanation to be at the level of "what would I actually see in the data," with a short worked example of the trace for at least one of the runs.
