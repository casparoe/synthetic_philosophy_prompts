I'm a retired ecologist, and for the past couple of years I've been slowly getting into ethics through a back door — mostly because I can't stop thinking about the "only suffering matters" version of negative utilitarianism, and I brought it up at a dinner with some friends last week and it kind of derailed the entire conversation. I definately know I mean the strong, hardcore version, the one where pleasure and happiness don't actually count at all in the moral ledger and the only thing with real weight is how much suffering gets reduced. The bit that keeps me up at night is the consequence I keep landing on: if the only thing that counts is suffering, then a person who lives a genuinely happy life and a person who was never born at all are morally the same, because neither one adds any suffering — the happy one's joy is just... not in the column. And a new baby's future happiness is basically noise; the only number that matters about them is how much they're expected to suffer. Anyway, I'd love for you to write me a satire that works entirely from inside that position rather than mocking it from the outside.

Make it a deadpan advice-column response, the kind you'd find in a self-help magazine — a reader writes in and the columnist, who is a committed strong negative utilitarian and never winks, never backpedals, and never slips into ordinary "aww, a baby, how wonderful" celebration, answers them. The reader's letter should be from a sympathetic, a little distressed layperson: they've just read about the view and now they can't enjoy anything, because every happy moment feels "pointless" or "wasted," and they keep concluding that a world where they'd never existed would have been morally better, and they're asking if they're crazy and what they're supposed to do. The columnist's reply has to sound genuinely helpful and dead-serious, and the jokes have to come from getting the ethics *right* — the way happiness is treated as morally inert rather than bad, the "one-column ledger" idea, the expected-suffering calculation for a new life, the quiet implication that the best outcome for any future person is that they never get the chance to suffer — so that someone who actually knows the view laughs instead of wincing, while someone who doesn't can still follow it. Keep it deadpan and legible, so the reader can tell exactly what's being ironized without the author ever breaking character. Aim for about 500-600 words, and by the end I want to actually feel the real question the view raises: whether taking "only suffering matters" seriously quietly dissolves the idea that any life is worth living, and whether that's a flaw in the view or maybe the whole point.
