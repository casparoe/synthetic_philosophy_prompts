I'm teaching an upper-level seminar on free will and moral responsibility — about 18 students, one 90-minute session — and I've been struggling to find a way to make Galen Strawson's Basic Argument feel less like a dry conceptual argument and more like something my students can actually feel the force of. I was discussing this with a colleague last week, and they suggested a mock trial, which I like, but I'm not sure a standard mock trial would capture the specific structure of the regress. I don't want to just describe the regress to them; I want them to run into it themselves, to feel the prosecution's position collapsing under the weight of the defense's antecedent-cause cards, before anyone names the argument. I've also been trying to get at the specific distinction Strawson draws between "deep" moral responsibility (being an "ultimate author" or "originator" of one's actions) and the ordinary reactive attitudes (blame, resentment, gratitude) that we still seem to owe each other even if no one is deeply responsible — this is the part I find most confusing, and I want the activity to make it tangible.

Please design a structured in-class activity — something like a mock trial or a "blame chain" game — where a "prosecution" team must establish that a defendant is "ultimately responsible" (an "ultimate author") for a specific action, and a "defense" team can always point to an antecedent cause (the defendant's temperament, their childhood, their genes, their environment, a triggering event) that the prosecution must then show the defendant is responsible for, and so on. The rules should be set up so that there is no "base case" — every link in the chain has its own antecedent cause — so that the prosecution is forced to keep going back until they run out of "ultimate" ground. I need the specific rules, the materials (including a set of "antecedent cause" cards and a "case" description), a timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened in the activity onto the structure of the Basic Argument (the prosecution's task of establishing "ultimate authorship" as the first premise, the defense's regress strategy as the second premise) and that brings in the "deep responsibility" vs. "reactive attitudes" distinction. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (e.g., students treating the regress as an empirical argument about the specific facts of the case rather than a conceptual argument about the notion of "ultimate authorship"), and be frank about which feature of Strawson's view the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in the debrief.
