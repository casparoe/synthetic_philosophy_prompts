I'm drawing up the question list for a panel conversation with a philosopher whose long-running project has been to argue that some contradictions are true in the strongest sense — that there are propositions that are both true and false, not because of a defect in our language or our reasoning, but because of the way things are. I need eight to ten questions that carry the discussion past the reassertion of the view and into the places where it is genuinely exposed. Skip the warm-ups — I can get "summarize the view in a sentence" and "what drew you to it" from his books; I want the hard ones.

Structure the list as follows. For each of the three pressure points below, write one question and, directly under it, a follow-up that assumes the reply he is most likely to give and presses further. Then add two or three questions of your own that a sympathetic but skeptical audience would want answered, and close with a single question about what could count against the view.

1. The regress of rejection. He has accepted the Liar sentence as a true contradiction. Now consider the sentence "the Liar is not a true contradiction." On his own acceptance, that sentence is false; but it seems to call for exactly the same treatment that made the Liar a contradiction, which would make it a true contradiction as well, and the pattern repeats up a tower. Ask him to say where, if anywhere, the tower stops and what principle does the stopping. He will most likely say the process terminates because the rejection sentence is not itself a dialetheia, or that we simply stop at a certain level; write the follow-up to ask what licenses the stop at that level that did not apply one level down, where he did accept the contradiction.

2. The boundary problem. He holds that only some contradictions are true. Ask him for the principled line that separates a true contradiction from a false one — a mere error. Is it self-reference, semantic paradox, something else? And does the line hold when we leave the semantic paradoxes and look at the Russell set, or at an inconsistent but empirically successful physical theory? He will most likely ground the line in genuine self-referential paradox; write the follow-up to ask whether that is a principled criterion or an ad hoc carve-out, and what happens to it once the contradictions stop being linguistic.

3. The logic of the logic. He blocks the explosion principle by reasoning in a paraconsistent logic. But that logic has to be presented and justified somewhere. If the meta-language in which he does so is classical, he is using the very reasoning his view discards to defend the logic that discards it; if the meta-language is itself paraconsistent, the question of what licenses that choice reappears a level up. Ask him what his meta-logic is and what it has that the contradictions he is defending do not. He will most likely say the hierarchy is acceptable, or that the meta-language can be paraconsistent too; write the follow-up to ask what, at the top of the hierarchy, stops the justification question from recurring.

Close with one question: what discovery — empirical or philosophical — would be strong enough to make him give up the view, and is there any such thing he could observe?
