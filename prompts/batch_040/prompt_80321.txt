I just spent two hours arguing with a friend about distributive justice, and I'd like to turn the argument into a tool I can use in a graduate seminar next month. The specific point of contention: I think the standard "equality of what" debate — welfare, resources, capabilities — quietly bakes in two further choices that the usual presentations never make explicit. First, whether the comparison is made ex ante, over the expected value of a person's prospects before they take risks, or ex post, over what actually turned out. Second, whether the target is strict equality, a sufficiency floor, or a prioritarian weighting of the worst off. My friend insists these are just implementation details that follow once you pick a currency, and I am no longer sure he is wrong. I want an instrument that would let us find out.

So please write a decision tree that takes a student from "I think some inequality is unjust" to a named position in the equality literature. Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: begin with the questions that genuinely separate the positions, and only then locate the actual views in the cells. I want the first branch to be the currency question — welfare, resources, capabilities, opportunity, or something relational rather than quantitative — and I want the ex ante / ex post choice and the pattern choice (equality, sufficiency, or prioritarian) to be branches as well, because I believe that is where my friend and I actually disagree. Phrase every branch node as a question a student could answer without already knowing which position it leads to; no "do you consider yourself a luck egalitarian?" nodes.

The leaves must include at least: welfarist egalitarianism, resource egalitarianism (the Dworkin–Cohen line), the capability approach, luck egalitarianism, prioritarianism, sufficientarianism, and relational egalitarianism. Where a combination of answers has no well-known occupant, mark the cell empty and give one sentence on why it is empty — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. And flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot actually separate, because I suspect the resource-egalitarian and capability leaves are closer than the textbooks admit, which was, in fact, the other half of the argument.

The audience is a small graduate seminar on distributive justice, about twelve students, and I want the whole thing to fit on two pages with room for a one-paragraph introduction. Please do not include citations or a reading list; I will add those myself. What I need is the instrument and the reasoning for why these particular branches do the sorting.
