I'm taking a political philosophy course this semester and we just got to the fragment in Pascal's Pensées where he works out the relationship between justice and force — the part where he basically argues that justice which can't be backed by force is powerless, that force which isn't grounded in justice is just tyranny, and that most of what we call justice is really a convention that got locked in by whoever happened to be strongest at the time. I keep trying to figure out what I actually believe about all this when I just go by my gut on concrete cases, and I cant get a consistent story, and I was going over it with a study group last night and we all landed somewhere different, which is annoying. Here's where I land: a law that was passed through a clearly rigged process but has now been enforced without interruption for sixty years and everyone treats it as settled — I feel like it's become legitmate, maybe even just, or at least it's stopped being tyranny, even though it started as pure force. A successful revolution that kicks out a tyrant and sets up genuinely fair institutions — I call that just, but I catch myself thinking the justice is really just the fact that the force won, and if the other side had won we'd be calling it a coup. A village custom of sharing food that nobody could enforce, no punishment, everyone just does it out of goodwill — I still think that's real justice, just fragile, which feels like it clashes with his whole point that justice that can't be backed by force is powerless. A fair law the state physically can't enforce in some remote districts, so it's a dead letter there — I don't think that counts as justice in those districts at all, just a piece of paper. And a tyrant's decree that's substantively awful but enforced perfectly — obviously tyranny, force without justice.

What's bugging me is that the sixty-year rigged law and the unenforceable village custom are pulling me in opposite directions: one says sustained force can generate justice over time, the other says justice doesn't need force at all. Is there one principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures all of these or at least gets close, and what does it say about a case I haven't listed — like a court ruling that's procedurally perfect but substantively wrong, where the whole binding force comes from the institution's power to enforce it? And be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, because I suspect I can't keep them all and I'd rather know now than on the final.
