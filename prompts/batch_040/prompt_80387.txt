In my statistics class, a debate has been going on for months between my instructor and a few students about what probability actually is, and it has completely stalled. My instructor defines probability strictly as long-run relative frequency: the probability of an event is the fraction of times it would occur if you repeated the experiment indefinitely, and he treats questions like "what is the probability that it will rain tomorrow?" as literally meaningless unless you specify a population of comparable days. Two students push back from opposite directions: one says a probability should simply be a rational degree of belief, constrained by the requirement that your betting odds not be exploitable in a Dutch book, which lets you assign a probability to any one-off event; the other says the coin itself has a real propensity to land heads a certain fraction of the time, and that both frequency and belief are just ways of tracking it. What stumps me is that all three sides agree on the entire probability calculus and on every calculation, and yet they cannot agree on what the number in "P(rain tomorrow) = 0.6" even is or what it commits you to. My working hypothesis is that this is a verbal dispute — everyone is doing the same math and just using the word "probability" for different things — but I have a nagging feeling that a real disagreement is hidden underneath, maybe about what would count as evidence against a probability claim, or about whether statistics is in the business of controlling long-run error rates or of representing degrees of belief. So: what kind of disagreement is this actually — verbal, substantive, or two different questions (objective chance versus degree of belief) being asked under a single name — and what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or is the impasse permanent, and why? Please don't just give me a tour of the three positions. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
