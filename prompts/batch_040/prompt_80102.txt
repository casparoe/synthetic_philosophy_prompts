I spent about thirty years in a molecular biology lab, mostly on gene expression, and I retired two years ago and, like a lot of us, I've been filling the void with philosophy. I'm in a book club that's been working through some philosophy of biology, and I'm the only lab scientist in the group, so this is the the kind of thing I keep meaning to raise and then let slide.

The slogan is "DNA is a code" — or "the code of life" — and at our last meeting it got used in two opposite directions in the same argument, which is what really bugged me. We were discussing whether a trait is "written in the genes," and one person said "DNA is a code, and a code is only a code relative to a reader — the sequence isn't even a message until transcription happens, so you can't just read the organism off the genome," using it to argue against genetic determinism. The other person fired back that that was exactly why it's a code — "a code carries the information; the environment is just the runtime, and the code is the program the cell runs," using it to argue for determinism. Same slogan, opposite conclusions, and I couldn't say which one was using it correctly.

There was also a side thing from that meeting I want folded in: somebody pointed at the degeneracy of the genetic code — sixty-one codons for twenty amino acids — and called it "redundancy in the code," like error correction in a digital system. I'm not sure that's the right way to think about it, and I suspect the answer depends on which reading of the slogan is actually true.

So here's what I want: the seperate precise claims "DNA is a code" could actually be making, from the most trivial to the most radical. I know it's a metaphor — don't just tell me the metaphor is imperfect, I've heard that a hundred times. I want the menu of readings, and for each one: is it a platitude, is it defensible, or is it actually false, and which of the two people at the club is using which reading. Assume I still think like a working biologist, not a philosopher, so use our vocabulary — codons, transcription, degeneracy, the central dogma — rather than philosophy jargon, and don't give me a reading list or citations; I want the argument, not the bibliography. Fold the degeneracy thing into whichever of the five it belongs to.

And be honest about what would change your mind: for each verdict, tell me what argument or consideration would flip it, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.

Our next meeting is in two weeks and I want to bring this in, so format it as a numbered list of exactly five items — the five readings, in order from most trivial to most radical — each item a sentence or two, no intro or conclusion.
