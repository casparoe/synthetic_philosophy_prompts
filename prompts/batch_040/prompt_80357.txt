Ok so I'm in a decision theory seminar this semester and the professor keeps bringing up these "counterfactual mugging" type scenarios in lectures, and I've been trying to figure out what I actually think about paying in them, going by gut. Here's where I land:

A super-smart predictor (basically never wrong) has already predicted I will not pay, and on the strength of that a mugger is on his way to my apartment to take $100. He's already en route and there's nothing I can do to stop him. If I pay him $10 now he cancels the job, but I'd get mugged anyway, so the $10 buys me literally nothing. Verdict: don't pay, obviously.

Same setup but the mugger is a time traveler from tomorrow, and he tells me he already saw me refusing to pay, so he's coming back to mug me; if I pay him $10 now he won't come back. I'm sure he can't be wrong about what I'll do. Verdict: still don't pay, feels exactly like the first one.

Now a reward version: a predictor (basically never wrong) has already watched a simulation of me decide, and if its prediction is "I'll take only the big box" it put a million dollars in the big box, and it always empties the small box. If I only take the big box I get the million, if I also take the small one I get $1. My taking the small box doesn't change what's in the big box, I know that. But I one-box. And this is the one that bothers me, because it looks structurally identical to the first case — in both, my action is causally irrelevant to what the predictor already did — but I feel like a fool for refusing $1 in the mugging case and feel smart for refusing $1 in this one. I can't make the difference I'm sensing into words.

Last one, a different flavor: a hacker threatens to leak some mildly embarrassing stuff about me to my whole department unless I pay $50. But the leak only happens if I pay (he wants the money AND the schadenfreude), so if I don't pay, nothing leaks. Verdict: obviously don't pay, and this one feels different from the first case even though it's also "threat + payment."

Is there one principle that gets all of these, or at least close, and what does it say about a case I haven't listed: same as the first, except the predictor is only 95% sure I won't pay, so the mugger is probably already on his way but there's a 5% chance he's not, and paying $10 definitely cancels the job. What's the principle, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause does the work in which case, and be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to drop — I suspect the mugging/one-boxing pair is the dilema that can't all be saved, but I'd like to see the argument for why, and whether the simpler principle that saves most of my intuitions is worth losing one of them.
