For my school's philosophy seminar this term, we've been working through the problem of divine hiddenness, and I've been stuck on one version of the argument long enough that I keep bringing it up in our group discussions. The rough shape I keep running into is this: if God existed and wanted a genuine personal relationship with each of us, then God would want us to have at least some non-defeating evidence that God is there, because it's hard to see how you could have a relationship with someone you can't recognize as being present; but we don't seem to have anything like that, so the argument concludes that God doesn't exist. The theist reply I keep hearing is that a real relationship actually requires the possibility of faith, and that handing out overwhelming evidence would destroy exactly the kind of openness God wants. I'd like you to write a dialogue between these two positions in which the speakers actually grapple with each other's points rather than just recite their standard lines.

To keep it concrete, I'd like them to test their views against a scenario I keep turning over. Suppose God chose to reveal himself clearly, with no ambiguity, to exactly one person in a small town, and that person's testimony becomes widely known but is never independently verified. Then ask two questions: does that single clear case solve the hiddenness problem for everyone else in the town, or does it open up a new problem? And if the person who was chosen is, as far as anyone can tell, no more virtuous or more seeking than anyone else around, does the whole thing start to look like a lottery, and if so, what does that do to the idea that hiddenness is part of a loving plan rather than an accident? I want both speakers to be sharp and to use each other's concessions as leverage, and I don't want either one to be a caricature. Please end the dialogue without either side fully winning, because I think the disagreement is real and not just a matter of words.
