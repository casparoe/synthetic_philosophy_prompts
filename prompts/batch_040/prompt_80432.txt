I'm studying the epistemology embedded in courtroom procedure, and I'd like you to work out what a specific institutional rule quietly presupposes. In US criminal trials, the jury is generally required to reach a unanimous verdict: every juror must be convinced beyond a reasonable doubt, and a single holdout is enough to hang the jury and force a mistrial. The system treats each juror's judgment as fallible — reasonable doubt is set well below certainty, and the law itself assumes any individual juror might be wrong — yet the verdict it issues is categorical and all-or-nothing: the defendant is guilty or not, with no probability attached. The structure of this rule resembles the lottery paradox. What view of belief, or of knowledge, does the unanimity requirement presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology — in particular, whether the rule quietly assumes that the conjunction of twelve high-probability propositions (that each juror's judgment is correct) is itself a high-probability proposition, and whether that assumption is what licenses issuing a categorical verdict — and say whether that assumption is coherent. Then do the arithmetic: if each of the twelve jurors assigns a confidence of 0.99 to guilt, what is the probability that all twelve are right under the assumption that their judgments are roughly independent, and does that fall below a plausible threshold for the standard of proof (take one, for the sake of argument, to be 0.95)? Now consider the rival case, where the jurors' judgments are highly correlated because they all weigh the same evidence: how does that change the probability that the unanimous verdict is correct, and does the lottery-paradox critique of the rule depend on which of the two cases you assume? Finally, tell me what would have to change in the unanimity rule, or in how the verdict is issued, if we replaced the implicit closure assumption with a straightforwardly probabilistic one, and whether the resulting system would be more or less coherent than the one we have.
