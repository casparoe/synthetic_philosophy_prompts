I'm on the program for a transitional justice conference in a few weeks and I've been dreading the question period for my slot, so I want a prep sheet before I finalize the slides. The room is going to be a mixed bag: mostly transitional justice lawyers who've spent their careers litigating for compensation and who treat state apologies with suspicion, because in their experience an apology is usually the thing a state says right before it tries to close the book without paying; plus a handfull of moral philosophers who think my central idea is interesting but underdeveloped; and a couple of representatives from affected communities who've sat through a lot of hollow "we deeply regret" statements and are not going to be moved by another one.

Here's the thesis I'm defending. The main moral work of a state apology for a historical wrong is not to express the remorse of the current government — that's incoherent or at best derivate, since none of its members did the deed — but to repair the standing of the relationship between the state and the wronged group. It's a performative that changes the parties' moral position toward each other, not a confession of feeling. I want to argue that this is why an apology without reparations isn't just words, but also why reparations without an apology leaves the relationship stuck in a permanent creditor-debtor asymmetry that money alone doesn't clear. I'll use the no-fault apology laws in a bunch of US states — the ones that let a hospital say "I'm sorry" without it being admissible as an admission of liability in court — as an analogy for an apology doing a structural rather than an emotional job.

So give me the sheet: the objections I'll actually get from this room, in order of how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it (the lawyer's vocabulary is not the philosopher's), with a short reply and a note on where my reply is softest. Tell me what I should concede up front rather than get caught on. And include the one objection I'm hoping nobody asks — my gut says it's going to be something about whether my account lets a state unilaterally close a moral claim the wronged group never agreed to close, but I can't quite frame it and I don't know how fatal it is.
