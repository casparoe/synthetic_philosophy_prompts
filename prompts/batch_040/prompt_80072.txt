I'm building a teaching case on moral uncertainty and I need a scenario engineered to a specification rather than a generic trolley-style dilemma. Set it in a realistic institutional context — a hospital, a research lab, or a firm — in one or two paragraphs, with no exotic physics and no superintelligent AI, in which a decision-maker must choose between two actions, A and B, and the two moral frameworks she takes seriously give opposite verdicts: a consequentialist framework that endorses A on net-welfare grounds, and a rights-based deontological framework that condemns A because it inflicts a serious harm on identifiable individuals for the sake of an aggregate gain. She is genuinely uncertain between the two; choose and state her fixed credences.

The case must be built so that, holding those credences fixed, two ways of doing moral uncertainty deliver different answers: the expected-moral-value rule (weight each framework's assessment of each action by her credence in that framework and pick the higher total) endorses A, while a satisficing or worst-case rule (for instance, refuse to do whatever any plausible framework condemns, or maximise the worst-case moral value) endorses B. The divergence must come from one explicit structural feature: the expected-moral-value verdict must depend on how her values are put on a common scale across the two frameworks — that is, it must be sensitive to a hidden stipulation about the exchange rate between aggregate welfare and rights-violations — whereas the satisficing verdict must be robust to that scale and require no cross-framework comparison at all. If the case cannot be built so that the expected-moral-value answer flips with the scale while the satisficing answer stays put, say so plainly rather than fudging it.

After the scenario, work through, for each rule, the exact computation or test that yields its verdict and why; name the structural feature that does the work; and state what happens if that feature is removed (does the case collapse so that both rules agree?). Finish with the strongest attack a defender of the rule that comes out badly would mount, and say which attack you think lands.
