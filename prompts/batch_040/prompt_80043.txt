I wrote an argument that seems to prove too much, and I can't find where it goes wrong. It proves that there are no genuine moral dilemmas — not that I fail to identify them, but that the case doesn't exist — and I no longer accept that conclusion. For most of the last decade I read the rationalist literature on moral uncertainty closely, and I came out of it a committed "dilemmas are just hard cases" person: moral value is a single scale, a would-be dilemma is really a case where you haven't finished the arithmetic, and once you have, there is a uniquely best option. To pin down exactly why I held that view I wrote the argument below. It is valid on the surface, every step looks individually fine, and it delivers precisely the conclusion I once wanted. Then a few years ago I was forced to choose between two people I was responsible for, and both options carried a real moral loss that I do not think was a defect of my epistemic state; I was not just bad at the arithmetic. So the argument now proves something I regard as false, and the step that is doing the illicit work is not visible to me.

(1) A genuine moral dilemma is a case in which two mutually exclusive options, A and B, are each morally required: you genuinely ought to do A, and you genuinely ought to do B, and doing neither is a moral failure. I grant this definition; it is what I mean by "genuine" — the conflict is in the moral value itself, not in my uncertainty about it.

(2) If an option is morally required, then it is at least as morally good as every other available option. A required option cannot be worse than an alternative; you are only required to do what is at the top of the moral ranking.

(3) So in a genuine dilemma, A is at least as good as B, and B is at least as good as A. (From 1 and 2.)

(4) The relation "at least as good as" is a partial order, and so is antisymmetric: if A is at least as good as B and B is at least as good as A, then A and B are on a par — equal in moral value.

(5) If two options are on a par, then neither is required. To require one of two exactly equal options would be to ground the requirement in an arbitrary tie-break, and moral requirements are not grounded in arbitrary tie-breaks.

(6) So in a genuine dilemma, neither A nor B is required. (From 3, 4, and 5.)

(7) But by (1), both A and B are required. Contradiction. Therefore there are no genuine moral dilemmas.

My questions. First, which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would rather keep my belief that genuine dilemmas exist than give up any single step, but I am not sure which step is load-bearing, and I suspect the argument is doing something subtle I am not seeing — in particular, I am not sure I have been using "at least as good as" the same way throughout, and I want you to check that specifically rather than take my framing for granted. Second, for each of the steps (2), (4), and (5), say what a coherent moral theory looks like if that is the step you give up, and what you pay for it — in particular whether you have to abandon the single-scale picture of moral value that I used to like, and whether you can still do expected-value arithmetic over moral uncertainty. Third, tell me whether the argument is salvageable for the weaker claim I actually still want to defend — that most *apparent* dilemmas are epistemic, i.e. that in most cases that feel like dilemmas one option really is better and the agent simply cannot tell which — or whether the same machinery that kills genuine dilemmas also kills that weaker claim. If the flaw turns out to be that the single-scale picture is what breaks, I want to know whether the weaker claim depends on that picture or whether it can be restated in a form that survives without it.
