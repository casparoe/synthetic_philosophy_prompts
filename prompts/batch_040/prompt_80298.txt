I work on the ethics of moral and political compromise, and I keep reaching for a framing I suspect is doing more argumentative work than it can actually support: the claim that compromise is essentially the same phenomenon as voluntary trade, so that the standard economic case for free trade — that an exchange creates gains for both sides, making a voluntary bargain a win-win — should carry over almost unchanged to the moral case. On this picture, two parties who start from different positions each "hold" a bundle of principles, and by trading concessions they can each arrive at an outcome they prefer to the status quo, so compromise benefits both in exactly the way a market transaction does.

I'd like you to audit this analogy rather than simply defend or dismiss it. Start by stating the economic "gains from trade" picture as precisely as you can: what exactly makes a voluntary exchange mutually beneficial — I have in mind the condition that the two parties value the goods differently (the marginal-rate-of-substitution idea), so that some reallocation falls inside the lens of gains from trade and is a Pareto improvement for both. Then give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the two traders, the goods, the initial endowment, the marginal rate of substitution, the Pareto improvement, and the contract curve, in the moral-compromise case. Next, identify the disanalogies that actually matter for the conclusion the framing is being used to license — namely, that compromise is routinely a win-win and should therefore be pursued — and separate those from the disanalogies that are merely cosmetic. I want you to press especially hard on at least three: whether moral "goods" are fungible and divisible the way traded commodities are; whether the relevant "preferences" are exogenous and fixed, or whether the very act of compromising alters the values being traded; and whether the "gains" are even measurable or verifiable in the way a price is. Finally, tell me whether the win-win conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if it does not, what a better analogy — or a more defensible, weaker claim — would be.
