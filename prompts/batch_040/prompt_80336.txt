I'm putting together a one-off workshop for a small philosophy-of-math reading group (about 14 people, mostly grad students plus a couple of undergrads, we've got 75 minutes and a whiteboard plus index cards) and I want an activity that makes people *feel* the difference between working inside a formal system and stepping outside it, rather than just hearing me talk about it. The idea I want to land is the one behind the MU puzzle: that you can enumerate everything a system can reach forever and never once conclude that some string is unreachable, and that the "it's impossible" verdict only shows up when you jump out to a meta-level property of the whole set of reachable strings. I don't want to just hand them the standard MIU setup though, because I suspect half these people have seen it and will recite the "number of I's is never divisible by 3" line without actually doing anything. So I want the activity built around a *modified* string-rewriting system that I'll specify, where the invariant is different, so nobody can pattern-match their way out of it.

Here's the system I want to use: alphabet is M, I, U; you start from the string MI; and you may apply any of these four rules any number of times, in any order -- (R1) append a U to the end of any string that ends in I; (R2) replace any occurrence of II with a U; (R3) delete any occurrence of UU; (R4) append II to the end of any string. Before you design anything, double-check for yourself that this system really has a clean invariant (I'm pretty sure it's about the parity of the number of I's, but I want the activity to rest on something that's actually true, not on my guess), and figure out which of these four targets are reachable and which aren't: MUIU, MIII, MU, and MUII. Then structure it in phases so the contrast does the work. Phase one should be a "doable" target that every team can actually derive in a few steps, so they get the feel of bounded, guarenteed-success derivation from inside the system. Phase two should hand every team an unreachable target and tell them flatly "either produce a derivation or prove it's impossible, and you have no idea which it is" -- the whole point is that the teams that keep grinding inside the system will burn the clock, and the ones that crack it will be the ones who notice some quantity about the string that no rule ever changes. Withhold the words "invariant" and "jumping out of the system" until the debrief; let them discover it. Give me the roles and a rough minute-by-minute timeline, plus a referee or peer-challenge rule so a team can't just blurt "it's impossible" without producing a reason that survives another team poking at it.

For the debrief, map the two phases back onto the theory: what "working inside" actually gives you (a finite chain, or an infinite non-terminating search) versus what the impossibility proof is (a claim about the entire reachable set, expressible in the meta-language but not derivable as a string), and why using arithmetic about the strings feels like cheating but isn't. I also want an honest list of what will predictably go wrong when I run this -- e.g. teams conflating "I haven't found it yet" with "it's impossible," or teams finding a *true but useless* invariant like "every string starts with M" (which is true and also true of MU, so it proves nothing) -- and how I should handle each. And please be frank about the one big feature this activity *can't* reproduce: I suspect it's that my system is fully decidable, so the "jump out" move always exists and always succeeds, whereas in a genuinely Gödelian setting that's not guaranteed -- so students will be tempted to over-generalize that stepping outside always solves it, which is exactly backwards. Tell me what the activity can and can't carry, and what I have to supply myself.
