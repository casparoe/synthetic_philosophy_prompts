I am preparing the question list for a recorded interview with a scholar of classical Islamic theology known for defending a robust reading of al-Ghazālī's occasionalism: on his account, what the philosophers call a cause — fire for burning cotton, a stone for rolling down a slope — does not produce its effect in virtue of anything in itself, but merely precedes the effect in a stable customary order that God sustains, so that every event is in fact produced directly by a divine creative act. I would like six to eight questions that go beyond having the guest restate this picture. Please make sure that at least one question addresses each of the following: the distinction within the tradition between a mere occasion and an efficient cause, and what work that distinction is supposed to do; how a custom that is never broken can still be a genuine object of prediction and of the natural sciences; the scope of the critique — whether it targets only the philosophers' claim that causes contain a necessary connection, or the causal order itself; and whether the same move can be made without the theological commitments, that is, whether a purely secular version of the view is coherent. For each question, please add a follow-up keyed to the answer the guest is most likely to give, anticipating the standard replies he can offer — for instance, the claim that the philosophers' causal necessity is merely an illusion born of repetition, or the claim that the custom is grounded in God's free choice and so remains genuinely contingent.
