The trouble is that this makes probability mysterious — if every outcome happens, it is unclear what it would be for one of them to be more likely than another — so many-worlds needs the Born rule, the rule that the probability of an outcome is the squared amplitude of its branch, as an extra postulate that unitary dynamics alone cannot supply. The best-known attempt to repair this is the decision-theoretic argument of Deutsch and Wallace, which shows that a rational agent about to undergo a quantum split must weight her future branches by their squared amplitudes, or else her preferences can be exploited in a sequence of bets. The book's upshot is that many-worlds is therefore not as parsimonious as its defenders claim: because it has to add the Born rule by hand, it is no simpler than the collapse interpretations it sets out to beat, and its main advantage over them evaporates."
