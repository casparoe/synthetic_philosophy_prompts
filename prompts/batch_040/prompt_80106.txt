I spent thirty years as a physicist and only recently turned to philosophy, and decision theory has me properly stuck on a puzzle that two former colleagues argued about at a dinner. I am reporting it as a bystander, because I honestly cannot tell which of them had the better case.

The puzzle: a driver leaves home to reach a destination. There are two forks on the route that look identical, and the driver is absent-minded in the sense that he cannot tell which fork he is at when he reaches one. Whatever rule he adopts, he applies it identically at both forks. Turning at the first fork gives a payoff of 1; passing the first fork and turning at the second gives a payoff of 2; passing both forks crashes him for a payoff of 0. He chooses a probability p of turning, to be used at each fork.

The argument ran roughly like this.

Colleague A: "This is trivial. Pick p to maximize the expected payoff of the strategy. That is E(p) = p·1 + (1−p)·p·2 + (1−p)²·0 = 3p − 2p², which peaks at p = 3/4. The problem is solved before he gets in the car."

Colleague B: "You have treated the driver as if he could aim for a particular path, but he cannot. The only real decision is the one at a fork, and at a fork he does not know which one it is. So he should assign a probability to being at the first fork. With no information, the principle of indifference gives 1/2, and that changes the answer — your 3/4 is an artifact of double-counting the branch in which he visits both forks."

Colleague A: "I am not double-counting. E(p) is exactly the expected payoff of 'turn with probability p at each fork,' and maximizing it is the right thing to do at the planning stage. It yields a definite, checkable number."

Colleague B: "But he has no persistent planning stage — he forgets which fork he is at. The decision exists only at the fork, and at the fork 1/2 is the honest probability. Run the indifference condition with 1/2 and you get an extreme, not 3/4."

Colleague A: "Then state the number your 1/2 produces and we will compare."

Colleague B: "It pushes him to turn with probability 1 — always turn. The point is that your method is the wrong method, whatever it spits out."

I cannot referee this myself. Please go through their exchange and tell me who is right about which specific claim, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
