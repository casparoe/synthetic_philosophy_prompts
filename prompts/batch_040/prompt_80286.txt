I'm preparing a decision-tree handout for a graduate seminar on the value of human survival. The tree should start from the stipulation that human extinction is bad and that its badness is not exhausted by the deaths it involves, and it should lead a student from that stipulation to a named position about what else is lost. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: each branch node should be a question the student can answer from their intuitions about the following cases, without knowing which position it leads to: (1) the last human dies, but all of humanity's artifacts remain in the solar system and no one ever again experiences anything; (2) humanity goes extinct but a posthuman successor species that inherits our culture and values continues; (3) an alien civilization later visits Earth and genuinely enjoys our art and literature; (4) a stipulated world in which total well-being in the universe is exactly the same as it would have been had humanity continued. The leaves must include Scheffler's "afterlife" view, the future-people or foreclosed-possibilities view, the view that value is relational and requires a valuer, and a deontological view on which the living have a claim to a future; add any other defensible positions you find in the space. Give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment, note any unoccupied combinations of answers and why nobody occupies them, and flag any pair of leaves the cases cannot really separate. Treat the deaths-only view and the view that extinction is not bad at all as excluded by the starting stipulation, and say in one sentence what each would have to give up.
