I'm in a philosophy of language seminar this term and we just finished the Austin and Derrida unit, and its been stuck in my head all week. Could you write me a villanelle that is itself an apology -- the speaker is apologizing to someone who died before they ever got to say the thing, so the apology has no chance of landing, of being accepted, of even being heard. Make one refrain the apology itself, the actual token, and the other one the thought that it still counts anyway. The stanzas should track a shift: at the start the speaker treats every return of the apology as a pure failure -- in Austin's terms a misfire, not an abuse, since with no addressee there the act isn't even happening, so there's nothing to be insincere about -- and then the middle should pivot to the Derridean point, the one that performatives only work because they can be repeated in a context where the original conditions are missing, a quotation, a stage, a poem, so the hollow context isn't a disqualification but the condition of the act. And the last two lines, where the refrains finally sit next to each other, should land the payoff: the villanelle-apology is exaclty the case Austin's felicity rules would exclude and Derrida's logic requires, so the poem itself is the argument. I want it accurate enough that someone who read the unit would recognize the misfire/abuse distinction and the iterability point doing actual work in the form, not just being name-dropped in a line.
