I want your help stress-testing an argument about the relational conception of personhood associated with ubuntu. On this view, personhood is not an intrinsic property of an isolated individual: a person is, in the Zulu saying, a person through other people, and some readings further treat personhood as a matter of degree, with a person's standing rising as they participate more fully in the moral life of the community.

Here is the argument, which I have put together and now suspect proves too much:

(1) On the relational conception, personhood is constituted by a person's relations to others.
(2) If personhood is constituted by relations to others, then the possession of personhood depends on the possession of relations of the relevant kind.
(3) A person in sustained isolation — a castaway on a deserted island, a prisoner held in solitary confinement, a child raised from birth in total seclusion — lacks relations of the relevant kind, or has only minimal ones.
(4) Therefore, such a person is not a person, or is a person only minimally.
(5) But a castaway, a solitary prisoner, and a child raised in seclusion are not, on any view we should accept, non-persons; and the ubuntu tradition is precisely the one invoked to defend the dignity of the outcast, the stranger, and the vulnerable.
(6) Therefore, the relational conception of personhood is incoherent and should be rejected.

Each premise looks defensible, and the move from (1)–(3) to (4) looks straightforward, yet I cannot accept (6). Identify which premise or inferential step you would reject, explain why it looked unobjectionable, and say what the defender of the relational view gives up by making that rejection. I would most like to preserve (5), but I want to understand what I am paying.
