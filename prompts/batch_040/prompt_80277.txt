My two roommates got into it over a proposal they'd read about: swapping the civics test for voting eligibility out for a "bias-trap" test — a set of questions built specifically to catch well-known reasoning errors (ignoring base rates, getting anchored to a number, that conjunction thing), where you'd have to clear a threshold to vote.

Roommate 1 was for it. His point was that this is the one kind of epistocracy that targets something you can actually measure and that actually makes people vote badly — their reasoning hygiene, not their trivia knowledge which is just memorization. And the Condorcet math: if you filter out the folks who are more likely than not to get it wrong, the aggregate decision gets more accurate, that's just how it works.

Roommate 2 was against. She said the test measures test-taking smarts, not political accuracy, because whether your vote is "right" is mostly a function of your values and what info you have acess to, not whether you fell for the anchoring trap. And the deeper issue: the test is written by someone, so you've just built a meta-epistocracy — the people who get to design the traps decide what "competence" even means, and that's the real power grab, not the voters.

Roommate 1 pushed back that the meta-epistocracy problem exists for any standard, including "be a citizen" or "be 18," so it's not a knockdown; and on the values point, the test isn't trying to settle values, just to make sure a person's vote tracks their own considered values instead of their biases.

Roommate 2 said that's a conflation — "track your own values" is a consistency requirement, not an accuracy one, and democracy was never supposed to produce the right answer, it was supposed to be legitimate because everyone got a say. So the whole accuracy framing just smuggles in the assumption that politics is a problem with a solution.

Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step, and what, if anything, both of them are missing?
