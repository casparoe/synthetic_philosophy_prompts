I've been volunteering with a restorative justice circle for a couple of years now and I've been trying to figure out what I actually think about guilt and shame, because for me the distinction keeps collapsing whenever the harm gets big. I don't want a textbook contrast between the two emotions -- I want you to look at my gut verdicts on a bunch of concrete cases and tell me what principle, if any, is doing the work behind them, and which of my verdicts you'd have to drop to keep that principle clean.

Here are the cases. A friend who quietly took money from a group fund confesses to the organizer alone and pays it back with interest: that's guilt, and I think it was the right register, the whole thing got resolved in a week, its almost too clean. A company whose data breach exposes customers' addresses issues a long public apology from the CEO: that reads as shame to me, and I think it was the wrong register entirely -- what the affected customers needed was restitution and a fix, not a performance of the CEO's remorse. An offender in my restorative justice circle sitting across from the person they harmed and actually listening: guilt, and the best outcome I've seen the process produce. A social media pile-on where someone gets publicly humiliated for a mistake: pure shame, and I think it's the worst of all, because the "punishment" never actually reaches the harm done. And here's the pair that's been eating at me: I keep saying private is where guilt belongs and public is where shame lives, but in the case of a factory that poisoned a whole town's water supply, I definately feel that an apology that stays private to the affected families is a non-apology, it has to be public to count -- which directly contradicts my public-equals-shame rule, and I don't know which of the two verdicts to give up.

So: what's the principle that captures as many of these as it can, stated precisely enough that it names the variable you actually think is doing the work (is it really public vs private, or is it something like act vs self, or repair vs self-punishment, or something else you see in the cases)? And then the design question that started all this: can a culture actually choose to be guilt-dominant rather than shame-dominant, or is that mostly out of its hands -- i.e., is there a real institutional design problem here (what forums, what sanctions, what the state is and isn't doing), and are there constraints on the choice, like society size or kinds of harm that are inherently public? Finally, tell me what the principle you land on predicts for a case I haven't listed: a surgeon who makes a serious, non-negligent error -- does the acknowledgment go to the patient privately, to the hospital board, to the profession, or public, and why? Please answer in American English.
