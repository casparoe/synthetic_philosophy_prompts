So me and my friend got into this absolutely massive argument about Libet and free will last night and I'm still a little rattled. He's the type who reads the pop-sci summaries and is convinced the whole thing is settled: the brain fires up the readiness potential way before you "decide," teh fMRI follow-ups can predict the move seconds before you're aware of choosing it, case closed, no free will. And I kept trying to explain that the experiment doesn't actually measure what he thinks it measures, and he just got more and more annoyed.

Here's my sticking point. In Libet's original setup the subject isn't making any real choice between options — they're just told to flex or extend their wrist whenever they feel like it. So the "decision" that's supposedly being preceded by the brain activity is really just "when do I do this," not a substantive choice at all. Which means the readiness potential might just be the motor system ramping up, not a decision. And if that's right, the whole "the brain decides before you do" story is a category error — you're comparing the time of a motor preparation to the time of a conscious decision, but those might not even be the same kind of thing.

My working theory is that the debate has been stalled for forty years because the neuroscientists and the philosophers are answering two different questions and calling it the same one. The neuroscientists are asking "can we predict the action before the subject reports deciding" and the philosophers are asking "is there a free decision to be had in the first place," and nobody's noticed they're not even talking about the same event.

Is that actually right, or is it a real empirical disagreement in disguise — like, is there some experiment or formal result that could actually settle it? Or is this one of those debates that's just going to run forever because the two sides can't agree on what "the decision" even refers to, and if so, what would even count as progress? I just want a a straight diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is, not a survey of who said what.
