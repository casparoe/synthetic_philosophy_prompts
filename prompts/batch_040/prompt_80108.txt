**Question.** Jeffrey conditionalization is a rule for updating credences when the new "evidence" does not arrive as a single proposition learned with certainty, but as a shift in confidence across a set of mutually exclusive alternatives.

(a) State Jeffrey's rule. Make explicit what inputs the rule takes and what it outputs.

(b) Show how Jeffrey's rule relates to the standard rule of conditionalization and generalizes it. Identify the special case in which the two rules give the same result.

(c) Jeffrey conditionalization is often defended by a dynamic Dutch Book (sure-loss) argument. State the conclusion of that argument and the condition or conditions under which it is said to go through.

**Student's answer.**

(a) Let the new evidence be a partition {E₁, …, Eₙ} of mutually exclusive and exhaustive alternatives, and let qᵢ be the new probability the agent assigns to Eᵢ after receiving the evidence, with the qᵢ non-negative and summing to 1. Jeffrey's rule says that the agent's new credence in any hypothesis H is the weighted average of the agent's old conditional credences in H given each alternative, weighted by the target probabilities:

P*(H) = Σᵢ qᵢ · P(H | Eᵢ).

The weights qᵢ are the likelihoods, P(Eᵢ | H) — the probability of the evidence given the hypothesis — so the rule averages the agent's old conditional beliefs, tilting the average toward the alternatives the evidence makes most probable.

(b) Jeffrey's rule generalizes the standard rule. If the new evidence is a single proposition E learned with certainty, the relevant partition is {E, ¬E} and the target probabilities are q(E) = 1 and q(¬E) = 0. The weighted average then collapses to the single surviving term, P*(H) = P(H | E), which is exactly the standard rule of conditionalization. So the standard rule is the special case in which the evidence is received with certainty, and Jeffrey's rule is what one uses when the evidence only shifts confidence across the alternatives.

(c) A dynamic Dutch Book argument shows that an agent who fails to update by Jeffrey's rule can be staked out a sequence of bets that guarantees a sure loss no matter how the world turns out: at each stage the agent is offered a bet acceptable at the old odds that is guaranteed to lose once the evidence is in. Because the argument only requires the agent's credences to be coherent — non-negative and summing to 1 — before and after the update, it does not depend on any further assumption about how the conditional probabilities P(H | Eᵢ) behave when the evidence is received.
