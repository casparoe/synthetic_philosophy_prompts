I'm trying to work out what I actually believe about comparing moral scenarios that contain infinitely many people, by recording my intuitions on a set of cases and then asking what, if anything, unifies them. Here is the setup. Each scenario is a world consisting of a countably infinite list of lives; each life has a well-being value in some arbitrary unit (call it a "u"), where positive values are good, negative values are bad, and zero is neutral. You may take the following mathematical facts as given, without deriving or justifying them: (i) an infinite series is divergent when its sequence of partial sums has no finite limit, and a divergent series has no ordinary sum; (ii) a conditionally convergent series can have its terms rearranged so as to converge to any prescribed real number, or to diverge altogether (the Riemann rearrangement phenomenon); (iii) the natural density of a subsequence is the limit, as N tends to infinity, of the fraction of the first N terms that fall in the subsequence, when that limit exists.

Here are my verdicts on five pairs of worlds.

1. World A is the list in which every life is +1. World B is identical to A except that one further life with value −1000 has been added. I judge A better than B: a finite addition of suffering makes the world worse, even though both worlds contain infinitely many happy lives.

2. World C is the list in which every life is +1. World D is identical to C except that one further life with value +1000 has been added. I judge D better than C: a finite addition of good makes the world better.

3. World E is the list +1, 0, +1, 0, +1, 0, …, with a +1 at every odd position and a 0 at every even position. World F is the list in which the +1's fall at positions 1, 4, 9, 16, … (the perfect squares) and every other position is 0. Both worlds contain countably infinitely many +1 lives and countably infinitely many 0 lives. I judge E better than F, because E has a higher density of good lives (one-half, against a density that tends to zero).

4. World G is the list +1, −1, +1, −1, +1, −1, …. World H contains exactly the same multiset of lives as G, but arranged in the order +1, +1, −1, −1, +1, +1, −1, −1, …. I judge G and H morally equal: the same people having the same lives is the same world, and the order in which I happen to enumerate them is arbitrary.

5. World I is the list +1, +2, +3, +4, …, so that the total diverges to +infinity. World J is identical to I except that the third life is −1000 rather than +3. I judge I better than J: a single finite amount of suffering makes the world worse, even though both totals diverge.

My questions. First: is there a single principle, stated precisely enough that it could be applied to a world I have not described, that delivers all five verdicts? If not, what is the simplest principle that saves the most of them, and exactly which of my verdicts does it force me to abandon? Second: I suspect that verdicts 3 and 4 are in direct tension once you accept that a world is a multiset of lives rather than an ordered list, and I'd like you to state that tension precisely, then say what a principle that resolves it in favor of 4 (order-invariance) must say about case 3, and what a principle that resolves it in favor of 3 (density) must give up. Third: for whichever principle you judge best, report its verdict on a pair I have not listed — World K is the list in which every life is +1, and World L is the list +1, 0, +1, 0, +1, 0, …; is K better than L, equal to it, or incomparable with it, and why?
