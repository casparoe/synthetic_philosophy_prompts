I've been staring at our game's reporting system for a while now and I cant get over one thing. You can do basically anything to a non-player character — drag it around, burn it, stand in a circle and beat it to death on a loop — and the game treats it as normal gameplay: no warnings, no flags, no enforcement. But the moment you do a comparible thing to another player's avatar, the reporting pipeline kicks in and you can get banned. Same engine, same damage numbers popping up, one side is protected and the other isn't. What does that line presuppose? Does it bake in the view that a character can't actually be harmed, or that the only possible victim is the human behind the keyboard? Does it rule out the view that the character itself can be harmed in a way that matters, or is it just compatible with that? And is the presupposition even what the company believes, or is the asymmetry just a tractability call — you can't tell 'roleplay' from 'cruelty,' nobody would take NPC protection seriously, the rules would be unenforceable? I also can't get over that the same game markets its NPCs as if they matter — 'the village is counting on you,' 'your companion is waiting' — while its own rules treat them as furniture. Does that inconsistency matter, or is it just marketing? And what would actually have to change — in the rules, in the design, in what we'd allow on stream — if we took seriously the idea that the characters themselves can be harmed, not just the players who play them? Oh, and one more thing that keeps coming back to me: players will spend hours grooming a companion or a pet and then feel genuinely awful when it dies in a raid, and the feelings are real. If the feelings are real, what does that do to the 'it's just a character' story? Please answer in American English.
