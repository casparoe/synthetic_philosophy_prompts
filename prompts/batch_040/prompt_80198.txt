I'm preparing a handout for an upper-level applied ethics seminar, and I want a decision tree that sorts students by their actual view on a question that keeps coming up in our discussions but that the standard "should we eat less meat?" framing doesn't quite capture: given the way modern food production interacts with wild animal welfare, does reducing one's meat consumption actually reduce overall animal suffering, or could it increase it? The reason this is harder than it looks is that a large share of the world's agricultural land is under crop cultivation or pasture, and much of that land — grazing land for beef in particular — is also functioning as habitat for wild animals, so the counterfactual of "less meat" is not "those animals simply don't exist" but "that land becomes something else (wilderness, different crops, or left fallow)," and each of those counterfactuals has a different effect on wild animal welfare. I also want the tree to take seriously the beef-versus-chicken asymmetry, since beef is land- and pasture-intensive while chicken is more feed-crop-intensive and far more land-efficient per unit of protein, so the "land freed up" effect and the "crop cultivation" effect differ a good deal between the two.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of named positions: start from the commitment "I want my dietary choices to reduce net animal suffering," and make each branch node a single claim on which the positions genuinely come apart — for instance, whether wild animal welfare counts as a moral consideration at all; whether "leaving nature to itself" has independent value that overrides a welfare calculus; whether farmland is best modeled as a net source of suffering for wild animals (competition, pesticide and fertilizer runoff, habitat fragmentation) or as a net-positive habitat (food, water, shelter, mowed field edges); whether the relevant "no meat" counterfactual is wilderness, alternative crops, or fallow land; and whether the sheer scale of wild animal suffering from predation and disease makes any individual dietary choice negligible. Each leaf should carry the name of the position (or "unnamed" if nobody really defends it), one sentence on its most defensible argument, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't actually separate, and note which combinations of answers are unoccupied and why — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Keep it to roughly six to eight branch nodes and no more than eight leaves, and phrase every node as a question a student could answer without already knowing which leaf it leads to. Don't include any citations or reading list — I'll handle the references myself. End your response with a single line that is a one-sentence summary of which leaf you consider most defensible and why.
