I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy, and I'm working through Kierkegaards stuff on the press and the public for a seminar, and a friend of mine (he does media theory, so he's a little loose with the primary texts) walked me into an argument I can't get out of. Let me set up the background the way I understand it, and correct me if I'm mangling it: Kierkegaard's diagnosis is that the modern public is a kind of pseudoperson — it's nobody in particular, but it has a collective force, and it's at once the most powerful and the weakest of beings. The public levels everything, flattening all distinction and individuality onto one plane, so that the exceptional and what he calls the single individual get worn down to nothing. And the daily press is the public's organ: it's what constitutes the public in the first place, and it does so by talking directly and immediately to an undifferentiated mass, handing out ready-made judgments so that no reader ever has to appropriate anything for themselves. Kierkegaard set his own writing up as the opposite of this — indirect communication, pseudonymity, irony, aesthetic form — which withholds the conclusion and provokes the single individual reader into an active relation to what's at stake, rather than delivering a thesis to the crowd.

Here's my friend's argument, and the conclusion is one I can't accept. (1) What makes the press leveling, on Kierkegaard's own account, isn't any particular content but a feature of its mode: it addresses an undifferentiated mass directly and immediately, with ready-made judgments, so that nobody appropriates anything. (2) That feature isn't unique to the daily press. It's shared by any published work that a mass actually reads and takes up as a body of thought to adopt. (3) Kierkegaard's own works — signed and pseudonymous alike — were published, widely read by the very public he was criticizing, reviewed in the very papers he was feuding with, and taken up as a body of thought. (4) So Kierkegaard's own works share the very feature that makes the press leveling. (5) Therefore Kierkegaard's own works are leveling in exactly the way the press is: his practice instantiates the pathology his theory condemns. (6) So either his critique is self-refuting (he's the very thing he attacks), or the honest upshot of his critique is that nobody should publish or speak publicly at all — no books, no sermons, no philosophy — since every such act levels. (7) The second option is absurd, it would forbid this very conversation and any philosophical writing; and the first is a verdict I'd rather not sit with if Kierkegaard was broadly right. (8) So one of these steps is doing work it shouldn't be. I can't tell whether one step is smuggling something in or whether the whole thing is valid and I just have to bite a premise, and I can't name where.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? And here's the thing that makes it feel worse to me: my friend says the argument is even more forceful now, because algorithmic feeds and LLM-generated summaries are the leveling machine perfected — they literally produce and serve back the statistical average of what the public already says, i.e. the pseudoperson made literal — so the the jump from "the press" to "any mass communication" looks even more natural in 2024 than it did in 1846. If I can't even defend Kierkegaard's own books from the leveling charge, I certainly can't defend my own dissertation, or this exchange. I'd rather keep the thought that Kierkegaard was basically right about the press than the thought that he's a self-refuting hypocrite, but I want to know what I'm paying to keep it, and whether the direct-versus-indirect distinction is actually doing the work it's supposed to once an ironic pseudonymous book gets flattened by a public that reads everything as a headline.
