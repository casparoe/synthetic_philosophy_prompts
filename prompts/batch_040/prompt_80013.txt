I'm a graduate student in a seminar on the philosophy of mind, and a large part of the course is built around a taxonomy our professor developed for the class: she sorts creatures into four kinds of minds, named after four thinkers — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian. To make sure I actually understand the differences rather than just memorizing the labels, I'd like you to write a short story that dramatizes all four kinds at once. For context, here is how the course characterizes each: a Darwinian creature is a mind that is the product of evolution, running on fixed, inherited heuristics and essentially unable to learn anything new over its own lifetime; a Skinnerian creature is a mind that is the product of reinforcement, with no internal models or planning, doing only what its history of rewards and punishments has shaped it to do; a Popperian creature is a mind that, after Karl Popper, can form internal conjectures and test them against a model of the world before it acts, so it can plan and avoid the costly errors a trial-and-error organism would have to pay for; and a Gregorian creature is a mind that, after Gregory Bateson, tracks not just the content of signals but the patterns, frames, and the differences that matter in a situation, so it can step outside a pattern and respond to the meta-structure of a situation rather than to the surface message.

Here is the setting I'd like you to use. A university lab has built a sealed, circular artificial habitat that the researchers call the Ring, and they have populated it with one organism of each kind of mind, which the narrator — a research assistant — monitors through a glass wall while keeping a written log. The story should take place over a single morning, in which the team introduces a single novel perturbation: a shallow well containing food that is only dispensed when approached from the side, while the obvious direct approach triggers a brief aversive puff of air. The well is deliberately counterintuitive, so that the naive, go-straight-for-the-food behavior is the wrong one. I want the story to show each of the four creatures encountering the well and responding in a way that is faithful to its kind of mind, with the narrator's log conveying, in real time, how the four responses differ.

Please keep it between 800 and 1,200 words, written in the first person as the research assistant's log, with a literary rather than clinical tone. Two constraints I care about. First, the narrator should be genuinely uncertain, especially about the Gregorian creature: it should remain unclear to the narrator whether that creature truly grasps the structure of the situation or is merely tracking differences at a level it cannot read, and the story should preserve that ambiguity rather than resolving it. Second, end on a small twist: at the very last moment, reveal one detail that makes the narrator, and the reader, reconsider at least one of the four creatures — for instance, that the Popperian creature's confident plan was built on a subtly wrong internal model it never caught, or that the Skinnerian creature's apparently random wandering was actually more structured than it looked.
