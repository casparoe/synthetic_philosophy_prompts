I'm trying to get clear on what a theory of knowability would actually have to deliver before I take sides in the Fitch debate. Write out the desiderata: the requirements that any adequate theory of knowability — one meant to ground truth in epistemic capacity without collapsing into the trivial equation of truth with what God would know — must satisfy, with the consideration behind each and the case it is meant to handle. Be explicit about the tension at the heart of the matter: the theory must be strong enough to make truth genuinely epistemic, yet weak enough to survive Fitch's result that the unrestricted knowability principle (every truth is possibly known) entails the omniscience principle (every truth is in fact known). A useful way to sharpen the requirements is to note that the standard proof runs from just three ingredients — knowledge implies truth, knowing a conjunction yields each conjunct, and the knowability principle together with a necessitation rule — so the theory must say which of those it is prepared to give up and what it loses by giving it up. Flag which of your desiderata are non-negotiable and which are the prejudices of particular camps (verificationists, intuitionists, modal logicians), and identify the single requirement that does the most work excluding candidates. Then, briefly, how do the main moves — rejecting the knowability principle outright, restricting it to a class of truths, switching to intuitionistic logic, and weakening the epistemic axioms — do against your list? A table is fine for the scorecard, but keep the weight on the requirements and their motivating cases.
