I am a PhD student in moral philosophy, and I have been trying to understand Adam Smith's account of sympathy without reducing it to emotional contagion or simple altruism. In Smith's account, sympathy is an imaginative operation: I place myself in your situation and form an idea of what you might feel, while you do the same with respect to me, so that our sentiments can be adjusted toward one another. The impartial spectator is the further step in which I stop asking what I happen to want and ask instead how my motives would appear to a judge who has stepped outside my particular interests. This is not just an external audience; it is an internalized standard that can still be distorted by the social world that formed it.

I want to test this against Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day. Stevens seems to be a man who has made his own life a matter of constant self-judgment, but the spectator he consults is not a neutral moral standpoint; it is a butler's conscience shaped by service, dignity, and deference. His relationship with Miss Kenton is especially telling, because his failure is not that he is incapable of feeling. He is capable of a very refined form of self-command; the problem is that he never makes the imaginative movement Smith requires, continuing to read her independence as an impropriety rather than as something that calls for a response. The ending, with his walk by the sea, is also difficult to classify. It is not a straightforward conversion scene, and it does not simply confirm that his whole life has been a mistake, but it does seem to mark the first moment at which he stops defending his conduct to the spectator he has always consulted.

Please write a critical reading of about 1000 to 1200 words that treats the novel as a case study in Smithian moral psychology. I would like you to say where the novel accurately dramatizes Smith's idea that moral improvement depends on learning to judge one's own passions from a less biased standpoint, where it shows that the impartial spectator can be corrupted by social role, and where Ishiguro resists the Smithian reading. In particular, I am interested in whether Stevens's case suggests that some failures of sympathy cannot be repaired by better self-judgment because the self that does the judging has been formed in the wrong place. Do not reduce the novel to an illustration of Smith, and do not end with a simple verdict that it is either Smithian or anti-Smithian; I want the tension between self-command and sympathetic failure kept alive.
