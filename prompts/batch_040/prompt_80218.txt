I spent thirty years in biochemistry, retired about four years ago, and to my family's mild horror I've been reading philosophy — mostly philosophy of action and the ethics of markets, which is where my old habits go to hide. Last week at a dinner with some old lab colleagues, two of them got into it about what we used to call "repugnant markets" — kidney sales, price gouging, selling your vote — and it went on for an hour while I kept refilling the wine.

Marta started: we should legalize the sale of kidneys. There's a shortage, people die on the waiting list, and the price mechanism is the most efficient allocator we've ever built; the ban is just paternalism — adults should be free to sell their own body parts, and the poor person who gets $50,000 for a kidney is better off than one who gets nothing. Dev pushed back: the point isn't efficiency. A market doesn't just allocate a thing, it transforms what the thing is. Once kidneys have a price, the kidney becomes a comodity, and the pressure spreads — first the poor become a resource to be harvested, then other parts, eventually the person. The ban is what keeps the body out of the market domain. Marta said that slippery-slope argument proves too much: by that logic we should ban deep-sea welding and mining, since the poor do those for money. And the ban doesn't stop the poor from selling kidneys; it pushes the sales underground, where there's no screening, no matching, no protection — so the ban doesn't protect the poor, it protects the rich, who can afford to buy transplants on the current system. Dev conceded the black-market point but said the labor parallel was wrong: a worker's labor is renewable — you can quit. Sell both kidneys and you die; sell one and you're permanently altered. The market assumes you can walk away from any trade, but a kidney sale is a trade you can't undo, so the "consent" in a kidney sale isn't the same kind as consent to a job. Marta then dragged price gouging into it: after a hurricane, the law caps the price of generators, and the result is that generators don't come and people die of heat; the price spike is exactly the signal that brings supply. So her opponent's anti-market intuition, applied consistently, justifies letting gougers gouge. Dev said there's a difference: the gouger just makes the victim poorer, but the kidney buyer permanently alters the seller's body — one commodifies a circumstance, the other commodifies the person. Marta's last shot was that Dev's "the person" argument is doing all the work and is undefinable — if you can't say what "commodifying the person" means beyond making it feel icky, it's not an argument — and she added, by your logic, why is selling a vote more repugnant than selling labor? Both are mine. Dev's reply was that a vote isn't an asset you own, it's a share of political authority — but he admitted corporations actually sell voting rights along with shares, so he wasn't sure the line held there. They both looked at me, and I honestly couldnt referee.

Can you walk through this exchange and tell me who is right about which specific point, and what, if anything, both of them are missing? I'm particularly curious about the labor parallel and the price-gouging move, since I think that's where the argument got real.
