I sit on my city's housing and commerce policy board, and we've been asked to rule on a new price-control proposal. Our own precedents so far: we approved rent stabilization — caps on annual increases, not fixed ceilings — for buildings in neighborhoods with documented displacement, with a five-year sunset; we rejected a citywide minimum-wage floor set above the state level; we approved a 90-day cap on the price of emergency repair and towing services after a major storm; we rejected a permanent price floor for produce at the city farmers' market; and we approved a two-year moratorium on converting long-term rentals to short-term rentals in one high-demand district. The new proposal would impose a permanent, citywide cap on the nightly rate for short-term rental listings, on the grounds that short-term rentals are driving up long-term rents and displacing residents. If we want to be consistent with ourselves, what should we decide, and which of our precedents is the closest analog? I'd also like you to lay out the distinct rules our past verdicts are jointly consistent with, what each of those rules predicts for this new case, and which future case would best tell those rules apart. And if our precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer, say honestly what principle we would be adopting by approving the cap versus by rejecting it.
