I've been stress testing my intuitions about punishment because in seminar everyone just says retributivism and consequentialism and I can't tell what work either is doing. My tentative principle is something like: you may punish a person only if they are blameworthy for the act, the punishment should be roughly proportional to their blameworthiness, and consequences like deterrence or incapacitation can shape the amount within that limit. But I keep getting stuck on cases. A murderer who has already served a long sentence that seems proportional to the crime, is genuinely reformed, and where extra incarceration would deter almost no one: I think further punishment is wrong, but I'm not sure whether that's because he has paid his debt or because there is no point. A negligent drunk driver who killed a child, has no realistic chance of driving again, and where punishment would barely deter: I still think some punishment is fair. A person who committed a serious violent act during a severe psychotic break and had no meaningful control over it: not punishment, that's treatment. An innocent person who is publicly punished to stop a mob from killing a dozen people: absolutely not, no amount of good consequences fixes it. A guilty low-level offender who is punished more harshly than their crime seems to warrant because it would deter a lot of more serious crime: I'm torn, but it feels less bad than the mob case, not just a matter of degree. A teenager who stole prescription medicine for a sick sibling: formal punishment is wrong, but maybe a warning or community service is okay, and I dont know if that's a difference in kind or in degree. A person who committed a crime under a coercive threat but had a small chance to refuse: I think some responsibility remains, but much less.

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or at least most of them, without just collapsing into the idea that blameworthiness is a switch and consequences do the rest? I especially want to know what it says about a case I keep going back and forth on: a guilty person who has already been punished by life-ruining collateral consequences, lost job, family, social standing, where a formal sentence would still be proportional to the crime and would deter some future offenses. I'd rather have one coherent account than a patchwork, but if the best account forces me to drop one of my verdicts, tell me which one and why.
