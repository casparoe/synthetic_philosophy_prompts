I'm doing a term paper on why nuclear deterrence is such a weird object for theory, and I keep going in circles, so I want to nail down the spec before I read much else: what does a theory of nuclear deterrence actually have to deliver to count as adequate? I'm fixing the purpose deliberately, because adequacy is relative to it. A theory good enough to explain (a) why the US and the USSR never fought a direct war after 1945 even though both had first-strike options, and (b) why the American guarantee to Western Europe in the 1960s was credible at all. Extended deterrence is the part I find hardest, because it looks like the chain-store paradox in disguise — a sequence of small crises where credibility has to be maintained without actually fighting.

Give me the list of desiderata: the cases it has to get right, the paradoxes it has to dissolve or at least take seriously (the credibility problem — the threat only works if you'd carry it out, but you'd never want to; the vulnerability paradox — the more exposed your second-strike forces are, the more incentive you have to use them first, which is exactly what makes your adversary nervous; the testability problem — a successful deterrence leaves no observable event, and a failed one just looks like an ordinary war), the distinctions it can't collapse (deterrence by punishment vs. by denial, direct vs. extended, and whatever the right cut is between rationalist and taboo-or-reputation-based accounts), with the case or consideration behind each requirement. And flag the pairs of desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfied — I have a hunch that "explain why deterrence kept working" and "be empirically testable" are in genuine tension, but I want that argued, not asserted.

Then, briefly, how the plain MAD / rationalist account, a reputation-and-signaling account, and a constructivist nuclear-taboo account do against the list — a scorecard is fine, but keep it short, the list is the point. One last thing: I've been rereading Schelling and I keep thinking deterrence is just a commitment problem in disguise, where the whole game is making yourself less rational — precommitting to retaliation, cutting off your options, deliberately raising the risk of miscalculation — so that a threat you wouldnt otherwise make becomes credible. I'd like the desiderata to say explicitly what a theory owes us on that role of deliberate irrationality and controlled risk, i.e. brinkmanship, rather than pretending the actors are just quietly maximizing.
