I work as a software engineer and have no formal philosophy background, but a question about contradictions keeps coming back to me, and I think the only way I will settle it is by looking carefully at my own reactions to a set of concrete cases.

In my day job, when a contradiction turns up in the data, I treat it as a bug, for a practical reason: if I let an inconsistent record stand, it tends to poison everything downstream. I have written query logic in which, once a single contradiction was present in the data, I could derive practically any fact I wanted about that data, and the only cure was to remove the inconsistency. I have since heard of a branch of logic concerned with tolerating contradictions without that kind of collapse, called paraconsistent logic, and of a stronger view, dialetheism, according to which some contradictions are not bugs at all but are genuinely true. I have not really realised how either is supposed to work, and I suspect my reactions to particular cases will show me where I actually stand.

So here are my verdicts on the following cases, about whether the contradiction is 'really' true in the sense that both sides hold at once, as opposed to being a bug, an artefact, or something in between:

1. The sentence 'This sentence is false.' I think it is both true and false, and I do not think there is a missing fact of the matter that would let me choose one side.
2. A pile of grains. There is some number of grains such that a pile of that many is not a heap, but a pile of one more is, and I cannot find the exact number. I think there is a borderline pile that counts as both a heap and not a heap. This is the case I keep going back and forth on.
3. A distributed database. Two nodes write different values to the same key, and the system holds both until a reconciliation rule selects one. While the conflict is unresolved I think the state is genuinely both A and not-A.
4. A plot of land that two overlapping statutes place on opposite sides of a jurisdiction. I think it is both inside and outside, until a court rules otherwise.
5. An account balance that, because of rounding, is both overdrawn and not overdrawn. I do not think that is a real contradiction; I think it is an artefact of how the number is represented.
6. A person whom the law says may hold only one nationality, who in fact holds two. I think that person is genuinely both.

The pair I cannot reconcile is cases 1 and 3. I accept both as 'really' true, yet I cannot recognise why they are the same kind of thing. The first has no rule that will ever settle it; the second has a reconciliation rule waiting to pick a side. I suspect that whichever principle explains my verdicts will force me to abandon one of those two, and I would like to know which one and why.

Is there a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures most of these verdicts? For each candidate, please state it precisely, identify which of my cases motivate each of its clauses, and say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. I would also like to know what the best candidate predicts for two cases I have not listed: first, a person who has been legally declared dead and who is later found to be alive; and second, a stretch of road at the point where it is both a hill and not a hill. And please tell me, plainly, which of my six verdicts the best principle forces me to give up.

Please write your answer in American English.
