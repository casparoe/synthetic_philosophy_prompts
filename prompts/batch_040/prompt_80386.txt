I write about science and ideas for a national newspaper, and I am currently preparing a feature on the combination problem in panpsychism — the worry that, even if the fundamental constituents of the physical world have some minimal experiential character of their own, it is far from obvious how those micro-experiences could ever give rise to the single, unified experience that a human being appears to have. As part of my research I have been interviewing a philosopher who is a committed panpsychist, and I have pressed her to give verdicts on a series of concrete cases: for each system, does it support a single unified experience, and if not, how many? Her answers so far are as follows. A single electron has some minimal intrinsic qualitative character, but is not a unified subject of experience. A single biological neuron is a unified subject, with an extremely simple experience. The brain of a nematode, with its few hundred neurons, supports one unified experience. An adult human brain supports one. A split-brain patient, whose corpus callosum has been completely severed, has two unified experiences, one in each hemisphere. And a honeybee colony, however impressive its collective problem-solving, has no colony-level experience at all — simply as many experiences as there are bees.

The case I would like to put to her next is a patient with a partial callosotomy, in whom the corpus callosum has been only partly severed, so that a small fraction of the normal inter-hemispheric traffic still gets through, but far less than in an unoperated brain. I would be grateful if you could work out what she is most likely to say about this patient, on the assumption that she is running one of a small number of coherent principles that fit all of the verdicts above. In particular, I would like you to: first, identify the candidate principles — for instance, some version of the view that unified experience supervenes on the causal or functional organisation of a system regardless of the substrate it is realised in, the view that unified experience arises only in biological neural tissue and never in anything else, and any other principle that fits the verdicts just as well; second, say what each principle predicts for the partial-callosotomy patient, and whether the predictions genuinely diverge; third, give your best guess as to which verdict she will actually give, with reasons for preferring one principle over the others on the evidence of her past answers; and fourth, name one further case — ideally one that could be settled by actual neuroscience rather than by further armchair stipulation — that would best discriminate between the principles that remain plausible.
