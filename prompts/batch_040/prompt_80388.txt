I've been following the decision-theory threads on the rationalist blogs for a few years now, and there's an objection to expected-value maximization that keeps coming up under the label "expected value fanaticism," but I've never been able to see why it's supposed to be a genuine problem rather than a mistake in the setup. As I understand the argument, the "fanaticism" is that if you really do maximize expected value, you're committed to acting on astronomically small probabilities whenever they're paired with astronomically large values — the standard example being that you should hand over $20 to a stranger who insists there's a 1-in-a-billion chance your money will save a million lives, because the expected value comes out positive.

Here's the part that's been bugging me, though. In that example, if the stranger is actually a con artist, the probability of the million lives being saved is just zero, not 1-in-a-billion, and the expected value of the donation is negative, so the expected-value calculation already tells you not to give the money. So it looks to me like the whole fanaticism only kicks in if you're willing to assign a nonzero probability to something that is in fact impossible, which is just an error in probability assignment, not a flaw in the expected-value principle itself.

So I have to ask: am I just missing something about why this is meant to be a substantive objection to maximizing expected value, or is the "fanaticism" really just a confusion about which probabilities you're allowed to assign?
