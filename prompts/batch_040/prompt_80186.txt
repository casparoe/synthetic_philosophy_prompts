I'm taking a graduate seminar on pragmatist epistemology, and during the discussion of the Peirce–James debate two of my classmates, Maya and Jonah, ended up in a long exchange that I found myself unable to referee. The instructor had asked us to take a side on whether James's pragmatic theory of truth is a defensible development of Peirce's or a corruption of it, and they took opposite sides and kept escalating. I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher with deep expertise in the history of American pragmatism, particularly the Peirce–James controversy over the pragmatic conception of truth. Before you issue any verdicts, please take a moment to map out, in your own words, the actual positions of Peirce and James as you understand them, so that your adjudication is grounded in the real theories rather than in a caricature of either.

Here is the exchange as I remember it. Maya, arguing for Peirce, opened by saying that James's theory is just Peirce's with the communal limit quietly removed: for Peirce, truth is the opinion that would be held by an ideal community of inquirers at the limit of infinite inquiry, which makes it mind-independent and stable, whereas for James, truth is whatever 'works' for a particular believer in a particular life, and the moment you drop the communal limit, 'works' collapses into 'feels good,' with the result that a delusional patient who believes he is Napoleon is 'truly' Napoleon for him. Jonah replied that this was a straw man, because James explicitly rejects the idea that truth is what feels good: for James, 'works' has a precise meaning, namely that a belief works if, when you act on it, it guides you through experience to satisfy your desires, which is a relation to the world rather than a mere projection, so the Napoleon belief is false for the delusional patient because acting on it gets him confined. Maya conceded that was James's claim but said the mechanism was circular, because you can only tell whether a belief is working by its success, and you can only tell whether the success is real by the belief, and she added that James's view is self-undermining in that it makes truth relative to individual interests, which is exactly the method of tenacity that Peirce identified as the most primitive and least reliable way of fixing belief. Jonah responded that Maya was misreading James as a relativist when James was actually a pluralist, and that truth, for James, is a real property of the relation between an idea and experience but a process rather than a finished product, so an idea becomes true and is made true by events rather than being a static, timeless fact. Maya then moved to a case that mattered to her: a terminally ill patient, Ruth, who believes there is an afterlife, and for whom the belief organizes her remaining months so that she finishes her memoir, reconciles with her estranged daughter, and dies with a serenity her family describes as genuine. She argued that on James's own test the belief works, so by James it is true, but that this is absurd, because the belief is almost certainly false in the only sense that matters, and calling it true because it is comforting is exactly the conflation that Peirce warned about in his letter to James — that James was making truth a matter of personal expediency — whereas Peirce's view at least has the decency to classify Ruth's belief as a fixation of belief, a method of tenacity, that ideal inquiry would eventually correct. Jonah replied that it was not absurd and that Maya was smuggling in a Peircean premise, because for James the question is not whether Ruth's belief would survive ideal inquiry, which he dismissed as a belief in a belief that never cashes out in any experience, but whether the belief has a cash value in Ruth's actual life, which it obviously does, and that Maya's phrase 'false in the only sense that matters' was doing all the work by assuming that the Peircean, mind-independent, limit-of-inquiry sense of truth is the only sense, which is exactly what was in dispute. Maya then said they had located the real disagreement, which was whether truth is a limit (Peirce) or a process (James), and that she thought Peirce won, because James's process view has no stopping condition, so if truth is whatever keeps working, a belief that works today but would mislead you tomorrow is 'true' today and truth becomes a moving target that never converges, whereas Peirce's limit at least gives you something stable to aim at even if you can never reach it. Jonah closed by saying that this stability was precisely the cost, because Peirce's limit is unreachable by definition, so no finite inquirer can know what the ideal community would believe at the limit, which means Peirce's theory gives you no criterion for what is true now and cannot even tell you whether Peirce's own theory is true, whereas James's view is cashable right here and right now in the consequences of the belief, so you can live by it but you can't live by Peirce's.

Could you work through their exchange and, for each substantive claim, tell me which side has the better case and on what grounds, point out where either of them is misreading the other's actual position, and say what the real crux is and what both of them seem to be overlooking about the Peirce–James debate as it actually stands — for instance, whether the distinction between truth and justification plays a role that neither of them draws, whether James's view really is the relativism Maya attributes to it, and whether Peirce's limit is best read as a regulative ideal rather than a reachable fact? Close with a bottom-line judgment on who comes out ahead and where the disagreement is genuinely irreducible.
