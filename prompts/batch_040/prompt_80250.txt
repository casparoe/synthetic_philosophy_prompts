I'm in a graduate seminar on social choice theory, and we're working through Harsanyi's aggregation theorem — the result that a social welfare function satisfying a Pareto condition, a non-dictatorship condition, and an independence of irrelevant alternatives condition (equivalently, the requirement that social preferences over lotteries be linear in the probabilities of the states) must be a weighted sum of individual utilities. My professor keeps insisting that the independence condition is "the whole game," that it is doing essentially all the normative work in the proof, while a classmate keeps trying to defeat the theorem by attacking the Pareto principle instead, citing rights and desert cases in which everyone's utility is higher but we plainly shouldn't prefer the outcome. I find their disagreement genuinely confusing, because the Pareto principle looks like it could be the more controversial assumption. Please argue, in my favor, that the classmate is aiming at the wrong target: that the independence of irrelevant alternatives condition is the assumption doing the real work in forcing the weighted-sum, utilitarian conclusion, and that a critic who wants to reject utilitarianism should direct the attack there rather than at the Pareto condition.

In particular, I'd like you to (i) explain why the Pareto and non-dictatorship conditions are, taken separately and even together, weak enough to be compatible with a wide range of non-additive social welfare functions — for instance a Rawlsian max-min rule that ranks social states by the welfare of the worst-off person — and so cannot by themselves rule out non-utilitarian aggregation; (ii) show how it is specifically the independence or linearity condition that excludes exactly those non-additive rules, by explaining concretely what goes wrong for a max-min rule when the social evaluation is required to be linear in the probabilities of the states; and (iii) make the parallel with Arrow's impossibility theorem explicit, arguing that the independence condition is the same kind of demanding assumption that makes Arrow's theorem bite, and that this is precisely why it is the most vulnerable assumption and the most worth attacking.

I want the argument to be crisp and technical, aimed at someone in the room who already knows the statement of the theorem, rather than a re-explanation of what the theorem says. And please end by saying plainly, on this analysis, whether the classmate's Pareto-based objections are beside the point or merely secondary, and what a non-utilitarian would have to give up in order to keep the independence condition.
