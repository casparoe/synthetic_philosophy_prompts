I work at a small foundation that funds conflict-prevention programs, and we're in the middle of a grant review where one of our board members keeps bringing up "the world is becoming less violent" as a reason to cut funding. His logic is basically: if violence is already on a long-term downward trend, why are we pouring money into preventing conflicts that history will solve on its own? We're just riding a trend, and our grants are reduntant. I keep feling like I can't push back because I'm not actually sure I know what the claim is, and I'm not even sure there's a single fact of the matter here versus the slogan just doing rhetorical work. Can you lay out the different precise claims that "the world is becoming less violent" could be making? I'm specifically thinking about the per-capita vs. absolute distinction (the population's gone from about 2.5 billion to 8 billion, so a per-capita decline can hide an absolute rise in violent deaths), which categories of violence are being counted (interstate war deaths, internal conflict, homicide, state and police violence, gender-based violence — these have very different trends and very different measurement problems), and over what time window (since 1945, since 1100, since the state emerged, or just the last couple of decades). For each reading, say whether it's defensible and whether it actually supports his conclusion that we shouldn't fund conflict prevention. And tell me which reading, if any, is decision-relevant for a foundation whose job is deciding how many lives its grants will save, as opposed to which readings are just a comforting narrative about long-run history. I want a menu I can bring to the board meeting, not a single answer.
