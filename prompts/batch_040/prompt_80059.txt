I'm taking a seminar on the epistemology of collective judgment, and we spent a week on rational information cascades — the setup in which agents decide one after another, each can observe everyone's earlier choice, and each also receives a private signal that is only weakly informative about the true state (whether a restaurant is any good, whether a security is overpriced, that sort of thing). A classmate raised a worry about this model that I then tried to steelman into a clean argument, and I can't find the bug: every step looks right on its own, and yet the conclusion is something I'm sure is false. Here is the argument as I wrote it up.

(1) In a rational information cascade, each agent compares her private signal against the belief that the sequence of earlier choices already encodes, and she follows the crowd whenever her signal is not strong enough to overturn that belief.
(2) Once a cascade is underway, the agents who sustain it are precisely those whose signals are too weak to overturn the crowd, so their choices contribute no new information about the true state beyond what the crowd's belief already contains.
(3) So once a cascade has begun, watching later agents act teaches nothing new: conforming choices are uninformative, and conforming choices are all the choices there are.
(4) If later choices teach nothing new, then a cascade that starts in the wrong direction can never be corrected by what happens afterwards; the first mistaken cascade, once triggered, is permanent.
(5) But mistaken cascades plainly do get overturned in the world — a bad restaurant's queue thins once enough people have actually eaten there, a mispriced security is walked back as information arrives, a fad dies.
(6) Therefore rational information cascades do not occur, and every cascade we actually observe must be kept alive by something irrational — herd instinct, social pressure, or a failure to do the Bayesian arithmetic.

The conclusion (6) is one I'm confident is false, and the claims I'm least willing to give up are (1), (2), and (5), since the first two are basically just how the model is defined and the last is just what I keep seeing around me. So I'd like a diagnosis rather than a defense: tell me which step you reject, why it looked plausible to someone who knows the model, and what I would have to pay to reject it. In particular, I want to know whether the fix is to weaken (3) in some way, to deny (4) outright, or to concede that the sort of "correction" described in (5) is not the sort of correction the model was ever supposed to deliver.
