I keep running into a distinction I need when I think about those cases where two people independently come up with the same thing — Newton and Leibniz on calculus, Darwin and Wallace on natural selection, that sort of thing — and I can't find a word for it. The question "who gets credit" seems to fork into two different questions that people just conflate. One is whether the discovery was inevitable: given what was already known, was someone bound to find it, so it was basically in the air and the individual is just the one who happend to be standing in the right spot? The other is whether it was contingent: did it hinge on some specific move or insight that only this person could have made, so that they really made it happen rather than just catching it? And I've noticed these two pull in opposite directions for credit. If it was inevitable, the person gets less credit, it was coming anyway. If it was contingent, they get more, only they could have done it. But the actual question of who gets the name attached — who we say found it first — seems to track neither of these. It tracks documentation and timing: who wrote it down, who published, who can show a dated notebook. That's a third thing, and I can't tell it apart cleanly from the other two. So is there an established term for this configuration, the way "who deserves credit" forks into "was it inevitable or contingent" and then gets answered by a third question that doesn't track either of them? And if not, what would you call the fork, and where exactly are the edges? Like, what about the cases where the discovery was inevitable but the specific formulation was contingent — Newton and Leibniz both got calculus, sure, but their notations and their whole approaches were different, so who do we say found calculus, and does the answer depend on which of the three questions we're actually asking?
