I am assembling problem sets for my graduate seminar on the philosophy of mind, and I would like your help auditing a passage I am considering using as a starting point. A recent popular book on consciousness gives the following account of the color phi puzzle and of the distinction it is supposed to generate:

"In the color phi experiment, two dots of different colors are flashed in rapid succession at two different positions on a screen. At certain intervals between the flashes, the subject reports seeing a single dot that appears to move from the first position to the second, and that changes color abruptly at the midpoint of its path. The puzzle is that the subject reports the color change as happening midway through the motion — that is, at a point in time before the second dot has even been physically presented — so it looks as though the subject had an experience of a color that the sensory input at that moment could not have supported.

The book says that anyone who keeps what is sometimes called the 'Cartesian theatre' picture — the idea that there is a single privileged moment at which content 'arrives' at the seat of consciousness and so becomes something the subject is conscious of — is forced to choose between two options. On the Orwellian option, the brain first registers the first dot in its true color, and only later, once the second dot has been seen, physically rewrites the stored record so that the color change seems to have happened earlier. On the Stalinesque option, the brain never registers the first dot in its original color at all; the two flashes are reconciled before anything reaches consciousness, and the subject is presented with a single, already-resolved percept in which the color change is built in from the start. The book concludes that the Stalinesque option is the more plausible of the two, because it avoids the implausible idea that the brain edits its own records after the fact, and that the color phi phenomenon thereby shows that conscious experience is a reconstruction rather than a direct readout of the sensory input."

The book's account is, I think, a loose popularization of the distinction between what are usually called Orwellian and Stalinesque revisions, as it is developed in the multiple drafts model of consciousness associated with Daniel Dennett. I have not yet decided how much of the book's version I can rely on, and I suspect that at least one load-bearing claim is off. Please give me an accuracy audit, claim by claim: which parts are accurate, which are common simplifications, and which are genuine misrepresentations that a defender of the underlying framework would object to. In particular, I want you to say whether the account correctly captures what the two options are supposed to agree about and where they are supposed to differ, and whether the conclusion the book draws — that one of the two options is to be preferred, and that the phenomenon thereby establishes a reconstructionist thesis about experience — actually follows from the parts of the account that are correct.

Finally, one of my students has proposed a way out that the book never mentions: that the whole dilemma rests on treating the relevant experience as if it were located at a single instant, and that once we treat the experience as a temporally extended "specious present" — a short window of ongoing experience rather than a point-like event — the color phi report is no longer paradoxical at all. I would like you to assess whether that move is a genuine third option, whether it is really just one of the book's two options in different clothing, or whether it simply rejects a premise the two options share. And tell me whether, whichever it turns out to be, it changes your verdict on the book's errors.
