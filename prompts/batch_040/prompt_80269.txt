I spent thirty-five years as a physical chemist, and only in the last two or three years have I taken up philosophy on my own initiative, working slowly through the arguments in the philosophy of well-being and, more specifically, in the defence and criticism of hedonism. One line keeps cropping up in everything I read and in the long conversations I still have with former colleagues over dinner: Mill's remark that it is better to be a Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied. It is invoked constantly, usually to shut down the objection that utilitarianism is the philosophy of swine, and it always seems to do some work, but I have never been able to tell exactly what it is doing. I would be grateful if you could take this one-liner and lay out, in the form of a menu I can actually bring back to those conversations, the distinct precise claims it could be making — not a single interpretation but the whole range, from the most trivial to the most radical.

What I especially want is for each candidate reading to be tested against what I have taken to calling the heterogeneity problem: if the pleasure of a good meal, the pleasure of working through a difficult calculation, and the pleasure of a warm bath are all to count as instances of the single intrinsic good that hedonism is built on, what exactly do they share, and does the Socrates line help that project or quietly undermine it? For each reading, please say whether it amounts to a platitude, to a substantive and defensible thesis, or to something that is actually false or misleading, and finish by telling me which reading is the one the people who repeat the line most likely mean when they use it to end a discussion. I would like the bottom line in the first sentence and then the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
