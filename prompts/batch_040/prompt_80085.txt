I'm a novelist, and this came up at a dinner with two of my friends — a linguist, Maren, and a fellow novelist, Tobias. Tobias is finishing a manuscript, and a passage in it set them off. The central character of his novel has been blind since birth, and he gives her a rich inner life by having her talk about the world in color and light: she calls her dead father's voice "a deep, patient blue," and a bad day "full of hard white light." Maren read the pages and wanted them cut. They argued for an hour, and I sat there taking notes because I'm mid-draft on my own book and keep hitting the same problem — how far can a character stretch a word before it stops meaning anything — and I honestly couldn't tell who was right. I've transcribed the shape of it below. Could you go through it move by move and tell me who is right about which specific point, and what, if anything, the two of them are both missing?

Maren: "This doesn't work. She has never seen a color. For 'blue' to mean anything to her, there has to be some fixed content — an experience or a criterion that pins down what 'blue' is — and she has none of that. So her color words are empty. She's producing sounds that happen to rhyme. It's a private language with no anchor, and private languages don't cohere."

Tobias: "You're assuming a word has to be nailed to a private sensation to mean anything, and that's exactly the picture Wittgenstein spent his later career dismantling. Her use of 'blue' is governed by public rules — she knows the color wheel, the associations, the way everyone else deploys color talk. What gives a word its meaning is its use, and this use is intelligible to a reader. There's no essence she's failed to hit."

Maren: "Even granting 'use,' 'blue' has a core: the sky, the sea, a band of wavelengths. Her usage is so far from any core that it isn't an extension, it's a different word wearing blue's coat. You can't let any use count, or the word dissolves. And notice who's deciding it's intelligible — you, the author, are the sole judge of your character's meaning, with no public check. That's the private-language problem all over again."

Tobias: "Two replies. First, 'blue' isn't as core-bound as you think: there is no single wavelength that is the blue, it's a family with fuzzy edges. Second, and this is the one that matters — even if we agreed on a rule for 'blue,' there is no fact of the matter about what that rule requires in a case like hers. A rule is a finite pattern and there are infinitely many cases, so any pattern can be made to fit any new use. Your appeal to a fixed rule doesn't buy you a fixed meaning; it just moves the mystery one step back."

Maren: "Then you've conceded it. If no rule fixes the meaning, meaning isn't fixed at all, and I'm right that her words are empty. You've only shown that my version was wrong, not that your passage works. And I'll add a point you can't wave away: some words genuinely do have cores — 'bachelor,' 'triangle,' 'seven.' For those I'm plainly right, so your general theory, which has to explain them too, can't be right."

Tobias: "I haven't conceded emptiness. I've said the meaning lives in the public use, and that use is checkable — by the reader, by the community, by the whole web of color talk. Your 'bachelor' point actually helps me: 'bachelor' has a core because of its use in a community, not because of some Platonic essence. So we agree that use does the work; we only disagree about how far a use can stretch before it breaks. And in her case it doesn't break — the reader tracks her."

Two asks about how you handle this. First, please keep apart what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who actually work in this area — I don't want a live dispute (say, over whether the private-language argument really succeeds, or over how much of our conceptual life is genuinely family-resemblance rather than core-bound) presented as if it were just the consensus. Second, organize your answer in these sections, in this order, roughly this long: "The moves" — a short map of the six moves and what each one is doing, about a hundred words; "Move by move" — the actual adjudication, rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justifying the rating, and this is the body, so give it the most room; "What both are missing" — the shared blind spots, about a hundred and fifty words; "Settled and contested" — what is settled in the philosophy of language here versus what is live debate, about a hundred and fifty words; and "Verdict" — who is more right overall, why, and what Tobias (and by extension I) should do with the passage, about a hundred words.
