I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm currently drafting a feature on how the press and the public talk about the perpetrators of state violence. In a recent interview, a historian kept reaching for Arendt's phrase "the banality of evil" — the one from her report on the Eichmann trial — to push back against my describing a mid-level state security official as a monster. I've noticed the phrase does remarkably different work in different mouths, and I'm not sure I can tell which version my interlocutor was deploying. Sometimes it seems to mean the perpetrators were ordinary, unremarkable people rather than fanatics; sometimes that the capacity for evil is universal and available to anyone; sometimes that thoughtlessness — a failure to think or to take another's perspective — is the mechanism that makes ordinary people capable of atrocity; sometimes that evil is simply a mundane feature of political life rather than some demonic abyss; and sometimes it slides, it feels to me, into the deflationary claim that evil is ordinary and therefore unremarkable.

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses this one phrase could actually be expressing, from the trivial to the radical, and for each say whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or actually false — and, where you can, which of those Arendt herself was plausibly committed to as opposed to which are later accretions or misreadings? I'd rather have a menu with verdicts than a single reconstruction of her view.

The specific work the phrase was doing in my conversation was this: my interlocutor was using it to argue that labeling a perpetrator a "monster" is both historically inaccurate and morally counterproductive, because it externalizes the evil onto a few inhuman individuals and lets the broader bureaucratic machinery off the hook. Which of the readings you lay out would actually support that argument, and which would not? And is there a reading on which "the banality of evil" is doing real philosophical work rather than just functioning as a rhetorical flourish that ends a conversation?
