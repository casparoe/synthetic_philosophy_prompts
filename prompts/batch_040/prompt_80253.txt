Last night I had a long argument with a friend about why people enjoy tragedies, and I'd like a second opinion. Her move was to dissolve the paradox of tragedy with the rollercoaster analogy: we enjoy the fear on a coaster because we know we're safe, and we enjoy the sadness of a tragedy because we know it's fiction, so the "paradox" was always just a confusion between real and safe negative emotion. I suspect the analogy is doing more work than it can bear. Act as a professional philosopher working in the aesthetics of emotion, and think carefully before answering. Begin with a short brainstorming list of the distinct puzzles the analogy is being pressed to dissolve — I believe there are at least two (why we feel anything at all toward fictional characters, versus why we enjoy the negative emotion we do feel) — and check whether that decomposition is right. Then give a correspondence table mapping elements of the coaster (the drop, the harness, the queue, the scream) onto elements of watching a tragedy. Then audit where the mapping breaks down: in particular, whether the analogy quietly assumes the negative emotion is intrinsically enjoyable, what it says or fails to say about the pleasure residing in the object rather than the emotion, and what "safety" even means for a sadness that, unlike coaster-fear, nobody wants to experience for its own sake — keeping in mind that the rider's fear is directed at a real body while the spectator's tears are directed at a character we know is not there. Close by saying whether her dissolution survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and, if not, what a better analogy or non-analogical account would look like. Write in American English, and keep the answer free of citations and reading lists.
