ESSAY QUESTION (as assigned):
Explain the disagreement between Mencius and Xunzi over the moral status of human nature (xing). In your answer, address each of the following: (a) what each thinker takes 'xing' itself to be; (b) the function of the 'four sprouts' (si duan) in the Mencian position and of 'artifice' (wei) in the Xunzian position; (c) how each thinker deploys the analogy of water; and (d) whether, in your judgment, the disagreement is best understood as a substantive disagreement about human nature, or as a terminological disagreement about what 'xing' means. Defend your answer to (d).

STUDENT SUBMISSION:
The Mencius–Xunzi debate is the classic Chinese discussion of whether human nature (xing) is good or evil. Both thinkers use 'xing' to refer to what a person has from birth, but they disagree about the moral valence of that endowment.

Mencius holds that human nature is innately good. His main evidence is the four sprouts (si duan): every person has an innate sprout of compassion that, developed, becomes humaneness (ren); a sprout of shame that becomes righteousness (yi); a sprout of deference that becomes ritual propriety (li); and a sprout of right and wrong that becomes wisdom (zhi). The thought experiment of the child about to fall into a well illustrates the first sprout: anyone who suddenly sees the child feels a pang of alarm and distress, and this is not in order to gain favor, to avoid blame, or for any social payoff, but is a spontaneous moral response. This shows that the source of goodness is 'in us' (nei) rather than 'outside us' (wai). We do not need to implant goodness from without; we need only 'extend' and nourish the sprouts we already have. On the analogy of water, Mencius argues that water has no fixed preference for east or west, but it does have a fixed preference for flowing downward; in the same way, human beings have a fixed tendency toward goodness.

Xunzi holds that human nature is evil. Human beings are born with desires for gain, for pleasure, and for status, and if we simply follow our nature and indulge these desires, the result is contention, scarcity, and social disorder. Goodness, then, is not to be found in our nature; it is the product of deliberate artifice (wei). Observing the disorder that results from following nature, the sages invented ritual (li) and law in order to transform (hua) and regulate human beings. The carpenter-and-wood analogy expresses this: a piece of bent wood is straightened by the steamer and the clamp, but the straightness is the product of the craftsman's work, not of the wood's own nature. On the analogy of water, Xunzi grants that water does flow downward, but he adds that water can be made to run upward or sideways by being channeled and dammed; in the same way, human beings can be made good through the channeling work of ritual.

I take the disagreement to be substantive rather than terminological. Both thinkers are using 'xing' in the same sense — the innate endowment a person possesses from birth — and they genuinely disagree about whether that endowment is good or bad. They are not merely talking past one another over a shared word; they are making incompatible claims about the same thing.
