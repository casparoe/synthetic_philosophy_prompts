I've been stuck on this for a while and I cant get it to line up, so I'm dumping it here. Precommitment devices: you know the family. Odysseus gets himself lashed to the mast so he physically can't steer toward the sirens; someone leaves their car keys with a friend after too much wine; you put money in a savings pot you can't touch for a year; a government publicly pledges "cross this line and we retaliate" and is counting on the threat being believable. The shared idea is that the you-at-the-moment-of-temptation won't be the you-who-planned-this, so you rig the situation in advance.

The thing that's bugging me is that the rigging seems to assume exactly the move the two-boxer makes in Newcomb's problem. In Newcomb, the two-boxer reasons: the prediction is already made, the opaque box is already sealed, so holding fixed what's already locked in, grabbing both is strictly better, therefore I take both. That's "re-optimise from the present state, ignore what's already decided." And that's precisely what precommitment assumes you'll do when the temptation hits — re-optimise, ignore the plan, take the locally better option. So on the surface the whole practice presupposes the two-boxing view of the agent: you are a moment-to-moment re-optimiser who will defect on the commitment.

But then the pieces don't fit. A *binding* device (lashed to the mast, keys gone) works even if you never would have caved — it just removes the option — so it doesn't seem to need the re-optimiser at all, it just makes the committed thing the only thing. Whereas a *pledge* (a threat you could quietly walk back) only has any force if you are NOT a re-optimiser, i.e. if you're the fixed type who keeps your word, which is the one-boxing side. So one half of the practice is built on the idea that you'll defect and the other half only works if you won't, and I genuinely can't tell which view of the agent the practice actually commits to, or whether it's just straddling both and getting away with it.
