I'm putting together a case study for a seminar on the epistemology of long-run prediction, and I want a worked example of what a well-run adversarial collaboration looks like once the two sides have been talking past each other for a long time. The parties here are a committed Malthusian and a committed Boserupian-technological optimist. The Malthusian holds that population growth, left to its own tendency, outstrips the growth of the food supply, so the long-run outcome is a Malthusian catastrophe — famine, disease, or some other positive check — and that the fact we have not yet hit that catastrophe is not a falsification but either that we are "right but early" (a matter of when, not whether) or that the checks are quietly doing their work and so the theory is being partially confirmed even now. The Boserupian-optimist holds that Malthus's load-bearing assumption (a fixed stock of agricultural land and diminishing returns that eventually dominate) is empirically wrong, that population pressure is actually the engine of agricultural intensification and innovation, and that the long record of rising yields — the Green Revolution included — is a genuine falsification of the Malthusian trajectory, not a temporary reprieve before the bill comes due.

Write the document such a collaboration is supposed to yield — the statement both camps can put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact. I want the claims both accept (about the actual history of population and food production, about what Malthus argued in his 1798 Essay, and about what each side concedes the other gets right); the precise statement of where they still disagree, each side's view phrased so that its own camp would accept the wording; and the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. I suspect at least two of the cruxes are these: whether a long-run prediction of the form "eventually X" is falsifiable at all, or whether the "right but early" / "the checks are working" move is a legitimate reading of the theory or an ad hoc rescue that renders it unfalsifiable; and whether the observed checks (famine, disease, declining fertility, the demographic transition) count as confirmation of Malthus or as evidence that the Malthusian dynamic has been broken. For each crux, give a concrete case, argument, or body of evidence both would agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts about it. If you conclude part of the dispute is verbal, say which part and why.

Format it as a numbered section of agreed claims, followed by a disagreements table whose rows are the residual disagreements and whose columns are the Malthusian's wording, the optimist's wording, and the crux each row hangs on. End with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that names, in one sentence, the one crux whose resolution would most move the debate.
