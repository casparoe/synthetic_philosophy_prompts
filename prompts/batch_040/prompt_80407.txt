Compare Hofstadter's superrationality with Aumann's agreement theorem. Both rest on the intuition that rational agents who know their counterparts are rational end up aligned, but one is a decision rule and the other a theorem about beliefs. In exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, address: (1) the shared core intuition each exploits; (2) what each actually governs — decisions versus beliefs, and why that distinction matters; (3) the load-bearing assumption each depends on (a common prior, versus a common decision procedure or "facing the same problem"); (4) what each does when its core assumption fails — a diagnosis of broken assumptions in Aumann's case, a predicted failure mode in the superrational case; and (5) whether Aumann-style agreement about beliefs is enough to license the superrational move in the prisoner's dilemma, given that the "event" in question is the other player's own action rather than an exogenous state, or whether the move depends on a stronger "common algorithm" principle that Aumann's framework does not supply.
