I'm preparing a decision-tree handout for a philosophy of mind seminar on panpsychism — the view, most prominently defended in recent analytic philosophy by Philip Goff, that mentality is fundamental and ubiquitous in the natural world — and specifically on the "combination problem": how, if micro-subjects exist, a unified subject like yours could arise from them. Write a decision tree that takes a student from the starting commitment "mentality is fundamental and ubiquitous" to a named position, with the following requirements.

Branch nodes must be claims that genuinely separate positions, not labels a student would have to already know. Phrase each node as a yes/no question the student can answer by reflecting on a case, without knowing which leaf it leads to. At least eight of the nodes should be concrete cases, and you must work in these three: (a) the fusion case — two beings with separate visual fields are surgically joined into one body with one unified visual field; does the unified field consist of, or is it constituted by, the two prior fields, or does something new come into existence? (b) the holographic case — a scattered, non-contiguous subset of the micro-subjects (say, every other electron in the brain) realizes the whole experience rather than a part of it; does that bother you, and why? (c) the exclusion case — suppose the micro-experiences are causally inert, with all the causal work done by the physical structure; does the theory still get anything from them, or have they been quietly excluded?

The leaves must include, at minimum: bottom-up panpsychism with a constitution story (the macro-experience is literally made up of the micro-experiences), bottom-up panpsychism with an addition or emergence story (combination yields something over and above the parts), cosmopsychism (a single fundamental cosmic subject whose experiences are the micro-experiences, top-down rather than bottom-up), Russellian monism (intrinsic physical properties are proto-experiential, so no brute gap remains), illusionism about unity (there never was a unified subject to combine into), and a deflationary leaf holding that the combination problem is a pseudo-problem. Each leaf should carry the position's name (or "unnamed" if nobody defends it) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate, and briefly say why. Note any unoccupied combination of answers the tree's branches allow, and for each say whether nobody is there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Format as a numbered tree, with a short final section mapping answer patterns to leaves. Around 700–900 words. No citations, footnotes, or reading list.
