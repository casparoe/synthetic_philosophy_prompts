Im the TA for a bioethics class and I'm grading a short answer from last weeks exam. I'd like a second pair of eyes on it before I lock in the score. The question was:

"In clinical practice, death is determined either by circulatory criteria (irreversible cessation of circulation and respiration) or by brain death criteria (irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brain stem). Some philosophers argue that the brain death criterion is justified not because the brain is the seat of the person, but because the whole brain is what integrates the organism. Explain this organism argument, and explain why it is thought to avoid the problem that the higher brain (neocortical) criterion faces with persistent vegetative state patients."

Here's what the student wrote:

The circulatory criterion is the older one: a person is dead when their heart and lungs stop and can't be restarted. The brain death criterion is the newer one, used for patients on a ventilator who have no brain function left. The important difference is that the brain death criterion requires the whole brain, including the brain stem, to have stopped, not just the higher brain.

The organism argument says that an individual is a living organism in virtue of its capacity to integrate and self-maintain its parts, and that the brain is the organ that does this integrating. So when the whole brain irreversibly ceases to function, the organism loses what made it a unified living thing, and that's why we call it death. The brain stem has to be included because it governs basic functions like breathing, so without it the body can't sustain itself. I think this is the view that Shoemaker defends.

The higher brain criterion, by contrast, says that death is the irreversible loss of the capacity for consciousness, which depends on the neocortex. This criterion runs into trouble with persistent vegetative state patients. A PVS patient has lost their neocortex but still has a working brain stem, so they are alive under the higher brain criterion but dead under the organism criterion, which seems backwards. The organism criterion avoids this problem because it grounds death in the loss of the organism's integrating capacity. And honestly, the organism argument and the higher brain argument aren't that different in the end, since the brain is what's doing all the work in both of them.
