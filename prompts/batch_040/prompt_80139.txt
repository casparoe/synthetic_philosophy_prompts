a friend who's into philosophy of language explains reference to me with a bucket-brigade picture: when someone first pointed at a substance and called it water, they started a line, and every speaker after is just a hand in that line passing the same bucket along, so what "water" means for me is fixed by the line I'm standing in, not by anything in my head. He uses this to argue against the idea that meaning is internal — that two people with identical beliefs and experiences could still differ in what their words refer to, if they're in different chains (his example is the Twin Earth case, where the twin's "water" hooks onto a different stuff).

I want you to audit how far the bucket-brigade picture actually holds. Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the first bucket, each hand, the line, and the water in the bucket. Then the places it breaks. I'm especially interested in (a) the fact that a bucket brigade loses water at every handoff, which is the opposite of what the picture is being made to do, and (b) the fact that real chains of language don't have clean single starting points and don't stay one unbroken line — you can pick up a word from a book with no live link to anyone who baptized it, and two different lines can merge or fork. Tell me what the picture quietly imports that reference doesn't actually have, and whether the anti-internalist conclusion he wants survives once those disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing all the work. Also, if you think a different picture would carry the point better, say which one and why.

when you're done, separate what's settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely still contested, and don't hand me a contested position dressed up as consensus. i don't need citations or a reading list, just the reasoning.
