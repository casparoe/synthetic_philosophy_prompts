I'm writing a long-form piece on the so-called gender-equality paradox — the finding that in the most gender-egalitarian countries, roughly the Nordics, women's career choices turn out to be more gender-seperated than in less egalitarian ones, with a higher share of women in care and education and a lower share in STEM than you'd expect. I'm not asking you to litigate the finding itself; just assume it's roughly real and help me think through the explanations I've collected, some of which I've written down sloppily.

Here's my shortlist. (1) The selection story: in egalitarian countries more women work at all, and the marginal women who enter the labor market are disproportionately the ones with a strong preference for family-friendly, socially oriented work, so the average gender gap in career choice widens even though no individual woman's preferences changed. (2) The complementarity story: when women have more bargaining power, couples sort more efficiently around who earns more, so the higher-earner works more and the lower-earner works less, making men's and women's career choices more complementary and the overall occupational distribution more gendered. (3) The unleashed-preferences story: in less egalitarian countries women are funneled into a narrow set of acceptable roles, so once you remove the constraints the observed distribution better reflects actual preferences, which really are more gendered on average. (4) The institutional-structure story: generous parental leave plus a large, protected public sector — which is heavily female — shapes the occupational landscape itself, so the "paradox" partly just reflects that egalitarian institutions create a big female-dominated sector. (5) The measurement story: the equality indices the finding leans on measure institutional or policy equality, not equality of actual outcomes or of opportunity, so part of the "paradox" is an artifact of what's being compared. (6) The identity story: in egalitarian countries being a woman is less tied to economic dependence, so gendered self-expression can run through career choice rather than domestic roles, making gendered preferences more visible in the data.

If I've botched or conflated any of these, flag it before you rank them. Otherwise, rank them (or group them into two or three tiers) by how much I should trust each as the main driver of the pattern — i.e., if I had to bet the piece on one explanation, which do I bet on, and which are really just compatible with it rather than competing with it? Give me one or two sentences per explanation on why it lands where it does, and for the top one or two, tell me what evidence or argument would most change your mind.
