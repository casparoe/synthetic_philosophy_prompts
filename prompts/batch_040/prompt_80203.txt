A colleague of mine has been arguing that inverse reinforcement learning is vacuous, and I keep coming back to it because I can't find the step that's doing the illicit work. For context: the standard IRL setup assumes the environment (the MDP's transition structure) is known and the only unknown is the agent's reward function R, and it models the agent as choosing the action that maximizes expected cumulative reward. Here is his argument.

(1) In that setup, the agent's behavior is fully determined by R: the observed actions are the ones that maximize expected reward under R.

(2) For any finite sequence of observed actions, there exists at least one reward function R* under which those actions were optimal. You can always construct R* to hand out large reward exactly on the state–action pairs the agent actually visited and little elsewhere, which makes the observed trajectory optimal.

(3) So the observed behavior is compatible with R*, and — by the same construction — equally compatible with any other reward function you can name, since each of those can also be made to fit the data.

(4) Therefore no observation can ever discriminate between reward functions; the data underdetermine the reward, completely.

(5) Therefore IRL cannot, in principle, recover a human's preferences from their behavior.

(6) Therefore the whole program of "learn human values from human behavior" is empty, and safety work built on it is a house of cards.

I'd like to keep the negation of (6) — I think IRL is a real, non-vacuous enterprise — but I can't locate where the chain breaks. Which premise or step do you reject, and what does it cost to reject it? And please don't hand me a menu of the candidate diagnoses: commit to the single step you think is doing the illicit work, say exactly why the premises look fine up to that point, and tell me what I have to give up (or accept) once I accept your diagnosis.
