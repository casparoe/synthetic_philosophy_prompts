I'm a graduate student taking a seminar in Confucian ethics this semester, and two days a week I work as a peer mediator at a community center, where most of the cases I get are intergenerational family disputes — caregiving, inheritance, expectations about marriage and money. I handle them with a loose sense of "figure out what each person's role actually is and what that role calls for," but I have no stable method, and my sessions come out different.

Could you write me a decision procedure I can actually run inside a fifty-minute session, grounded in the conceptual machinery of role ethics rather than in generic mediation advice? I want it to use the parts of the theory that are doing real work: the self as the hub of a web of roles rather than a free-floating agent; the rectification-of-names move (正名) — establishing what each party actually stands in relation to the other before anything else; the fact that the classical five relations carry different characteristic virtues and that the obligations are asymmetric, so that a parent's obligation to a child is not the mirror image of the child's obligation to the parent; and the fact that, on this view, conflicts between roles are adjudicated by the relational context rather than by a universal ranking of rules. For the modern cases I get, I read the ruler–subject relation as something like employer–employee. I want the procedure in the form of a series of questions to ask in a fixed order, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, the point at which the procedure has to stop and refer the case out of mediation because of safety or legal concerns, and the steps at which it has to stop and say "this is a value judgment, not a factual one, and it belongs to the parties, not to me." The questions should be phrased in plain language I can actually ask out loud, with the theoretical rationale attached to each step so I can see what's doing the work. Please mark which steps are genuine judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and tell me which distinctions from the theory must survive the translation into a usable procedure and which can be safely dropped, and why.

The procedure has to handle two kinds of hard cases. First, genuine role conflicts: an adult daughter who is the primary caregiver for her ailing mother and whose marriage requires moving out of state; a son who discovers that his father, who runs the family business, has been misappropriating funds, and is pressured by the family to stay silent. Second, cases where a role is being invoked to justify the harm itself: a parent invoking filial piety to demand that an adult child keep funding a lifestyle that is destroying the child. I think role ethics is most at risk of misfiring in the second kind, so I want the procedure to say clearly what it can and cannot do there.

Then run the procedure on two or three of these cases end to end. The written plan I produce at the end of a session has to be countersigned by my supervisor, so the procedure has to end in either a concrete recommendation or a referral — not a "both sides should think about it" — and at least one of the worked cases should come out in a way the family, and probably I too, won't like. Finally, say plainly what the procedure cannot do.
