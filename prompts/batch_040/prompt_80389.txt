I'm working through a position I want to stress-test, and I'd rather have you attack it than confirm it.

Here is the position, stated as precisely as I can. I hold a law-like determinism version of historical materialism. The core claim is that the mode of production — the economic base — is the primary, law-like determinant of the superstructure (legal, political, and ideological institutions). I do not claim the base is the sole cause, and I allow for secondary, residual, and lagging factors. But I do claim two things: (1) the base is the primary cause in a strong, predictive sense, not merely the most important among a set of co-equal causes; and (2) the relation is law-like, so that two societies sharing the same mode of production must develop broadly compatible superstructures, and any observed divergence in the superstructure is ultimately traceable to a difference in the base, or to a residual base variable I have not yet identified.

Given that, tell me what would count against it. I want a map of its exposure, not a restatement of the standard economistic critique:

- Which specific observations, historical cases, or developments would count against the law-like compatibility prediction in particular — for instance, the same base yielding genuinely incompatible legal or political orders that no base difference, past or residual, can explain?
- What does my view predict that its rivals (idealist, cultural, institutional, or plain multiple-causation accounts) do not predict, such that a failure of those predictions would be a genuine, asymmetric cost to me?
- Which of the counterexamples or arguments would be decisive — they would make the view false or incoherent — versus merely awkward — they would only require me to add a residual variable or a time lag?
- Which of these would a committed defender of the view themselves concede as counting against it, and which would they dismiss as a misreading of the base/superstructure distinction?
- Has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability — i.e., is every divergence explainable by appeal to an unobserved residual base variable — and if so, is that a problem for a view of this kind?

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. I would personally find most persuasive a sustained case in which the same base produced genuinely incompatible superstructures with no traceable base difference, so weight that kind of case accordingly. And I'd value an honest assessment of how exposed the law-like compatibility clause actually is, since that is the part I added beyond the standard base-determines-superstructure claim and the part I most suspect is doing all the explanatory work.

On the last line, begin with "Verdict:" and give, in a short phrase of no more than about eight words, the single most decisive counterexample or argument against my view as you've assessed it.
