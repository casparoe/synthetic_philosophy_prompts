I'm a science journalist working on a longform piece about scientific realism, and I keep running into a stock version of the pessimistic meta-induction: since so many past theories once thought approximately true (caloric, phlogiston, the luminiferous ether) turned out to be false, we should expect our current best theories to meet the same fate, so we shouldn't believe even our most successful current theories are approximately true. Most of the popular accounts I've read stop there and then wave vaguely at "but realists have replies." One reply I keep hearing about in interviews with philosophers of science is John Worrall's structural realism — the move from claiming continuity of unobservable *entities* across theory change to claiming continuity of *mathematical/structural* content, with the textbook example being how the Fresnel equations survive, reinterpreted, inside Maxwell's electromagnetic theory of light.

What I can't quite get my head around is why this move isn't just a retreat that relocates the target of the induction rather than defusing it. Presumably historians of science can also dig up cases where the structure itself failed to carry over, not just the ontology built on top of it. So why should structural continuity have a better track record than continuity of entities or causal mechanisms? Could you explain, with some real texture rather than the usual thumbnail summary, how a structural realist is supposed to answer this "meta-level" pessimistic induction, and what actually grounds the claim that mathematical structure is more likely to be preserved across future theory change than the entities or mechanisms realists traditionally cared about?
