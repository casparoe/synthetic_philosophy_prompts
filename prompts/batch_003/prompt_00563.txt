I am writing a historical novel whose central figure is a fictionalized version of Henry Sidgwick: a rigorously skeptical utilitarian moral philosopher who nonetheless devoted decades to the Society for Psychical Research, from early table-turning sessions through the census of hallucinations to his sittings with the medium Leonora Piper. As I understand it, this was never mere eccentricity on his part but a considered attempt to find empirical evidence for personal survival after death, since he found it unsatisfying to rest the "dualism of practical reason" between rational self-interest and impartial duty on an unevidenced Kantian postulate of immortality alone. Please describe the most important implications of how that research actually proceeded and ultimately failed to settle his doubts, even by the time of his death, for how I might portray this character's private motives, his discipline through repeated disappointment, and the note on which his arc should close.
