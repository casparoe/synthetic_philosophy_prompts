Random Principia Ethica thing thats been bugging me for my ethics seminar. In the hedonism chapter, moore runs his isolation test (imagine a thing existing totally alone, stripped of everything else, and ask if it's still got value) on an entire uninhabited world, and decides a gorgeous world nobody's ever born to see is still better than an ugly one that's basically "one heap of filth" -- so beauty by itself, cut off from any mind, keeps some value. Then in the ideal chapter, where personal affection and aesthetic appreciation get crowned the greatest goods, he runs the exact same move on the parts of that combo: the beautiful object alone gets a small value, bare admiration alone gets little or none, and the huge gap between that and the combined whole's value gets treated as exactly what you'd expect if organic unities (a whole's value isn't the sum of its parts) is really true. Can you argue moore isn't entitled to use the isolation test like that -- that once organic unities holds, a part's isolated value can't tell you anything about what it contributes inside an actual whole, so all his "isolate it and see how little it's worth alone" moves are doing zero legitimate work? Push the inconsistency as far as it'll go, don't just hand-wave that the two principles could technically coexist.
