Hi all — I'm a retired experimental physicist (35 years in a university physics department before I hung up the lab coat), and now that I've got time on my hands I've been slowly working through the history of philosophy, which I never got around to before. Right now I'm going through Aristotle's "On Interpretation" in a creaky old 1853 translation by a guy named Octavius Owen that's free on Wikisource (the decent modern translations were all checked out of my library). I hit chapter 9, the famous "sea battle" argument about future contingents, and there's one specific move Aristotle makes that I can't get unstuck from, even after reading it about six times - its just not clicking for me.

Here's the bit that's got me tied in knots:

"...it is not necessary that every being should be, nor that non-being should not be, since it is not the same thing for every being to be from necessity, when it is, and simply to be from necessity, and in like manner as to non-being. [...] to be or not to be is necessary for every thing, also that it shall, or shall not be, yet it is not requisite to speak of each separately, but I say, for instance, that it is necessary for a naval action to occur or not occur to-morrow, yet it is not necessary that there should be a naval action to-morrow, nor that there should not be; it is necessary, however, that it should either be or not be."

I follow the big-picture worry: if every statement about tomorrow is already true or false today, the future looks locked in and there's no point deliberating about anything. What I don't follow is the specific logical trick being used to escape that conclusion. He wants "it is necessary that (there's a battle tomorrow OR there isn't)" to hold, while "it is necessary that there's a battle" and "it is necessary that there isn't" are both false. To my untrained eye that just looks like a scope distinction - necessity sitting outside the "or" versus necessity glued onto each side of it individually - the kind of thing you'd sort out with a truth table in twenty minutes. So here are the two questions I'm hoping to bring back to my library philosophy group with something smart to say: first, is that scope-distinction reading basically correct? And second, if it's really that simple, why has this one paragraph kept scholars busy from Boethius through the medieval logicians all the way down to Lukasiewicz's three-valued logic in the twentieth century, which seems like alot of ink spilled over a scope distinction. Is there some further step where Aristotle needs more than just separating the scopes - something that actually forces him to say future-tense statements aren't true or false yet? Or does the whole ancient puzzle basically dissolve once you're careful with your quantifiers, and everyone since has just been elaborating on an already-solved problem?
