I have no formal training in philosophy, mathematics, or computer science; everything I know about this comes from working through primary material on my own, starting with AI safety blogs and eventually the original papers. For the past several months I have been chewing on the "logical induction" framework MIRI published in 2016, and I have written up my current thinking below - closer to structured notes than a finished essay, but something I would like to eventually turn into one. I would appreciate your comments, particularly on the questions raised at the end.

Ordinary Bayesian epistemology is built for uncertainty about the world, but it quietly assumes logical omniscience: an ideal reasoner assigns probability 1 to every logical truth and updates instantly on every deductive consequence of what it already believes. That breaks down for any bounded reasoner, who can be perfectly certain of some premises and still have no idea what follows from them - whether some enormous number is prime, what a distant digit of pi is, whether an open conjecture is true. Logical induction tries to say, precisely and computably, what good reasoning under that second kind of uncertainty looks like.

The construction is a betting market. An idealized "deductive process" churns out proven theorems, one batch per day, forever. A market assigns every sentence of the language a price between 0 and 1, treated as a share that eventually pays $1 if the sentence is proved true and $0 if it is proved false. A population of "traders," each an efficiently (polynomial-time) computable strategy, can buy and sell these shares against the posted prices. The system counts as a logical inductor exactly when no trader can extract unboundedly large profit from it over time: the prices can never be predictably and cheaply beaten forever.

What struck me is that this is explicitly offered as a computationally honest descendant of the Dutch book argument for ordinary probabilism. Instead of requiring immunity to every conceivable sure-loss bet, which classical coherence arguments demand and which no reasoner facing genuine logical uncertainty could satisfy, it only requires immunity to the sure-loss schemes some bounded strategy could actually compute and run. A surprising amount falls out of that one requirement as a theorem rather than an assumption: prices converge, and converge to 1 on provable sentences and 0 on disprovable ones; they track the right frequencies in sequences that look statistically patterned; jointly exhaustive, mutually exclusive pairs of sentences get probabilities summing to 1 well before the underlying proof process actually settles them; and the market never locks in certainty on anything undecided.

Here is the worry. The classical Dutch book argument has a well-worn objection attached to it: showing an agent could be forced into a guaranteed loss in a contrived betting setup does not, on its own, show its beliefs are epistemically defective, unless we have already assumed that vulnerability to guaranteed loss is an epistemic vice rather than a merely practical one - in which case the argument is not deriving a norm of rationality so much as restating one. My first thought was that logical induction sidesteps this, since so much substantive epistemic behavior - calibration, coherence, sensitivity to statistical structure, resistance to premature certainty - is proved to follow from the bare no-exploitation requirement rather than fed in by hand. That looks like real justificatory work, not a restatement.

Then, in one of the online venues where people working on this material argue out the details, I came across a discussion of a result showing that the actual limiting belief state a logical inductor's prices approach does not itself satisfy the logical induction criterion: if you take that limit and hold it fixed as a standing market, it turns out to be exploitable, even though no stage of the process converging toward it was. If that is right, the justificatory story concerns only the never-ending sequence of approximations and has nothing to say about any single, coherent, "ideal" state of having gotten the logic right, because no such admissible state exists. There is no resting point the process approaches that would itself pass the very test invoked to justify getting there.

I go back and forth on whether that is a deep problem or a shallow one. Maybe "cannot be efficiently caught out at any finite stage, forever" is simply what justification amounts to here, and wanting a privileged endpoint is an old habit inherited from more traditional epistemology; work on inductive learning has long been comfortable with "identification in the limit" results that do not treat the limit itself as special. Or maybe the missing endpoint shows the whole apparatus is an elaborate consistency-maintenance device with no truth-tracking story underneath, which would undercut the very response I wanted to give to the classical objection above.

I would appreciate comments on three things: whether my summary of logical induction and its relationship to Dutch book arguments is accurate; whether the tension between the criterion's justificatory ambitions and the fact that its own limit fails to satisfy it is a real philosophical problem rather than a confusion on my part; and, if you think it is genuine, how you would sharpen it into a proper thesis for the essay.
