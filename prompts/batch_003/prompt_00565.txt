I'm a PhD student in philosophy currently drafting a dissertation chapter on Nietzsche's naturalism, and I keep getting stuck on the same stretch of Ecce Homo that came up in my reading group last week without our reaching any real agreement. The relevant part of "Why I Am So Clever" spends the better part of ten sections on things like the superiority of Piedmontese cooking to German or English cuisine, why coffee is "gloomy" but strong morning tea is fine, why small doses of alcohol left him "out of sorts" while larger ones made him almost jovial, and why dry climates like Provence supposedly support genius while "ill-starred" places like Naumburg and Leipzig only bred mediocrity in him. It's tempting to read all of this as a long, faintly absurd exercise in self-mythologizing — until section 10, where Nietzsche generalizes the point in a way that seems to me to be doing real philosophical work, though I can't decide of what kind.

Here is the passage, in Anthony Ludovici's 1911 translation, which I've been using precisely because it predates Kaufmann's smoother, more "respectable" rendering of Nietzsche's stranger locutions:

To this I reply that these trivial matters—diet, locality, climate, and one's mode of recreation, the whole casuistry of self-love—are inconceivably more important than all that which has hitherto been held in high esteem! It is precisely in this quarter that we must begin to learn afresh. All those things which mankind has valued with such earnestness heretofore are not even real; they are mere creations of fancy, or, more strictly speaking, lies born of the evil instincts of diseased and, in the deepest sense, noxious natures—all the concepts, "God," "soul," "virtue," "sin," "Beyond," "truth," "eternal life." ... But the greatness of human nature, its "divinity," was sought for in them....

My advisor's view is that this is simply rhetorical shock therapy aimed at German idealism and shouldn't be read as a serious explanatory claim about the relation between body and belief. That seems too quick to me, though, since the same basic move — explaining supposedly autonomous ideas by tracing them back to the physiological condition of the person who produced them — is exactly the diagnostic method Nietzsche uses throughout the Genealogy of Morals against concepts like guilt and bad conscience. But if this passage really is continuous with the genealogical method, I don't see how Nietzsche avoids an obvious reflexivity problem: his own claims about diet and climate are, by his own lights, equally products of "intestines," so why isn't this passage just one more symptom for the physiologist to diagnose rather than a piece of insight we're supposed to trust? I'd appreciate help thinking through whether the passage is best read as a literal physiological-reductionist thesis, as a genealogical unmasking move, or as something else entirely, and whether Nietzsche's texts anywhere give him real resources for blocking that reflexivity worry.
