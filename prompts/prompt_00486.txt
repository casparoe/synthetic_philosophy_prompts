I've been reading about philosophy of science on my own time, mostly through blog posts and old Stanford Encyclopedia entries, and I keep getting stuck on this debate between Humeans and anti-Humeans about laws of nature. As I understand it, the Humean view says that laws of nature are basically just compact summaries of the patterns we observe in the world (the "Humean mosaic"), rather than being some kind of hidden mechanism that forces events to happen the way they do. But if that's true, it seems like there's nothing actually stopping the pattern from suddenly breaking tomorrow, since the law is just a description after the fact, not a real necessity governing things.

This makes me think anti-Humeanism must be the more scientifically respectable position, since it says there are real necessitating relations between properties (or some kind of governing power) that actually make future events conform to past patterns, and that this is what justifies our confidence in things like predicting that the sun will rise tomorrow. Doesn't this mean Humeanism basically undermines the rational basis for scientific prediction, while anti-Humeanism gives us a solid metaphysical guarantee that the future will keep resembling the past? I feel like I must be missing something, because when I bring this up with friends who like Hume, they don't seem worried at all, but I can't figure out why the anti-Humean picture wouldn't just be strictly better on this point.
