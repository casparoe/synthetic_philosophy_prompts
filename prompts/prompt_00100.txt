I've been sitting in on a philosophy of probability seminar this semester, and last week we spent almost the whole session on the Doomsday Argument — the Carter-Leslie style reasoning that uses our own birth rank among all humans who will ever live to infer something about how many humans are likely to come after us. What struck me afterward, walking home with a friend from the stats department, is how much the argument leans on exactly the kind of probabilistic machinery that Pierre-Simon Laplace pioneered: indifference principles, inverse probability, and reasoning from an observed sample to an unknown total population (his own "Rule of Succession" is eerily close in spirit to the self-sampling assumption).

Given that Laplace never had access to anything like the Self-Sampling Assumption or Bostrom's later refinements, but did spend a great deal of intellectual energy on how to reason from limited samples to unknown finite or infinite totals (population statistics, celestial mechanics, the probability of causes), how do you think he would have reacted to the Doomsday Argument if it had been presented to him? Would his commitment to classical indifference and his frequentist leanings about "equipossible cases" have made him sympathetic to treating one's birth rank as a random draw from all humans who will ever exist, or would he have found some methodological flaw that anticipates the modern objections (e.g., reference class problems, or the tension between Bayesian updating and treating temporal position as evidence)? I'd love a reconstruction of his likely stance, grounded in what we know of his actual writings on probability and inference.
