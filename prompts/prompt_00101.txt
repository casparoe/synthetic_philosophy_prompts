I've been reading Thomas Reid's writings on common sense epistemology for a seminar on 18th-century philosophy, and something occurred to me during a side conversation with a classmate about modern epistemic game theory. Reid was deeply interested in the structure of testimony, trust, and how rational agents come to rely on each other's judgments—he thought we have a basic epistemic entitlement to trust others' faculties much as we trust our own, barring specific defeaters. This seems adjacent to, but importantly different from, the kind of common-knowledge-of-rationality assumptions that underpin epistemic game theory and coordination problems (think Aumann's agreement theorem, or backward induction arguments that rely on iterated levels of "I know that you know that I know...").

Given Reid's commitment to common sense as a foundational epistemic bedrock, and his skepticism toward purely formal or hyper-idealized models of the mind (he was, after all, reacting against Hume's associationism and what he saw as overly abstract theorizing about cognition), how do you think he would have reacted to the formal apparatus of common knowledge and iterated rationality assumptions in epistemic game theory? Would he have endorsed the idea that rational agents can attain genuine common knowledge of each other's rationality, or would he have found the whole framework to be exactly the kind of artificial, disembodied idealization he spent his career resisting? I'm especially curious whether his views on testimony and trust could be repurposed to ground something like common knowledge from below, so to speak, rather than assuming it as a formal precondition.
