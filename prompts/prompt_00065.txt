I've been TAing an undergraduate seminar on private language and rule-following, and last week's discussion spiraled into a weird tangent I can't stop thinking about. One student pointed out that Wittgenstein died in 1951, well before the rise of digital computing culture, cognitive science's "language of thought" hypothesis, and especially before large language models trained purely on text managed to produce fluent, contextually appropriate sentences without (arguably) any grounding in shared lived experience or a community of embodied rule-followers in the traditional sense. This seems like exactly the kind of case his private language argument was built to rule out as impossible or parasitic on ordinary language, yet here we are.

Given his actual arguments in the Philosophical Investigations about following a rule, the impossibility of a private language, and his emphasis on shared forms of life and public criteria for correct usage, how do you think Wittgenstein would have assessed something like a modern LLM's use of language? Would he treat it as a case of genuine rule-following that merely piggybacks on the human community whose text it was trained on, making it derivative rather than private, or would he find something more troubling in the fact that no single instance of "checking" against communal practice happens the way it does when a child is corrected by other speakers? I'm especially curious how he might have responded to the objection that the model exhibits consistent, correctable, self-correcting behavior across contexts, which sounds suspiciously like it satisfies his own criteria for meaningful language use, and whether he'd have seen this as vindicating or undermining the private language argument's scope.
