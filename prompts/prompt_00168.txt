I'm working on a feature about decision-making under radical uncertainty, and I've been reading through L.A. Paul's work on transformative experience as background. I've got the headline thought experiments down cold — the vampire case, the choice to become a parent — where the basic problem is that you can't know in advance what an experience will be like from the inside, which seems to break standard expected-utility reasoning that requires comparing the value of outcomes you can actually conceive of. Where I'm getting tangled up is in Paul's distinction between *epistemic* transformation (learning something that was simply inaccessible to you beforehand) and *personal* transformation (the experience actually changing your preferences, values, or sense of self). A philosopher I interviewed for the piece insisted that it's really the personal transformation piece, not just the epistemic surprise, that does the damage to rational choice theory — but he didn't have time to unpack why.

Could you walk me through how these two dimensions of transformative experience interact, and make the case for why personal transformation poses a distinct problem for rational agency beyond simply "you don't have enough information to calculate expected value"? I've also seen some commentary suggesting this connects to debates about authenticity versus rational agency — something about whether it even makes sense to ask what "you" should rationally choose, if the choice itself determines which "you" ends up existing to evaluate it. I want to represent that connection accurately in the article rather than flattening it into a simpler claim about uncertainty, so any help disentangling the layers here would be useful.
