I'm currently taking a graduate seminar on moral epistemology, and for the past two weeks we've been working through the cluelessness literature, starting with Lenman's original formulation and moving on to Greaves' more systematic treatment. I understand the basic worry well enough at a surface level: our actions ramify outward through a causally diffuse web of consequences that we have no realistic hope of tracking, so any consequentialist evaluation of an act seems to rest on radically incomplete information. What I'm struggling with is Greaves' distinction between "simple" cluelessness and "complex" (or "systematic") cluelessness. My professor emphasized that this distinction is load-bearing for the whole debate, since without it cluelessness just collapses into a fairly mundane form of empirical uncertainty that expected value reasoning could, in principle, absorb without much fuss.

Could you explain in some detail what actually separates complex cluelessness from ordinary uncertainty about outcomes, and why the presence of apparently "symmetrical" considerations on both sides of an act's downstream consequences isn't sufficient to neutralize the worry for someone who still wants to hold onto a broadly consequentialist standard of rightness? I keep losing the thread on why Greaves thinks the two cases are epistemically disanalogous rather than just differing in degree, so a step-by-step account of that argument, ideally with a concrete illustrative case, would help me a lot.
