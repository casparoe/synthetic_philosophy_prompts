I've been working through David Hume's writings on personal identity and causation for a seminar paper, and something occurred to me that I can't shake. Hume was deeply skeptical of our ordinary notion of necessary causal connection—he thought all we ever really observe is constant conjunction, never the "glue" that binds cause to effect—and yet he was also a compatibilist who thought liberty and necessity are perfectly reconcilable once we get clear on our terms. What I keep wondering is how Hume would have reacted to something like the Kalai-Smorodinsky bargaining solution and the broader tradition of formal decision theory it belongs to, especially the way it presupposes agents with well-ordered preferences who "could have chosen otherwise" within a defined feasible set. Would Hume have seen KS bargaining's axiomatic treatment of individual rationality (in particular the individual monotonicity axiom, which says a bargainer's payoff should weakly increase whenever their opportunities improve relative to the disagreement point) as just a formalization of the same compatibilist intuitions he already had about motives and voluntary action? Or would his broader empiricism have made him suspicious of the idealized, quasi-Platonic feasible sets and utility representations that this kind of bargaining theory requires, given his general hostility to reasoning about entities we can't ground in impressions? I'd love your best reconstruction of how Hume's epistemology and his compatibilist account of free will might have collided with, or perhaps anticipated, this style of axiomatic bargaining theory that only emerged two centuries after his death.
