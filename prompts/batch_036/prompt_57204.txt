I am trying to decide what would count as a successful formal account of *heuristic estimation* in mathematics — the reasoning that supports claims such as "there should be about x/(log x)² twin primes below x" without proving anything — and before committing to a framework I want the specification rather than the candidates.

Set-up, so that we are discussing the same object. Take a heuristic estimator to be a procedure that, given a claim about a fixed mathematical structure together with whatever background information the agent possesses, outputs a numerical estimate or probability along with a warrant for it, and is obliged to revise the estimate when further relevant information turns up. Its central engine is what has come to be called the presumption of independence: in the absence of specific information about the correlation between two quantities, default to E[XY] = E[X]E[Y]. That default is what makes the twin-prime estimate come out at all, and it is equally what misfires when the quantities are correlated by construction.

Write the desiderata for such a framework. For each requirement, give three things: the requirement itself; the concrete case that motivates it, in enough mathematical detail that I can check whether the case really has the force you claim; and a label saying whether it is non-negotiable, negotiable, or a preference of a particular camp — and if it is the latter, which camp is smuggling it in (analytic number theorists organizing their own practice, logicians wanting a semantics, people who want an estimator an automated system could rely on when it cannot check the answer). I am as interested in the demands that look reasonable but are not genuine requirements as in the ones that are.

Make sure the list is tested against the following sorts of cases, adding others you think sharper:

- the move from the naive x/(log x)² to the Hardy–Littlewood constant times that quantity, i.e. what the framework must do with local obstructions and the singular series, and what it takes for a correction factor to be principled rather than fitted;
- Maier's result on irregularities in the distribution of primes in short intervals, which contradicts what Cramér's random model predicts, and Granville's proposed replacement constant 2e⁻ᵞ — what has to be true of an estimate for a theorem to *refute* it, as opposed to merely informing it;
- the Cohen–Lenstra prediction for class groups of imaginary quadratic fields, where the weighting by 1/|Aut(A)| shows the natural measure is not the naive uniform one;
- random-matrix predictions for the moments of the zeta function on the critical line, whose warrant is structural analogy rather than any independence assumption — must a framework cover these, or is it entitled to exclude them, and what does it lose either way;
- the Erdős–Kac theorem, as a case where a heuristic became a theorem, and the question of what the framework should say about the relation between the two;
- multiplying local densities when a global obstruction of the Brauer–Manin type is present, so that a variety has points everywhere locally and none globally;
- sparse sequences such as n!+1, where treating the object as a random integer of its size ignores that it is by construction coprime to every prime up to n — a case whose whole difficulty is deciding which background facts count as specific information about correlation;
- claims like the expected finiteness of Fermat primes, where the predicted counts are so small that no amount of computation can corroborate or falsify them.

Then the part I most want: identify any subset of your requirements that you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, and argue it, rather than reporting that there are tensions. One genuine impossibility in the list is worth more to me than ten requirements that any sufficiently vague framework could meet. Be explicit about whether coherence conditions of the usual kind — no endorsement of contradictions, additivity, invariance under logically equivalent reformulation of the same claim — can be had together with an estimator that does not already have to settle the questions it is estimating, and about what the probabilities in such a framework are probabilities of, given that the integers are a fixed structure with no chance in them.

Finally, name the single requirement that does the most to exclude candidate frameworks, and close with a short verdict — two or three sentences each, no survey — on three families: concrete random models of the integers in the Cramér tradition; formal coherence-based approaches that build consistency and additivity into the estimator by construction; and the working mathematician's practice of singular series plus local corrections with no semantics attached at all.

State wherever the list depends on the purpose I have fixed, and what would drop out if the framework only had to organize expert practice rather than be trusted by an agent unable to verify the result. I do not want references, a bibliography, or a reading list; I want the reasoning.
