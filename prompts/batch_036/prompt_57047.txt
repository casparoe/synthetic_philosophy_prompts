I'm a third-year metaphysics PhD student and I need a proposal for a one-year writing fellowship my department's research centre hands out — they fund two people, deadline in three weeks, and the committee is deliberately mixed: one logician, one philosopher of biology, two of us, so the thing has to be readable by people who know what grounding is but have never had a reason to think about dependent choice. I did a maths minor as an undergrad and took a set theory course, but that was four years ago and I don't entirely trust myself any more, which is part of why I'm asking rather than just writing it. The topic is the well-foundedness premise in the argument from grounding to a fundamental level. The standard move: grounding can't descend forever, so there has to be an ungrounded base, and everything else is what it is in virtue of that base. The objection, roughly: the descent could go on forever and nothing would be lost, no explanatory failure, so the premise is just a prejudice about turtles. What I want the proposal to do is argue that both sides are much sloppier than they should be about *which* condition is being asserted and denied, because there are at least four candidates and they come apart.

Here's the mess in my head — please sort it out into a project rather than just nodding along. (1) "Grounding is well-founded" could mean: no loops; no infinite descending chains of grounds; every nonempty bunch of things has at least one member with no grounds; or, strongest, everything sits at some finite distance from an ungrounded base. As far as I can tell the third only follows from the second if you've got a choice principle hanging around, and the fourth doesn't follow from the third at all. I think there's a perfectly well-founded structure with a genuine base where one thing is fully grounded by infinitely many things, each of them finitely deep, with no uniform bound on the depth — so there's a floor, no infinite descent, and still something that isn't finitely derivable from the floor. If that's right then the slogan that does all the rhetorical work, something like *the base is what makes everything else intelligible*, needs the fourth, while the argument for a base only delivers the second or third. (2) Grounds are usually plural — what grounds a fact is a whole bunch of facts, not one — so the descent is a tree and not a line, and I'm not convinced the chain formulation is even the right shape for the premise. Something König-ish feels like its lurking here about when an infinite tree is forced to have an infinite branch, and about what an infinite regress even is if the tree is infinite but branchless. (3) My worry, which a friend in logic pushed hard on: maybe all of this is a set theorist's red herring with no metaphysical upshot, and the honest answer is that everyone's premise was the second reading all along and the disagreement is untouched.

So write me the proposal, call it 1300–1500 words. I need one question, stated sharply enough that a reader could tell whether it's been answered; an account of why the current standoff doesn't already settle it; a defence of the conceptual route against the obvious alternative of just going looking for real examples of infinite or branchless grounding structures and seeing which intuitions they trigger; a month by month plan for the twelve months; what a successful result looks like as a deliverable; and — the committee always asks — what would count as the project failing, including which of hunches (1), (2) and (3) you'd build around and which you'd cut, and why. Be blunt about scope too: tell me whether this is a year of work or six weeks plus a shrug, and if it's the latter, say what I'd have to add to make it a year. No bibliography and no citations, please don't invent any, I've got the literature and I'll slot it in myself.
