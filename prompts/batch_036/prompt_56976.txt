Last week I spent four hours arguing with a friend who teaches political philosophy, over the remains of a takeout meal at his kitchen table while I was in town for a facility audit, and I came away convinced that we had never once been speaking the same language. He had just assigned his students Nozick's discussion of animals in *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* — the hybrid proposal he labels utilitarianism for animals, Kantianism for people, on which animals' interests may be aggregated and traded off, so that some animals can be used or sacrificed for the greater good of animals and persons, while persons are protected by side constraints that rule out using them in that way at all. As I understand him, Nozick ties the stronger protection to a capacity animals purportedly lack: the ability to formulate long-term plans, to decide on the basis of considerations one frames for oneself, and thereby to shape one's life in accordance with some overall conception of the kind of life one wants to have; and he is openly unsure about the basis of rights, which my friend says is why the whole scheme should be read as provisional rather than as settled doctrine. I kept answering him with measurement and he kept answering me with status, and neither of us budged.

So I would like you to perform the translation rigorously, into the vocabulary I actually work in. I run welfare-assessment programmes for a livestock producer and sit on a research ethics committee; my native terms are animal-based versus resource-based indicators, the five domains of welfare (nutrition, environment, health, behaviour, and the affective state these give rise to), preference and motivation tests in which I measure how hard a hen or a sow will work for a resource and try to read something like a demand curve off the result, coping ability and the difference between acute and chronic stressors, positive welfare as something over and above the absence of suffering, harm-benefit analysis as the procedure by which a protocol is approved or refused, and the replacement-reduction-refinement requirements that bind what may be done even when the aggregate numbers look favourable. Take Nozick's position apart and restate it piece by piece in those terms — the aggregative treatment of animals, the side constraints on persons, the plan-shaping capacity supposed to ground the asymmetry, and his avowed uncertainty about what grounds rights — as faithfully as you can manage. Then give me the residue report: which pieces land cleanly (I suspect harm-benefit analysis is very nearly what he means by treating animals on utilitarian lines), which land only after being bent out of shape, and which have no expression in my framework whatsoever. Be scrupulous about not smuggling my framework's assumptions in and labelling the result a translation: if the honest verdict for some piece is that there is no counterpart, say so rather than manufacturing one out of refinement rules or committee procedure. And where I have misdescribed Nozick above, correct the description instead of faithfully translating my error.

What I really want settled is what the residue means. My friend's view is that welfare science is conceptually impoverished — that it can only ever register how well an animal is doing and so cannot represent the thought that a being may not be used however well the use pays. My suspicion is the reverse: that the pieces refusing to translate are not deep facts my discipline lacks machinery for, but claims that were never determinate in the first place, since the plan-shaping capacity meant to draw the line presents itself in my data as a continuum of behavioural flexibility, choice competence, and environmental control across species rather than as a threshold, and since the institutional practice that actually implements his utilitarianism for animals — harm-benefit analysis — never consults any such threshold. Tell me which of these readings the residue supports, or whether it supports neither, and what would have to be true of my discipline for the other reading to be right. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and give the reasoning after it, with no preamble and no restatement of my question; then close by telling me how confident you are in the main claims you have made and singling out the one point you are least sure of.
