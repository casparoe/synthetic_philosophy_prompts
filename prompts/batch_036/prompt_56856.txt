I'm working through some inverse reinforcement learning stuff and I've got two underdetermination problems tangled together and I can't pull them apart. Can you help me see the difference?

First one I sorta get. Everyone says IRL is ill-posed because lots of different reward functions give you the same optimal policy — that's the potential-based shaping result, and more generally you can never pin the reward down beyond whatever equivalence class leaves the argmax unchanged. Fine, I accept that's a real limit, but notice it's a limit *internal* to the reward function. You're choosing among rewards.

The second one is what's bugging me and I think it's a genuinely different animal. Suppose the demonstrator's beliefs about the world are just wrong: they don't know a shortcut exists, they think some route is dangerous when it isn't, they have a bad model of how their own actions turn into outcomes. Or, slightly different flavor, they're up against a constraint the learner doesn't know about — a locked gate, an unwritten social rule, a physical cost that isn't in the learner's transistion model. In every one of those cases I can reproduce the same trajectories by putting the deviation in the reward instead. So here the ambiguity isn't inside the reward, it's *between* the reward, the dynamics, and the constraints. I want a clear account of how these two forms of underdetermination differ, and specifically whether they fail in the same way or not. Related: people keep saying more data fixes things, but my gut says neither of these is a finite-sample or variance problem, they're identification problems, so more data of the same kind is useless. Am I right about that? And is there any condition where data *does* break the tie — interventions rather than observation, demos collected across several environments, demonstrators who are noisy or boundedly rational instead of near-optimal, or just asking the human questions instead of watching them?

Then the part I actually care about, which is why I'm asking. Suppose you train on real human behavior rather than gridworlds — actual people living under actual constraints. A bunch of those people have adapted to their circumstances in exactly the way the adaptive preference literature describes: Elster's sour grapes, and Sen's point that a chronically deprived person may report being fine precisely because they've stopped wanting things they used to want. Their behavior looks optimal given a shrunken reward. And a well-built IRL learner will faithfully recieve that shrunken reward, because the shrunken reward genuinely is what best explains the trajectories. So the method isn't malfunctioning here, it's succeeding at something we didn't want it to succeed at. Explain what's going on conceptually. Is what comes back still a preference, or is it a preference-and-circumstance composite with no visible seam? And do the usual moves actually touch this, or just relocate the seam somewhere harder to see — maxent IRL with its entropy term, Bayesian IRL that hands you a posterior over rewards instead of a point estimate, hierarchical or option-based IRL that recovers rewards over subgoals, explicit modeling of the demonstrator's bounded rationality, or trying to infer the reward the person *would* have under better conditions? I'd genuinely rather get an honest no-and-here's-why than a tour of techniques that all sort of help.
