I am a novelist, and next month I give a forty-minute festival talk, without slides, to an audience of general readers on Dennett's metaphor of the self as a centre of narrative gravity. I do not want the talk written for me; I want it planned. Give me the order in which the components must arrive and what each depends upon — I take it that I cannot open by telling people the self is an abstraction until I have given them something to abstract from, and I am unsure how much of the gravitational analogy they need before it begins to do any work. Then choose the single case that should carry the whole talk: I had in mind my own narrator, who has left two irreconcilable written accounts of the same childhood summer, but tell me if that is the wrong instrument, and what the example must contain and must conspicuously lack. Map the misreadings I should expect — that this reduces the self to mere fiction, that it licenses inventing whatever past one prefers, that it is the old soul under a new name — and mark the exact point in the sequence at which each should be defused. Name one thing I ought to refuse to explain at this level, and close with a single question I can put to the room that will reveal whether the idea has landed or merely charmed them.
