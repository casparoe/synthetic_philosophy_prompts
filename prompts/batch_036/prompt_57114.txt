I do security-review work for a semi-governmental metrology outfit and lately we keep getting asked to bless these "device-independent" randomness beacons and QKD boxes. Roughly how they work: you buy a sealed device from a vendor, each round you pick a measurement setting with your own random number generator, the device produces correlated outputs on two sides, you log everything. Then you check whether the observed statistics violate a Bell inequality by a big enough margin, and if they do the claim is that the outputs are unpredictable to anyone — including the vendor who built the box, including whoever might have tampered with it — without you having to trust anything about the physics inside. No trust in the hardware, that's the whole selling point. Except the proofs always lean on one more thing, that the settings you chose aren't correlated with whatever the source emitted, which the papers call free choice or measurement independence and then move on from. And separately, every one of these experiments runs a statistical check that the no-signalling conditions hold, and the writeups note approvingly that nothing in the data lets you send a message faster than light, even when the headline is that the results confirm quantum nonlocality.

So what is this practice actually assuming, philosophically? A few things I cant get straight:

- When the protocol says trust nothing, what's the real scope of that distrust? It treats the box as a black box whose internals are in principle unknowable, but treats my lab's own RNG and the wiring from it to the box as fully knowable and guaranteed uncorrelated with the source. Is that a principled line about what can be certified from data alone, or an artifact of who's writing the spec?
- Is the free-choice assumption a claim about human agency or free will, or is it purely a causal-independence claim that could be perfectly true in a fully deterministic universe? I want to know which one the practice actually needs, not which one it sounds like when people talk about it.
- Which positions does it rule out without ever saying so — I gather people who think the assumption is a cop-out, and people who think causes can run backwards, both have complaints about it. What does the practice strictly exclude versus merely fail to endorse?
- Is it internally consistent to advertise the output as true randomness while also saying the thing is safe precisely because the nonlocality can't be used to signal? That feels like two different notions of randomness and two different notions of locality getting traded on in the same document.

Reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can, and seperate out what the practice strictly presupposes from what just happens to fit comfortably with it — my suspicion is that more than one account of causation would license the exact same protocol, and I'd like to know where it's underdetermined. Then tell me what would concretely have to change in how these systems are specified, tested, or sold if the free-choice assumption stopped being taken for granted.
