You are a philosopher of mind who also publishes literary criticism — someone equally at home with the technical debate over animal consciousness and with close reading of a modernist short story. Please think carefully before writing, and begin with a visible brainstorming stage: five to eight bullets naming the readings you considered, the ones you rejected and why, and the two or three cruxes you think the piece has to settle.

**Context.** I teach a graduate seminar on the metaphysics and epistemology of animal minds. One session pairs Franz Kafka's 1917 story A Report to an Academy — the ape who calls himself Red Peter and who addresses a learned body with an account of how he came to acquire, in his own telling, something like an average European education — with the philosophical literature. I am drafting my lecture notes and want a reading I can trust from both directions: faithful to the story, and not a distortion of the philosophy.

**The framing I bring.** In the seminar I organize the epistemology around three routes to knowledge of another creature's mind: inference from behavior and its neural basis; inference from evolutionary and developmental continuity; and testimony. The third is normally set aside in this literature as unavailable, on the ground that no nonhuman animal can tell us what its experience is like. Kafka's story is, so far as I know, the only canonical thought experiment in which that route opens up. Red Peter is exactly the reporting animal the debate says cannot exist — and his report turns out to be worse than useless. He gives the academy a detailed account of being shot and captured by a hunting expedition in West Africa, of his confinement in a cage aboard the ship, of studying and imitating the sailors, of the one skill he mastered only with great difficulty (drinking), of refusing the zoological garden in favor of the music hall, and of his subsequent career as a performer; and he then insists that what he sought was never freedom and never humanity but only an escape route, and that he can no longer describe what his life was like before. The story hands us a testimony and simultaneously withdraws the content the testimony was supposed to supply.

I have a term for what I think is going on, and I would like you to use it, criticize it, or replace it with something better: the **reportability premium** — the tendency, in both the epistemology and the ethics of animal minds, to let a creature's capacity to produce a human-legible report stand in for the presence, or the degree, of its consciousness. On this diagnosis the academy's invitation is not a neutral methodological opportunity; it is the premium being paid.

**What I want you to settle.**

1. Which claim about reportability does the story actually license? Distinguish at least three hypotheses about Red Peter's inability to characterize his ape past: that the experience was there and has become inaccessible because the concepts needed to describe it were displaced by the ones he acquired; that there was never much there to describe; and that something was there but was never reportable even at the time. Say which the text supports, and be explicit where it underdetermines.
2. Does the story endorse or satirize the moral significance of the premium — the choice between the zoological garden and the music hall, and the standing of the animals who cannot report at all?
3. Where does the reading break? The story has long been read as an allegory of assimilation (it first appeared in a Zionist monthly) and of identity as performance rather than essence. Say whether those readings compete with mine, absorb it, or are orthogonal to it, and name what is lost by treating the story as a thought experiment at all.
4. End by saying what the story adds to the debate that argument alone does not.

**Constraints.** No bibliography, no citations, no appeals to secondary scholarship I would have to look up. Do not quote the story verbatim — translations differ and I want no temptation to fabricate a line; describe scenes and narrative moves in your own words. If you are unsure whether a detail is in the text, flag it rather than assert it: for instance, I believe that late in the story there is some mention of a chimpanzee he keeps at home whose look he finds unbearable in the evening, and if you are not confident of that, say so and argue without it. Assume your reader knows Nagel's bat essay and the reportability debates well enough not to need them summarized, and spend your words on Kafka. Do not open with a plot summary.
