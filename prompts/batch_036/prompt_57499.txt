I'm on a dissertation committee and I've been asked to draft the standard we'll apply to the student's central claim: that the human chin is an adaptation. Her chapter offers four things — (1) no other primate, and no other hominin, has a projecting bony chin; (2) a finite element model in which chin shape lowers strain during chewing; (3) a cross-cultural rating study where more prominent chins scored higher on attractiveness; (4) the observation that the chin only shows up once the face and the tooth rows start shrinking. Two members say none of that can ever count, because a one-off trait with no living homolog doesn't permit the comparitive or experimental work an adaptation claim requires; a third says she's supplied exactly the selection analysis that morphology papers usually dodge.

Before we argue about her, write the spec: what a demonstration that a unique, deep-time morphological feature is an adaptation actually has to deliver, with the case or consideration motivating each requirement, and with each marked as non-negotiable or as the preference of a particular camp. Most of all: is any subset of these requirements jointly unsatisfiable for a trait like this one, and if so which one has to go and what do you lose by dropping it? Then tell me which single requirement disposes of the most of her four items, and what the byproduct alternative owes us — an outright refutation, or just less plausibility than her story. Skip the overall verdict on the chapter.
