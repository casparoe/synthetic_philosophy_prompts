I am a junior at my high school and I sit on our Environmental Committee, which must recommend one of four native-tree proposals for replanting the perimeter of our athletic fields. I would like a rigorous evaluation of the four options, because our usual decision procedure has produced an embarrassing result and I am unsure how to report it to our faculty adviser.

The committee rated each proposal from 0 to 10 on five criteria: canopy shade after twenty years, ten-year drought survival, affordability, low maintenance, and value to local wildlife. (The underlying measurements are in different units — square metres, percentages, dollars, hours per year, and a wildlife index — which is why the chair had us convert everything to ratings in the first place.) The ratings we agreed on are:

| Proposal | Shade | Survival | Affordability | Maintenance | Wildlife |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 9 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 3 |
| B | 5 | 8 | 4 | 6 | 9 |
| C | 3 | 6 | 9 | 4 | 7 |
| D | 7 | 7 | 5 | 5 | 5 |

Our chair's rule is that X beats Y whenever X carries the higher rating on at least three of the five criteria. Applying it, A beats B, B beats D, D beats C, and C beats A. The rule therefore cycles and selects nothing. Worse, I do not think the cycle is merely the rule's fault: when I set the checklist aside and simply ask myself which outcome I would rather have, I still prefer A to B, B to D, and D to C — and, because C is the only proposal the principal says we can genuinely afford, I prefer C to A.

A second difficulty unsettles me. We must also fix how many trees to plant. If we planted thirty of proposal A instead of twenty-eight, I could not honestly call one plan better than the other; I would call them equally good, and I would say the same about any two quantities within a couple of trees of each other. Yet thirty trees is plainly better than four, and four plainly better than none. So "equally good" seems to fail transitivity along that series, which makes me distrust the confident pairwise judgments above as well.

In light of all of this, please:

1. Sort A, B, C, and D into tiers by all-things-considered desirability, placing options in the same tier if you judge them genuinely tied, with one or two sentences on why each lands where it does.
2. Assign each of the four a score from 0 to 100 for the strength of the case in its favour, and say explicitly what you treated as commensurable and what you refused to trade off.
3. Say whether "better than" should be regarded as intransitive in a situation of this kind, or whether the appearance of intransitivity is better explained some other way — and state what follows for how our committee ought to proceed: keep the chair's rule, total the ratings, adopt some other procedure, or decline to produce an ordering at all.

Please finish with a single line in exactly this form:

Verdict: <the four letters arranged using ">" for strict preference and "=" for a tie> | <TRANSITIVE, INTRANSITIVE, or INDETERMINATE>
