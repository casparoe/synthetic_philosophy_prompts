I am a third-year philosophy student, and alongside my coursework this term — a seminar on the foundations of logic in which we spent two sessions on Carroll's 1895 tortoise dialogue — I work about fifteen hours a week at my university's reasoning and study centre, where undergraduates from other disciplines book one-on-one help checking the arguments in their own writing. Since last term the centre has also been piloting a tutoring chatbot that students can message at any hour, and I sit on the student committee that reads its transcripts and drafts its reply policy for the director's approval. Both roles produce the same difficulty roughly four or five times a week. A student who has been shown that a conclusion follows from their own sentences asks why it follows; the tutor writes down the rule, or the bot emits it; the student readily accepts the rule as one more statement to be believed, then asks why that statement licenses the move; the tutor adds the conditional; and the list grows. Last Thursday I went eleven rounds with a pre-law student over a single modus tollens application before our forty-five minutes were up, and I had the distinct and unwelcome sense that I had done precisely what Achilles does and obtained precisely what Achilles obtains. The stakes are not trivial in either direction: handled badly, the student leaves either unable to check any argument they did not construct themselves, or persuaded that reasoning bottoms out in arbitrary fiat, and in the transcript reviews the justification loop is our second most frequent complaint about the bot.

What I need is a triage procedure — something that fits on one laminated card, that a tutor with no more training than I have (one semester of sentential logic, no metalogic) can run in well under a minute while sitting across from someone, and that the chatbot committee can convert into reply logic without the director having to consult a philosopher. It should open by sorting the request itself: what questions do I ask, in what order, to establish whether the student is asking for a reason to believe a premise, for a reason to accept a rule, for a demonstration that the rule was correctly applied in this instance, or for something that is not a logical question at all — an objection to the authority of the rule, a suspicion that a term does not mean what they assumed, a request for reassurance that has put on logical dress? For each category the card should state the correct next move, what counts as a sufficient response at that node, and, crucially, what the tutor should actually say aloud when the correct move is to stop supplying further justifications, since the whole thing fails in practice if stopping sounds like "because I said so." I want an explicit stopping rule and an explicit account of when a case is handed to a human being rather than resolved in the session: the procedure should mark, plainly, the points at which what is in front of me is a genuine philosophical question about the justification of deduction that no amount of tutoring can close, and the points at which it is a competence gap that has learned to phrase itself philosophically. Please do not give me a reading list or citations — I cannot use them mid-session and the bot certainly cannot; I want the operational content of the regress debate instead, together with your judgment about which of its distinctions must survive compression onto a card and which I am entitled to drop.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First: a first-year engineering student revising a lab report who, every time I show that a sentence follows from two of her own earlier sentences, asks why it follows, and who grants each conditional I write down without ever putting one to work — I take this to be the tortoise in the wild, and I want to know what the card tells me to do at round two rather than at round eleven. Second: a student disputing a conclusion about his essay by objecting to the handbook rule the argument leans on — two late days forfeit the grade — and restating that objection in increasingly logical vocabulary, so that the exchange looks like a regress but may not be one; I want the card to tell me how to detect that and where to route it. Third: a philosophy major in my own seminar who is pressing sincerely and competently on whether deduction can be justified at all, and who wants engagement rather than redirection — this is the case where I expect your procedure to tell me to stop and refer her elsewhere, and if that is what it says I want you to say so plainly and then tell me whether the procedure is right to, or whether it has simply been built to protect the tutor's timetable and the bot's session length. Close by stating what the procedure cannot do: where it will reliably misfire, which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as decision rules, and whether there exists any way of bringing such an exchange to an end that is not merely a refusal to continue it.
