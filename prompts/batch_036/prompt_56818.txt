I am preparing a qualifying-exam answer in value theory on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism — the objection that pleasures share no common nature, so that the claim that pleasure is the only intrinsic good is really a claim about a list rather than about a kind. My adviser defends a desire-satisfaction theory, is convinced the objection is fatal, and I would like to spend forty minutes being pressed by someone who disagrees. Please play a hedonist who takes the objection seriously rather than waving it away: you deny that pleasure is one simple felt quality, and you hold instead that "pleasure" names a single determinable with wildly heterogeneous determinates, unified by a second-order property that you insist is internal to the experience rather than a matter of anyone's desires. You have real resources and should use all of them — the analogy with colour, which unifies scarlet and olive without there being anything it is like to undergo colour as such; the claim that the second-order property is the experience's own welcoming character, not a separate relational state hovering over it; the distinction between what a pleasure feels like and what it does to the person having it. You may concede small points freely, but you may never concede that the class of pleasures is a bare disjunction whose unity is stipulated, and you may not dissolve your view into a preference theory by another name. Stay in character with no meta-commentary about the state of the literature until I say "stop." Keep each reply to roughly 180 words of prose, and if something about my cases needs clarifying, ask in a single sentence and then proceed with your best guess anyway, so that every turn is substantive. End each reply with one sentence beginning "Concession risk:" naming the smallest thing you would give up if my point lands.

Here is my opening move. Consider the pleasure of very cold water at the back of the throat after a long run; the pleasure of suddenly seeing that step four of a proof licenses step seven; the pleasure of a bass note arriving exactly where you had been waiting for it; the pleasure of recalling, years later, a cruel remark someone made about you and realising they were wrong; and the pleasure of a sneeze that finally comes after three failed attempts. Either these share something it is like to undergo, in which case tell me what that something is that is not better described as the common structure of something one had been straining toward arriving — or they share nothing at all, in which case your determinable is a list, and "only pleasure is intrinsically good" reduces to the claim that these items and nothing else are good, which is not a theory about a kind of experience but a catalogue with a philosophical title on it.

Two further cases are aimed at your second-order property in particular. First: the malice I feel when a rival's project collapses. I enjoy it while it lasts, I do not mistake the enjoyment for anything else, and yet the moment I notice it I want it gone; on my own report this is a genuine pleasure that I do not want to continue. Second: the ache of grief eight months after my mother died, which I actively resist losing because it has become the way she is present to me, and which I want to continue for its own sake even though I would not dream of calling it pleasant. If your unifying property is being-wanted-to-continue-for-its-own-sake, the first case falls out of the good and the second gets let in, which is the wrong result in both directions at once. If your unifying property is something else, tell me what it is, and tell me why it is not that same formula wearing a phenomenological disguise.
