I have been assembling my own verdicts about which betting dispositions make an agent irrational, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to. The cases are my own constructions and the numbers are load-bearing, so please work through the arithmetic rather than treating each case as an illustration of a familiar moral. If any construction of mine does not in fact have the feature I attribute to it, correct me before using it.

1. **Die.** My credence that a die lands 1–4 is 0.6, and my credence that it lands 5–6 is also 0.6. Offered separately are a ticket costing $0.55 that pays $1.00 on 1–4, and a ticket costing $0.55 that pays $1.00 on 5–6. Each is worth $0.05 in expectation by my own lights, so each acceptance is fine. Bought together they cost $1.10 and return $1.00 in every possibility. Verdict: the pair is a genuine defect, but neither acceptance was. So the norm cannot attach to individual acts of acceptance — which raises immediately the question of what collection of dispositions it does attach to, and what makes a collection the relevant one.

2. **Hundred tickets.** A second agent, whose credences are internally impeccable, pays $10 each for a hundred independent tickets, each paying $1,000 on an event to which she assigns probability 0.005. Every ticket is worth minus $5 in expectation by her own lights. Roughly sixty-one times in a hundred she wins nothing and is down $1,000; roughly thirty times she wins exactly once and breaks even; roughly nine times she wins twice or more and profits. There is no possibility in which she is guaranteed to lose, so the usual sure-loss test finds nothing to indict. Verdict: she is considerably more irrational than the agent in case 1, and her defect is of the same kind, not a different kind.

3. **No good options.** A third agent must take one of three packages. The first guarantees a $5 loss, the second a $50 loss, the third a $500 loss. She takes the first, and is accordingly sure to lose. Verdict: no indictment of her reasoning; the book measures her circumstances. So guaranteed loss is not sufficient for the defect I am trying to name, and case 2 suggests it is not necessary either.

4. **Information.** A bookie will show me, free of charge, which of three envelopes holds the prize before I bet on it. I decline and bet at my prior. Verdict: a defect, though nothing is lost — there is no book against me, only a forgone gain. Now a variant: I pay $5 for the look and then bet as though I had never received it. Verdict: a worse defect, and yet the $5 I am guaranteed to have wasted is measured against a baseline of my own making, not against where I started.

5. **Priced swaps.** An agent prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A. A trader offers swaps at $0.01 each; she cycles once and is down $0.03. Verdict: irrational, and exploitability is what shows it. Now suppose each swap costs $0.05 while the differences she sees between the options are only a few cents. She declines every swap and loses nothing. Verdict: I want to say she is now unexploitable and therefore fine — but I cannot accept that a fee schedule makes her preferences coherent. The same attitudes are condemned in one market and excused in another, and that is the pair I most want you to press on.

6. **Deferring to my future self.** I am confident that after tonight's debate I will be far more sure of X than I am now. A bookie offers to bet with me now at odds matching my predicted future credence. I refuse. Verdict: refusing is fine; I decline to be bound by opinions I do not yet hold. But I recognize that a bookie who buys from my future self and sells to me can thereby lock in a loss, so I am knowingly accepting pumpability — which sits badly beside case 5.

7. **The bookie knows more than I do.** A book that spans two times requires the bookie to know in advance what evidence I will receive and to have contracts waiting. Suppose he knows something about my future evidence that I do not. Verdict: such a book does not indict me; the loss measures his information advantage. I suspect this verdict is too generous and that any restriction I state will either collapse into "every book that catches me out is unfair" or exclude ordinary two-time books.

My tentative principle: *a pattern of betting dispositions is irrational if and only if it leaves the agent strictly worse off, in every possibility she assigns nonzero credence, than some alternative pattern that was available to her and whose availability she could have foreseen at the time of planning.* Clause one is meant to handle 1, 3, 4, 5 and 6; the foreseeability clause is meant to handle 7. I already know that case 2 breaks clause one, and I suspect case 4 breaks the notion of a baseline that clause one quietly assumes.

Here is a case on which I have no verdict, and I want one derived rather than guessed. At noon my credence that the meeting is in Room A is 0.5. I sign a standing agreement with a bookie: at noon I will buy or sell any bet he offers on the room at a price equal to my then-current credence times the stake, and at 12:30, once I have been told which room it is, I will again buy or sell at a price equal to my then-current credence times the stake. I also plan that, on being told the room, I will move to 0.9 in the room announced and 0.1 in the other, because I want a margin for the possibility that the message is wrong. My instincts are that the 0.9/0.1 policy is a virtue rather than a vice, and that the real mistake was signing away my ability to decline a bet. Can he arrange stakes at noon and at 12:30 — knowing my plan but not the outcome — that cost me money in both rooms? If so, give the stakes; if not, say why not. Then tell me which of my two instincts your answer disappoints.

What I want back:

- One principle, or a small number of candidates, precise enough that I could apply it mechanically to a new betting situation, with each clause paired to the case it exists to save.
- An explicit answer on what the object of assessment is — credences, preferences, policies for accepting bets, or whole packages — since cases 1 and 5 turn on this, and an account of how collections of bets are individuated.
- A verdict on the standing-agreement case, plus one further case of your own devising that you regard as genuinely diagnostic, with an explanation of why you chose it.
- An honest list of which of my verdicts in 1–7 your recommended principle cannot accommodate, ranked by how much it costs me to give them up.
- If a more complex principle saves every verdict, state it and price the complexity; if you recommend the simpler one, say which verdict it kills and why that verdict was wrong.

No bibliography and no reading list. You may use standard names for results if you are confident of them, but I care about the reasoning: I should be able to check every case you give me with arithmetic alone.
