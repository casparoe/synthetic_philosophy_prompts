I'm auditing a grad seminar on moral responsibility this semester and we've just come out of the standing-to-blame stretch -- Wallace's paper on hypocrisy and moral address, the general line that blaming someone for something you're guilty of yourself is defective because you lack the standing to address the demand. I bought it going in and now I dont, not because I feel any urge to defend hypocrites but because I think the whole framework mislocates the problem, and I want to find out whether the alternative can be made into a real argument instead of a vibe. So argue for this claim, as strongly as you honestly can: that having committed the very fault you're now blaming in someone else can improve your standing to blame them -- not merely restore it once you've repented, but make available a form of blame that the faultless blamer structurally cannot offer. Since I've been using labels in my own notes, you can too: call the ordinary case clean-handed blame, and call the other reflexive blame, meaning blame whose content the blamer is herself inside, where the norm being invoked has already been applied to her and she knows what that application cost. On the view I want argued, standing doesn't track compliance with the norm, it tracks your practical relationship to the norm -- whether you understand what it demands from the inside, whether you've actually met the reasons that make it hard to meet, whether you can address the person you're blaming as someone who has been where they are. First-person commission plus acknowledgment buys an authority a spotless record cannot.

Here's the case I keep turning over; modify it or swap in a variant if that makes the argument cleaner. A retired structural engineer sits on a professional integrity panel reviewing a young member who signed off on calculations he knew were under-checked and who is now insisting it was a one-off, not a pattern. Twenty-five years ago she did exactly that on a project of her own, it got caught, she lost two years of her career over it, she has never repeated it, and she has said publicly and often that she did it. The standard picture seems to leave only two options: her past forfeits her standing, or repentance plus time washes it away, in which case she ends up with precisely the standing of everyone else in that room who never signed a bad sheet. I want the argument to explain why both of those are wrong, and why she in particular can blame this man in a way his spotless colleagues cannot.

Some constraints on how you do it. Give me one argument with a single load-bearing premise, name that premise, say why it's the most exposed part of the whole thing, and say how a defender should motivate or patch it -- I'd rather have one developed line than five considerations listed. Then take on the reductio, because the first objection I got when I floated this is that it can't be right, it implies the worse you've been the better your standing, and it would have us staffing integrity panels out of the pool of people who've been disciplined. So the account has to do genuine discriminating work: explain why the serial wrongdoer who acknowledges nothing has no authority at all here, and why acknowledgment isn't quietly doing all the work by itself with the wrongdoing tagging along as a useless add-on. Tell me also what happens to the classic hypocrite -- currently guilty, unrepentant, blaming someone else for the very thing -- since the honest version of my view says his problem isn't that he lacks standing but that he isn't blaming at all, and I want that made good on rather then asserted. Finally, say what the view predicts about cases the standard account gets wrong, especially the moralizer of untested virtue who has never once been tempted, and say something about whether a theory that hands extra authority to the previously guilty is one we'd actually want institutions to run on -- the panel case is a design question and not just a thought experiment. Don't hedge your way into on-the-one-hand-on-the-other, commit to the position and let it be assessed. American English please, and you don't need to cite or recommend any sources, I'm not building a bibliography, I just want the argument.
