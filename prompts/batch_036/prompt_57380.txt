For eleven years I have painted murals on commission for the tenants' association in the building where I grew up, and in October a juror on a regional prize panel told me, meaning it as a kindness, that my work was admirable advocacy but not really art — that it told people what to think before it gave them anything to see. I am thirty-nine, the first in my family to be in a graduate program, and I have enrolled part-time in an M.A. where the seminar paper I care about most is now due. My topic is Du Bois's 1926 essay *Criteria of Negro Art* and its argument that art is, and has always been, propaganda. The juror's remark is the reason I chose it, and I am worried that my draft has quietly turned into an argument with him rather than an argument with Du Bois, which is a confusion I would like help seeing clearly.

Here is my thesis and structure, still in notes rather than polished prose. (1) The reading I take to be standard: Du Bois is prescribing, telling Black artists that their work ought to serve the race and that the doctrine of art for art's sake is a luxury they cannot afford. (2) My reading, which is where I think the paper lives: the propaganda claim is not primarily a prescription about the artist's intentions but a description of the conditions of reception. A public already trained not to see Black life as beautiful supplies a polemic to any depiction of it, hostile or admiring, whether the artist intends one or not; neutrality is simply unavailable. So Du Bois's point, on my account, is that the artist is choosing which propaganda, not whether to propagandize. (3) My coinage for this: I call it the loaded default, the idea that an audience has already signed a standing commission the work cannot decline. (4) I want to connect this to the contemporary debate over whether a work's moral defects can also be its artistic defects, and I have been toying with importing the notion of testimonial injustice to explain why Du Bois thinks the white critic is not a neutral perceiver; I cannot tell whether either move is a bridge or an anachronism. (5) The section I am genuinely stuck on concerns beauty and truth and the freedom of the artist: I have paraphrased Du Bois as holding that beauty can be enlisted but not conscripted, and I cannot work out whether that formulation is doing interpretive work or merely flattering my own position as a muralist. (6) I end on the juror, which may be self-indulgent.

I would like comments of four kinds, in this order of priority. First, accuracy: tell me bluntly which of my attributions to Du Bois you think are wrong, overstated, or indistinguishable from what everyone already says, and flag any historical or publication detail you are not confident about — I cannot get to a library this month, and I would much rather you write that you are unsure than fill a gap with a plausible reconstruction. Second, interest: is the loaded default distinction earning its keep, or is it a relabeling of the truism that no art is made in a vacuum? If it is a relabeling, say so, and tell me what would have to be true of it for it to become a real claim. Third, the stuck section: help me see whether beauty can be enlisted but not conscripted is a defensible reading or a comfortable one, and if you think it collapses, tell me what harder question Du Bois is raising there instead. Fourth, give me one objection I have not anticipated — ideally one that could be voiced from the juror's chair, since I suspect I have built a straw man of a man who was, in person, perfectly sincere. Please do not send me a reading list, a bibliography, or citations of any kind; I cannot chase them down, and what I need is your judgment of my argument rather than pointers toward other people's. Finally, if you think the paper would be stronger as something narrower — the reception claim on its own, or the encounter with the juror on its own — tell me which, and tell me what I would have to give up to get it.
