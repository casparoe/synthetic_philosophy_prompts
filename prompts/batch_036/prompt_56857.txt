You are a philosopher of risk regulation who designs decision problems for a graduate seminar on the precautionary principle. Please think the problem through carefully: before committing, sketch two or three candidate settings and check each against the constraints below, but show me only the finished case and its analysis.

Your task is to construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. Its purpose is to test whether the precautionary principle does any work of its own over and above ordinary risk aversion; a case that merely displays a cautious decision-maker being cautious therefore fails, as does a case that announces its verdicts rather than generating them from the theories.

Setting: realistic and institutional — a port authority, a blood transfusion service, a seed-approval agency, the operator of a tailings dam, or something of that kind. No science fiction, no exotic technologies, no existential risks. The protagonist is one identifiable decision-maker working to a deadline.

Constraints on the case:

1. The scenario proper is to be a single paragraph, with every probability and payoff stated explicitly and finitely. No harm may be so vast, and no probability so vanishing, that the case degenerates into a Pascalian wager.
2. The harm that the cautious option avoids must be irreversible in the strict sense that no subsequent compensation could undo it. You must then say whether the case is really turning on irreversibility rather than on precaution under uncertainty, since the two are routinely run together.
3. The self-paralysis reply must be unavailable: the precautionary measure may not itself carry a threat of comparable harm, so that a critic cannot simply observe that the principle forbids its own remedy.
4. You must exhibit one definite concave utility function, anchored in risk attitudes the decision-maker reveals elsewhere in the story, under which expected-utility maximisation still recommends the risky option.

Deliverable: the scenario, followed by (a) what an expected-value maximiser recommends, (b) what your risk-averse expected-utility maximiser recommends, with the arithmetic shown, and (c) what maximin — and hence the precautionary principle — recommends. Then check the case against the features usually said to make maximin a sensible rule of choice (no reliable probabilities, indifference to gains above the guaranteed floor, worst outcomes that are not merely bad but unacceptable), and say which of these your case genuinely has. Identify the single feature doing the work, state what happens to each verdict if that feature is removed, and finish with the strongest attack a critic in the mould of Cass Sunstein can make on the case together with your judgement of whether it succeeds.

Please write your answer in British English.
