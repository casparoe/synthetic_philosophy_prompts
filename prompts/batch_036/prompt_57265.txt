I'm in a decision theory seminar and this week we did the St Petersburg game plus Bernoulli's 1738 fix — people don't care about ducats as such, they care about utility, and since a gain matters less the richer you already are, the game's real worth comes out finite. What I keep wondering is what Bernoulli himself would make of the modern people who run his own argument in the opposite direction: the longtermists who say a policy is obviously worth adopting becuase it nudges up the odds of an astonomically valuable far future, or the mugging-type cases where you're offered an unthinkably huge reward in exchange for a small payment now. Would he recognize that as his move, or as a misuse of it? My instinct is he'd say they dropped the load-bearing part — that utility was always indexed to what you already have rather than floating free of your situation, and that the series only converged because the extra gains stop mattering fast enough — but is that an actual reply or just a restatement of his own model? Menger's objection was that you can always cook up a faster-growing payoff that breaks any unbounded utility function, so would Bernoulli accept a bounded utility as the honest way to finish the job, or would he think bounding it destroys something he took to be true about human welfare? And there's the variant where the payoffs alternate in sign so the expected-value series never settles on any determinate number — I figure he'd have to admit his theory has nothing to say there, but tell me if I'm wrong about that too.
