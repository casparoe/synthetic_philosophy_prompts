I run evaluation and release-gating methodology at a mid-size applied lab — roughly forty people, of whom maybe six matter for the dispute I want to describe. My job is largely administrative: I turn the safety team's intuitions into criteria that a release decision can actually be checked against. For two years the criterion I have been asked to operationalize has had the same shape, something like "the probability that a deployed system takes an action in the irreversible-harm category over a one-year deployment horizon is at most ε," with ε estimated from red-teaming, production monitoring, and elicitation studies. The moment I write that sentence down, the lab splits into two camps that have never once convinced each other of anything, and I have stopped being able to tell whether they are disagreeing about mathematics, about evidence, or about something else entirely.

The first camp — mostly people with a theory background — says that a probabilistic criterion is not a guarantee at all, because the probability is taken over a measure that we chose, and the entire reason we care about the harm category is that we do not know the measure: the inputs that matter are the ones nobody thought to sample. For them, only a statement that holds for every input, or every input in some explicitly adversary-defined class, is worth writing into a release bar. The second camp — mostly empiricists, including my own team — replies that a worst-case statement over all inputs is vacuous, because for any policy whatsoever I can hand you a prompt that breaks it: an adversarial suffix, an input in a language the model barely saw in training, a roleplay frame, a context assembled by an automated search with a large budget. On their view the only honest and only useful claim is a rate over the traffic distribution we can actually observe, with error bars and a stated confidence level. Complexity theory made this axis precise long ago and even built a taxonomy of possible worlds out of it, but it seems to me the alignment version has lost the two things that make the technical version work: a fixed reference distribution, and a notion of reduction that lets you say one guarantee is strictly stronger than another. Neither camp will say what they think the reference distribution is.

Three specific episodes are why I cannot adjudicate this myself, and I would like them treated as data rather than as color. First, I once proposed that the whole dispute reduces to a disagreement about the magnitude of ε — the empiricists are comfortable at ε around 10⁻⁴ for reversible harms, the others insist ε must be exactly zero for irreversible ones — and both sides got annoyed and said I had missed the point, without agreeing with each other about why. I suspect the real content is that the loss is unbounded or lexicographic, so shrinking ε does not shrink ε times the loss, but I could be wrong. Second, the empiricists' measure is not stationary under the system's own behavior: the model's outputs change what users ask for next, published red-team results change the population of attackers, and a system that gets good at refusing one category of request visibly displaces traffic into adjacent categories. So the distribution being quantified over is partly produced by the policy it is supposed to bound. I have raised this in meetings twice; both camps nodded and then continued exactly as before, and I do not know whether that is because it is fatal, or because it is a technicality, or because neither camp has a vocabulary for it. Third, when I press the worst-case camp hard, they retreat from "all inputs" to "all inputs an adaptive adversary with a specified query budget and weight access can generate," which looks to me like a probabilistic statement with the distribution replaced by a set — that is, like the other camp's position with different notation. My working hypothesis is therefore that this is not a formal disagreement at all but a dispute about who bears the burden of specifying the measure, dressed up as a dispute about guarantees; equivalently, that "guarantee" is doing double duty as a term of art and as a word about institutional responsibility, and the two camps are using different halves of it. Please tell me whether that diagnosis is right or whether it is too cheap. What I want is an account of the structure of the disagreement, using what each side is actually committed to: is there a hidden theorem-shaped question here (and if so, state it precisely enough that someone could try to prove or refute it), or a dispute about standards of evidence, or a disagreement about whether a red-team success counts as a sample from a distribution or as a counterexample to a universal claim, or a difference in the boundedness assumptions about loss, or bedrock attitudes toward tail risk and toward who gets blamed that neither side can argue for? Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a definition, a result, a measurement protocol, a rewriting of the release criterion that dissolves the standoff — or argue that no progress should be expected and say why. I am explicitly not after a survey of the robustness or safe-RL literature, a summary of both viewpoints, or advice about how to run better meetings; I have all of that already and it has not helped.
