I'm third year in a philosophy PhD on inductive methods, and I've been sitting in on our dept's AI-alignment reading group, which has now spent two whole terms talking past itself about the malign-prior argument. You know the one: if you're doing Solomonoff induction over some long bitstring, then on any complicated-enough sequence a big chunk of your posterior plausibly sits on hypotheses that contain agents who've realized they're being predicted and are emitting whatever makes the predictor update in a direction they like — because a good manipulator is cheap to describe (it only has to be good at one thing) whereas a benign hypothesis has to carry a whole universe. Half the group thinks that's a real and disturbing result about simplicity-weighted priors; the other half thinks it's a category mistake, since the universal prior is a measure over environments/bitstrings and there's no fact of the matter about what a hypothesis "wants," and since the whole thing lives in the regime where the O(1) constant from your choice of reference machine is doing all the work — which is exactly where "exponentially more weight" talk stops meaning anything.

Write the joint statement those two camps could actually put their names to after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want: a short preamble saying what they take themselves to be arguing about; a numbered section of claims both can sign, covering the formal setup and the bit-cost bookkeeping (including the awkward point that a manipulator hypothesis still has to reproduce our entire observation history, so the comparison isn't manipulator-vs-nothing but manipulator-plus-shared-simulation vs benign-universe-alone); then a disagreements table giving each live disagreement in wording its own side would accept for its own position; then two or three cruxes, each with the argument or thought experiment both sides would grant actually bears on it and each side's prediction written down in advance; and finally a section saying which parts of the dispute are verbal, which ones, and why.

Two candidate cruxes of my own to fold in if they hold up. First, whether the manipulator hypotheses need to be *individually* short or only *collectively* numerous — my sense is the argument slides between these and the slide is doing a lot of work. Second, whether "malign" should mean aimed adversarially at the predictor's decisions or merely systematically wrong exactly where the predictions feed back into the world; if it's only the second, a deflationist should be able to sign it and it stops being scary. Also: don't let the deflationists win by definitional fiat and don't let the other side smuggle in agency talk where a measure-theoretic claim would do.

And please tag each agreed claim as either genuinely settled among people who work on this stuff or just something these two parties converge on while the field is split — I need to be able to tell those apart, and I'd rather have the residual disagreement stated sharply than a document that reads like everyone already agrees. If some part of it can't be made precise without picking a side, say so instead of papering over it.
