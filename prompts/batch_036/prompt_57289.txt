I've reconstructed a colleague's argument about attributing beliefs to large language models, and it appears to terminate in eliminativism about belief, which neither of us accepts. What I can't locate is where it goes wrong.

(1) A system has beliefs just in case attributing beliefs to it is the best way available to us of predicting what it will say and do.
(2) For ordinary people no rival strategy comes close: neurophysiological description cannot predict a person's next sentence, and any prediction it did manage would itself have to be cashed out as a belief attribution before we could use it. So (1) is satisfied by people.
(3) For a current-generation language model, belief attribution likewise beats the alternatives we actually have — inspecting weights, or predicting token frequencies directly — because the attributions that work best are contentful and generalizing: it believes the user is probing it, it believes the passage in front of it is Hume's, it believes that answering directly will be penalized.
(4) The criterion in (1) quantifies only over predictive strategies, so it cannot register what a system is made of, how it acquired its dispositions, or whether its states are hooked to the world in the right way.
(5) Therefore models have beliefs on exactly the same footing as people.
(6) But having a belief requires more than being usefully describable as having one: it requires that the state be capable of being wrong in a way that matters to the system, and that what the state is about be fixed by something other than our descriptions. Nothing in (3) establishes this of a model, and (4) guarantees that nothing could.
(7) So models do not have beliefs.
(8) Since (5) and (7) cannot both stand, (1) is false. And once (1) is false we have no non-question-begging account of what having a belief consists in — (2) was doing all the work for people as well — so the conclusion is general: nothing has beliefs.

I would rather keep (2) and (3) than (1), and rather keep (6) than (5), though I don't know that both preferences can be honored at once. Which is the first step you would reject, why did it look innocent when I wrote it down, and what does rejecting it cost — for the ordinary practice of explaining people, and for the practice of explaining machines?
