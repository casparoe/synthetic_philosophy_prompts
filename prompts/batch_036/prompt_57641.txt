Last week my second-year seminar did Strawson's Basic Argument — the impossibility-of-moral-responsibility one — and afterwards two of my PhD students, Naledi and Tomás, got into it at the pub and neither would budge. I mostly sat there with a notebook. Ive tried to keep their actual wording rather than cleaning it up into seminar-speak:

1. Tomás opened by saying the argument is decisive in a way people underappreciate, precisely because it doesn't need determinism. If you're going to be truly responsible for what you do, you have to be truly responsible for the way you are, at least in the mental respects that actually produced the act, and nobody is self-made in that sense. So it kills moral responsibility whether the world is deterministic or full of indeterministic gaps, which means the entire free will debate has been arguing about an irrelevant variable.

2. Naledi said that's the selling point and also the tell. Take determinism out and what's left is the claim that a random or uncaused source of your action isn't your source either — which no compatibilist ever denied. So the 'works either way' version isn't more general than the consequence argument, it's just question-begging: it assumes responsibility requires ultimacy, characterises ultimacy so nothing could satisfy it, and then announces the discovery that nothing does.

3. Tomás said it's diagnostic rather than question-begging. What it tells compatibilists is something true about their own position: when they say responsibility only needs the action to issue from your own reasons-responsive machinery, they aren't meeting the traditional demand, they're swapping it for a different one. Perfectly respectable — but then say so, instead of claiming to have rescued the ordinary notion of what we do to people when we blame them.

4. Naledi said fine, call it a revision — and then she turned the regress on itself. Her move: the argument says that to be responsible for D you must be responsible for the state that issued in D, and for the state that produced that, all the way down. Now run the identical regress on belief. To be justified in believing p on that standard, you'd have to be responsible for the cognitive dispositions that produced the belief, and for the dispositions behind those. Nobody is. So applied consistently the requirement yields global epistemic skepticism. A requirement whose consistent application destroys justification altogether isn't a very strict requirement, it's a broken one, and the moral case is the same broken requirement with a different subject matter painted on it.

5. Tomás said that's a category confusion and moreover it plays into his hands. Justification has never required that you authored your own perceptual apparatus — nobody thinks you have to be responsible for having eyes before you're permitted to trust them. Which is his point exactly: in the epistemic domain everyone already agrees that ownership of your faculties isn't a condition for those faculties counting as yours. The moral domain is the one place anyone insists on it, and what Strawson is really doing is showing how strange that insistence looks once you notice nobody would tolerate it anywhere else. So the parity doesn't refute the Basic Argument, it is the Basic Argument, run on a case where the conclusion is uncontroversial.

6. Naledi said then they agree on the structure and disagree only about which way it runs — and if the epistemic case shows the requirement is unmotivated, the right response is to repair the moral concept the same way, dropping the ownership condition and keeping everything else. That repair keeps blame, praise, and holding to account fully intact, so the impossibility result is a verbal triumph. She finished with a dilemma: either the regress states a real requirement we're failing to meet, in which case it also wrecks justification and Tomás is a skeptic, or it states a requirement nobody outside the moral case ever endorsed, in which case there's no impossibility result, only a stipulation.

7. Tomás took the second horn without blinking. He said a stipulation about a concept that's actually in use is still a concept in use, and concepts in use can be shown to have empty extensions — phlogiston was a perfectly respectable notion for a long time and turned out not to pick anything out. Demonstrating that the ordinary notion of true moral responsibility has no instances is a substantive finding about our concept, not a trivia point about a definition somebody picked. Then he added a sting: if the requirement was never really ours, why does the argument land the way it does, and why do compatibilists burn so much effort explaining away the intuition that it lands?

Thats where I'm stuck. I genuinely can't tell whether they disagree or whether they've both got hold of the same argument and are calling it two different things. So: go through 1 to 7 and call each one — does it land, does it half-land, or is it a mistake — with a reason for each call, not just an overall verdict. I especially want to know whether the epistemic-parity move in 4 is a real objection to the Basic Argument or whether Tomás is right in 5 that it's the same argument wearing a disguise, because if he is, I want to understand what's left of Naledi's position. Tell me whether the phlogiston comparison in 7 is doing the work he thinks it is. And point out anything either of them conceded without noticing that they'd conceded it — I have a suspicion one of them did, but I couldn't say which while they were both talking over each other.
