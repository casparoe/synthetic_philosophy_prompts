Below are the five principles that govern how I size bets on my own portfolio. I have defended each of them separately many times; I have never had to defend them jointly, and I want to know whether they can be.

1. I accept the ergodicity-economics line: for one agent living through a multiplicative process, the time-average growth rate of wealth is the operative criterion and the ensemble average is a fiction. So maximizing the expected logarithm of my wealth is not a claim about my psychology or my utility curvature — it is a claim about the dynamics. I neither need nor believe in concave utility layered on top of it.
2. Accordingly, on repeatable bets I stake the full Kelly fraction computed from my own probability estimates, and I reject fractional-Kelly shading. Halving the stake would be a tacit concession that risk aversion beyond the logarithm is legitimate, and on my view it is not.
3. I also hold that no policy is acceptable to me if it carries any nonzero probability of my portfolio dropping below a floor of one year's living expenses. A superior growth rate does not buy off that constraint; the floor is absolute.
4. For genuinely one-shot opportunities I evaluate by expected value and consider myself risk-neutral. Concretely: offered a single, non-repeatable fair-coin flip paying 1.2-to-1 on whatever I stake, with any proceeds reinvested in the same portfolio, I would stake 40% of my net worth. The bet is worth plus 4% of net worth in expectation, so I take it.
5. My horizon is finite. I expect to stop contributing and start drawing the portfolio down in roughly twenty-five years, and I give away a fixed 10% of it annually. Colleagues insist that (1) and (2) cannot survive either fact: that a finite horizon obliges me to de-risk as the endpoint approaches, and that a standing proportional donation mechanically lowers my optimal stake.

Please audit the set rather than lecture me on Kelly. Tell me which of these genuinely pull against each other and which pairs only look like they do — I expect at least one of the objections reported in (5) is a mistake, but I do not know which, and I may be wrong about that too. Rank the real tensions by severity instead of presenting them as equally bad, and for each one state what has to give, what the cheapest revision is, and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the list. Give me a verdict, not a menu: name the commitment I should drop or rewrite, and do not retreat into saying that everything turns on how I define my terms.
