You are a philosopher of physics with substantial experience teaching the foundations of statistical mechanics to non-specialists. Treat what follows as a curriculum-design problem rather than an occasion for exposition, and think carefully before answering: sketch the alternative arcs you considered, and why you rejected them, before settling on one.

I am a third-year doctoral student working on the metaphysics of time, and next month I run a seventy-five-minute session for my department's reading group, which is almost entirely metaphysicians. They handle formal argument happily and have read the survey-level material on temporal direction. They are B-theorists, and they will arrive convinced the topic is philosophically exhausted: if all times are equally real, then the "direction" of time can only be a matter of how entropy happens to be distributed across the manifold, and that distribution is a contingent matter of fact about which there is nothing further to say. I want a plan for the session, not the session.

Please address the following.

1. **Order and dependence.** What must be installed before what, and why — in particular, what has to be in place before "the universe began in a very low-entropy state" can be heard by them as a substantive claim about the world rather than as a redescription of the fact that we have records and they do not.

2. **The carrying example.** My proposal is to run everything on a short clip of ink dispersing in water, played forwards and then backwards, asking them to identify which is which. Assess whether that can bear the weight I need. If it cannot, say precisely what any replacement must have and must lack.

3. **The misconception map.** My guesses: (a) "the arrow just is the direction in which entropy increases", which renders the whole account circular; (b) "disorder is in the eye of the describer", hence the arrow is anthropocentric; (c) "the second law is merely statistical and therefore not a law in the same sense as the underlying dynamics" — and its nastier corollary, that nothing has actually been explained, because typicality is always typicality relative to a measure, and the choice of measure looks as arbitrary as the thing it was meant to explain; (d) they will collapse the thermodynamic, psychological and radiative arrows into a single problem. Tell me which of these are real, where I have mischaracterised them, what else they will bring, and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. I also expect someone to produce the reversibility objection and the recurrence objection as though they were the same objection, and as though either were fatal.

4. **A check.** A closing question or brief task that would show the idea has landed rather than that they have politely agreed with me — ideally one on which a confident B-theorist who has not understood would give a characteristic wrong answer. Tell me what that answer sounds like.

5. **Refusals.** What I should openly decline to cover at this level, and say so to them.

Please write your answer in British English.
