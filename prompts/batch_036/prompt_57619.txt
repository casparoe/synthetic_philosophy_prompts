I spent thirty-six years studying early mammalian development in a laboratory and retired two years ago. Philosophy has since become my principal occupation, and I have now reached the debate over whether an organism's origin is essential to it — the thesis, most famously defended by Kripke, that a person could not have come from a different sperm and a different egg. What strikes me is how little of that discussion is disciplined by what embryos actually do. Before I invest further in the arguments, I want to know what should be asked of a theory in this area at all.

So: write the specification. What must an adequate account of origins deliver in order to count as a solution rather than a restatement of an intuition?

The purpose, since adequacy is relative to it: I want an account I could defend in a philosophy of biology seminar and that would not embarrass me in front of colleagues who make embryos for a living. The standard is therefore joint acceptability to metaphysicians and to developmental biologists, and any account that quietly assumes a picture of conception no embryologist would recognize fails on that ground alone.

Requirements for your answer:

1. State each requirement together with the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as the preference of a particular camp. Where it is camp-relative, say which camp, and what it costs them to give it up.

2. Say what the theory must take the origin to be — the pair of gametes, the zygote, the genome, the cytoplasmic environment, the developmental process initiated at fertilization — and note that this choice, rather than the modal claim, determines which problems arise. The requirements should be stated at that resolution.

3. The account must handle the following without special pleading:
   (a) Monozygotic twinning, where one zygote yields two individuals; in humans the split may occur anywhere from the two-cell stage to the second week, and late splitting gives conjoined twins. In the nine-banded armadillo a single zygote reliably yields four genetically identical offspring.
   (b) Deliberate embryo splitting, routine in livestock: an early embryo is bisected or its blastomeres separated, each part regulating into a complete animal, and the parts may be carried by different surrogates or one of them frozen for years and transferred later.
   (c) Chimerism, where two early embryos fuse into a single individual carrying two distinct cell lines.
   (d) Nuclear transfer, in which an adult somatic nucleus is introduced into an enucleated egg.
   (e) Mitochondrial replacement, in which a child's nuclear DNA comes from two people and its mitochondrial DNA from a third.
   (f) Artificial activation of an unfertilized egg, which in some vertebrate species produces viable offspring.
   (g) Intracytoplasmic sperm injection, where one sperm out of hundreds of millions is chosen by a person holding a micropipette, so that no fact determines which gamete would otherwise have fertilized the egg.

4. It must supply identity conditions for the entities it appeals to. An ovulated human oocyte has been arrested in meiotic prophase since before its mother was born; a sperm is a short-lived cell budded off a stem-cell population that renews itself continuously and generates hundreds of millions of competitors. If that very gamete is not a determinate object, an account anchored in gametes inherits the indeterminacy, and I want that counted as a requirement rather than an aside.

5. It must say what force the necessity bears — metaphysical, nomological, or something weaker — and it must have something to say about the sliding-scale worry: if an organism could have arisen from gametes differing from the actual ones in progressively less significant respects, where does the slide stop, and why.

6. Identify any subset of these requirements you believe cannot be jointly satisfied. This is the part I most want. If a no-branching condition, the twinning and splitting cases, and the demand that origins not be brute facts pull against one another, I want that stated as a result with the argument sketched, not smoothed over by a distinction introduced at the last minute.

7. Tell me which single requirement eliminates the most candidates, and where the live disagreement actually lies once the requirements every account already meets have been set aside.

You may close with two or three sentences on how the best-known accounts fare against the list. Keep it short: the scorecard is the part I can do myself, and I do not want a reading list, as I have one.

Finally, an epistemic request. Tell me what would change your mind: which of these requirements you hold least securely, and what argument or what piece of biology would make you withdraw it or demote it from non-negotiable to sectarian. And tell me where you expect me to catch you out — a case described inaccurately, an empirical claim stated with more confidence than it deserves, or a modal intuition mistaken for a fact about organisms.
