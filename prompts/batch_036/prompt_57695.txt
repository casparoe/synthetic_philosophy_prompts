You are to write as a novelist with serious training in the philosophy of the social sciences — someone who could publish a story in a literary magazine and a methods paper on measurement in a specialist journal, and who is as much at home with sampling frames and index construction as with scene, voice, and restraint. Think carefully before drafting. Begin with a design note of no more than 250 words in which you sketch three different narrative frames for the same material (for instance a bureaucratic one, a domestic one, an archival or epistolary one), say which you are choosing and why, and then list the three mutually incompatible *explanations* of your central phenomenon, naming for each the character or document that will carry it, together with the place where a fourth and different kind of question will surface — the normative one, about whether an equal distribution of choices is what justice demands at all, or only the absence of obstruction. Then write the story: 1,800 to 2,500 words, with a title.

The phenomenon is the gender-equality paradox — the finding, roughly, that in the societies with the most thoroughgoing legal and institutional equality, the divergence between men and and women in fields of study, occupations, and self-described dispositions tends to be larger rather than smaller. Take the phenomenon as given; do not lean on the real literature. Name no real researcher, study, journal, country, or statistic anywhere in the story, and if a number appears it must be invented and consistent with every other number in the piece. For your setting, invent a small, wealthy, famously egalitarian country — plainly fictional; if you want a name, use something like Vessland — and inside it a Directorate whose annual Divergence Report has become an institution. Its senior statistician has spent fifteen years producing a figure showing that the sorting of men and women into work and study grows steadily finer among a cohort that has never met a measurable barrier: a national birth-cohort study in which every child born in one particular year has been followed since birth, with school, clinic, apprenticeship, and employer records linked, so that formal discrimination is not merely discouraged but auditable, and has largely been audited away. She also maintains the comparison series against a designated reference country where equality is nominally much worse and the divergence index is stubbornly, embarrassingly small — and she has begun to notice that the reference country's figures come from a voluntary survey in which the women who respond are overwhelmingly urban, credentialed, and self-selecting, while nearly all of the men respond. Build in concrete human stakes: at least one cohort member who chose against the grain (a girl from a fishing town who went into marine engine work, say, or a boy who went into infant care), and the statistician's own adult child, who is in the cohort, made the expected choice, is unambiguously content, and refuses to have that contentment reclassified as a symptom. Let one character from the reference country make the uncomfortable observation that the backward place produces less finely sorted lives than the advanced one does.

Craft requirements:

1. Keep all three explanations live through the final paragraph. The story must not adjudicate among them, and the narrator must never explain the paradox to the reader in expository voice; it should be visible only in numbers, meetings, forms, and lives.
2. Include at least one passage of dialogue in which the position that these are free choices, and that calling them symptoms of an injustice nobody can locate is an insult to the person making them, is stated so well that a reader who holds it would nod; and at least one in which the position that no preference was ever formed in a vacuum, and that the absence of interference is not the same thing as freedom, is stated so well that its holders would nod. Neither speaker is a strawman and neither gets the last word.
3. Do the one thing an essay cannot: show a figure changing its meaning without changing its value — the same number read one way early and another way later, because of something the reader has learned about how it was collected, who answered, or who was in the room when it was defined.
4. Give the normative doubt a home too, but keep it distinct from the explanatory quarrel: someone should notice, quietly, that the Directorate's founding mandate said obstacles, and that identical distributions were written in afterwards by nobody in particular.
5. Resist the two cheap endings — she does not falsify or leak the report, and she does not have an epiphany. End on an image, or on a small decision whose meaning stays ambiguous.
6. No more than four named characters. Prose should trust the reader: no scene may exist solely to state a thesis.
