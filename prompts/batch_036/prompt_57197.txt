hey — I write a weekly science-and-ideas column, mostly physics-adjacent stuff for a general audience, and this week im finally doing the piece I've been putting off for years: the many-worlds version of russian roulette. The one where you hook the trigger up to a quantum event so the gun fires in some branches and clicks empty in others, and then the argument that from your own point of view you always end up in the branch where it clicked.

I want to open the column with a short poem instead of a lede paragraph, and the form that actually does the job is a pantoum — the one where lines 2 and 4 of each stanza come back as lines 1 and 3 of the next, and the last line of the poem is its first line. That recycling basically *is* the argument: nothing new ever enters, the poem keeps handing you back what you already had, and there's no stanza in which the thing finally ends.

So: write me a pantoum, six or seven quatrains, from inside the head of somebody on the fourth or fifth pull. Constraints, roughly in order of how much I care about them:

- No death on the page from the first person. The fatal branches can absolutely be in there — I want the other people, the ones standing in a room where it went off, and their account of the very same events — but the speaker never gets to see that version, and the poem should make the reader feel that asymmetry without ever explaining it.
- Don't let it be a triumph. I want the part the pop accounts skip: that surviving and surviving *meaningfully* aren't the same thing, that by the fifth pull the branches where you're still breathing get thinner and stranger, and that always observing the click is doing something closer to a trick than a promise. A physicist who's thought about this should nod at that rather than roll their eyes.
- Keep the physics right: no collapse, no single true outcome that gets selected, branches rather than "other universes next door." No name-checking anyone, dead or alive.
- Plain diction — the gun, the room, the counting, the people waiting outside. No archaisms, no moonlight. Rhyme is optional, but the repeated lines have to be reused exactly, not near-echoes; the whole effect depends on the reader recognizing the identical line coming back around.

Then, after the poem, one section titled **Why it works**, about 200 words, telling me which lines you chose to recur and what each one is carrying on its second and third pass, where you put the third-person material and why it goes there, and how the closing loop in the final stanza is meant to land. Write that part for me — a working journalist who needs to know whether the poem is doing what I think it's doing before I hand it to an editor — not for a literature seminar.
