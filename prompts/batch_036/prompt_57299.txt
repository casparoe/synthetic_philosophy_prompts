I'm a staff engineer at a payments company. I own the risk-scoring pipeline that decides whether a transaction is declined outright, released, or escalated to a human analyst. I've been offered one of two places in a year-long program that pairs industry engineers with an academic philosopher, and the application requires a research proposal of about 1,200 words. I have no formal philosophy training; I've been reading on my own about vagueness, mostly because it kept rhyming with a problem I can't solve at work, and I now suspect the rhyming is doing more work than I can evaluate by myself.

The problem. The decision boundary is a single float in a config file, set at 0.73, and it has sat there for roughly six years. The engineer who chose it left the company, the design document is gone, and the backtest that supposedly justified the value was run against a distribution of traffic that no longer exists. So the boundary is perfectly sharp and perfectly knowable in the trivial sense: anyone on my team can open the file and read the number. What nobody can say is whether it belongs there. If I ask what happens at 0.75, the answer is that verdicts change, and no one can tell me why 0.73 rather than 0.75.

My workaround, which is the part I think is philosophically interesting. Rather than defending one cutoff, I evaluate the decision across a band of cutoffs I regard as defensible, and I treat as unstable exactly those cases whose verdict flips somewhere inside the band; those go to a human. Structurally this resembles the supervaluationist picture: a range of admissible precisifications, a claim treated as settled when it survives all of them, and a residue of borderline cases handled by escalation instead of by semantics. Whether that resemblance is anything more than a pun is precisely what I cannot judge, and I want the proposal to treat it as a hypothesis to be tested, not as a premise.

My half-formed idea. As I understand it, the standard debate assumes that if there is a sharp boundary it is fixed by how the predicate is used and hidden from us; the epistemicist's argument seems to run through a principle that limits what can be known at cases very close to the boundary. My thresholds are the reverse: sharp, fixed by an explicit act, visible in plaintext, and still unjustifiable, because the reasons for the particular value were destroyed by staff turnover rather than by any limitation of language. That looks like a third position, or at least a case neither theory was built to handle — the ignorance has migrated from where the line is to whether the line should be there at all. There is also a temporal wrinkle: traffic drifts, so the same number picks out a different population every quarter, which feels like a sorites series generated by the world rather than by our descriptions of it.

Please turn this into the proposal. I need the question stated precisely enough that it could have a determinate answer; an account of why the two existing positions don't already settle it; a method I could realistically carry out in evenings over a year, given that the underlying data is proprietary and I can only describe the system rather than publish it; what a successful result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and an honest verdict on whether this is too large, or too dependent on inside knowledge, for one person without a philosophy degree. I would also like you to flag where I have most likely got the philosophy wrong, and where the analogy to precisifications is likeliest to break — I would far rather see that written into the proposal than discovered by my supervisor in month three. Please don't include a bibliography or citations; name positions, arguments, and authors where you need to and I'll track the texts down myself, and paraphrase rather than quote.
