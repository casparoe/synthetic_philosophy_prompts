ok so I've been reading the LW-adjacent stuff for something like fifteen years and I've finally been made to write my actual position on disagreement down in one place, because someone in a reading group said "you can't keep saying all of these things, right?". Turns out I probably can't. I'd like an audit rather than a lecture: go through the list, tell me which items genuinely pull against each other and which ones only look like they do, rank the real conflicts by how bad they are, and for each say what the cheapest fix is and what I'd lose by paying it. I'm not telling you which ones I'd rather keep, because I don't know, and I don't want you assuming I'm attached to anything.

The set:

1. Credences are probabilities, full stop, and I should conditionalise on everything I learn — including what other people tell me about their own credences.
2. Aumann's result is one of the most important things in social epistemology: two honest Bayesians with a common prior who come to common knowledge of each other's posteriors for some event can't have different posteriors. Agreeing to disagree is incoherent, not just rude.
3. So when a friend I respect and who knows roughly what I know disagrees with me, that's genuine evidence I've gone wrong somewhere, and I move. On empirical questions I move a lot — something like the equal-weight view seems right to me there.
4. But I don't believe in common priors, really. A prior is whatever your particular cognitive history happened to install in you; two people can be equally rational and start from genuinely different places. There's no fact about which of them is the correct starting point.
5. And that's my main reason for being a steadfast-er about philosophy and ethics: clever, informed, honest people have disagreed about free will and about what makes a life go well for two and a half thousand years with no convergence, and I don't think that shows there's no fact of the matter. Disagreement only bites in domains where you'd expect convergence.
6. Relatedly, common knowledge never actually obtains in real life either. I've never been in a conversation where we had common knowledge of each other's credences — at best we get third-order approximations, and neither of us is a perfect Bayesian in any case. Practically speaking the theorem is vacuous.
7. Still, I'll do a double crux with anyone about anything, and I assume that if two people sat down long enough they'd find the factual crux underneath most disagreements.
8. One case where I definitely don't move: if a friend and I disagree about something we've both been carefully over with the same evidence in front of us — a proof, a dataset, a video we've both watched — I don't split the difference at all. That sort of disagreement just means one of us has made an error, so I go hunting for the error. Averaging credences there would be silly.
9. And I do care about calibration, and I do feel bad when a calibration check shows me systematically overconfident, even though per (4) I've just said priors are personal and there's no correct starting point.

So. Which pairs actually conflict? Which of my worries are unfounded and dissolve once you look at them properly? And is there one revision that would clean up the most mess? Two specific things I want checked: whether every one of my case-level verdicts is actually deliverable by the principles I've stated, or whether I've smuggled one in; and whether (2) is really doing the justificatory work I keep letting it do in (3), given what else I've said.
