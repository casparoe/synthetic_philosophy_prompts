I teach philosophy of language at a mid-sized university and, with two colleagues on leave, I have been drafted this term to run a graduate seminar on the ethics of political office — material I know only as an educated outsider. In preparing it I have kept circling back to a pattern in how political communities handle one particular kind of case, and I cannot find a name for it, though I suspect the specialists have one. Start from the familiar shape of the case: a leader does something that by ordinary standards is a serious wrong — authorizes a strike he knows will kill people nearby who have done nothing, breaches a levee and floods one town to save three, signs off on a method of interrogation that everyone concedes is torture — and there is a respectable argument that the wrong was, all things considered, what had to be done. From what I have skimmed, the literature seems agreed that something morally sticks to the agent in such cases, and that the fitting posture afterwards is anguish, acknowledgment, or the voluntary acceptance of some cost. What I keep noticing instead is a systematic social process by which that sticking is not merely neglected but actively converted. The community takes up the very act that would have marked the leader as tainted and makes it the ground of admiration: the burden is spoken of as courage, the sleepless nights become the theme of the farewell address, the survivors of the flooded town are seated as honored guests at the dedication of the flood memorial, and the memoir in which the minister admits what he did is reviewed as a model of candor. The wrongness is never denied — it is insisted upon, since only a real wrong can make the resolve impressive — but the remainder that ought to stay on the agent is taken up by the collective and discharged in the currency of esteem. And if the agent then goes on acting as though he still owes something, that is read as self-indulgence; the mood turns to something like 'we do not need your anguish, we need your resolve.'

I want to know whether this has a name. I can guess at the neighborhood — the standard problem of dirty hands, Bernard Williams's notion of agent-regret, talk of moral residue and of taint, and the psychological literature on how prior good conduct licenses later bad conduct — but none of these appears to be the thing itself. The dirty-hands problem concerns the agent's predicament rather than what the public subsequently does with it; agent-regret describes the attitude the agent ought to have, whereas what I am describing is precisely the social machinery that makes that attitude optional and then embarrassing; moral residue names the stuff that is left over, and my phenomenon is a way of getting rid of it without paying it to anyone who was harmed. Nor is it ordinary whitewashing or historical revisionism, which operate by denying that the act was wrong at all, since here the wrongness is granted and then capitalized on. And it is not scapegoating, which transfers blame onto a third party rather than transmuting it into credit for the person who acted.

So: tell me what this is. If some established concept does fit, name it and show me exactly where my description overshoots or undershoots it. If nothing fits — which is what I expect — give me a term you would defend, a definition stated in conditions rather than in a mood, and the boundary cases that do the real work: does the phenomenon still obtain when the honors are paired with genuine compensation to those who were harmed; is it a distinct thing when the agent refuses the honors and demands to be blamed instead, and if so does that refusal have a name of its own; does it matter whether the admiring audience is the polity as a whole or only the leader's own supporters; and at what point does this simply collapse into propaganda, so that the concept earns its keep instead of duplicating something we already have a word for? I am not asking for references or a reading list, which I can assemble myself once I know what to look for. I am asking you to commit: tell me whether you think this is one phenomenon or two, tell me what you would call it, and argue for the edges you have drawn rather than setting out a menu of candidates and leaving the choice to me.
