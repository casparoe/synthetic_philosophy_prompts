I keep going in circles on the pramāṇa-count dispute in classical Indian epistemology and I want a diagnosis of why nobody has budged, not another survey. Set up as I read it: Dignaga says there are exactly two pramanas, perception and inference, because there are only two kinds of cognition -- one free of conceptual construction, one shot through with it -- and so testimony gets folded into inference as just a special case of reasoning from a sign. The Naiyayikas say four, and they count by causal route rather than by the shape of the resulting awareness, so word-testimony is its own thing even though it obviously depends on knowing what the words mean and having some grip on whether the speaker is reliable.

The sub-case I keep coming back to is postulation, arthapatti: the Bhāṭṭas say "the fat man who doesn't eat by day must eat at night" can't be inference, because the cognition just lands, nobody runs a middle term through their head; the Naiyāyika says that's a report of what it feels like from the inside, and there's an inference there whether you notice it or not.

My hunch, offered as a hunch: the two camps aren't disagreeing about knowledge at all, they're applying two different criteria for counting -- form of cognition vs. causal instrument -- so each is internally right and neither can see the question the other is asking. Is that fair, or is something substantive riding on it, like whether testimony can ever be checked without already trusting testimony? And what would actually settle it -- a case where two pramanas give conflicting verdicts plus a rule for which wins, some argument about individuation, or should we just expect permanent stalemate?
