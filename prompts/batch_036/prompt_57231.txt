I'm a novelist and I've got a problem that sounds like craft but is actually metaphysics. My narrator is 71 and keeps circling back to what she did at nineteen, and every time I hit one of those passages I have to decide what her "I" is picking up: the whole person stretched across fifty years, or just the old woman at the kitchen table, with the girl as a seperate thing she used to be. I know endurantism and perdurantism are supposed to come out the same on nearly everything observable, which is exactly why I can't just lift the debate into the book.

So write me a decision procedure I can run on each of these scenes — there's maybe a hundred of them, and the ending depends on getting the last one right. It has to work for me and my co-writer, who's never taken a philosophy class, so plain language only: no talk of temporal parts or worldlines in the tests themselves, just ordinary questions about who owes what to whom, whether an apology from her can be accepted, whether the girl or the woman is the one who was wronged, whose name goes on the deed. Each test should be answerable in five minutes with the manuscript open, and the procedure has to say out loud where the metaphysics genuinely forces my hand and where it's only voice and I'm free to choose.

Then run it on three scenes: one where she's proud of the younger self, one where she's ashamed and half-wants to disown her, and one where she takes credit for something the nineteen-year-old did that the seventy-one-year-old would never do now. One of the three has to come out against what I want — if it never tells me no, it isn't a procedure. Finish with what it can't settle.

Gaurd rule: don't let the answer collapse into "perdurantists write she, endurantists write I." If that really is all there is, say so and explain why, but the tests should be keyed to responsibility and ownership, not pronoun aesthetics.
