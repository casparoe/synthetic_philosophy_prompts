**Question 3 (25 points), as it appeared on the exam:**

Robin Hanson's futarchy has elected representatives define and manage an after-the-fact measure of national welfare while speculators trade assets on welfare conditional on each candidate policy; his basic rule of government is 'When a betting market clearly estimates that a proposed policy would increase expected national welfare, that proposal becomes law.' Now consider one concrete implementation, which we will call Constitution F.

(i) The welfare metric is a legislated index W, rebased quarterly.
(ii) Filing a proposal P opens a pair of conditional assets. W|P pays the realized value of W eight quarters out if P is adopted; W|~P pays the realized value of W eight quarters out if P is not adopted. If a branch's condition fails, all positions in that branch are unwound and refunded at the price of the unconditional W asset as of the filing date.
(iii) P becomes law iff, after 90 days of trading, the closing price of W|P exceeds the closing price of W|~P by a margin calibrated so that the difference is significant at p<0.01 given observed volume.
(iv) Representatives retain a veto over changes to the definition of W, and over nothing else.

(a) [10 points] What is the unwinding clause in (ii) for? Identify the problem in a conditional market that it solves, and say what it leaves unsolved.
(b) [15 points] Objection: whoever can move the price can make law, so F legislates by auction to the deepest pocket. State the best futarchist reply, then say whether that reply disposes of the objection for Constitution F in particular, and what would have to be true for it to.

**Student's answer:**

(a) In a conditional market a trader is really betting on two things at once: how high W will be, and whether the condition gets satisfied. F makes that worse than usual, because the condition — P adopted — is itself settled by the price, so somebody who buys W|P just because they think P looks likely to pass isn't expressing any view about welfare at all, and the two branches stop being comparable to each other. The unwinding clause strips that out. If your branch loses you get refunded at the filing-date price of W, so the only thing you can lose on is the level of W, which means the closing prices are clean estimates of expected welfare conditional on adoption rather than mixtures of welfare and adoption-probability. What it leaves unsolved is that the traders know their own trades feed the rule that decides adoption. Refunding the payoff fixes what the asset pays, it doesn't fix what the trader wants. Two smaller things: if the unconditional W asset is thin or stale on the filing date then the refund price is basically arbitrary and informed traders can play the gap; and eight quarters is long enough that the market ends up dominated by whoever can stand to lock up capital that long.

(b) The objection has the incentive backwards. To pull W|P up the bidder has to actually buy shares that pay realized W if P is adopted, so if her estimate is wrong she eats the difference between what she paid and the truth. Manipulation is an expensive way of lying, and the market pays other people to catch the lie — anyone who sees the bias can sell the overpriced branch and collect. Hanson's own reply to this objection adds a legislative backstop, since the representatives can veto any proposal whose margin falls inside the noise band, so a manipulated price doesn't automatically become law. The p<0.01 threshold in (iii) does real work here too: a proposal only passes when the gap between the branches is significant, which means the market is right about 99 percent of the time, so there isn't much room for a bidder to work with. So no, F doesn't hand legislation to the richest bidder, it hands it to the best forecaster, and over time those come apart from the richest, because capital bet badly is capital lost. That's also why futarchy is neither a plutocracy nor an epistocracy: the wealthy get no extra votes, and democracy still sets W by legislation instead of by price.
