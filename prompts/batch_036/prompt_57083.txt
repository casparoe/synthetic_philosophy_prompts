I am a third-year PhD student in a group that argues about whether the entities reported in interpretability papers — features, directions, circuits — are real structure in a trained network or artifacts of the descriptive scheme an interpreter brings to it. Last term I built the following argument for the deflationary side, largely to aggravate the realists in my cohort, and I have since lost control of it: I am now fairly confident the conclusion is false, but I cannot locate the step I am entitled to reject. The empirical setting is not a thought experiment; item (2) describes something a colleague of mine actually did, and that is what makes the argument hard for me to shake.

Setting. We trained small two-layer transformers on four-digit base-10 addition. In one of them we identified a direction *d* in the layer-1 residual stream that separates trials in which the correct answer requires a carry from trials in which it does not, at 98 percent accuracy. Ablating *d* costs roughly thirty points of accuracy on carry trials and nothing on no-carry trials; patching *d* from a carry trial into a no-carry trial reliably converts the model's output into the wrong answer in the characteristic carry-shaped way. Our deflationist colleague then ran an automated search over Boolean combinations of cheap surface features of our finite evaluation corpus — digit sums, parities, string lengths, corpus indices — until she found a property she calls P*: "the sum of all operand digits is divisible by three, XOR the trial's index in the corpus is prime." By construction, P* has exactly the same extension as *requires a carry* over every trial in the corpus. Consequently every experiment we can run on that corpus — correlation, ablation, patching, probing, any causal-scrubbing-style check — has precisely the same results under the reading on which *d* is the carry direction and the reading on which *d* is the P* direction.

The argument.
(1) For a component of a network to have content P, there must be a fact that selects P over rival candidate contents: an attribution is correct only if some attributions are ruled out.
(2) For any finite corpus, any direction, and any candidate content that direction tracks, a rival content can be constructed that is extensionally indistinguishable from it over that corpus, so no measurement confined to the corpus rules the rival out.
(3) Therefore the evidence available to interpretability practice underdetermines content attribution for every component of every trained network: whatever attribution we can support, a rival is supportable to exactly the same degree.
(4) If the available evidence never settles between rival contents, then there is no fact of the matter which content a component has; underdetermination this general is not an epistemic shortfall but the absence of a determinate answer.
(5) Hence no component of a trained network has determinate content, and reports of "the carry direction" or "the induction circuit" are useful fictions licensed by our interests rather than descriptions of structure in the world.
(6) Nothing in that reasoning is specific to networks: for any finite physical system and any finite description of its state transitions, rival mappings are available on which it implements some entirely different computation, so nothing — not my transformer, not a digital circuit, not a brain — determinately implements anything. This is the triviality objection Putnam pressed against the computational theory of mind he had helped create, and my argument appears to reinstate it.

I cannot accept (5), and I find (6) close to absurd: the training script and the arithmetic are fixed by construction, the model gets the answers right, and if it does not compute addition I no longer know what "compute" could mean. I also cannot see how to reject (2), since my colleague's construction looks less like a controversial assumption than like a mathematical fact about finite data. If forced to choose I would rather keep (2) and attack (4), which looks like the only step doing distinctively philosophical work — but I recognize that rejecting (4) commits me to some constraint on which properties are eligible to be contents, and I cannot say what makes a property eligible without appealing to the naturalness intuitions I was trying to avoid in the first place. Note also that (6) seems to be self-undermining in a way I cannot pin down: if no system determinately implements anything, then neither does the search my colleague ran, and neither, presumably, does the reasoning that produced the argument.

Which step do you reject? For each live candidate — (2), (4), and the inference from (3) to (5) — tell me what made it look acceptable, what rejecting it costs, and whether rejecting it blocks (5) only, or (6) as well. Then tell me what each of the standard positions on content determinacy has to pay in order to block the argument: the Quinean inscrutability line, Lewis-style eligibility or naturalness constraints, informational and teleological accounts of content, and the view that content attribution is interpreter-relative all the way down and (5) is simply the correct conclusion. Say explicitly whether you take the argument to be invalid or valid with an overpriced premise, and whether (2) and (6) are really instances of one move or of two different moves that I have conflated — I suspect the second, but I cannot articulate the difference.
