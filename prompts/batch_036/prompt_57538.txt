I'm giving a fifteen-minute paper in my historiography seminar on Tuesday and I want to walk in already knowing what's going to be thrown at me.

The room, precisely: about ten of us, mostly early modernists who spend their lives in notarial registers, parish accounts and print-shop inventories and who are profoundly allergic to grand frameworks; one medievalist whose whole shtick is the twelfth- and thirteenth-century translation business and the Oxford calculators and Buridan on impetus, so he holds that any talk of a seventeenth-century break is nonsense; two people who've done some philosophy and will demand I commit to an ontology; and my tutor, who's a get-on-with-the-archives type and thinks disputes about whether periods are 'real' are a way of burning a research grant without producing a single footnote.

My thesis is this. I'm not going to argue that 'the Middle Ages', 'the Renaissance' or 'the Scientific Revolution' pick out stretches of time with a shared essence, and I'm not going to argue they're mere ideology to be swept away either. I want to say they're real as sorting infrastructure — and then run a bootstrap argument nobody in the module has run. Once a label is welded into library classification, syllabi, journal titles, grant categories and the period filters on a document database, it starts determining which records get digitised, indexed, taught and cited alongside one another. So the corpus the label is suposed to describe gets progressively assembled *by* the label. That has a nasty consequence for the debunkers: the familiar move of piling up counterexamples of continuity can't land, because the counterexamples were never selected into the corpus in the first place. I've been calling the mechanism evidential lock-in, and I use it to explain why the debunking literature comes round again every generation and never settles anything — you can't refute a filing system with a counterexample, and labels like these aren't beliefs anyone is holding, they're load-bearing.

I know the weak point already: 'the Dark Ages' did get substantially retired by the profession, so lock-in obviously isn't invincible, and the feudalism mess shows a construct can be taken apart from inside the discipline. I plan to concede that up front, so tell me what the concession costs me.

Give me the five objections this particular room will actually raise — not the ones a general reader or a book reviewer would raise, these people, in their own vocabulary — ranked by how much damage each would do. For each of the five: the objection roughly as they'd put it, the reply I can get out in twenty seconds, and a blunt flag on where that reply is thin (I half suspect my whole account is a functionalist just-so story with no agent in it, so if that's the killer, say so). Then, no introduction and no wrap-up: exactly five numbered items of two or three sentences each, followed by one last line telling me which of the five I should be praying nobody asks, how confident you are in your read of this room, and which part of your own answer you're least sure of.
