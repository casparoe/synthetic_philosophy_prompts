Sorting out a handout for my grad seminar next week on the reference class problem, and what I really want is a decision tree that drops each student into a named position on how to pick the class — built up from the logical space rather than from a list of names, and with every node phrased so a student can answer it without guessing which leaf it's steering them toward.

Hang the whole thing on one running case: a court has to estimate the chance that a particular claimant would have gone on to develop a condition. She's 44, left-handed, a former competitive swimmer, a non-smoker, lives in one smallish town — keep conjoining properties and her class shrinks to a single member, namely her, while the only frequency data anyone actually has sit at the coarser levels. Nodes I'd like covered, though feel free to reorder or add: whether the probability of a single case is well defined at all; whether you refine until the statistics run out or until nothing further screens off the outcome; whether over-specifying can itself be the mistake; whether the right class depends on what question you're being asked; and whether the puzzle just dissolves once you conditionalise on your total evidence.

Leaves should each carry a name — the Reichenbachian narrowest-class-with-reliable-statistics rule, the homogeneity/maximal specificity view, the contextualist, the deflationist who thinks single-case probability needs no class at all, the subjectivist dissolution — and label anything genuinely unoccupied as unnamed. Then tell me which combinations of answers are empty and why (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored), and flag any two leaves my tree can't actually seperate. British English throughout, please.
