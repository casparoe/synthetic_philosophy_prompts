I'm a third-year in phil of mind and I'm giving a departmental talk next month where I want to use the Blake Lemoine / LaMDA business from summer 2022 as a case study for Dennett's counterfeit people worry, but the longer I sit with it the less sure I am the episode actually fits. Facts as I have them: Lemoine was on the responsible-AI side at Google, chatting with LaMDA as part of the job, concluded in the spring of 2022 that it was sentient, took the claim to two executives, got put on paid leave after the Washington Post story ran in June, and was fired in July for violating confidentiality. He released edited transcripts, told Wired the model was a person in the sense the Thirteenth Amendment would care about, and apparently hired a lawyer on LaMDA's behalf because it asked him to. Google said the sentience claim was completely unfounded and that they had plenty of evidence it wasn't sentient.

So: run the episode through the counterfeit people lens, and then tell me where the lens cracks.

Start with the mapping, because I dont think it's as clean as it looks. Who's the counterfeiter — Google, Lemoine, or the edited transcripts themselves? Is LaMDA the counterfeit, or is the counterfeit the narrative built around it, given that the model was never released and almost nobody had actually talked to it? And here's the bit that bugs me: the standard worry is about mass uptake, millions of people unable to tell the difference, and that's exactly what didn't happen. The public and pretty much every AI researcher reacted with eye-rolling, while the one person who got taken in was the in-house expert with the raw transcripts and the most reason to know better. Does the counterfeit people story have any resources for that asymmetry, or does it need a bolted-on extra? I've been fiddling with a distinction that might do the work — call it artifact-side counterfeiting, where the thing is built to solicit person-directed responses, versus uptake-side counterfeiting, where those responses get triggered by something that wasn't built for it and isn't especially good at it. Tell me whether that's a real distinction or just a redescription of the same thing twice.

Then the intentional stance question, which is the one I most need to get right. For Dennett, taking the intentional stance toward something is often just the correct predictive strategy, and the system has beliefs pretty much to the extent the stance pays off. So was Lemoine's attribution a misuse of the stance, or was he doing exactly what the stance tells you to do and getting punished for it? If it's the latter I worry the counterfeit people stuff sits badly with the rest of Dennett's view, since it treats being taken in as the failure rather than as the stance doing its job. And there's the moral twist: his error, if it was one, wasn't mainly predictive — his forecasts about what LaMDA would say were presumably fine — it was about status, about whether we owe the thing something. Is that still the same worry, or a different one that the counterfeit framing quietly bundles with the epistemic one?

Finish by saying what you think the episode does to the concept. My hunch is it's a case the framework wasn't built for, because in 2022 the artifact wasn't yet counterfeit enough, and the actual damage ran through one over-committed interpreter rather than through anything like a market in fake persons. If you agree, say what the framework would have to become to cover cases like this; if you don't, say what I'm missing. Please also flag anywhere above that I've got the episode or the Dennett wrong — I'd much rather hear it from you now than in the Q&A.
