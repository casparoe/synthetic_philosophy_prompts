I am a doctoral candidate in economic history, currently drafting the methodological chapter of my dissertation on the Malthusian debate, and before I circulate it to my committee I would like a consistency audit of the position I have actually arrived at. I have tried to state my commitments plainly below, including several that I suspect sit awkwardly together without being able to locate the awkwardness. I am deliberately not telling you which of them I would be prepared to abandon, because I do not yet know, and I would prefer the audit to settle that rather than my instincts.

1. I hold that for essentially the whole of agrarian history the Malthusian framework was correct in its central claim: real income per head was confined to a narrow band because population adjusted to income, so that improvements in technique registered themselves as more people rather than as richer people.

2. I also hold, on the evidence for northwestern Europe, that most of this adjustment was accomplished by the preventive check — age at first marriage moving with wages and with the availability of a household to support — rather than by famine and epidemic mortality. The feedback was real, but the misery-heavy version of it is not what the pre-industrial English record shows.

3. I hold that the escape from this regime beginning around the turn of the nineteenth century was a structural break and not a change of parameter values: a model that reproduces sustained growth in income per head is a different model, not the Malthusian model with different coefficients.

4. I take the decisive datum to be the reversal in the sign of the relationship between income and fertility during the demographic transition — richer people having fewer children — which is not an attenuation of the Malthusian feedback but its inversion.

5. Methodologically I am strict about rescue. Where a theory is saved from a failed prediction by an auxiliary that can be set so as to accommodate either outcome, I think the theory has lost content and should be said to have lost it. I apply this criticism to Malthus himself: in adding moral restraint to vice and misery as a third check in the greatly expanded second edition of the Essay in 1803, after critics had objected that the first edition's implied inevitability of misery was neither demonstrated nor desirable, he rendered the theory compatible with rising wages and virtue as readily as with falling wages and immiseration.

6. Notwithstanding (5), I want to resist the standard objection that two centuries of sustained growth in income per head have falsified Malthusianism. My defence is that Malthus identified a mechanism that was genuinely operative under one set of conditions and ceased to be operative under another, and that it is legitimate to say of a theory that it was right about its own regime: the honest charge against him is that he was right but bounded, not that he was wrong.

7. I also think Malthusianism is owed some credit for longevity. A quantitative claim about the future that withstands scrutiny for two centuries has, on my view, been tested more severely than one overturned in twenty years, and that counts in the theory's favour.

8. At the same time, when Marxists say that the theory of crisis was correct and only its timing was off, or when those who forecast resource exhaustion in the 1970s say that the mechanism was right and the date was wrong, I am inclined to dismiss the move as an immunising stratagem rather than accept it as a vindication.

9. Finally, I do not think the line between a legitimate restriction of a theory's domain and an ad hoc rescue can be drawn by asking whether the theorist was sincere or acted in good faith. A restriction offered with complete honesty can still be ad hoc, and I want an account of the difference that does not turn on intentions.

What I want back is an audit rather than a survey. Identify which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another and which merely appear to, and order the genuine tensions by severity rather than presenting them as a flat list in which everything is equally bad. For each genuine tension, state what would have to be given up or restated and what the loss would consist in — the smallest available revision, not the most thorough one. Where a tension is only apparent, say so explicitly and say why it dissolves, because I need to know which of my worries are unfounded. Then tell me which single revision would repair the most damage at the least cost, and what I would no longer be entitled to claim once I had made it.

Please assume I know both the Malthusian argument and the history of the demographic transition, and do not recapitulate either. If the set is fundamentally sound with one soft spot, say so; if it is not, say that instead.
