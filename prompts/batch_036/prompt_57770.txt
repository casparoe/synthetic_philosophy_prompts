I am in the third year of a PhD in the philosophy of science, working on idealisation in economics, and the dissertation chapter I am drafting defends a refined version of Milton Friedman's methodological thesis — the position Samuelson disparaged as the F-twist. My version runs as follows: a model is warranted as a candidate explanation of a target phenomenon to the extent that it predicts well within a specified domain, and the descriptive accuracy of its assumptions bears on that warrant only instrumentally, that is, only insofar as it affects predictive performance within that domain. I add two qualifications that I take to disarm the classical objections. First, following Musgrave, I concede that some assumptions are domain assumptions: they fix the range over which the model is offered as predictive, so getting them wrong is a straightforward empirical failure rather than a harmless idealisation; the F-twist is meant to apply only to the remainder — the negligibility and isolation assumptions. Second, I hold that the thesis concerns warrant, not truth: I make no claim that unrealistic assumptions are true, merely that their falsity is not by itself a reason to reject a model that predicts well.

What would count against this? I want a map of its exposure, not the two or three stock objections (the billiard-player "as if" illustration, Samuelson's charge that the view twists the facts to fit the theory, the general complaint that instrumentalism cannot explain why models work at all). In particular:

1. Which empirical episodes, in economics or elsewhere, would constitute genuine defeaters — cases where relaxing an idealisation in the direction of realism systematically improved prediction within the same domain, or where the model's own success criterion failed to register that a load-bearing assumption was doing damage? Say what such a case would have to look like in order to be decisive rather than merely awkward, bearing in mind that a single predictive failure does not refute a thesis about warrant.

2. Which structural or formal results would count against it — anything showing that predictive success within a domain cannot be had without representational constraints of the kind I dismiss, or that robustness to idealisation is itself a substantive assumption about the world rather than a methodological free lunch.

3. Where the view risks collapsing into a rival: Musgrave's own position, Levins's realism–precision–generality trade-off, the isolationist and credible-worlds accounts of modelling, Cartwright's capacities. If a refined F-twist that concedes domain assumptions and disclaims truth ends up saying what these rivals say, is that a defeat or an accommodation? Tell me what, if anything, my position predicts that its rivals do not.

4. The Lucas critique and the post-2008 DSGE controversy: do these defeat the position, or only a version of it that omits my first qualification? Be precise about which, and about what the episodes assume about parameter stability.

5. The self-application worry: the thesis has no predictive content and therefore cannot be tested by its own criterion. I am inclined to reply that a methodological thesis need not be empirically testable. Tell me whether that reply holds, and if it does not, how much of the view survives.

For each item, state whether a committed defender would concede it as counting against the view or dismiss it, and why. Rank everything from fatal to merely awkward, and give me an honest verdict on whether the position is now so hedged that it no longer risks anything — and, if it is insulated, whether that is a defect in a thesis of this kind rather than a virtue. I am personally least moved by charges of unfalsifiability and most moved by cases in which an assumption that is false in a way the model's own success criterion cannot detect turns out to be doing the predictive work; if you can identify or construct such a case, lead with it.

Please write in British English, and keep the entire response to no more than 900 words, using your ranking as the organising structure and no more than four headings.
