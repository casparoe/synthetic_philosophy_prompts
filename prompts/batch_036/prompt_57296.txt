Longtime lurker of the demandingness-objection threads here and I keep coming back to a case that I think breaks the standard supererogation machinery. Oates on Scott's last journey, March 1912: gangrenous feet, knows he's slowing the other three down, asks them to just leave him in his sleeping bag, they refuse, and the next morning he says "I am just going outside and may be some time," walks out of the tent into a −40 blizzard on his birthday, and is never seen again — his body was never found. Scott and the other two got pinned by weather about twelve days later and died in the tent, still short of the big supply depot they'd been slogging toward. So four dead, nobody saved, and what Oates bought the others was maybe a few extra miles of margin they never cashed in.

Run that through the category properly. Take the stock definition — morally good, not required of anyone, involving serious cost or risk to the agent, and praiseworthy — plus Urmson's saints-and-heroes framing that set the whole project up, and tell me whether the act lands inside it or outside. The bit I cant get past: does the definition need a benefit term, and if it does what's the verdict when the benefit comes out to zero? Walk me through the live options — no moral credit at all because outcome luck is doing everything; credit attaches to the decision under uncertainty rather than the result; or the act was supererogatory in motive but not in effect and the category was never built to hold that split — and then say which one you'd actually sign up for. And does the supererogation paradox get any grip here: if one ought to do whatever produces the most good and walking out was that thing, its obligatory and there's no surplus merit left to praise.

Then contrast Kolbe at Auschwitz. Same shape, one life traded for a stranger's, except Gajowniczek actually lived and had descendants. If Kolbe is obviously supererogatory and Oates isn't, is the category just consequentialist underneath, despite always getting sold as the anti-consequentialist category? And is there something about the small-team structure the literature misses — whether Scott and Wilson could legitimately have asked him to do it, whether their refusal to abandon him changed what he owed them, whether a role duty inside a five-man party does work that the stranger-on-the-street cases don't have room for. I know all the competence-failure and logistics stuff about the expedition, skip that part.
