I'm on the committee for a cluster of allotments on the edge of town — about 90 plots, mostly retired people plus a few of us in our thirties who clearly don't know what we're doing — and we've been told to produce a written pest policy. Sounds trivial until you try. Slugs taking everything, aphids on the beans, vine weevil, pigeons, rats around the compost heaps, and the question of whether anyone's allowed to use a systemic insecticide given what we all half-understand about pollinators, or a rodenticide given what happens to the owls and hedgehogs down the food chain. Every draft I write collapses into either "spray whatever works" or "don't touch anything", and both feel like they're ducking the real question. So I've gone down a rabbit hole on the ethics of pest control and insecticides, and what strikes me is that people mostly argue case by case — this pesticide, that invasive species, this mosquito programme — instead of saying what a framework would actually have to be able to do before it gets to weigh in on any case.

So: before I read another word, write the desiderata. I want the specification for an adequate ethical framework governing pest control and insecticide use — every requirement it has to meet, with the case or consideration behind each one. Here's some of what I suspect belongs on the list; use these, improve them, or tell me I've got them wrong. It has to handle the fact that a control decision usually involves three parties rather than two: the pest, whatever we're protecting, and the non-target bystanders who get hit by the spray or by the knock-on through the food web. It has to say whether "pest" is a status a creature can have independently of the systems we built — monocultures, cities with endless waste streams, imported ornamentals — or whether judging an intervention without judging the regime that manufactures pests in the first place is a category error. It has to cope with numbers, since the individuals at stake are so vast that any aggregating view seems to swamp everything else in the picture. It has to separate interventions you can switch off from ones you can't, like releasing a parasitoid that establishes itself and can never be recalled. It has to handle the difference between killing animals and merely failing to prevent them being eaten alive, parasitised, or starved, and say whether vector-borne disease control is a genuinely distinct category or just the same problem with louder stakes. And it has to be usable by someone standing in a garden at nine at night with a slug problem, not only by a ministry — if the framework's verdicts are unactionable for the people who have to act, I want that on the list as a failure.

For each desideratum, say whether it's non-negotiable or whether it's really one camp's prejudice dressed up as a neutral criterion — my strong suspicion is that a lot of what gets insisted on here is just welfarism or ecocentrism smuggling itself in. Then do the part I most want: identify any subset that can't be jointly satisfied. My guess is that taking invertebrate moral status seriously at any non-negligible probability, permitting aggregation across the enormous numbers involved, and still vindicating ordinary food-growing pest control are inconsistent with one another, but argue it rather than agreeing with me, and if I'm wrong tell me which one should go and why. Say which single requirement does the most excluding work. Finally, and only briefly — half a page at most, I care about the criteria not the scoreboard — how a welfarist or animal-liberation view, an ecocentric or land-ethic view, and a plain food-security-and-public-health view come out against the list. No citations or reading lists please, I can't chase them up and wouldn't anyway. Numbered list or table is fine. British English, and while you're at it: the UK's animal sentience legislation already covers crabs and octopuses but has nothing at all to say about aphids, so if that asymmetry is a datum your framework has to explain, or explain away, say which of the two it is.
