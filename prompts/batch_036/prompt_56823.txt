I teach a fourth-year seminar on constructive mathematics, and next month our department is holding a public evening to launch it — an invited lecture, a wine reception afterwards, an audience of perhaps eighty: mostly mathematicians, a scattering of philosophers, a few members of the public who like being baffled. I have been asked to read something short before the lecture. I want to commission a pantoum of five quatrains, and I want it to do a piece of work that no amount of explanation from the podium will manage.

The work is this. The complaint I hear most often from colleagues — and the one I most want the poem to disarm — is that intuitionistic logic is simply classical logic with the law of excluded middle struck out: a subtraction, an austerity, a refusal. What the objection misses is that on the constructive reading a negation is not the absence of a proof but a positive piece of machinery. To prove ¬A is to possess a construction that would take any hypothetical proof of A and convert it into absurdity. Absence has content; it is a function, not a gap.

I want the poem built on the semantic clause rather than the slogan. In the information-state picture — states partially ordered by extension, forcing hereditary, so that whatever holds at a state holds at every state extending it — the clause for negation is that a state forces ¬A exactly when no state extending it forces A. Note the asymmetry, which is the whole engine of the poem: positive truth is local and now, a thing you can point to at the state you are standing on, while negation is universal and forward-looking, a claim about every possible future growth of the state. That is precisely why ¬¬A does not collapse into A. The double negation gives you a guarantee about all your futures; it does not give you a proof to hold at the present one. If you want a fixed point to anchor the piece, it is that the double negation of excluded middle is itself intuitionistically provable even though excluded middle is not — and, worth knowing so that the poem does not overreach, that at the propositional level every classically valid formula becomes intuitionistically provable once wrapped in a double negation, while this tidy embedding stops behaving once quantifiers are involved.

My own proposal for the mapping — adopt it, improve on it, or discard it, but tell me which you did — is that the pantoum's return rule is the hereditary condition wearing ordinary clothes. Once a line has been established it must be inherited, word for word, by every later stanza, and nothing in the form permits a retraction. Construction happens at positions one and three of each stanza, where new lines are made; positions two and four are inheritance, and the line a stanza constructs at one and three becomes, in the next stanza, something it did not choose and cannot alter. The final stanza should then return the poem's own first line as its second line and its second line as its fourth, and the effect I am after is enclosure without completion: the form closes on itself and yet there is still something it has not delivered, and that residue should be legible as exactly the distance between ¬¬A and A. If the last stanza lands as a resolution, a consolation, or a solution to a riddle, the poem has failed.

Three further requirements of content. The speaker must be one voice at one state — not a dialogue, not two positions trading stanzas; the interest is in what a single growing present can and cannot say about itself. The imagery should make the forward-looking character of negation concrete and particular: things not yet built, a room not yet entered, a debt entered in a ledger but not yet paid, a sentence whose verification has not arrived — whatever you find, so long as it belongs to a present that is growing rather than to a space of finished alternatives. And the poem must never explain itself. No line should read as a gloss on the semantics; the reader should come away feeling why the double negation does not collapse, from the shape of what was said, not from being told.

It has to work as verse first. I want it audible in a hall: loose iambic tetrameter, with the usual licence for a substituted first foot or a feminine ending, but the returning lines must scan cleanly on every appearance, and each quatrain must stand up as a stanza in its own right rather than as four lines of a mechanism. A reader who knows nothing of the subject should find the poem strange and exact; a logician in the third row should find nothing to wince at.

Constraints, which I will check:

1. In each stanza after the first, line 2 must be the previous stanza's line 1 and line 4 must be the previous stanza's line 3, each reproduced word for word. Punctuation and capitalisation may shift; wording may not. Unlike a sestina, which lets its end-words change grammatical company, nothing here may be inflected or recontextualised at the level of the words themselves.
2. Within each quatrain, line 1 rhymes with line 3 and line 2 with line 4. Prefer full rhymes; do not let a word serve as its own rhyme. The inherited lines carry their rhymes forward, so the burden falls on the constructed positions.
3. Five quatrains: twenty line-slots filled by twelve distinct lines, twelve of them appearing twice and the final stanza's line 3 appearing once, alone, as the one thing in the poem that is never inherited and never returns.
4. The second and fourth lines of the final stanza are, verbatim and in that order, the first and second lines of the opening stanza.
5. Name no mathematician, no logician, and no theorem; quote nothing. The poem should carry no trace of the apparatus, only its shape.
6. Do not assert or imply that ¬¬A entails A, that a proof of ¬A is merely the lack of a proof of A, or that intuitionism adds a third truth value. If a line tempts you toward one of these for the sake of a rhyme, lose the rhyme.

Treat yourself as both a poet fluent in received forms and a logician who could teach this semantics without notes. Work the whole thing out line by line — the twelve distinct lines, the ledger of which clause each carries, the rhyme pairs — before committing to a first line, and then discard the exercise and write the poem. Do not show me the working.

Begin your response with the pantoum itself, under a short title, with no preface, no scene-setting, and no account of your intentions beforehand. After the poem, and only after it, supply the checking apparatus: a ledger of the twenty slots, given by stanza and position, each carrying its line verbatim and the semantic role it is doing; a verification of constraints 1 and 3 stated explicitly; and then a note of no more than a hundred and fifty words identifying the single clause on which the poem most depends, admitting candidly wherever you bent the logic to save the verse, and naming the line you would surrender first if I asked you to trade some of the machinery for some of the music.
