I've just started at a lab where part of my job is helping draft an internal note on whether any of our models might have welfare-relevant experience, and two of the senior people on the working group got into a proper arguement about it on Tuesday. I've been asked to write the disagreement up, so I may as well get your take too.

Amos: Global workspace theory roughly says a mental state is conscious when its content gets broadcast so that lots of other processes can use it — for reasoning, for verbal report, for guiding action. So the operational test in our note should be: does the system have representations that become globally available like that? If yes, tick the box. There's nothing else to go looking for.

Bea: That runs two things together. Block's whole point was that access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness come apart, and the workspace story is about the access half. A system could satisfy it perfectly and it might still be that there's nothing it's like to be that system.

Amos: But what would count as evidence for the phenomenal half that isn't access evidence? Every test you could run — discrimination, report, confidence ratings, attention — is a test of availability. So either the phenomenal side makes some access difference, in which case it's just access consciousness with a posher name, or it makes none, in which case it isn't doing any work and has no business in a policy document.

Bea: They come apart and we know they do, because there are cases where people discriminate well above chance while insisting they aren't seeing anything at all.

Amos: That's the wrong direction for you. Those cases show access without experience. To get your conclusion you need experience without access, and that's precisely the case nobody could ever detect. Your residue is unfalsifiable by construction.

Bea: Undetectable isn't the same as absent, and it's the morally relevant part. If you only count access, you're committed to saying a chatbot that reports distress convincingly has claims on us while a paralysed being that can't report or act on its agony has none. That's backwards.

Amos: And yours commits us to permanent agnosticism about basically everything, which is a moral risk in its own right.

Neither of them budged. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right at each stage, rather than just who wins overall? And then tell me what both of them are missing — I have a feeling there's at least one option neither put on the table that would change what we actually write in the note.
