I am drafting a paper whose centrepiece is a case that neither party to the Malcolm–Dennett debate about dreams ever addressed, and before I commit to it I want to check whether I have the logic of the two positions right and where you think each of them would land. Please respond in British English throughout.

**The background as I take it.** Malcolm's *Dreaming* (1959) argues that a dream is not an experience: when someone says they dreamt such-and-such, the report concerns impressions the subject has on waking rather than an episode that unfolded during sleep, and there is no criterion by which anyone — the dreamer included — could distinguish a genuine nocturnal experience from a mere waking impression, hence nothing for a dream-report to be getting right or wrong. Dennett accepts the negative conclusion but reaches it from the multiple-drafts model and the rejection of a Cartesian theatre: the content of a dream is never determinately *there* at any time during sleep, and its apparent recovery on waking is construction — possibly of the rewriting-after-the-fact kind — rather than retrieval.

**The recorded verdicts.** Here is how I read each of them on a run of cases. If any ascription below is wrong, or too crude to bear the weight I am putting on it, say so at the outset, substitute the corrected verdict, and carry on with that; I would rather the list be right than have my question answered on my own terms.

1. Sleeper woken from REM giving a long, detailed report. Malcolm: no experience occurred; the report is of impressions on waking. Dennett: same conclusion, the report being the first determinate fixing of content.
2. Sleeper woken from non-REM giving a short, thought-like fragment. Both: no difference in kind; REM has no special privilege here.
3. A dream dreamt and wholly forgotten, with no residual impression. Malcolm: no nocturnal event is needed to make sense of this, since the grammar of *dream* does not presuppose an experience. Dennett: unproblematic — nothing was ever determinate enough to be forgotten.
4. A report matching a story the subject read before bed. Malcolm: no different in kind; the reading supplies material for the waking impression.
5. Repeated awakenings across one night, reports of the same dream growing steadily richer. Malcolm: elaboration, not the recovery of a stored episode. Dennett: exactly what rewriting predicts.
6. A waking daydream or an after-image. Malcolm: these are experiences properly so called, because a contemporaneous criterion exists — the subject can attend to the item while it lasts. Dennett: even here, an experience is not something presented at a moment to an inner audience.
7. A physiologist offers an imaging signature of visual mentation during REM as evidence that an experience occurred. Malcolm: a signature cannot do the work of a criterion; at best it correlates with a disposition to report. Dennett: the finding concerns processes, not any moment of presentation, and leaves the question where it was.

**The new case.** Take the lucid-dreaming signal paradigm — the Stanford work from the late 1970s onwards, in which a sleeper in verified REM executes pre-arranged eye movements to show that they know they are dreaming — and extend it. Before sleep, S agrees two signals: one left–right–left eye movement for "I know I am dreaming now", and two such patterns for "I have just heard my own name spoken in the room". During the night S is in verified REM; the experimenters play S's name once, quietly, over a speaker; eight seconds later the polygraph trace shows the two-signal pattern; nothing further; S is woken four minutes later and reports a dream whose content includes hearing someone call their name. The stimulus, the response and their interval are all publicly recorded while S is still asleep.

**What I want from you.**

(a) For each theorist, set out the candidate rules that accommodate verdicts 1–7 equally well yet come apart on the new case. For Malcolm I have in mind at least an impressions-on-waking rule, on which nothing occurring during sleep can be an experience whatever it is correlated with, and a no-criterion rule, on which the disqualifying feature is specifically the absence of any independent, contemporaneous test of whether given content was present to the dreamer. For Dennett, a rule denying determinate content and a rule denying only determinate timing. Refine, replace or add to these as you see fit: what matters is that the rules be genuinely underdetermined by 1–7 and that they diverge on the new case.

(b) Give the predicted verdict on the new case under each rule, then your best single guess for each theorist with your reasons — telling me how much weight you place on the signal being behavioural rather than phenomenal, and on the content being fixed by an experimental agreement rather than by S's recollection. Where you are genuinely torn, say so, and say what would tip you.

(c) State what each theorist would surrender by going the other way: which principle they would be adopting in conceding, and which of their other commitments that concession would strain.

(d) Identify one further case — something a sleep laboratory could actually run, or a thought experiment of the same structure — that would discriminate between the rules you have isolated, and say which result favours which rule. I want the sharpest single case you can find, not a menu.

(e) Assess my own instinct: that the signal paradigm is standardly invoked as though it refuted Malcolm, when it may leave his central argument untouched and instead embarrass Dennett, who owes an explanation of why a publicly timed, stimulus-locked response should count for less than a private report. If you disagree, locate the disagreement precisely.

No references, bibliography, page numbers or publication details, please: reason from the arguments as you understand them, and mark clearly wherever you are reconstructing a position rather than reporting one.
