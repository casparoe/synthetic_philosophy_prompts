Three tickets landed on my desk this week and they're all the same ticket wearing different clothes: somebody wants a new category node whose name is a compound. Grade 8.8 hex bolt. Stainless hex bolt. Yellow zinc bolt.

I run the catalog and search taxonomy at a mid-size industrial fastener distributor — about 45k SKUs, mostly bolts, nuts, washers and anchors plus some tooling. We have a category tree three levels deep, and attribute facets layered on it: material, finish, thread size, length, drive type, standard, property class. Search resolves to a tree node first and then filters by facet. The attributes exist but theyre populated inconsistently; the good suppliers send full spec sheets and the cheap ones send a part number and a shrug.

I get maybe a dozen of these tickets a week from sales reps and category managers, and I have to say yes or no to each one. If I say no and they were right, customers cant find things and the reps route around the catalog entirely by emailing spreadsheets to each other, which is worse than the problem. If I say yes too often, in two years we have four thousand leaf nodes, every modifier combination has its own home, and a search for plain hex bolt no longer returns most of the hex bolts we actually sell. Two junior catalogers screen the tickets before they reach me. They're sharp but they've never studied taxonomy or philosophy and they are not going to read a theory, so whatever you give me has to run in about 15 minutes a ticket, in plain English, and the only sign-off gate is me.

Here's the part that'll sound strange. I've been reading the White Horse dialogue in the Gongsun Longzi — the School of Names one where a speaker argues that a white horse is not a horse — and I think it's genuinely about my problem and not just an analogy I'm forcing onto it. As I understand it the argument runs: horse is the name for the shape, white is the name for the color, and what names a color isn't what names a shape. Then the move I care about: if you ask for a horse, a yellow one or a black one can be delivered, but if you ask for a white horse, a yellow or black one cannot be delivered — so the two names are not interchangeable in a request even though every white horse is a horse. There's also the hardness-and-whiteness dialogue, where the stone gives you its whiteness by eye and its hardness by hand and each sense misses what the other picks up, so the two properties come apart and each one drops out of view when you're not attending to it. And the name-and-actuality chapter, where every thing is supposed to sit in its proper position, a dislocated name is what generates the confusion, and rectifying a name means putting it back in the position that matches its actuality.

If you think I've mangled any of that, tell me — I'm working from memory of translations I read a while ago and I don't want to build a company procedure on a misreading. But please don't hand me citations or a reading list, I have no way to chase them and I'd much rather have your reasoning than your references.

So: turn that into a decision procedure. What I need is

- the questions to ask, in the order a cataloger should ask them;
- what counts as a sufficient answer to each one — not "consider whether X" but what in the ticket, the SKU record, or the supplier's paperwork actually settles it;
- where the procedure terminates in a NO (this is a filter, not a category) and the sentence the cataloger writes back to the rep;
- where it terminates in a YES and gets escalated to me, and what has to be in the note so I can sign it off without redoing the analysis;
- where it stops being a factual question and hands the call to a human because it's a tradeoff — catalog bloat versus customer friction, or liability, or a commercial bet — and how the cataloger should frame that so whoever decides has what they need;
- how it handles the cases that usually break procedures like this: a modifier that's physically invisible on the part and only knowable from a mill certificate or a head marking, a modifier that means different things to different suppliers, and a modifier that's really about the buyer's use or regulatory regime rather than about the item at all.

Two of my own hunches you should test rather than accept. One: I think the whole question reduces to a warehouse-floor test — would the picker be allowed to substitute a thing that lacks the modifier, and would anyone downstream be harmed if they did. If substitution is fine it's a facet, if substitution is a breach it's a kind. The other: I think a lot of these tickets are actually complaints that an attribute value is sitting in our data but nothing surfaces it, so the property is there but hidden, and the rep asks for a node because a node is the only thing they know how to ask for. If that's right the procedure should be able to detect it and send the ticket to a data-quality fix instead of a taxonomy change.

Be honest about which of the four ideas above actually carries weight here and which is decoration I should drop. I suspect at least one of them is just pretty and won't survive contact with a fastener database, and I'd rather you say which one than quietly build the procedure on the three that work.

Then run the procedure end to end on the three real tickets:

1. Category manager wants grade 8.8 hex bolt as its own node under hex bolts. Property class is invisible on the part, it comes off a mill cert or a head stamp, and if a rep swaps a 4.8 into a customer's structural joint we are having a very bad conversation with our insurer.
2. A rep with three large accounts wants stainless hex bolt as a node. Material is already a facet and populated for maybe 80% of SKUs. He says customers type the compound as one phrase and get junk back.
3. Yellow zinc bolt. Half our suppliers mean a yellow chromate, a third mean a clear trivalent passivate that looks faintly yellowish under warehouse lights, and one means a coating that is literally dyed yellow.

At least one of these should come out in a way that makes the requester furious. If all three come out yes, you haven't written a procedure, you've written a rubber stamp.

Also say plainly what the procedure can't settle, and where you'd expect it to misfire most often.

Before you write any of it, spend a short section brainstorming out loud: which of those ideas does the load-bearing work, what would have to be true about our data for each to be usable, and what's the cheapest version of the test that still tells you something. Think it through rather than pattern-matching this to a generic product-taxonomy blog post. Write as someone who has done both halves of this — a philosopher of language who has also had to build and maintain a live product ontology and got blamed when it broke.

Last thing, and it matters to me: end with a section on what would change your mind. Which argument or which piece of evidence would make you say the white-horse framing is the wrong frame for this decision and I should be running something else entirely? And inside the procedure you give me, which single step is most likely to fail in practice, what would that failure look like in our ticket queue, and how would I notice it before a customer notices it?
