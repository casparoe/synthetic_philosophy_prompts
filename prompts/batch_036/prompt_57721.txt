I'm prepping a session on the letter-spirit problem — what makes every 'a' an 'a' accross typefaces that barely share a stroke — and I want a thought experiment built to order rather than the usual wave at family resemblance.

What the case is for: I want to find out whether the relational account of letter identity is a genuine third position or just the style account wearing a disguise. So the case has to be able to drive them apart, and it can't do that by fiat — if the verdicts are sitting in my description rather than falling out of the accounts, I've got nothing.

Setup, to be written up as a single realistic paragraph — a working British type foundry, no sci-fi, no memory-wiping: the foundry is drawing a new text face, a revival with a strong house style (high stroke contrast, wedge-shaped terminals, a small closed 'e'). Two candidate lowercase 'a's have been drawn, both competent, neither a dud.

- The first is the traditional double-storey 'a' — closed upper bowl, curved lower bowl, the skeleton you meet in almost all print — but drawn against the house style: even-weight strokes, a wide open aperture (the gap where a stroke doesn't close back on itself), bluntly cut terminals. On its own it reads instantly. Set in a line of the new face it looks like it fell out of a geometric sans.
- The second is a single-storey 'a', the form most of us were taught to write by hand, but built out of the house's own parts: same contrast axis, same wedge terminals, bowl struck from the same circle as the 'o', tail cut like the 'r's. In a line it's seamless. Shown on its own to someone who's only ever met print forms, it makes them hesitate.

Make the case force three accounts apart: (a) a prototype/template account, on which the 'a' is whatever matches a stored exemplar or a checklist of diagnostic features; (b) a style-or-spirit account, on which letter identity is downstream of the manner the face is drawn in; (c) a differential account, on which the 'a' is whatever occupies the right role in a system of contrasts, so its identity depends on the neighbouring letters and on what else the system has to keep apart.

Constraints: neither candidate may be the simply-bad drawing, since that turns the exercise into a test of quality rather than of identity; and no appeal to how readers happen to have been trained, because that's the question, not a premise. Both glyphs must be defensibly in on at least one account and defensibly out on another.

Then the bit I actually care about. Get (b) and (c) to agree on the first pass, and then add one stipulated change that touches neither the glyphs' own features nor the house style — only what else the letter has to sit among. My suggestion, though you can find a better one: the family will also ship a companion italic whose 'a' is single-storey, as italics usually are, and that italic is what marks emphasis against the roman. Show that the flip moves (c)'s verdict while leaving (a) and (b) exactly where they were, and say plainly what the variable is and why the other two accounts are structurally blind to it.

Finish with the honest part: which feature of the case is doing the work, what the three verdicts look like if I take that feature back out, and how a defender of whichever account comes out worst would attack the case — ideally by saying I've staged a row about type design and mislabelled it a row about letter identity.

Audience is a mixed room of design and philosophy students, so gloss any anatomy term in half a clause, and keep the whole thing to about 700 words all tolled.
