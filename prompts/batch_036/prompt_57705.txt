I retired three years ago after a long career in molecular biology, most of it spent on the biochemistry of infectious disease, and I have since taken up philosophy with more seriousness than I expected of myself. I am in a monthly reading group of former academics, and last week one of our members circulated an essay from a general-audience science magazine on deep disagreement. Its central paragraphs run as follows:

'Wittgenstein spent the last months of his life arguing with G. E. Moore, who had claimed to know such things as that here is a hand and that the Earth existed long before his birth. Wittgenstein's reply, worked out in the notes published after his death as On Certainty, is that these propositions are not known at all. They are the hinges on which all our ordinary doubting and testing turn: that the Earth is very old, that things do not cease to exist when nobody is looking, that our memories are broadly reliable. A hinge proposition cannot be proved or disproved, because any test we might bring against it already assumes it; and it cannot genuinely be doubted either, since a doubt that lacks grounds is not a doubt but a pose. Hinges are therefore not beliefs in the ordinary sense — nobody holds them on evidence, and most of us never formulate them — but they are beliefs we are entitled to hold without evidence, and it would be irrational to surrender them, since without them no inquiry could get started. A community's hinges function much like its paradigm: adopted because they pay off, and abandoned when a rival framework proves more fruitful. The consequence is that the whole edifice of knowledge rests on something that is not knowledge but ungrounded certainty. This is why the deepest disputes — over religion, over what counts as evidence at all — cannot be settled by argument. Two people with different hinges have no common ground to stand on; each can only attempt to convert the other. Wittgenstein accepts as much: he observes that when reasons give out, persuasion remains, and he pictures this by way of missionaries bringing people round to a different view of the world. The difference between a scientific and a superstitious culture is therefore not that one has better reasons — on both sides the reasons run out at the same depth — but that they play different games.'

I found this stimulating and, on reflection, suspicious, though I cannot yet say precisely where. I should like a careful audit rather than a defence of the essay or of my doubts. Going through it claim by claim: which parts would a serious reader of those notes endorse as they stand; which are harmless simplifications; which are subtly wrong in a way that matters; and which are simply not Wittgenstein's view at all. Two things I want pressed in particular. First, the essay says hinges are not beliefs, then says we are entitled to hold them and would be irrational to give them up — can both stand, or has the author quietly turned something arational into something justified? Second, the picture of a framework as a thing a community takes up because it pays off and drops for something that pays better feels to me like the philosophy of science I actually absorbed as a graduate student rather than anything in those notes; is my nose right, and what is lost by running the two together? Finally, does the relativist conclusion survive the corrections: if the author is wrong about the fixity of hinges, is there anything left to support the claim that two people with different hinges have no common ground, and is the missionary remark really a concession of that thesis or something else?

Then I have a case of my own that I would like you to turn on the essay, because it is the reason I did not simply put the piece down. For many years in my field it was assumed without argument that anything infectious carries nucleic acid. When a proposal was made that an infectious agent might consist of protein alone, it was not, for a long while, treated as a hypothesis with weak evidence. Colleagues did not ask what the experiments showed against it; they asked what the proposer could possibly mean, and several of us said, in so many words, that it was unthinkable rather than improbable. It later became ordinary, testable science, and was awarded a Nobel Prize. Does that episode refute the essay's claim that hinge-level propositions cannot be revised by inquiry, or does the position have the resources to absorb it — and if so, what does the episode show about what a hinge is? During the years of dismissal, was the nucleic-acid assumption operating as a hinge in the relevant sense, or was it merely a very entrenched empirical generalisation, and is there a principled way to tell the two apart while one is inside the episode, or can the label only be applied afterwards? I should add that my own inclination is to say the episode proves the foundations of science are revisable and hence not arbitrary; I want to know whether that inclination is supported by anything in the position I have been reading, or whether I have simply attached my own conclusion to it. Please be blunt about my errors as well as the essay's. I am not looking for suggestions of further reading.
