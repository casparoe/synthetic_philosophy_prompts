I'm writing the third chapter of a thesis in philosophy of mind on self-deception, and I've come to suspect that the wall I've hit is not a wall in the subject matter but a wall in how the debate has been set up. Here is the seminar-standard picture. One camp — the intentionalist or dynamic accounts — holds that self-deception proper involves an intention to acquire a belief: the subject in some sense sets out to get herself to believe something she has reason to think is false. That camp owes an answer to the two familiar paradoxes, the static one (she cannot simultaneously believe p and fail to believe p) and the dynamic one (she cannot intend to deceive herself, since she would have to know she is being deceived in order to plan it), and the standard answer is to partition the mind so that the deceiving subsystem and the deceived subsystem do not share their contents. The other camp — the deflationary or static accounts — says no intention is needed at all: motivated attention, biased evidence gathering, selective retrieval, and a lower evidential bar for hypotheses one welcomes are ordinary, well-documented cognitive operations, and stack enough of them and you get the phenomenon without any paradox to solve. Both camps claim the same cases. Both insist that the other's paradigms are either not self-deception or are self-deception only by courtesy. Nobody has moved anyone in forty years, and the arguments on both sides are by now so well rehearsed that new papers seem to consist mainly of re-describing the old cases.

Two things from my own work make me think the impasse has a specific shape, and I'd like you to test rather than flatter the idea. For a side project I coded 41 first-person written accounts — mostly from bereavement and family-addiction support forums — looking specifically for how the writer describes coming to believe what she now thinks she did not believe. Almost no account reports forming an intention to believe anything. But a large majority reports deliberate, present-tense-at-the-time information control: not opening the statements, changing the route home, declining to ask the one question whose answer was obvious. And roughly a third closes with a retrospective claim to have known all along, sometimes within the same paragraph as an insistence that she had no idea. So the agency the narrators themselves locate is over evidence, not over belief, while the retrospective knowledge-claim is what the intentionalist most wants and what the deflationist is best equipped to explain away. Note also that both camps seem to accept a common premise — that an intention the subject cannot report is not doing any explanatory work — and then draw opposite conclusions from it, the one toward partitioning and the other toward dropping intentions entirely.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the dispute is not over whether intention is involved but over where agency has to sit for the label to apply — at the level of belief acquisition or at the level of evidence management — and each side has so thoroughly identified its own answer with the concept itself that it reads the other as denying the phenomenon rather than relocating it. Diagnose this properly. Is the disagreement verbal, turning on what 'intend' or 'belief' is stretched to cover; substantive but resting on a hidden empirical question about introspective access and retrospective report, which both sides treat as settled while disagreeing about its verdict; a difference about which cases count as paradigmatic data; a difference about method, in that one camp is doing conceptual analysis and the other inference to the best psychological explanation and neither says so; or bedrock intuition that no further argument can move? Tell me whether my locus-of-agency hypothesis survives contact with what each side actually argues, or whether you would offer a different diagnosis, and in either case say what would count as progress given the diagnosis you settle on — a distinction, a kind of case, a study design, a formal result, or a change of question — or argue that none should be expected and why. Please don't survey the literature and don't tell me what to read; assume I have read the canonical papers and am interested only in the structure of the disagreement.
