I teach a 75-minute seminar for eighteen second-year philosophy students in a module on the structure of justification. About half of them have had a single term of sentential logic; none of them has read Lewis Carroll's 1895 dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise, and I want them not to learn where the activity comes from, or what the puzzle is conventionally called, until well into the debrief.

Please design a complete, runnable in-class activity whose mechanics put the students inside the problem that dialogue stages: a reasoner who cheerfully accepts premises but refuses to draw the conclusion until the rule licensing the step is itself written down as an additional premise, and then demands a further premise covering that one, and so on without end. They should collide with the regress in their own hands, in the first half of the session, before anyone has explained anything to them.

The mechanic I would like you to build from, though you are welcome to revise it: the class divides into teams of three, each team trying to get a short derivation certified by an "Officer" — a student playing the role from a sealed instruction sheet the team cannot see — where every application of an inference rule consumes one slip from a strictly limited supply, each slip stating the rule and the warrant for it. Slips can be replenished at a desk, but only by surrendering a slip together with a written justification for why another is needed. Adjust the economy as you see fit — number of slips, whether teams compete or cooperate, what certification is worth — but keep two features: the scarcity has to bite, and the price of justifying a step must be paid in the same currency as the thing being justified.

Hard constraints. No projector, no phones, and nothing printed beyond what I can photocopy and cut the morning of. Nothing may depend on color-coding or on small type, since two students have significant visual impairments and will be working from large-print materials. The derivations themselves must be so simple that nobody is defeated by the logic: I want every ounce of pressure to come from the warrant economy rather than from symbol manipulation, and I would rather the content be trivial or mildly absurd than mathematical.

I also want a second phase in which the tables turn — students stop petitioning an Officer and instead draft their own finite acceptance policy, then apply it to another team's derivations, and discover that any policy short enough to write down either licenses steps they want to reject or refuses steps they want to accept. If you judge that phase unworkable in the time remaining, say so plainly and give me something that is workable.

Please write in American English and organize the answer in exactly these sections, in this order, with roughly the lengths indicated:

1. **What they should come away seeing** — about 150 words, before any mechanics, so I can check that the design is aimed at the right target.
2. **Materials** — about 150 words: everything to photocopy and cut, including the Officer's sealed instruction sheet reproduced in full and any large-print adaptations.
3. **Roles and rules** — about 500 words, precise enough that a colleague could run the session from the page without needing to ask me a single question.
4. **Timeline** — about 150 words, minute by minute across the seventy-five.
5. **The debrief** — about 500 words, mapping moments that will actually have occurred in the room onto the structure of the puzzle: the difference between a rule and a premise; why adding the rule as a premise changes nothing; and what the students' successful workarounds — I expect there will be several, including someone who simply starts reasoning at the Officer rather than writing for them — reveal about where the licensing actually lives.
6. **What will go wrong** — about 250 words: the three or four most likely failure modes on a first run, and what I should do in the moment for each.
7. **What the activity cannot show** — about 200 words, candidly: which features of the problem the game fails to reproduce, and therefore which parts I will have to supply myself.
8. **Settled and contested** — about 250 words. Separate clearly what is common ground among people who work on this puzzle from what remains a live dispute, and do not present any one way of blocking the regress as though it were the standard answer. If there are recognizably different families of response, name the families and say what each one costs its adherents — but keep this to what you can defend on the strength of the arguments alone. I want no references, no bibliography, and no reading list anywhere in the response.
