Weird ask, and I want you to actually write the speech, not plan it.

Some background. I've been reading rationalist blogs since roughly the Overcoming Bias days, which dates me, and for the last nine years I've done data work for a mid-size city — I sit between the analytics team and the neighborhoods, which is a thankless place to sit. Here's what happened: the city is about to deploy a system that allocates a fixed annual pot (call it $14M) for non-emergency infrastructure requests — sidewalk repair, street trees, cooling centers, bus shelters, crosswalk lighting. Residents submitted requests through a portal and at library tables. The flyer the mayor's office put out says the system "will do what the community tells it to."

Thursday night is the first session of the citizen panel that's supposed to review it, in a school gym, about 180 people: two council members who want a photo, the public works union head who's worried about his crews, a big contingent from the east side that filed something like 4,000 requests over two years and got basically nothing, maybe fifteen people who came to say the whole thing should be scrapped, and three of our own engineers who built it and are proud of it. Also, third row, an old friend of mine who used to be deep in the AI safety weeds and now consults for the vendor. He will catch every fudge.

I've been given six minutes for the opening remarks.

My problem is that the flyer's sentence is false, and not for the interesting-to-me reason. There is no such thing as what the community tells it until somebody picks a rule for combining what different people told it, and every rule picks winners. Per capita — every resident counts the same, and the east side loses because it isn't the biggest. Weighted by unmet need — canopy cover, heat, crash rates — and now we're measuring something and someone will fight the measure. One request one vote — and the organized neighborhood with a civic association and a spreadsheet wins by volume. All three sound fair. All three produce different maps. On top of that, the ethics board told me in writing that the opening remarks must not endorse any particular view of what the city should value, and thats the actual trap, because choosing how to count is not neutral either, it's just the part nobody has to defend out loud.

So: six minutes that walk back the flyer's promise without telling 180 tired people their input is meaningless, without turning into a social choice theory lecture nobody signed up for, without taking a side, and without saying anything my friend in the third row would roll his eyes at. No jargon, no Arrow name-dropping, no citations, nothing I'd have to explain afterward.

The hard constraint to write against: the speech is never allowed to say anything like "the community's values" or "what we all want" — every time it gestures at a collective, it has to expose the rule that made the collective, in plain words a parent who came straight from a shift can follow. And it has to end by handing the panel its first real job, stated concretely enough that they can start on it the second I sit down.

Give it to me in these sections, in this order:

- Cold open (~120 words) — the first thing out of my mouth, should get one real laugh and plant the argument
- Walking back the flyer (~150 words)
- Three ways to split a pot (~180 words) — the $14M, five districts, three rules, three different maps
- What you're actually here to decide (~150 words) — the exercise, in the imperative
- The landing (~80 words) — last lines, and please not cheaply uplifting
- What I left out on purpose (~100 words) — two or three things a smoother speaker would have put in and why I'm not

Speech total around 600 words. Read it in your head out loud while you write it: I'll be standing, holding a mic, in a gym with terrible acoustics, so short sentences, and nothing that depends on a pause landing perfectly.
