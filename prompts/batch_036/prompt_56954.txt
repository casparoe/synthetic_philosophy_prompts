I am drafting the third novel in a sequence about an invented island jurisdiction, and its central set piece is a constitutional crisis that I want to get right rather than merely sound right. The charter my characters live under contains an amendment clause — call it Article Twelve — providing that the charter may be amended by a two-thirds vote of the assembly. Article Twelve says nothing about its own status, so the assembly in my book does the thing that gives me the chapter: it uses Article Twelve to repeal Article Twelve and replace it with a new clause requiring a simple majority in the assembly plus ratification by popular referendum. Two characters dispute the intelligibility of the act. One is a retired appellate judge who considers the vote perfectly ordinary and expects that any court would treat the new clause as valid law by the following morning. The other is a constitutional theorist who thinks the assembly has attempted something that cannot be done, and that the resulting document is either void or the instrument of a small coup. I need to know which of them holds a position a specialist would respect, and what the other can say without my having written him as a fool.

To that end I put the puzzle to a friend who teaches jurisprudence, and she sent me the following, which I now suspect is a confident compression of a real debate: "What your assembly has run into is the paradox of self-amendment. Kelsen's picture is that the validity of every norm is derived from a higher one, all the way up to a basic norm that is presupposed rather than enacted; Ross then showed that the amendment clause is the place where that picture breaks. An amendment has to be modeled as an inference — if the procedure in the clause is followed, the new provision is valid law; the procedure was followed; therefore the new provision is valid law. When the clause is used on itself, the conclusion contradicts the premise from which it was supposed to follow, because the new clause is inconsistent with the old one that authorized it. That is a genuine logical contradiction, of the same family as the sentence that declares itself false, and a contradiction cannot be law. Ross's own workaround — that the new clause is not the old clause amended but a fresh clause created by the old one, which then expires — does not repair the problem, because it amounts to conceding that the charter was never amended at all, only replaced. So your vote is either a nullity or a revolution. There is no middle position, and a court that upholds the new clause is simply declining to apply logic to law."

Could you audit this for me sentence by sentence, the way a scholar in the field might mark up a friend's letter? I want to know which parts are accurate and would be endorsed, which are the sort of simplification that survives a lecture hall but not a seminar, and which are actually wrong — in particular whether the contradiction she describes really belongs to the same family as the liar sentence, whether Ross's own proposal is characterized fairly and what it was meant to save, whether Kelsen is being made to say something he would not have said, and whether the supposed absence of a middle position is a result or an assumption smuggled in as one. I am especially interested in the resources available to the theorist's opponent: are there respectable ways to hold that the act is neither void nor revolutionary, and does anything turn on the fact that the assembly in my book replaces the clause with a genuinely incompatible procedure, or would the puzzle bite just as hard if they had only fiddled with a quorum requirement? Please do not send me reading lists or citations, which I can neither chase nor fake. What I need is the shape of the disagreement, so that when I put it into dialogue each character is defending something a reader who knows this literature would recognize as a live position — and so that I know whether it is the judge's confidence or the theorist's alarm that my chapter can afford to make look naïve.
