**DRAFT: "A Language-Like Technique: Isotype and the Coherentism of Pictures"**

Neurath's pictorial statistics is almost always filed under design history. The Vienna Method of Pictorial Statistics was developed at the Gesellschafts- und Wirtschaftsmuseum in Wien from 1925; Gerd Arntz drew the signs and Marie Reidemeister performed the "transformation" from source material to finished chart; the method was exported to the Soviet statistical institute IZOSTAT and renamed Isotype around 1935, after its practitioners were driven out of Vienna; and Neurath's *International Picture Language* (1936) remains the fullest statement of the technique. On the standard reading, what results is a body of ergonomic rules for making numbers memorable, summed up in the museum's own maxim that it is better to remember simplified pictures than to forget accurate figures. I want to argue that the rules are also logical rules, and that Isotype is best read as the graphic wing of Neurath's philosophy of science.

Neurath's epistemology is anti-foundationalist. In the protocol-sentence controversy he denied that observation reports are privileged by any direct contact with reality; they are accepted or rejected by comparison with other statements, in the way his mariners must rebuild their ship plank by plank at sea. If that is right, then a picture-language project is not a naive retreat to the given. The pictogram is not an impression, and it does not acquire authority by resembling anything. It is one more item in the corpus, warrantable only by its place in the whole. This is what the transformation rule institutionalizes: Reidemeister's task was not to copy the data but to reconstruct it under constraints, and the reconstruction is answerable to the source documents, to the rest of the museum's output, and to audiences' responses — but not to a pre-linguistic given.

The first rule of Isotype is usually glossed as a perceptual matter. Greater quantities are shown by more same-sized signs, never by a larger sign, because a scaled figure invites the question whether what is being compared is its height, its length, or its area, whereas repeated signs, each standing for a fixed quantum, can simply be counted. That gloss is right but incomplete. Repetition also converts a magnitude into a plurality, and in doing so keeps the denotation of the sign fixed across the whole chart. A scaled pictogram is a symbol whose meaning varies continuously with its size; a repeated pictogram is a symbol with one meaning, deployed many times. In Goodman's vocabulary, the first is dense and the second approaches a notation. On this reading Isotype's central rule is not about making comparisons easy for the eye but about buying determinacy at the price of density — the trade any notation makes. Resemblance does no epistemic work here; the work is done by the discipline of the repertoire.

That reading makes "words divide, pictures unite" easy to mishear. The slogan is standardly taken for a semantic claim: pictures mean the same thing to everyone, words do not. But Neurath insisted that Isotype was never to replace verbal language, only to accompany it as a "helping language," and that it was a "language-like technique" rather than a language. What it lacks is not vocabulary but syntax. It can show a distribution; it cannot negate, cannot generalize over cases not shown, cannot state a conditional. A chart can show that twelve ships out of twenty were lost. It cannot show that had the convoy sailed in June, fewer would have been lost. Pictures inherit here exactly the difficulty the picture theory of the *Tractatus* faces: how a representation that works by depicting a state of affairs is supposed to depict that no such state of affairs obtains.

So the slogan is sociological rather than semantic. What unites is the audience, not the content: a chart can be read across language communities where a sentence cannot be carried over without interpretation and dispute, but what is read is not a proposition. This is why Isotype fits Neurath's physicalism less smoothly than it appears to. The unity of science he wanted was a unity of statements in an intersubjectively checkable jargon, and Isotype was never going to supply that. It supplied something different — a shared object of attention around which statements could be made, tested, and contested. The epistemic unit remains the statement; the picture is scaffolding.

The obvious objection is that this deflates Isotype into a teaching aid, and sits badly with a museum that treated its charts as carriers of findings in their own right. If the picture is only scaffolding, why insist so fiercely on the rules? My answer is that scaffolding which leads a viewer to assert something false is worse than none, and the rules are precisely constraints on the inferences a chart licenses.
