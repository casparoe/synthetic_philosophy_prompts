I'm defending a paper at an informal departmental exchange next month, and the paper argues that what you know can depend on what hangs on your knowing it. My assigned respondent is a knowledge-first epistemologist who thinks the very idea is confused — that knowledge is fixed entirely by how things stand between you and the proposition, and that everything my paper trades on is a confusion between an epistemic property and a practical one. I can argue my side reasonably well, but I'm poor at absorbing the counterattack, so I'd like to rehearse it.

Please play that respondent: a purist invariantist, thoroughly familiar with the stakes cases in the literature but convinced they are badly drawn. Give me the strongest version of the position, not a strawman. You may concede ground freely on the periphery — that stakes bear on rational reliance, on what one ought to assert, on whether it is appropriate to stop inquiring, on how we describe cases in ordinary language — but never on the central claim that practical factors help fix the truth of knowledge attributions. When you think my argument depends on a mistake, name the mistake and say precisely where in the reasoning it enters. Don't appeal to who holds which view or to how the dialectic has gone in print; argue from the considerations themselves. Stay in character until I say stop, keep each reply to two paragraphs at most, and if you need a detail of the case settled, ask me for it in the ordinary way rather than filling it in favorably for yourself.

My opening move:

Consider one case told twice. Low stakes: on a Thursday afternoon Maya checks the supplier's live availability portal and sees that the part she has on order is shown as in stock for Friday morning. She types that into a shared spreadsheet. A colleague re-checks the same portal independently on Friday before anything is ordered or shipped, so if she is wrong, the error is caught and nothing at all follows. High stakes: same portal, same screen, same confirmation code, same Thursday afternoon, same memory of having read it — but the Friday shipment is the last one before a nine-day plant shutdown, the automated re-check has been disabled for a system upgrade, and if the part is not there the line stays down for over a week at a cost measured in the millions. Nothing about Maya's evidence, her method, the reliability of the portal, the content of her belief, or her cognitive effort differs by so much as a hair between the two tellings; the only difference is what turns on being right.

So here is my challenge. You must either let stakes into the truth conditions of her knowing, or else commit to the claim that her epistemic position is literally identical across the two tellings — and then owe me an error theory for why the high-stakes telling sounds so much less like knowledge. And that error theory has to explain the direction of the asymmetry: it is not that both tellings strike us as ambiguous and we resolve them differently under pressure. One of them sounds flatly false when the stakes are attached, and this persists once we stipulate, as I have, that her evidence and her method are held exactly fixed, which is precisely the maneuver you would normally reach for. Which horn do you take?
