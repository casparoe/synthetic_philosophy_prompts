I serve as secretary of the Ashgrove Sixth Form Philosophical Society, and at the end of each term we print a short paper recording what we argued about. This term we spent nine meetings on the raven paradox and ended up split, roughly evenly and rather acrimoniously, into two camps. Our faculty adviser has suggested that instead of publishing two rival summaries, we should publish the single document that an adversarial collaboration between the camps ought to have produced — one that a member of either camp could sign without feeling misrepresented. I have been asked to draft it, and I would like your help.

Let me first state the shared starting point, which nobody in the society disputes. Take the hypothesis H: every raven is black. H is logically equivalent to the claim that everything which is not black is not a raven. An observation of a white shoe is an instance of that second generalization and not of the first. Nicod's criterion holds that only observations of ravens should bear on H — positive instances support it, a non-black raven would refute it, and non-ravens are evidentially neutral. Some principle of equivalence holds that whatever confirms one formulation of a hypothesis confirms any formulation logically equivalent to it. The paradox is that these two commitments cannot both be kept in full generality, and something has to give.

The two camps differ about what gives.

The first camp, which I will call the degree camp, holds that confirmation is a matter of degree and is properly measured by how much an observation shifts a rational agent's confidence in H. On this view the white shoe does confirm H, strictly speaking; Nicod's neutrality claim is simply false as a general principle; and the reason the result feels absurd is that in our actual epistemic situation the shift is vanishingly small, because we already know a good deal about the coloration of ravens and because non-black things vastly outnumber ravens. The equivalence principle is retained.

The second camp, which I will call the aboutness camp, holds that confirmation is not a two-place relation between a sentence and an observation, but a three-place relation involving also the agent's background knowledge and the question the agent is pursuing. On this view an observation confirms H only if it bears on H's subject matter — roughly, only if it could discriminate H from some rival that differs over ravens and their colors. Relative to the question whether there are any non-black ravens, the shoe is not an answer at all, and saying that it confirms by a tiny amount changes the subject rather than resolving the paradox. Some in this camp want the equivalence principle restricted or relativized to a way of stating a hypothesis; others want Nicod's criterion retained with a proviso.

In week seven I proposed two cases, and they are now the society's shared testing ground.

Urn A. A sealed urn holds exactly one hundred beads. Each bead is either raven-shaped or shoe-shaped, and each is either black or white. A card on the urn tells you three things and nothing more: that exactly five of the beads are white; that among those five white beads, each is raven-shaped or shoe-shaped with equal probability, independently of the others; and nothing else. Let H* be the claim that every raven-shaped bead in the urn is black. A bead is drawn at random and shown to you. It is white and shoe-shaped.

Urn B. The same urn and the same H*. You are told that the urn was filled by one of two machines and that you have no reason to favor one over the other. The first machine makes every bead black, assigning shapes as it pleases. The second machine makes exactly fifty of the beads white; among those fifty, each is raven-shaped or shoe-shaped with equal probability, independently of the others. Again a bead is drawn at random and shown to you: white and shoe-shaped.

My own conjecture, which the degree camp calls confused, is that these two cases show the smallness of the shift is not a necessary feature of shoe-evidence at all but an artifact of what we happen to assume about how the rest of the world is arranged; what does the work is not the ratio of ravens to non-black things but what the observation tells you about the process that produced everything you have not yet seen. Note that in Urn A raven-shaped beads are not rare — roughly half the urn — and yet the observation seems to make H* more credible by a substantial factor, while in Urn B the same kind of observation seems to all but destroy it.

Please draft the joint statement. I need:

1. A numbered section of claims both camps can sign, including claims about the logic of the setup, about what the urn cases do and do not show, and about what has been conceded on each side. Each claim must be signable without qualification, not merely true under a loaded description.
2. A table of the disagreements that survive: the disagreement, the degree camp's own preferred wording of its position, the aboutness camp's own preferred wording of its position, and a verdict on whether the disagreement is substantive or verbal — and if verbal, the stipulation that would remove it.
3. Three to five cruxes, meaning sub-questions on which a determinate answer would move at least one camp. For each, state the case, argument, or calculation both camps accept as bearing on it, and record each camp's prediction in advance. Where Urn A or Urn B admits of an actual calculation, do the calculation, give the numbers, and say explicitly whether it settles the crux or only sharpens it.
4. A short section stating what each camp would accept as a reason to abandon its own view, and whether there is anything both agree would genuinely move the debate rather than entrench it.
5. A closing paragraph identifying which parts of our year's argument turned out to be about words.

Two constraints. Our rule is that the document must be signable by both camps, so please do not adjudicate or declare a winner, however tempting. And we have no library access: argue from the cases and principles as I have described them, and give no citations or reading list at all, since I cannot check any you produce. Two or three pages is plenty.
