I run a 75-minute upper-level seminar on probability and evidence — 14 students, maybe half of them will happily do the arithmetic and half would rather die. No real money, one page of handout max, and I don't want to lecture first. I want them to walk into a problem before they hear its name.

The problem I want to stage is a stripped-down cousin of van Fraassen's Judy Benjamin problem, built from my memory of the *shape* rather than the original wording, so please check my construction and tell me where it drifts. Here's what I've got:

- A mini-deck of 12 cards: hearts and spades, ace through six. One card is drawn face down and stays face down until the end of the round.
- Props they can trade on: H = "the card is the ace of hearts", R = "the card is red", plus a couple of compounds you pick.
- Round 2 uses a second shape, where the target card is *not* already inside the consequent: S = "the card is the ace of spades", and the informant says something like "if the card isn't the ace of spades, then it's red."
- In both rounds the informant is a student I've briefed beforehand, and I stipulate out loud that the informant is completely reliable — no hedging about whether to trust them. I want the only question on the table to be *what does testimony of this form license*.

The mechanic I have in mind is a market: everyone posts a buy/sell price out of 100 for each prop before the informant speaks, then re-posts after, and we score the whole thing with a proper scoring rule so the prices actually cost them something. My prediction is that most of the room will roughly double their price on H after round 1, and then be unable to say what they learned about aces. I want that to be visible on the board — everyone's before-and-after numbers written up where they can see the pattern, not just feel it privately.

Please design the whole thing. Sections, in this order:

1. **The kit** (~120 words): deck, prop list, point endowment, what goes on the single handout, what I need to prep the informant on.
2. **Market rules** (~250 words): how pricing and payouts work exactly, how the scoring rule is computed without a calculator, and any rule needed to stop people gaming it or freezing up.
3. **Timeline** (~200 words): minute-by-minute for 75 minutes, two rounds plus whatever you add.
4. **Where it should land** (~120 words): the specific beat where they're supposed to notice something is wrong, what I say (ideally almost nothing), and what I do if nobody notices.
5. **The policies, in numbers** (a small table + ~150 words): prior and posterior for H, R, and your compounds under (a) treating the conditional as a proposition and conditionalizing on it, (b) a minimal-change update that holds the conditional credence fixed, and (c) the line that keeps the prior probability of the antecedent intact. Show that (a) and (b) coincide in round 1 and explain why, and show that they come apart in round 2.
6. **Debrief** (~300 words): map each beat of the game onto the options, press them on what each policy has to give up, and bring it out of card-land into conditional testimony in the wild — a doctor, a reviewer, an intelligence briefer, a colleague saying "if the sample isn't contaminated, the effect replicates." Only here do they get the name of the problem.
7. **Wreckage report** (~150 words): what predictably goes wrong the first time I run this, with 14 people and two rounds of noisy resolution — including the fact that the group using the worse policy can still win on points, and how I handle that when someone points it out smugly.
8. **What the market can't show** (~100 words): the feature of the puzzle the game structurally can't reproduce, that I'll have to supply by hand.

Also: anywhere my mini-deck breaks the analogy with the original problem, say so plainly rather than papering over it. And keep the math at a level where the non-mathy half can follow the numbers on the board even if they can't derive them.
