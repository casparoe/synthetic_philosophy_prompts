Context: I've been chewing on the paradox of tragedy for years in a very rationalist way. The puzzle I care about isn't "why is sad art good" in the abstract, it's "why do I knowingly buy a ticket to feel bad, and why do I feel ripped off when the third act fails to wreck me." Every solution I've read — the conversion ones, the feeling-bad-about-feeling-bad / meta-response ones, the quasi-emotion ones, the whole mastery-and-control family — looks to me like it has the same failure mode. So I wrote the failure mode out as an explicit argument. The trouble is I don't believe the conclusion and I cant find the leak.

(1) Whatever else we say, the audience chooses the work in advance and knows what it will do to them.
(2) So they choose it under a description that includes "this is going to make me grieve."
(3) If you choose X under a description that includes a state S, then S is among the things you're choosing X for — part of the aim, not a side effect.
(4) For anything you aim at, the only things that make one route to it better than another are how much of it you get and what it costs you, the chooser.
(5) Grief at a performed death is grief at a real death minus the belief: same phenomenology, same object-structure, everything else the same, with the missing belief only dialling the intensity down rather than changing the kind of state it is.
(6) So the real case is strictly the more intense version of what the play delivers, and by (4) the only other consideration is cost to the chooser — while the extra costs in the real case fall on third parties, who aren't the chooser.
(7) Therefore the rational tragedy-goer should prefer the funeral to the play and the raw disaster footage to the drama about the disaster, and should feel about the play roughly how I feel about a weak third act, only more so.
(8) Nobody prefers that. We actively think worse of the people who do — grief tourists, the crowd filming the accident, the guy who follows the ambulance.
(9) So either (3) is false, in which case grief isn't the aim and I genuinely don't know what I'm paying for, which means there was never a paradox to solve in the first place — or (4)/(5) are false, in which case every solution that treats the audience's sadness as the thing to be justified has been solving the wrong problem.
