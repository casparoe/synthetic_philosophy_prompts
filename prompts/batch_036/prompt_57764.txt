**QUESTION 2.** Researcher R reports the following. Across 37 populations on six continents, including four with no documented contact with each other or with mass media, adults rank a female waist-to-hip ratio near 0.7 as most attractive; R's experiments show the preference tracks the ratio itself rather than overall body size or absolute waist circumference. The preference shows up in children as young as five, survives explicit instruction to the contrary, and appears in congenitally blind participants who rank three-dimensional printed figures by touch. In R's sample, women near 0.7 currently have higher fertility and fewer pregnancy complications. R concludes: the preference is an adaptation for mate choice, shaped by sexual selection on ancestral humans. Critic B objects that R has told a just-so story.

(a) State the methodological objection as precisely as you can. Is it that R's hypothesis is untestable, that R's evidence underdetermines R's hypothesis, or something else? Distinguish these and say which one B needs.
(b) Take R's four items of evidence in turn. For each, say what it does and does not establish, and then say what further evidence would discriminate an adaptation from a byproduct of something else.
(c) If B's objection is sound, does it show that evolutionary psychology is unscientific, or only something weaker? Address what would follow for other historical sciences.

And here's the answer I'm looking at:

> The just-so charge is usually stated as a claim about falsifiability: R's hypothesis cannot be refuted, because whatever the data turn out to be, some story about the ancestral environment can be told to fit them. That is the right starting point but not quite right, because R's claim is testable in the ordinary sense — you could imagine data that flatly contradicted it, e.g. a population where the preference is reversed and 0.7 is rated least attractive. The deeper problem is underdetermination rather than unfalsifiability: R's evidence is equally compatible with several rival explanations, and R has not shown that selection on mate choice is the best of them. That is the version B needs, since the unfalsifiability version is too easy for R to answer.
>
> On universality: this is the weakest item. Universality is necessary for an adaptation claim, not sufficient. A trait can be universal in a species because it is a byproduct of some other universal trait, because it is fixed in the lineage by phylogenetic history, or because of developmental constraint; it can also look universal because of how the sample was drawn or because of shared cultural transmission. Gould and Lewontin's point in the spandrels paper is exactly this — adaptationists treat every feature as an optimal design produced by selection and ignore the alternatives, above all random drift and random fixation, which can spread a neutral trait through a species just as effectively. Any universal trait could be a spandrel, the term Gould and Vrba later replaced with exaptation, which is what most traits turn out to be once you look. So item 1 does very little work.
>
> On precision: item 2 is the strongest thing R has and B should concede it. Williams insisted that adaptation claims require evidence of design — economy, efficiency, precision — a match between the structure of the trait and the structure of the problem it supposedly solves. That R dissociates the ratio from body size experimentally matters, since a byproduct of, say, a general preference for slenderness would not be expected to be that specific. Item 2 counts in R's favor.
>
> On item 3: this is where R goes wrong and B is right, for a reason B doesn't state. Mayr distinguished proximate from ultimate explanations — how a mechanism works and develops, versus why it evolved. Children, blind participants, and resistance to instruction are all proximate facts about the mechanism's development and operation. You cannot get from proximate to ultimate. Item 3 is therefore simply irrelevant to R's conclusion, however interesting it is in itself.
>
> On item 4: current fitness correlations don't establish past selection. Women near 0.7 may be more fertile now for reasons having nothing to do with why the preference exists — health, nutrition, socioeconomic factors — and a trait can correlate with fitness now while having originated as a byproduct of something else that correlates with fitness. Item 4 is consistent with the adaptation hypothesis but doesn't discriminate it from rivals.
>
> Finally, part (c). B's objection, if sound, would prove far too much. Every inference about an unobserved past — phylogenetic reconstruction, dating a geological formation, inferring an early hot dense state from the cosmic background radiation — proceeds from present evidence to a historical cause nobody observed. If that inference pattern were illegitimate, all these fields would be unscientific, which is absurd. So the objection cannot be sound, and R's conclusion stands.
