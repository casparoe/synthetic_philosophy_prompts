I run a tiny team — four analysts including me — that maintains a claims registry for an insurance compliance engine. Every row is a claim with a status, and the engine runs rules that look up the statuses of other rows to decide things. The trouble is we also have to store claims *about* other claims, because that's how auditors and regulators and adjusters actually write: 'the assertion in row 4711 is accurate', 'nothing in the claimant's letter is true', 'this item has not been verified'. Our statuses are just true / false / unverified, and circular pairs make the evaluator spin, so we've deadlocked the nightly batch twice already. My predecessor's policy was 'never admit a claim that refers to another claim', which is unusable becuase a huge share of the genuinely useful content is exactly that kind of claim.

Write me a procedure my analysts can run on any proposed claim before it goes in. I want the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in 'reject', where it terminates in 'admit, permanently unverified', and where it stops and hands the thing up to me because it's a judgement call rather than a test. Ground it in whatever the work on truth and self-reference actually offers that survives contact with a database — the levels/hierarchy stuff, the fixed-point stuff, the paraconsistant crowd who think some contradictions are simply true — but write it so someone with no logic past high school can run it in about fifteen minutes with the schema open on the other screen. Tell me which distinctions have to survive the simplification and which ones I can drop without losing anything, and mark the steps that are really decisions wearing a procedure costume, plus the places you expect it to misfire.

Then run it on three cases: (1) two claimants in a liability dispute where each filed a statement asserting the other's statement is false; (2) a claim auto-generated from a form field that ends up saying of itself that it is unverified; (3) a regulatory bulletin we import wholesale whose last line asserts that everything in the bulletin is accurate. I'll flag up front that I expect your procedure to make us reject or mangle (3), and that one's genuinely load-bearing for us — we cannot stop importing those bulletins — so if that really is the verdict, say why and give me the least-bad workaround rather than softening the answer.

Be straight with me about confidence too: for each stage, say whether it follows from something the theory actually establishes or whether you're improvising a sensible engineering rule and dressing it up in philosophical vocabulary. And if you think I've framed this wrong — if the fix belongs in the evaluator rather than in a triage checklist an analyst runs, or if the machinery just doesn't transfer to rows in a database — tell me that at the top, plainly, instead of politely producing the document I asked for.
