I work in the money-markets and settlement function of a central bank, and I spend my days with balance sheets: reserve accounts, intraday liquidity, repo, settlement finality, and the endogenous-money picture in which deposits are created by lending and money is a liability of somebody. I have no Greek, no training in the history of philosophy, and no interest in acquiring either. What I have been reading, on the side, is Aristotle's treatment of money — the discussion of exchange in *Nicomachean Ethics* Book V and the account of wealth-getting in *Politics* Book I — in translation and mostly in summary. I want to find out how much of it survives being restated in the vocabulary I already know.

So: restate each of the following claims in that vocabulary — reserve accounting, claims and liabilities, unit of account versus medium of exchange, money creation, liquidity, the value of money as a price-level phenomenon — as faithfully as you can manage, one section per claim.

(a) Money is not natural but conventional; it exists by law or agreement, and those who use it can alter its value or render it useless.
(b) Money came into use because the goods people actually need are not easily carried between distant parties, so a commodity was agreed upon that was both useful in itself and portable.
(c) Money stands in for need or demand and acts as a kind of surety: if you do not want a thing now, you will have it when you do; and everything that is exchanged must be made commensurate through a single measure, which is need itself.
(d) Household management acquires goods within a limit, because the goods serve a bounded end — the life of the household and the city — whereas the art of money-making aims at increase without limit and treats accumulation as its own end.
(e) Lending at interest is the most contrary-to-nature way of getting wealth, because there money is made to breed money instead of doing the work it was introduced for, namely serving as an instrument of exchange.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. For each claim, sort the content into three buckets: what translates cleanly, what translates only by being bent (say precisely what you bent and why the target vocabulary forced it), and what has no expression at all in my vocabulary. Two tests in particular. First, claim (c) seems to map onto precautionary demand for liquidity almost too neatly; tell me whether that fit is real or an artifact of translating loosely enough to produce it. Second, claim (d) has no obvious counterpart in a framework where wants are taken as given and the question is only about the allocation of scarce means to them; decide whether that absence is a genuine hole in my vocabulary or a sign that the Aristotelian claim is not a claim about money at all. Throughout, do not let the modern framework do the work for you: rendering (a) as "money is fiat" would make it trivially true and empty, and if that is what the translation yields, say so rather than presenting it as a success. Where a claim cannot be expressed, judge whether the failure is a defect of my vocabulary — and if so, a technical or a normative one — or whether the original claim was less determinate than it looked.

Two housekeeping points. Work in paraphrase: no verbatim quotation from Aristotle and no bibliography, which I would not be able to check anyway. And if any of my formulations (a)–(e) misdescribe the position rather than merely simplify it, correct me before you translate, since a faithful rendering of a claim I have misstated would be worse than useless. Aim for roughly fifteen hundred to two thousand words, in numbered sections, closing with a one-row-per-claim summary table giving the verdict: clean, distorted, or inexpressible.
