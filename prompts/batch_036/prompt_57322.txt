A friend and I argued for most of an evening about David Hume's monetary thought and ended up in the place that makes me want an outside adjudication: not about what he said, but about what he would say now.

The disagreement runs as follows. My friend reads Hume as, in effect, an early dove. He points to the claim in Of Money that a slow and gradual increase of the circulating medium is favourable to industry, since in the interval between the arrival of new money and the general rise of prices the additional money quickens diligence and enlarges profits; to the argument in Of Interest that low rates of interest are a symptom of a flourishing commercial nation rather than a sign of mere monetary abundance; and to the approving treatment of paper credit, which allows the precious metals to be sent abroad and put to work there while paper performs the business of circulation at home. On that reading Hume would have shrugged at large-scale asset purchases, welcomed a two percent inflation target as the institutionalization of his own preferred gradual expansion, and been relaxed about debt denominated in a currency the state itself issues.

My reading is close to the reverse. I take the gradual-increase argument to concern a windfall of specie earned through trade rather than a policy instrument; I take the dominant conclusion of Of Money and Of the Balance of Trade to be long-run neutrality, with money functioning as the oil that eases the wheels of trade rather than as one of the wheels, and with the insistence that a nation acquiring more money than its level will lose it again, since dearer labour and commodities hand its foreign markets to cheaper rivals; and I take his caution about over-issued paper together with his alarm in Of Public Credit to be of one piece — the danger of a state financing itself on the strength of its own promises. On my reading, his mechanism requires that the new money arrive unannounced and reach the hands of the industrious, and a rule announced in advance, at a fixed rate, with the injection made by exchanging central-bank reserves for government bonds, meets neither condition.

Which of us has him? And, having settled that, what would he say about each of the following, taken severally rather than in the aggregate?

1. Central banks expanding their balance sheets by purchasing government debt, where the money created enters the system as reserves rather than as wages or merchants' balances.
2. A standing, publicly announced target for the annual rate of inflation.
3. The United States running current-account deficits for nearly half a century without the correction he predicts, because the rest of the world prefers to hold dollar assets to spending them — does the level-finding argument he presses in the essays on money and the balance of trade have any answer to an instrument that others choose to hoard indefinitely?
4. A fixed-supply, decentralized money over which no magistrate can adjust the canal.
5. Trade restrictions justified not by jealousy of a rival's riches but by industrial policy and the security of supply chains — does the concession he appears to make in Of the Jealousy of Trade, that uneasiness about a rival's naval power is not wholly without foundation, license this, or is it being stretched past breaking?
6. Public debt above a hundred percent of national income, serviced for a decade at rates near zero and now at modest ones.

For each item give me a verdict — approving, disapproving, unmoved, or genuinely indeterminate — together with the argument from his own reasoning that produces it, and, where his framework lacks the resources to produce one at all, the premise he would have to surrender in order to reach any verdict whatsoever. I would rather have committed answers with the degree of confidence attached to each, and a clear marking of which parts rest on what he actually wrote and which are extrapolation from the structure of his argument, than a balanced survey of how hazardous it is to speak for the dead. If my friend or I has him wrong on the eighteenth-century questions, say so and say where.

And please do not comply politely if the request is a bad one. If you judge that the counterfactual is simply not determinate — that a framework built on metallic money, convertible paper, and automatic level-finding cannot yield a position on a fiat base money or a reserve currency without smuggling in assumptions Hume never held — say that plainly at the outset, and tell me what narrower version of the question could be answered honestly instead.
