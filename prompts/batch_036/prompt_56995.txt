Next month I deliver a forty-minute talk, followed by twenty-five minutes of questions, in an academic medical center's humanities-and-addiction-medicine seminar series. I am an ancient philosopher, and for nine years I have been in recovery from an alcohol use disorder; two of the clinicians who treated me in that program will be seated in the room. That is part of why I would rather have this prepared in advance than trust my instincts at the podium.

The thesis I am defending has two halves, and the second is the contentious one. First, that Plato's treatment of wine in the opening books of the *Laws* is not a grudging concession to festival culture but a deliberate theory of acquired self-command: a disposition that has never been exposed to the thing capable of corrupting it is not virtue but luck, and a legislator can therefore train citizens by administering the corrupting agent in graded, supervised, socially scaffolded doses. Second — and this is what I expect to be attacked — that the position is a philosophical ancestor of harm reduction rather than of abstinence-based moralism, and that it names a real cost of total-elimination environments: they can produce people whose self-command is situational rather than dispositional, and who consequently come apart when the scaffold is removed.

The argument as I will present it:

1. Plato's Athenian insists that wine cannot be properly evaluated by argument alone; it must be evaluated by observing people under its influence in a deliberately controlled setting, presided over by someone who is himself unaffected.
2. He assimilates the procedure to testing the mettle of a thing by exposing it to the agent that would spoil it, rather than by inspecting it at rest.
3. From this he draws a general claim about character: a courage that has never been tried against pleasure is a different and lesser thing from one that has.
4. The institutional conclusion is therefore not removal of the agent but design of the exposure — the timing, the dose, the company, the presider, and the age at which it is first permitted.
5. In the second book this becomes a developmental claim: wine belongs to the mature, whose seriousness requires loosening, and is withheld from the young, whose characters are not yet settled.
6. So the *Laws* offers a dose- and stage-sensitive model of moral risk management, structurally closer to harm reduction than to prohibition.

The room: the division chief, an addiction psychiatrist who directs an abstinence-oriented residential program and is himself in long-term recovery; a clinical psychologist who works on relapse and on what happens to a person's self-efficacy after a single violation of abstinence; a longitudinal epidemiologist of alcohol-use-disorder remission who has no patience for mechanism talk and thinks in population terms; a health economist evaluating a contingency-management pilot; one philosopher of medicine who works on causal inference in clinical research and will be the most courteous and the most surgical questioner present; two residents; and a social worker from a syringe-services program who is a committed harm-reductionist but of the opioid variety and doubtful that anything transfers to alcohol. There are no classicists. Nobody has read the *Laws*; one person read the *Republic* in college and will speak from that.

I want a prep sheet, not a defense. Specifically:

- The eight or nine objections I will actually receive from *this* room, ordered by how much damage each would do if I handled it badly — weigh likelihood and severity together and tell me which of the two you are weighting.
- Each one phrased as that particular person would phrase it, in their own vocabulary. I want to hear the epidemiologist's and the residents' diction, not a philosopher's paraphrase of it.
- For each, a reply deliverable in roughly two minutes; a blunt note on where the reply is soft, or where it rests on a claim about the text that a careful reader could contest; and a verdict on whether the correct move is to concede, to distinguish, or to counter-attack.
- A separation of the objections that target my exegesis from those that target the substantive claim, and your judgment about which kind is more dangerous in this particular room.
- What I should concede in the first five minutes to purchase credibility with clinicians who will otherwise hear an ancient-philosophy talk as evasion.
- The single reply that has to land for the talk to be received as a contribution rather than as a recovering academic rationalizing.
- And the question I am hoping nobody asks — the one that exposes the seam between my exegesis and my biography, or the one empirical result which, if someone says its name out loud, leaves my position with no good answer.

Where an objection turns on evidence, tell me what that evidence is generally said to show and what an honest acknowledgment sounds like from someone who has not read it. Do not invent citations, author names, trial titles, or effect sizes; if you are not confident about a specific, say so and give me the shape of the concession instead of a plausible-looking reference.
