So I got into a nearly three-hour argument with my flatmate Dani last night and neither of us budged, and I'd rather have a stranger's read than another round of it. Context: we both volunteer on the research desk of a small documentation NGO. We're not a court, we don't prosecute anybody, we publish briefing notes, and our steering group has one standing instruction that matters here — a note may only use the g-word about a situation if that call is consistent with the calls we've already published. Not whatever makes the strongest headline this year, consistant with our own track record. Dani thinks that instruction is incoherent and we should decide each file on its merits; I think there's a rule sitting behind our five published calls and that it decides the new one. I can't prove either of us right, which is the whole reason I'm asking.

Our five published calls, roughly in order:

1. A state killed something like a third of a group that was concentrated in one province and left the same group alone in the rest of the country. We said genocide — destroying a group in part is enough.
2. A regime killed hundreds of thousands, but the victims were picked out by class and political loyalty rather than ethnicity or religion. We refused the label and said crimes against humanity.
3. A state removed a minority's children en masse into residential institutions in the majority region, banned their language in schools and in public life, and demolished their places of worship. Most adult deaths came from neglect and starvation during the removals rather than from a killing campaign. We said genocide, and our published reasoning leaned on two things at once: the limb of the Convention about transferring children out of the group, and the claim that the group was being dismantled as a group.
4. A campaign of mass rape and enforced sterilisation aimed at stopping a group having children, running alongside displacement. Genocide.
5. An occupying power destroyed a religious minority's shrines, manuscripts and cemeteries and outlawed their liturgy, but expelled the members rather than killing them; the community had reconstituted itself across the border within a couple of years. Not genocide. I wrote that one and I still think it's right.

The new file: a state facing a separatist insurgency by a linguistic minority has moved maybe 40% of the minority's school-age children to boarding institutions in majority regions where their language is forbidden, criminalised any public use of it, and renamed every village and stream in the territory. Adults stay put and worship freely. No birth-prevention measures. Combat deaths are in the low hundreds, mostly adult men, and our field people can't find evidence that anyone planned to kill them as such. Children are sent home at sixteen — but the reports say they come back unable to speak the language, and the adults are too surveilled to teach it to them openly.

Dani's line is that 3 and 5 pull in opposit directions, so our precedents are junk and we should decide from scratch. Mine is that at least two rules fit all five, and I roughly know what they are but not which one we've actually been running: a narrow one where the group has to be destroyed physically or biologically, and a more Lemkin-flavoured one where a group can be destroyed as a group even if every member survives, because what makes it a group is its language, institutions and the way it hands itself down to the next generation. Lemkin's own conception clearly had room for the second, and the cultural-destruction language floated during drafting got voted out of the Convention, so I honestly don't know how much that history should bind a body like ours that isn't a court and doesn't have to apply the treaty as written.

What I want:

- the call we should make to be consistent with ourselves, plus which of the five is the closest analog to the new file and why — argued, not asserted;
- every candidate rule you can find that fits all five precedents, with what each implies for the new case and which feature of the new case is doing the work;
- a further hypothetical case, not covered by our five, where your top two candidate rules come apart, so we know which one we'd be committing to;
- and, if the precedents really do underdetermine it, a plain statement of the principle we'd be adopting by going either way and what we'd be stuck defending in future files.

Finish with a JSON object I can paste into the desk log, exactly these fields: recommended_call (string, yes or no); closest_precedent (integer 1-5); why_closest (string, two or three sentences); candidate_rules (array of objects, each with rule_name, statement, fits_all_five (boolean), verdict_on_new_case (string), decisive_feature (string)); discriminating_case (string); principle_if_yes (string); principle_if_no (string); confidence (number 0-1); where_i_am_extrapolating (string).

One last thing: keep settled and contested seperate. I'm fairly sure everyone agrees there's a special-intent requirement and that the list of acts is closed as a matter of law, but I have no idea whether reading the child-transfer limb narrowly — so it only bites when the removal is permanent — is the consensus among people who work on this or just one camp's view. Don't hand me a contested position as though it were the received one, and mark which parts of your answer are you reasoning from the internal logic of our five calls versus which parts reflect what lawyers and scholars actually hold.
