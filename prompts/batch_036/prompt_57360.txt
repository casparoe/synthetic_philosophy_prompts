**An adversarial collaboration on archival silence: drafting the joint statement**

Two archivists have sat on the same repository's acquisitions and description committee for three years and have never once agreed. Their disagreement is not personal, and it is not politics in the ordinary sense. It concerns what an archive is for, and what the archivist's claim to professional authority rests upon.

Camp A, the custodial impartialists, hold that the reason anyone should trust an archive is that its holdings are the unselfconscious residue of business actually transacted: records made for other purposes, by people who were not addressing posterity and did not expect to be read as witnesses. On this view the archivist's own contribution must be driven as close to nil as the work permits. Appraisal selects, but on the basis of the record's provenance and original function; arrangement reproduces the creator's own order; description transmits the creator's categories in the creator's vocabulary, glossed only where the gloss is philological rather than evaluative. The moment the archivist begins correcting the record — supplying what the institution omitted, re-labelling what the institution named in its own idiom, favouring one series over another because of who is complaining loudest this year — the archive ceases to be evidence and becomes advocacy, and the distinct authority that justified its existence is spent. A gap in the sources should be met by describing the gap faithfully and explaining, from the provenance, why it is there.

Camp B, the reparative interventionists, hold that this self-description is not so much false as unavailable. There is no default condition of non-intervention to retreat into: what survives was already selected by the creator's own filing habits and by earlier archivists' retention schedules; what is findable was already decided by whoever wrote the catalogue; what is sayable about a person was already framed by the vocabulary in which the record speaks about them. Silence, on this view, is not an absence but a deposit laid down by a sequence of decisions, most of them taken by parties with an interest in the result. Since intervention cannot be avoided, the only live question is whether it is made explicit and contestable or concealed behind a professional claim to have done nothing at all. The archivist therefore has standing — and on some accounts a duty — to solicit records from those the institution never recorded, to re-describe in language that does not reproduce the creator's characterisation of the people it characterises, and to weight preservation effort towards counter-records that are physically at risk and administratively inconvenient.

**The case.** Treat the following as stipulated rather than as a report about an actual repository.

A county record office holds the administrative papers of a residential institution for children, closed in 1974 and since absorbed into a successor public body. The surviving series is overwhelmingly administrative: admission registers, staffing rosters, matrons' disciplinary logs, inspection reports, governors' minutes. The children appear in it almost exclusively as objects of record-keeping; where they speak at all, it is through disciplinary entries describing incidents. Four matters are now before the committee.

1. A former residents' association offers to deposit some sixty hours of recorded oral testimony, gathered by the association itself over the past decade, and asks that it be catalogued within the same series structure as the institutional papers rather than as a separate accession.
2. The deposit deed by which the institutional papers were transferred allows the successor body to withhold the annexes to the inspection reports until seventy-five years after closure. The association argues the closure protects the institution rather than the recorded subjects.
3. The existing catalogue description was written in the 1980s and adopts the institution's own administrative vocabulary throughout, including its terms for punishment and for the children who incurred it. The association asks for re-description; the cataloguing staff note that any new wording will itself reflect a standpoint, and that the current wording at least tells a user precisely how the institution spoke.
4. There is money in the digitisation budget for one project this year. The registers are stable, heavily consulted by genealogists and by claimants pursuing compensation, and cheap to scan. The oral testimony is on deteriorating magnetic tape, requires obsolete playback equipment, has so far generated very few reader requests, and is expected to become unrecoverable within a decade if not transferred.

In addressing the case, distinguish four kinds of silence, because the two camps will not even agree that all four are archival problems: silence in the source (no record of an event was ever created); silence in survival (records were created but were destroyed or never transferred); silence in description (records are present and intact, but the finding aid renders them invisible to the person who would need them, for instance by filing an individual's life under an institutional subject heading); and silence in the conditions of access (records are present and described, but closed, undigitised, or held in a format that cannot now be played).

**The task.** Draft the document this committee should have produced three years ago: the joint statement that both camps could put their names to after a genuine adversarial collaboration. Do not adjudicate between them, and do not dilute either position until it becomes something no one would dispute — the value of the exercise is in formulations that are exact enough for each side to recognise itself and sharp enough to exclude the other.

Structure the document as follows.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims: propositions both camps can sign, including claims about the case itself (what the four kinds of silence above amount to here, what the repository is capable of doing, what the deed permits). Keep this section honest; a claim both sides sign because it says nothing is a failure, not a success.

Second, a short terminology audit. Several of the words doing the heaviest work in the dispute — neutrality, evidence, intervention, provenance, silence itself — are used by the two camps in non-coincident senses. Say which disputes are substantially verbal, what each camp means by the contested term, and what residue of genuine disagreement survives once the wording is fixed.

Third, a table of the residual disagreements, with columns for: the issue; Camp A's position, phrased so that Camp A would accept the wording; Camp B's position, phrased so that Camp B would accept the wording; and a final column stating whether the difference changes what the repository does, or only what it says about what it does. That last column matters: if the two camps in fact converge operationally on three of the four matters, the document should say so plainly and locate the disagreement where it actually lies.

Fourth, the cruxes. Identify the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side, and for each give the case, argument, or piece of evidence that both camps would accept as bearing on it, together with each side's prediction recorded in advance — not a hope or a preference, but what each expects to be true if the crux is tested. Where a crux concerns the future of this collection, make the prediction concrete enough to be checked in ten years. If you conclude that no single crux could move both camps, state that explicitly and explain what kind of dispute is left.

Fifth, joint recommendations on the four matters: what the committee should actually do about the oral testimony, the closure, the re-description, and the digitisation order. Where the two camps cannot sign the same recommendation, record the failure and identify the precise point at which the reasoning diverges.

Two further constraints. If you find yourself needing a fact about the case that I have not supplied, stipulate it openly and flag it as a stipulation rather than letting it slide in unannounced. And write in British English throughout.
