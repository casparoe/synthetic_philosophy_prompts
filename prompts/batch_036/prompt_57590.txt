"Let us draw an arrow arbitrarily. If as we follow the arrow we find more and more of the random element in the state of the world, then the arrow is pointing towards the future; if the random element decreases the arrow points towards the past … I shall use the phrase 'time's arrow' to express this one-way property of time which has no analogue in space."

The part I actually need help with: my seminar split into two camps over this passage and I don't think either is fully right, but I can't say why.

Camp A says it's circular. It defines past and future by which way the randomness goes, but "entropy increases" is only a claim about a direction you already have to have in hand, so the arrow gets presupposed rather than found. They add that it's really just a stipulation about how to use the word "future" — note he tells you to draw the arrow *arbitrarily* — so there's no empirical content at all.

Camp B says the opposite. Drawing the arrow is arbitrary only in the trivial sense that you can point either way along a line, and the real content is the empirical claim that one of the two directions will show increasing randomness. So it's a criterion the world can satisfy or fail to satisfy, not a definition in disguise.
