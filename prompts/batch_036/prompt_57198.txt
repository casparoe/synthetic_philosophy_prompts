Been down an xphi rabbit hole for about two weeks and I want someone to tell me I'm overreaching before I write this up as a blog post. Starting point is the Knobe effect: the chairman who doesn't care about the environment one way or the other, gets told the new program will raise profits and also harm (or, in the other version, help) the environment, shrugs that he only cares about the profit, program goes ahead, environment ends up harmed/helped. Way more people say he harmed it intentionally than say he helped it intentionally. I've read the follow-ups where they mess with the vignette — bigger harm, smaller harm, different cultures, phrasing it as a foreseen side effect of something the agent is actively trying to avoid — and the asymmetry mostly hangs around, plus I vaguely remember some neuroimaging replication I can't find again.

Here's what I think I've concluded, and this is the part I want pressure-tested: 'intentionally' turns out not to be a descriptive psychological term at all, it's a moral evaluation wearing a psychology costume, kind of like how 'witch' looked like a natural kind term for a long time. If that's right a bunch of downstream stuff looks confused to me. Like when criminal law sorts murder from manslaughter on whether the defendant acted intentionally versus recklessly, we're letting a term whose application flips with the moral valence of the outcome do load-bearing work, which seems obviously broken and fixable. And the debate about whether ordinary folk are "natural compatibilists" seems to be measuring with the same contaminated instrument, so I don't see what those surveys are actually establishing.

So my question: if the asymmetry is as robust as everyone says, why is the philosophy of action literature still treating intention as a mental state with its own functional role, instead of writing the whole thing off as a debunked folk kind? I can see two answers and I dont know which is right. One is that they're just defending their turf. The other is that I'm conflating something, because people keep saying either that the concept could be perfectly fine while our *application* of it is noisy, or that what's driving the asymmetry isn't morality per se but something adjacent — how much skill the agent displayed, a trade-off between causal contribution and disapproval, or that saying he did it intentionally is just a conversational backdoor for assigning blame. Is that second family a genuine alternative to my reading or is it the same claim repackaged to sound less alarming? Basically: am I looking at a real bug in the concept that we should engineer around (and has anybody actually tried, e.g. redoing legal intent standards so they're robust to outcome valence), or am I looking at noisy performance of a concept that's tracking something correctly, in which case the debunking framing was never on the table for me to begin with?
