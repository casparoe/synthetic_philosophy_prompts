I am a third-year PhD student in historical sociology, working on fiscal administration and collective identity in early modern Europe, and for this exercise I would like you to take the part of a specialist in nationalism theory who is equally at home with the modernist canon — Gellner, the 1983 Hobsbawm and Ranger volume on invented traditions, Anderson's *Imagined Communities* — and with its critics, from Smith's ethnosymbolism through Hastings's medieval England to the more recent long-durée arguments. Please work through the problem carefully before you answer, and in particular think first about which of the sub-questions below really do independent work; what I want on the page, however, is only the finished decomposition.

Here is the proposition I want a number on, stated as precisely as I can manage: that nations, in the sense in which Anderson and Hobsbawm were arguing about them, are a modern phenomenon — specifically, that no European polity before roughly 1780 was a nation in that sense. Anderson's stipulation is that a nation is "an imagined political community—and imagined as both inherently limited and sovereign," and the modernist reading treats that stipulation as tracking an empirical claim about what became possible only with vernacular print, the loss of legitimacy of divinely ordained dynastic rule, and a state apparatus that reaches far enough into everyday life to address ordinary people as members of a bounded political community. The medievalists reply that England at least was doing something very like this with royal chancery practice, vernacular scripture, war taxation and parish organization centuries earlier. My own credence is about 0.75; a medievalist colleague of mine — we keep landing at the same table because we read the same tax rolls — puts hers at about 0.25 and insists that the modernists have simply defined the Middle Ages out of existence by fiat. I want to know how much of the distance between those two numbers is a matter of stipulating the extension of a term, and how much is genuine empirical disagreement.

Please answer with actual probabilities rather than observing that everything depends on how one defines a nation and stopping there. Give me exactly five numbered items, each of one or two sentences (item 5 may run to three), with no introduction and no concluding paragraph:

1. The probability that the modernist–medievalist dispute is substantially verbal — that most of the gap between us is fixed by choosing a definition of nation rather than by any disagreement about the historical record.
2. Conditional on the modernist criteria being the right ones (mass vernacular literacy, print-mediated simultaneity, sovereign rather than dynastic self-legitimation, and enough administrative penetration that ordinary people are addressed as members of a bounded community), the probability that no European polity before 1780 satisfied them.
3. Conditional on bracketing what we choose to call the result, the probability that at least one European case has a documented institutional and cultural continuity running from the medieval period into the modern nation, with Hastings's England as the standing candidate.
4. The probability that whatever non-verbal disagreement survives items 1 through 3 can be settled by the evidence we actually have, given that the population at issue was overwhelmingly non-literate and our sources are mostly the products of clerks, chroniclers and administrators who had their own reasons for describing communities the way they did.
5. Your overall credence in the proposition as stated, how you derive it from the four terms despite their obvious lack of independence, the term your number is most sensitive to, the single argument or piece of evidence that would move it most, and which term you think my colleague and I most likely disagree about.

In every item, tell me whether the figure you assign reflects something broadly settled among specialists or your own judgment on something genuinely contested, and please do not dress a contested position up as the consensus of the field. I am not asking for references — I know the literature — I am asking for numbers and the reasoning that produces them.
