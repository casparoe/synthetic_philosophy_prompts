Here's the question exactly as it was handed out:

> Some critics object that Confucian role ethics, by making persons out of the roles they occupy, leaves it with no resources for criticizing roles that are themselves oppressive — say, a father whose understanding of fatherhood includes a demand for absolute deference from his children. Explain how role ethics, in the form Ames and Rosemont give it, is supposed to answer that objection, and say whether you think it succeeds. Then explain what, if anything, really separates role ethics from a virtue ethics that simply counts relationship-sustaining dispositions among the virtues.

And here's the student's answer, verbatim: "The core claim of role ethics is that you aren't first a self who then acquires roles. Ames and Rosemont say the roles are what you're made of: daughter, student, younger brother, neighbour. Take all of those away and there's no residue left over that is the real you. A person is more like a place where relationships are happening than a thing that happens to have relationships. Ren fits this — it isn't a property you possess but something you do, and it's grown in the family roles first, which is why filial piety and fraternal deference get called the root. On the objection, I think the answer has to be that the standard for criticizing the father isn't outside the roles at all. There's no impartial moral point of view to climb up to, and role ethics is right to say so. But the father isn't doing fatherhood well. Fathering includes nurturing, teaching, being responsive to the children, and a man who demands total deference fails at the role he's in, the same way you judge a bad pianist by piano standards rather than by standards imported from somewhere else. So the criticism is immanent, and that's supposed to be enough. Where I think it's weak: if the person just is the roles, then the content of the role is whatever the people in it take it to be, and the father can contest the community's sense of fathering from inside it as easily as anyone else can. There's no fact of the matter to appeal to. And the children have no way out, because you can't resign from being someone's child — that's a fact about who you are, not a job you can quit. So the objection does land to some degree, even if the pianist analogy shows it isn't fatal. For the comparison, my view is that role ethics is basically virtue ethics with relationships swapped in for character traits. In both, the ethical unit is a disposition, and role ethics just says the dispositions get their content from roles rather than from a general account of good character. The big difference is that role ethics denies the individual is prior to the relationships, but Aristotle can agree with that in a weaker form — we become just by doing just acts inside a polis, and nobody is self-sufficient alone — so the difference is mainly one of emphasis. Both also assume there's a human nature with a telos that the roles or the virtues are there to realize."
