I'm preparing for this week's discussion section in my political philosophy course on global justice, and the topic is whether compatriots have claims on us that strangers simply lack. The assigned reading defends a strong priority for compatriots, and the engine of the argument is an analogy with the family: just as parents may permissibly — and indeed must — devote far more of their time, attention, and money to their own children than to other people's children, so citizens may devote far more of their collective resources to compatriots than to foreigners. The reading uses the analogy to support two concrete conclusions: (a) a wealthy state may cap refugee admissions well below the number it could accommodate without serious cost to its own citizens, and (b) it may spend vastly more per head on domestic poverty relief than on equally severe poverty abroad without violating any demand of justice.

Our section leader thinks the analogy is carrying both conclusions and cannot bear the weight. She offered a replacement that she considers more promising: compatriots as fellow participants in an ongoing joint enterprise whose goods no one could produce alone — something like the crew of a research station, or the members of a cooperative that manages a shared fishery — where the special claims arise from the cooperative undertaking itself rather than from affection or intimacy.

Could you audit both analogies? For each one, I'd like: an explicit mapping (a table is fine) saying what in the source plays the role of the citizen, the foreigner, the state's distributive scheme, the special claim, and the limit on that claim; a separation of the disanalogies that actually threaten (a) and (b) from those that are merely surface differences such as scale or emotional register; and an account of what each source quietly imports that the political target lacks.

Three specific questions I want the audit to settle, because I suspect they decide the outcome. First, is parental partiality itself constrained — by duties owed to other people's children, by conditions on when favoritism is permissible — in ways that would cap the political version even if the analogy otherwise holds? Second, in each source, is the claim-generating feature the relationship as such, or something particular that the parties have done within it, and does that difference matter when membership in the target is neither chosen nor easily exited? Third, can either analogy survive contact with the fact that the outsiders' options are partly shaped by rules and institutions the insiders help set and benefit from — the research-station case, for instance, presumably has no such feature, or does it?

Finally, tell me plainly whether either analogy can carry (a) and (b), whether it can carry only a weaker conclusion (and if so, which), and if some third analogy does better for whatever survives, say what it is and why it is an improvement rather than a restatement.
