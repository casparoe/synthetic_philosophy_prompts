I want a real case analysis here, not a vibe check, and it should run in both directions — theory on the episode, episode on the theory.

Episode: the 1939–41 Thanksgiving date fight. I'll dump what I've got so you're not guessing at the facts, but tell me if you think I've garbled any of it, since I typed this from memory and you cant look it up either.

- Washington proclaimed a Thanksgiving in 1789, but the national annual holiday is really Lincoln's, 1863, and it was preceded by roughly three decades of lobbying by Sarah Josepha Hale, who edited Godey's Lady's Book — at that point the biggest-circulation magazine in the country, something like 150,000 subscribers. Lincoln set it on the *last* Thursday of November and every president after him just followed the custom. Custom, not statute: for 76 years there was no law about it.
- 1939 was a five-Thursday November, so the last Thursday landed on Nov 30, leaving only about 24 shopping days before Christmas. Fred Lazarus Jr., head of Federated Department Stores, lobbied FDR to move it up a week, and in August 1939 FDR proclaimed Nov 23 instead. The stated reason was Depression-era retail sales; there was then a strong norm that stores didn't put up Christmas displays or run Christmas sales before Thanksgiving (the later erosion of that norm is what people now call Christmas creep).
- Backlash was immediate. Alf Landon, FDR's '36 opponent, called it "springing it upon an unprepared country with the omnipotence of a Hitler." The chairman of the selectmen of Plymouth, Massachusetts — Plymouth — heartily disapproved. The mayor of Atlantic City coined "Franksgiving." A late-1939 Gallup poll had Americans opposing the change 62–38, sharply partisan: Democrats favored it 52–48, Republicans opposed it 79–21.
- The country literally split. 23 states plus DC took Nov 23, 22 kept Nov 30, and Colorado, Mississippi and Texas declared both days holidays. Calendar makers had already printed the year. College registrars and the football season got wrecked, since a lot of teams ended their season with rivalry games on Thanksgiving and some conferences only permitted games through the Saturday after.
- He did it again in 1940 (Nov 21 vs. Nov 28), and by then 32 states plus DC went with him while 16 held out on what got called the "Republican Thanksgiving." Same split in 1941 (Nov 20 vs. Nov 27). A 1941 Commerce Department survey found the change hadn't meaningfully expanded retail sales. Then in December 1941, right after Pearl Harbor, Congress passed a joint resolution and FDR signed it, fixing Thanksgiving on the *fourth* Thursday of November permanently — which isn't the same as the last Thursday, because roughly two Novembers in seven have five Thursdays.

Now what I actually want:

1) Hobsbawm. The stock picture of an invented tradition is elites manufacturing ritual continuity to produce cohesion or legitimacy, always dressed in a fake past. FDR's move is close to the reverse: an old ritual, edited by the most powerful man in the country, for commercial coordination reasons, with zero antique dressing — he just announced it in August. So does the invention-of-tradition frame even apply? Is this an invention, a capture, a failed *dis*-invention, or something the framework has no slot for? My worry is that if one theory can equally describe Hale building a holiday and FDR failing to move it, its not buying me anything. Tell me plainly whether that worry is right.

2) The datum I find most interesting is the outrage. If the holiday's authority were just a story told downward by elites, the guy holding the pen should have been able to rewrite the story, and nothing legally stopped him. Instead 62% of the country treated a calendar date as sacred and non-negotiable, and the presidency eventually had to go get an act of Congress to change it — and even then only in the direction that ratified what had already happened on the ground. What does that say about how much work "invented" is doing in "invented tradition"? Is there a real asymmetry between installing a ritual and editing one, and what's the mechanism — perceived antiquity, sunk coordination, the fact that the date is a focal point everybody expects everybody else to hold? Related: how fast does the inventedness stop mattering? By the 1960s nobody remembered the fight and the fourth Thursday felt immemorial. Is there a principled line between "still invented" and "just a tradition now," or does the distinction collapse into "old enough that nobody alive remembers the decision"?

3) Anderson. The whole force of the day is simultaneity — everyone does the same thing on the same day, and the calendar is the medium that makes it possible. In 1939–40 that simultaneity broke, and what appeared in its place was a massive national conversation entirely about the same date, which arguably displays the imagined community better than an uneventful Thanksgiving would. Is that right, or am I just doing the thing where a theory gets credit for predicting its own counterexample? Also weigh this: Hale built the holiday through print in the first place, which is extremely Andersonian, while the state's proclamation failed to edit it. Does that mean print installs and executives can't? And is the timing of the congressional fix — December 1941, weeks after Pearl Harbor — evidence that this kind of ritual legislation needs a moment of peak national solidarity to go through, or is that me pattern-matching on a coincidence?

4) The commercial thread. Lazarus started it, the Commerce survey says it didn't work, and yet the biggest ritual now attached to the date — Black Friday — is younger than all of this, was never legislated by anybody, and is arguably more salient than the holiday it hangs off of. Which grows traditions better, markets or states? Give me a mechanism, not a slogan.

5) And the skeptical question I care about most: strip out Hobsbawm and Anderson entirely and just describe the Thanksgiving date as a coordination equilibrium / Schelling point that one mover tried to shift unilaterally and that ~62% of the players didn't want shifted. How much of the episode does that bare game-theoretic account eat, and what is genuinely left over that needs the nationalism-and-ritual machinery? If the answer is "almost nothing," say so.
