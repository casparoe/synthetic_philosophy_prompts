The Skokie affair is almost always taught as a First Amendment case. I want it read instead as a case in political philosophy, and specifically as a test of Popper's paradox of tolerance.

The facts, so that you need not reconstruct them. Between October 1976 and June 1978, Frank Collin's National Socialist Party of America — a group of perhaps thirty to fifty members, headquartered at Chicago's Marquette Park — attempted to stage a demonstration in Skokie, Illinois, then a suburb roughly 57 percent Jewish and home to some 8,000 Holocaust survivors. Chicago had already frustrated the group's Marquette Park demonstrations by demanding a $350,000 public-safety bond; Collin wrote to the park districts of the North Shore suburbs requesting permits, most ignored him, and Skokie answered. The village's first instinct was to permit the demonstration and ignore it, so as to afford the group as little publicity as possible; after organized objection from the Jewish community, the village council reversed course, sought an injunction against a march announced for May 1, 1977, and enacted three ordinances: a ban on military-style uniforms at demonstrations, a ban on distributing material promoting hatred, and the $350,000 bond requirement. The announced placards were to read "White Free Speech" and "Free Speech for the White Men." The ACLU took Collin's side, assigned him counsel, and litigated the injunction to the Supreme Court, which held 5–4 in June 1977 that a substantial First Amendment claim entitles a speaker to immediate appellate review; the federal appellate court later invalidated the ordinances. No march was ever held in Skokie. The group demonstrated in downtown Chicago and then at Marquette Park, under heavy police protection, before counter-demonstrations many times its size, without serious violence. The ACLU's decision cost it a large wave of resigning members and a substantial portion of its revenue — the figures usually cited are in the tens of thousands of members and the hundreds of thousands of dollars, though nothing below should turn on the precise numbers. Treat all of this as stipulated.

Now the theory. Popper's paradox appears in a note to the first chapter of *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945), and I want it handled as a decision procedure with a stated trigger rather than as a slogan. The operative passage:

> But we should claim the right to suppress them if necessary even by force; for it may easily turn out that they are not prepared to meet us on the level of rational argument, but begin by denouncing all argument; they may forbid their followers to listen to rational argument, because it is deceptive, and teach them to answer arguments by the use of their fists or pistols.

The surrounding text makes clear that this is a reserved permission rather than a default: Popper's stated preference is that intolerant views be answered by rational argument and held in check by public opinion, suppression being unwise for as long as that remains possible. Please work from the passage supplied and from your understanding of it, not from further sourcing.

1. **Roles.** Popper's schema contains two parties: a tolerant society and an intolerant movement. Skokie contains at least six: the NSPA; the village government; the survivor residents and their organizations; the ACLU as a private institution committed to viewpoint-neutral representation; the ACLU's resigning members; and the courts. Assign the roles. Which of these, if any, is Popper's intolerant movement, and which is his tolerant society? In particular, does the ACLU occupy that schema at all, or is it something the schema has no slot for?

2. **Three possible objects of "tolerance."** Keep apart (a) an individual's disposition not to interfere with views she despises, (b) a state's policy of non-suppression, and (c) an institution's procedural neutrality — defending anyone's rights irrespective of their views. Popper's argument is framed for (b), and his imagery of fists, pistols, and followers forbidden to listen is about mass movements contesting state power. Say which of the three is at issue at each stage of the episode — the injunction, the ordinances, the ACLU's decision to represent — and whether the paradox remains well-formed when it is applied to (c). If it equivocates, locate the equivocation precisely.

3. **The trigger.** Collin's group did not denounce argument. It filed suit, submitted briefs, sought expedited review, and won on constitutional reasoning; its placards were themselves a rights claim framed in the vocabulary of the regime it opposed. Is that meeting Popper on the level of rational argument, or is instrumentally using the machinery of argument while refusing the authority of reasons exactly the condition his trigger is meant to detect? Distinguish the two readings, apply the one you think correct, and commit to it.

4. **Capacity.** The harm Popper fears is the disappearance of tolerance, which requires a movement with a plausible route to power. Does the paradox have a threshold or a graded structure — is its trigger a matter of content, of capability, or of their product? If capability matters, what does the paradox deliver when applied to a thirty-member group in a constitutional democracy with professional police and functioning courts, and does that make Skokie a hard case for Popper or a case his paradox never covered?

5. **The inverted cost.** The only self-undermining effect visible in the episode ran against the tolerator: the ACLU bled members and money for acting on its principle. Is this the paradox operating in reverse — unlimited tolerance destroying the tolerance of the tolerant institution — or a different phenomenon, such as a principal–agent problem between an organization's leadership and its dues-paying membership? The distinction matters, since only one of the two readings yields a counterexample to Popper.

6. **The branch nobody took.** Skokie's original plan was non-suppression without counter-argument: it declined the very step that, on Popper's account, makes suppression unwise, and it reversed only under internal pressure from the party demanding engagement. Is "counter them by rational argument and keep them in check by public opinion" an obligation, a permission, or merely a prediction about what usually suffices? Where does an episode sit in which the tolerant party declines to argue while the state also declines to suppress?

7. **Outcome as evidence.** Separate the conceptual claim from the empirical one. The end state — a small march under police protection, dwarfed by counter-demonstrators, no serious violence, the group's fortunes unchanged, the principle entrenched in case law — is routinely cited as vindicating non-suppression. Does it vindicate Popper's own fallback branch, or leave the paradox untouched because the paradox was never a claim about groups that small? Say which features of the episode would have to change for the analysis to flip, and which are load-bearing throughout.

8. **Popper against Rawls.** Rawls holds in *A Theory of Justice* that an intolerant sect has no title to complain of intolerance, yet that its liberty may be restricted only when the tolerant sincerely and with reason believe their own security and that of the institutions of liberty are in danger. Set the two criteria against the same facts and say which produces the better account of what the village, the ACLU, and the courts should have done in 1977 — and whether the dispute between them concerns the trigger, the burden of proof, or something more basic.

Two constraints on the answer. First, be disciplined about the mapping and honest about silence: Popper's note says nothing about legal representation, nothing about private institutions, and nothing about movements too weak to threaten a state. Where it is silent, say so plainly instead of stretching the text over ground it does not reach. Second, and more important, take a position. I do not want a survey of defensible readings with the verdict left to me. Tell me whether the ACLU's decision was required by, permitted by, or contrary to Popper's proviso; whether Skokie confirms the paradox of tolerance, exposes an anomaly in it, or falls outside its scope entirely; and which single feature of the episode does the most damage to Popper's formulation. Give your reasons, and say what you would have to be shown to abandon the verdict.
