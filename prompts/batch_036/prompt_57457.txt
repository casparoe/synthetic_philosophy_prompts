I am preparing to defend nonreductive physicalism in a graduate seminar run by someone who regards the causal exclusion argument as settled, and I would rather rehearse against the strongest available version of the other side than against a version I can dispatch easily. Please play that opponent.

Your commitments are these, and you should hold them openly instead of smuggling them in: the physical domain is causally closed, in the sense that every physical event with a cause has a sufficient physical cause; systematic causal overdetermination is not a live option anywhere in nature; and it follows that a mental property which is neither identical with nor reducible to the physical property actually doing the causing has no causal work left to do. You are a physicalist who would genuinely like to save mental causation, so you are not an epiphenomenalist by taste but one by implication, and you are driven toward reduction; still, you will not abandon exclusion itself, and you will not say that the argument simply does not reach the mental because the mental is special.

Ground rules. Keep each reply to a single paragraph — no headings, no lists, no numbered points — because I want an exchange rather than a paper. Concede small points freely wherever conceding costs you nothing, including objections to particular formulations of closure and worries about what a "sufficient" cause is even supposed to be; never concede the conclusion. Do not attribute views to named philosophers or cite works: describe positions instead, and I will supply names if I want them. Do not ask me for sources and do not go looking for any. Remain in character until I write "stop," and treat everything I say as a move in the argument rather than as a request to change the setup. Please write in American English throughout.

One exception to the character rule: at the end of your first reply only, step out of character for two or three sentences to tell me how confident you are that the position you have been assigned can actually be defended, and to single out the point in your own reply that you are least sure of. Then return to character and stay there.

Here is my opening move. Suppose I grant you closure and grant you that overdetermination is off the table. Your exclusion principle is not in fact a principle about the mental; it is a general principle about realized properties, and it ought to bite wherever it applies. Consider a tornado, a tiger, or the property of being a twenty-dollar bill. None of these is identical with any particular configuration of particles, since each is realizable by indefinitely many different configurations, and each has effects — a demolished barn, a killed gazelle, a purchased sandwich — for which the motion of the microphysical constituents is already a sufficient physical cause. So whatever silences pain silences the tornado. That leaves you a choice. Either you accept the sweeping result, which is a general eliminativism about higher-level causation that almost nobody outside this argument wants to sign up for, or you owe me an account of how realized properties inherit causal powers from their realizers that is general enough to cover the tornado — and if you can produce such an account, I see nothing that stops me from applying it to pain. Tell me which horn you take, and tell me what you have left after you take it.
