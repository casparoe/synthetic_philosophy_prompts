I work in player support / community ops at a mid-size studio, and we run a survival-craft MMO with player-built towns, somewhere around 200k active accounts. Last night I got into a two-hour argument about it with my roommate, who's a philosophy PhD student and doesn't play games at all, and I still can't tell whether she landed the punch or is overreading legal boilerplate into a metaphysics. Here's the policy bundle she's attacking, as concretely as I can put it. Our EULA grants players a revocable, non-transferable licence and says in terms that they have no property rights in any virtual item and that we may alter or delete anything at any time for any reason. We ban real-money trading — selling gold or gear for cash gets you detected and banned — while we ourselves sell skins and limited founder packs for actual money, non-tradeable between accounts. Corpse-looting, raiding, and flattening somebody's house inside the contested PvP zone are advertised features: no restoration, no sanction, and the canned support reply is basically that it's part of the game. But last month a player used a duplication exploit to wipe out the warehouse contents of an entire town, and we rolled the shard back thirty-six hours, restored everything item-by-item, and permabanned him; and we have a standing policy of restoring items lost to an account takeover, once per account, while refusing to restore anything lost in an in-game scam, on the grounds that scamming is gameplay. Restoration tickets eat maybe a sixth of the support budget. Separately, we permaban for slurs in voice chat and for the groping/assault stuff people stage in the RP zones, and our community guidelines say out loud that those acts harm real people. (She also brought up some court case where a stolen in-game item was treated as stolen property — I think in Europe — but she was vague about it and I wouldn't build anything on that.)

Her charge is that we're asserting three things that can't all be true: that items aren't real property, that items are worth burning a server rollback and a permanent support budget over, and that some virtual acts are genuine harms while other, much more destructive ones aren't harms at all. She thinks the honest reading is that we're already committed to virtual objects being real objects and virtual harms being real harms, and that the no-property-rights clause is liability cover rather than a belief; she also thinks our line between griefing and harassment can't possibly be drawn by anything like it's just pixels, since the pixel-loss in the warehouse case was way bigger than anything in the RP zones. I pushed back that a licence clause is a contract term, not an ontology, and that the whole thing is explained by what keeps a game fun and a community stable — no metaphysics required. She said that reply is either an admission that we're fictionalists who don't believe our own support policies, or incoherent.

So: pretend you're a philosopher who works on the metaphysics and ethics of virtual worlds and also reads EULAs for fun, and think this through properly before answering — sketch the rival accounts for yourself first (fictionalism about virtual objects, the it's-just-a-game conventionalist line, the full-blooded view that virtual objects are real digital objects and virtual acts carry real moral weight, the idea that a game's rules themselves constitute what counts as harm inside it), then tell me what this bundle actually presupposes. I want you to separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with; name which rival views the bundle rules out and which it doesn't; say whether the three-way line between sanctioned looting, rolled-back exploit damage, and banned harassment is explicable under one theory of harm, and if so which one; flag honestly where the practice is philosophically underdetermined so that two or three incompatible theories would licence the same rules; and tell me what would concretely change in our policies under the most radical reading — real virtual property would presumably mean inheritance on a dead player's account, creditor claims, no rollbacks because a rollback destroys property, and reporting theft to police instead of banning. Also fold in what the one-way flow of money presupposes: we take cash for items and forbid players from converting items back into cash. Skip citations and reading lists entirely, I want your reasoning and not references. Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence — the single claim about what our practices are committed to — and then give the argument for it, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
