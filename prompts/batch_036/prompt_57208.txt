I work in the strategy unit of a national statistics office. Our minister has announced that the country will adopt a single official well-being target, and one adviser has proposed that the target be our rank in the annual World Happiness Report. My briefing has to sort out the possible positions on what such a ranking measures and what a government may legitimately do with it — but it is not meant to be an argument for or against the adviser's proposal. It is meant to be a classification instrument, so that the reader can locate their own view by answering questions rather than by choosing a label.

Please write a decision tree that takes a reader from an agreed starting point to a named position on whether a country should try to top the ranking. The starting point, which nobody in the room disputes: Gallup asks nationally representative samples in each country to think of a ladder whose top rung, 10, is the best possible life for them and whose bottom rung, 0, is the worst possible life for them, and to place their own life as it stands today on that scale; the Report ranks countries by a multi-year average of those answers alone, and separately reports how much of the variation across countries is accounted for by national income per head, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and perceptions of corruption.

Requirements:

1. Between eight and ten branch nodes. Every node must be a concrete case or a specific question about the measurement that a reader can answer without knowing which position the answer leads them to. No node may contain the name of a view, a theorist, or a technical term of art. Nodes phrased as "do you think well-being is X?" are disqualified; nodes phrased as "here are two respondents, here is what they were asked, here is what they answered — do they differ in how their lives are going?" are what I want.

2. Among the nodes, please make sure to include the following pressure points, in whatever order serves the tree best:
   - that the ladder's top and bottom rungs are defined by the respondent's own conception of the best and worst possible life, so the scale is anchored differently in different heads;
   - that the ladder asks for an evaluation of a life, not a report of how one felt during it, and whether those come apart;
   - a policy that raises the national average score while making the worst-off tenth of the population worse off;
   - a government that improves its average score partly by publicizing the ranking and encouraging citizens to think of their country as a happy one;
   - the fact that the explanatory decomposition attributes most of the cross-country variation to variables a government would pursue anyway for unrelated reasons;
   - whether the ranking is being used as a diagnostic to be read or as a target to be hit, and whether that difference changes its reliability.

3. The leaves must include, at minimum: someone who accepts the ladder as a serviceable measure of well-being and holds that maximizing the national average is the right aim of policy; someone who accepts the measure but rejects the averaging; someone who holds that the ladder measures the wrong thing even in the countries where it measures that thing accurately; someone who doubts that scores from different countries are comparable in principle rather than merely noisy; someone who thinks the ranking is a legitimate diagnostic that becomes an illegitimate target; someone who thinks the instrument is destabilized by its own publicity, so that acting on it changes what it measures; someone who thinks the ranking is really a wealth-and-trust ranking wearing a psychological label; and someone who wants a dashboard of indicators rather than a single ordered list. Add further leaves if the logic of your nodes generates them.

4. For each leaf, give a name for the position — an established one where there is one, a descriptive coinage where there isn't — and one sentence stating a commitment the position has to swallow that its advocates tend not to state out loud.

5. Where your nodes generate combinations of answers that no leaf occupies, list them and diagnose each: is that combination incoherent, merely unattractive, stable but neglected, or already occupied under a different name that your tree failed to route to?

6. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot actually separate — I expect at least one, and I would rather see it named than papered over. Say what additional question would separate them, if any does.

7. Close with a short paragraph identifying which single node does the most sorting work in your tree, and which node is closest to a matter of taste rather than of substance.

Audience: senior civil servants who are numerate, have no training in philosophy, and will not tolerate hedged generalities. Format: an indented outline for the tree itself, with each node's question in plain prose. Roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words. Do not include references, a bibliography, or attributions to particular authors or reports beyond what I have given you above; if you are unsure whether a distinction has a canonical name, describe it rather than naming it.
