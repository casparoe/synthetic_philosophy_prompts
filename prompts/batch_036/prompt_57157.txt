I am in the final year of a philosophy degree and am currently enrolled in a seminar on social epistemology. This week's topic was what one ought to do upon discovering that an acknowledged epistemic peer has reached the opposite conclusion on a question one has studied carefully. Our running first-order case was whether Frankfurt-style examples succeed in showing that moral responsibility does not require the ability to do otherwise. Two members of the seminar, whom I will call D. and A., disagreed about that question and then spent most of the hour disagreeing about what to do about disagreeing. I attended the whole exchange, took notes, and did not participate, so I have no stake in either position and I am not asking you to settle the Frankfurt question itself. I would like your help working out who was right at each stage.

Here is the exchange as I reconstructed it:

1. D. opened by saying that the discovery itself is decisive. Having established that A. has read the same literature, is equally careful, and has no incentive she lacks, she said she is obliged to treat A.'s conclusion as exactly as likely to be correct as her own, and therefore to move her credence to the midpoint of 0.5 rather than remain where she was. On her telling, the reasoning each of them did is now just one vote among two, and there is no non-question-begging way to keep one's own vote weighted more heavily.

2. A. replied that this cannot be right, because D.'s judgment that A. is her peer is itself a product of the very reasoning she has been told to discount. If D. genuinely treats A.'s conclusion as equally trustworthy, she must also treat A.'s assessment of the argument as equally trustworthy, and A. assesses it as sound; likewise she must give equal weight to every other careful person who happens to agree with A. D.'s position, he said, is not modesty but a refusal to use the only instrument she has, and if she is prepared to abandon that instrument on the strength of a bare difference of opinion, she cannot explain why she trusted it in the first place.

3. D. said A. had described the difficulty rather than dissolved it: yes, her assessment of A.'s competence depends on her own reasoning, but that is a reason for caution, not for confidence, since the same could be said of any belief whatsoever. She then raised what she took to be the fatal problem for her own view and asked whether A. could answer it. If one ought to conciliate in peer disagreement, and one then discovers that one's peers on the meta-question reject the conciliation principle, the principle instructs one to conciliate about the principle itself, so one should end up agnostic about the rule that generated the advice to go to 0.5. She said she accepts this and regards it as consistency rather than collapse.

4. A. said that a principle whose correct application leaves you with no reason to follow it is not a principle you can act on, and that D. had conceded his case while describing the concession as a virtue. He added that if the conciliation principle really licenses agnosticism about itself, then in any dispute where the parties have also disagreed about how to disagree — which is most of them — D. has no advice to give at all.

5. At this point a third member, R., said she sided with D. on both the meta-question and the Frankfurt question. A. immediately claimed this changed nothing for him: two people who reach the same conclusion after reading the same two papers constitute one line of reasoning instantiated twice, not two independent confirmations. D. answered that this is precisely the case her view handles neatly, since credence can be distributed across the disagreeing parties, with the share she retains falling as the number of disagreeers rises. A. responded by asking whether, if ten people had formed their beliefs by copying D.'s, she would really retain only one-eleventh of her original confidence, and observed that once the answer is negative, the operative variable is independence, and neither of them has an account of what makes reasoning independent.

6. D. conceded that independence matters but argued that A. cannot escape by appeal to it. His prescription is to re-examine the argument on its merits; but if the merits were discernible on examination, careful people examining them would not keep diverging, so the persistent divergence is itself evidence that the question admits more than one reasonable answer. And if it does, she said, both of them are already within their rights, which makes A.'s steadfastness and her conciliation the same advice under different names — his objection dissolves rather than being answered.

Neither would concede anything further.

Could you adjudicate this exchange move by move? For each of the six moves, please assign a rating on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification, and say explicitly wherever a move contains a valid point wrapped in an invalid inference. Then tell me who has the better of the exchange overall, and what, if anything, both D. and A. missed that a bystander could have raised at the time. Please resist the temptation to settle the Frankfurt question; it enters only insofar as it bears on the meta-dispute, and I am not asking for your view on it. If any of my reconstruction seems to turn on an assumption I have left unstated — for instance, about how much of D.'s evidence A. actually shares, or about whether the seminar counts as a single dispute or several — state the assumption you are adopting and note whether the rating would differ under a different assumption.

Finally, I would like you to be explicit about the fragility of your own verdicts. Identify the move you are least confident in rating and say what argument or consideration would move your rating of it; where you have rated a move clearly mistaken, say what a defender of that move would have to add for you to change your mind, and if you think no such addition is available, explain why not. Please also flag any point at which you suspect your assessment is being driven by which of the two disputants you find more sympathetic rather than by the arguments themselves.
