For eight years I taught constitutional theory to law students, and secession was a chapter I could deliver largely from memory. Four months ago it stopped being a chapter. I have been seconded to the joint technical committee that our regional government and the national government established after last spring's consultative vote, and we are drafting the financial and citizenship terms of a possible independence.

Our side's lead negotiator has a rhetorical habit that has become load-bearing. In nearly every session he frames secession as divorce, and specifically as no-fault divorce of the kind our own family law has permitted for decades. Three conclusions are drawn from that framing each time: that a polity cannot be compelled to remain in a union it no longer wants, so the national parliament's consent is legally unnecessary; that the departing party nevertheless owes an equitable division of jointly acquired assets and jointly incurred debt, which is how he defends the proposed severance schedule; and that the settlement should aim at a clean break ending fiscal dependency in both directions, which he uses to resist any open-ended transfer mechanism.

I have begun to suspect that the analogy is carrying more weight than it can bear, and that some of what it licenses is being smuggled in rather than argued for. I would like a careful audit of it.

Please set out the mapping explicitly: what in the divorce case plays the role of the two spouses, the marriage itself, the matrimonial property, the children, the court that holds jurisdiction over both parties, and the clean-break order. Then identify where the mapping fails, separating the failures that genuinely threaten his three conclusions from the ones that are merely inelegant. Two places interest me particularly. First, in a divorce each party leaves as an already competent adult; nothing in the analogy seems able to capture that a new state may or may not acquire the administrative and fiscal capacity to function, and I want to know whether that gap defeats the clean-break premise or only constrains it. Second, in a divorce neither ex-spouse retains membership rights in the other's household, yet our draft protocol contemplates precisely that: a portable status for residents of the region who voted against independence, allowing them to hold the national citizenship and the pension entitlements attached to it while living under the new state's law. Tell me whether the divorce frame has any resources for representing this, or whether it is simply invisible from inside the analogy, and what that implies about using the analogy to structure citizenship questions at all.

Finally, assess whether the three conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and if they do not, whether a better source domain exists. I have privately been toying with two: the corporate demerger, in which dissenting shareholders receive an appraisal right and the entity is reorganized rather than dissolved, and the housing cooperative, in which a member may withdraw but does not take the building along. Say which, if either, would carry his conclusions better, and specify what adopting each would force him to surrender.

Do not trouble yourself with citations or with surveying the literature; I know it and will supply the references. What I need is the conceptual audit.
