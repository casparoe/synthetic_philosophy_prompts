I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy, working on a chapter about Nietzsche's physiology, and I would like the thesis the chapter is built on to be stress-tested rather than defended.

The thesis is this. The diet, climate, and regimen material — above all the passages in Ecce Homo's chapter Why I Am So Clever dealing with nutrition, with place and climate, and with recreation, but also the great health passages in the preface Nietzsche added to The Gay Science in 1886 and in Book Five, and the physiological polemic running through Twilight of the Idols and The Antichrist — is philosophically load-bearing rather than biographical decoration or symptom. More precisely: Nietzsche treats the felt condition of his own body as an instrument of judgment, so his reports about how a food, a drink, a season, or an altitude affects him are evidence-bearing claims and not mere self-expression; and his diagnosis of decadence in morality, religion, and culture draws on that somatic evidential base rather than merely being illustrated by it. On my reading, if you delete the material, the revaluation loses a premise, not an ornament.

Three considerations are already said against it, and I want them treated as candidates, not assumed: first, that Nietzsche insists no single regimen can be prescribed for everyone and that his own prescriptions are indexed to his own physiology, which suggests the material is evidence about him rather than about value; second, that his own doctrine in Beyond Good and Evil §6 — that a great philosophy is a kind of involuntary and unconscious confession of its author, and that the moral intentions of a life are the seed of the whole plant — licenses exactly the deflationary reading of his diet chapter as memoir; third, that the regimen failed, since he stayed ill, worked through years of migraine, gastric crisis, and failing eyesight, and collapsed in Turin in January 1889 on any of the several retrospective diagnoses still in dispute.
