ok, slightly weird ask but it's for work. I write about the numbers-and-ideas beat for a magazine — forecasting, statistics, why smart people are wrong about the future — and one of my recurring sources is a guy named Harlan Draper, an ex-pension-fund actuary who runs a paid newsletter applying reference-class forecasting to great powers. He's the most disciplined outside-view person I know. His next column is on the obvious one: the rising power whose share of world manufacturing passed the incumbent's something like ten years ago. I've got about a day before he publishes and I want to get ahead of what he'll say — though honestly the better story might be whether his method can produce a call on this case at all.

His track record, from my notes over the years:

- A challenger whose industrial output had just overtaken the leader's, asked about back in the mid-2010s: he sorted it into the class of "powers that overtook the incumbent's manufacturing share," counted seven members since 1500, noted five of them ended up in a major war with the incumbent within thirty years, and called violent rupture likely.
- A big European power with collapsing fertility and a shrinking material share, but sovereignty pooled inside a deep institutional union with its old rivals: he flatly refused to forecast. Said the class contains nobody who voluntarily gave up sovereignty, so there's no frequency to compute — "you can't do a base rate on n=3."
- A personalist petro-state, heavy military spending, no succession rule, trivial share of world output: he forecast internal rupture within a decade off the class of personalist resource-dependent regimes, and said explicitly that material share was irrelevant to that question.
- A reserve-currency hegemon with a huge fiscal deficit: slow relative decline, no rupture. He called the deficit arithmetic "an inside-view story dressed up as data."
- A reader asked him to shade that last probability down because of a particular leader's conduct. He refused, and wrote that post-hoc adjustment for leadership is how people talk themselves out of base rates. Basically: classify, look up the frequency, stop.
- Except that in another column he said a base rate whose generating mechanism you can't state is numerology. And he once re-split the class "empires that lost a major war" into initiated versus dragged-into, arguing the two have different base rates and the old class was drawn too wide.
- He won't touch horizons under ten years, says base rates have no resolution at that scale.

The feature list he's told me he's working from for the new column: manufacturing share overtook the incumbent's about a decade back and is still climbing; median age shooting up after a long below-replacement stretch; the two are each other's biggest trade partners and both sit inside an economic rulebook the rising power helped draft and grew under; no overseas bases and no treaty allies that can project power; succession opaque, no published rule. And he's already flagged in print that in five of the seven overtaking cases, the two powers were in seperate economic blocs with a contested territorial frontier.

So — what rules is he actually running? I can see at least three candidates: a flat structural outside view where nothing after classification is allowed to move the number; mechanism-gated base rates, where the frequency only transfers if you can say what produced it; and classification-as-judgment, where you abstain when the class is too thin or too heterogeneous. They can't all be his rule, and I'm not sure I've split them at the right joints — if you think the real partition is different, say so. For each candidate, tell me what it makes him write next month. Then give me your best guess at the actual call and why you prefer that rule over the others given the record, and point at any verdict above that doesn't sit well with any of them. Finally: what would a discriminating case look like? I get him on the phone next week and I want the one hypothetical I can put to him that forces the difference out into the open.

To be clear I don't want your view on the country, or whether anything actually happens — just what he'll say and what he's committed to saying. And when you land somewhere, tell me how confident you are in it, plus the single step in your reasoning you're least sure of.
