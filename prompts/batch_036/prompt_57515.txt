ok so I'm a junior and I read the actual Les Misérables novel this summer (the giant one, not just the musical — though I know the musical cold too), and separately my social studies class did a unit on welfare conditionality, the deserving vs undeserving poor, and that old English "less eligibility" idea where relief is deliberately set below the worst paid job so nobody wants it. And now I can't stop reading the book through that lens. I have an essay due and I want to sanity-check my thesis before I build it out.

My thesis is kind of mean: Hugo isn't against the deserving/undeserving distinction at all, he just re-sorts who goes in which pile. Fantine fails every single moral test anyone applies to her — unwed mother, sells her hair and her teeth, then does sex work — and she ends up the novel's saint, so the test is obviously a fraud. But then the Thénardiers are poor too, and Hugo doesn't explain them by poverty at all, he explains them by wickedness. So has he actually dissolved the two-poor idea, or just moved the line to somewhere he finds more sympathetic? And Gavroche, who's a homeless kid living in a statue and cheerful about it — isn't that romanticizing child poverty in a way that would make him a poster boy for the "they're fine, they don't need help, it builds character" crowd?

Help me work through some specific things:
- the factory. Fantine gets fired for having a kid out of wedlock, and it's basically a moral character test attached to a job, run by Valjean's own overseer while he's distracted and doesn't really look into it. Is Hugo indicting conditionality there, or just indicting a bad manager?
- Valjean's 19 years for a loaf of bread. How much of that is literally less-eligibility logic — make the offender's condition worse than the worst free laborer's — and does the book know it's doing that?
- the Bishop's silver. It's a transfer with no means test, no behavioral condition, no follow-up, given to a guy who just robbed him, and it works. Is that Hugo's argument against conditionality, or is it grace, which can't be institutionalized — i.e. exactly the objection people make to building a welfare state on charity instead of entitlement?
- Javert. He's basically a sanctions officer who can't process an exception. Does his ending argue against rule-bound administration, or against mercy?
- Marius handing Thénardier money at the very end and Thénardier going off to do something awful with it, if I remember right. Is Hugo admitting unconditional transfers don't reform anyone?

And the philosophy part I actually care about: is Hugo a luck egalitarian avant la lettre, where Fantine gets compensation because her bad luck wasn't her fault and the Thénardiers don't because theirs was? I've read that Elizabeth Anderson thinks that whole framework is rotten because it forces you to prove you're innocent to get help and turns aid into a humiliating public audit of your character and your looks. Does that objection bite on Hugo himself — like, does he rescue Fantine by making her a spectacle of suffering, so she never actually has standing or agency in the book, she's just an emblem? Where's the line between Hugo the reformer and Enjolras at the barricade, and does the book ever endorse the barricade as the answer instead of relief?

Tell me which scenes I should build the essay around, where this lens strains or is just anachronistic (he wrote before any of this policy existed), and what the novel shows about conditionality that the policy arguments don't get. I don't need quotes or a bibliography, I have the book — I need to know whether my reading holds up or whether I'm forcing it.
