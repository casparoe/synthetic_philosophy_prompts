I'm running an upper-level formal epistemology seminar this spring — 12 students, one 90-minute block, mostly philosophy majors with two who've had a bit of CS. The day's topic is what happens to Bayesianism when the thing you're uncertain about isn't a fact about the world but a mathematical or computational question whose answer is already settled. I do not want to open with a lecture. I want them to walk in, do something, and get caught in it before anyone gives the problem a name.

So: build me a betting game for that session.

The shape of what I'm after:

- Everyone has to commit to actual numbers on a set of propositions, and the numbers have to be comparable and scored at the end. No real money, please — I'm not being the professor who paid his own seminar. Tokens, points, some grade-adjacent currency, whatever runs cleanly for twelve people.
- The propositions must all be about things whose answers are already fixed and available in principle to the students: no empirical unknowns, no future contingents, nothing anyone could reasonably call indeterminate. But nothing can be resolvable by a person sitting in a chair for three minutes. Phones and laptops stay in bags — that's non-negotiable, or the whole thing evaporates.
- I have to be able to settle every answer myself, at home, beforehand, with pencil and paper or at most a five-minute calculation, and I want to be able to resolve any dispute on the spot without booting up a machine.
- At least two of the propositions have to be equivilent to one another — or one has to strictly entail another — in a way the students will not notice while they're pricing them. This is the part I most want to land. I want to uncover it in the debrief and watch the room go quiet. Ideally the answers have been sitting in a sealed envelope on the table since minute one, so nobody can retreat to "well, there wasn't a fact of the matter."
- Somebody has to be able to exploit the resulting incoherence: a bookie, a market, a trading round, your call. But here's the wrinkle I want built into the mechanics rather than talked about afterwards — exploiting the incoherence should cost the exploiter real work. I want them to run into the fact that the usual no-arbitrage argument for coherence quietly assumes the bookie gets to see the logical connection for free, and that once seeing it costs something, being Dutch-booked stops looking like the clean indictment it's supposed to be.

Then give me: full rules and a materials list; a minute-by-minute timeline that fits in 90 including the debrief; the scoring scheme; and the answer key — or at least the recipe for building one, plus half a dozen concrete propositions with their answers actually worked out, since I'll want to swap in my own.

For the debrief, map the moments onto the claims. Where does the vocabulary finally arrive — I'm fine with them not hearing terms like logical omniscience until after they've been burned. And write me answers for the two students who show up every single time: the one who insists they weren't really uncertain, they just hadn't done the calculation yet, so nothing weird happened; and the one who concludes that since thinking moves your credence without any new evidence coming in, the whole conditioning story is broken and we should all go home. Both of those need a real response, not a brush-off.

Finally, be honest with me about the limits. Which feature of the actual problem does this game fail to reproduce, or actively distort, such that I'm going to have to supply it by hand afterwards? I'd much rather know going in than have it come out in the course evals.

American English, please. And if this genuinely can't fit twelve people into 90 minutes, say so and hand me the cut-down version instead of padding the timeline — I'd rather run a smaller game that works.
