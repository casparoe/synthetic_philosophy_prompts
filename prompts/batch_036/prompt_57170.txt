I am a software engineer. My working life has been distributed systems: consensus protocols, sharding, load balancing, service discovery, and the observability pipelines through which you infer the state of a system that no single component ever sees in full. I have no formal training in philosophy or economics, and what I know of the socialist calculation debate comes almost entirely from secondhand summaries and blog posts, so please assume nothing beyond that.

Here is what I would like. Translate the debate — Mises's claim that rational economic calculation is impossible under common ownership of the means of production, Hayek's claim that the real problem is knowledge that is dispersed, local, and largely inarticulate, and Lange's reply that a Central Planning Board could set prices parametrically, hand plant managers rules for cost minimization and output, and revise prices by trial and error until markets cleared — into the vocabulary I actually use. Concretely: processes and nodes, partial observability, message-passing versus shared state, telemetry aggregation and compression, gossip and epidemic propagation, consensus, single-writer architectures, control plane versus data plane, feedback controllers and fixed points, simulators and mocks, and the distinction between a value that is merely computed and one that is causally load-bearing for the component reporting it.

I want the translation done systematically, not impressionistically. For each of the three positions, state what its premises become, what its conclusion becomes, and whether the argument still goes through once it has been restated that way. Then give me the residue report, which is the part I care about most: for every claim you handled, classify it as translating cleanly, translating only with distortion (say precisely what was distorted), or having no counterpart at all in my vocabulary. For the second and third categories, tell me whether the failure is on my side — the systems vocabulary missing something, and what — or on theirs, in that the economists' formulation was less determinate than it sounds.

Three points on which I want you to be blunt, because I suspect my own framing is smuggling in assumptions and calling it translation:

First, I have been telling colleagues that the price system is essentially a gossip protocol — massively redundant, low-bandwidth, eventually consistent propagation of local state — and that central planning fails because it is a single-writer architecture attempting to serialize what is inherently concurrent. My guess is that this captures Hayek and misses Mises entirely. Is Mises's claim about the absence of an information channel, or about the absence of a well-defined objective function: not that the planner cannot collect the relevant data, but that under the institutional arrangement in question there is no data of the relevant kind to collect? If so, my vocabulary may have no way of expressing "there is no such quantity," as opposed to "the quantity exists but is hard to obtain."

Second, Lange's proposal looks to me like a tâtonnement loop run against a simulator: the auctioneer is a control-plane process, prices are entries in a configuration store, managers are workers obeying rules derived from those entries, and the iteration is a controller hunting for a fixed point. My objection, as an engineer, would be that simulating a mechanism does not preserve the mechanism's behavior once the messages it exchanges stop having consequences for the parties sending them — an optimizer driving a mock service that never commits a transaction. Tell me whether that is a fair restatement of the objection actually pressed against Lange, or whether I have quietly converted a conceptual argument into an empirical one about incentives, which is a very different kind of claim.

Third, where does entrepreneurship go? I can express discovery protocols, health checks, and adaptive routing, all of which presuppose a space of possibilities already enumerated and searched. I cannot tell whether the discovery of opportunities that were not previously representable to anyone is expressible in my vocabulary at all, or whether everything I would call discovery is really just search.

Finish with a short verdict on whether the residue is predominantly a defect of my framework or evidence that the debate, as its participants framed it, was less substantive than it appeared. I would rather be told that the translation is doing violence to the argument than be reassured that the analogy holds. Please do not include a bibliography or citations; name positions and arguments and I will look them up myself.
