I teach an upper-level political philosophy seminar — 18 students, one two-hour block, they've had a lecture on transitional justice and skimmed something or other, so assume basically no priors. I want to run an activity that makes them feel the difference between a blanket amnesty cut by elites over everyone's heads and an individualized, truth-conditional one of the sort South Africa's amnesty committee ran — and then feel that the second one isn't the free lunch it sounds like, since it buys a public record by letting named people walk, and the record it produces is skewed toward whatever applicants found it useful to admit.

Design me the thing. Roles with private briefings (who knows what, who physically controls the archives, who can still make trouble), the mechanics, a minute-by-minute timeline for the two hours, and materials I can print — keep seperate role sheets so people can't read over each other's shoulders. I want some payoff or stakes scheme that doesn't need real money. And I want the bargain to be structurally forced rather than argued: a finite number of prosecutions the new state can actually afford, evidence that's unevenly distributed, and some card or resource the outgoing security apparatus is holding that makes the transition itself contingent — so they discover on their own that the price of amnesty is set by power, not by moral desert.

Two things baked in. First, a mid-game envelope: partway through, one side gets a card saying an international court has just claimed jurisdiction over the worst category of offender, so the domestic deal no longer fully protects them. I want the game to visibly reprice truth-telling at that moment and shift incentives down the chain of command — roughly the confession-and-sentence-reduction dynamic you get with Rwanda's lay village courts — and I want them to run into it rather than be told it. Second, I keep a facilitator copy of the ground truth of what actually happened in the fictional country, and at the end we compare it against the official record the class produced. The gap is the punchline.

Then the debrief: map specific moments onto the concepts, tell me what predictably breaks the first time I run it — where the coercive card gets played too early, where the victims' delegation gets steamrolled and just goes quiet, where they strike a deal no real transition would ever have gotten — and how to fix each one. Be honest about which feature of the real cases the activity can't reproduce and I have to supply by hand.

Keep it a made-up country with made-up groups. I've had students from affected places in past cohorts and I dont want to turn anyone's actual family into a prop. Tell me what to do if one of them signs up anyway, and give me a stripped-down 50-minute, 8-person version in case my room gets cut.
