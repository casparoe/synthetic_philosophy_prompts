so i TA the discussion section for our undergrad metaphysics course and my prof asked me to write the practice final for my own section, which is kind of terrifing. One of the four questions has to be on the identity of indiscernibles. Problem is, every version I can think of can be answered by someone who memorised the setup of Black's two-sphere universe and then wrote the words "haecceitism" and "brute fact" somewhere in it. My prof says she wants a question where reciting the dialectic gets you a bare pass at best.

Here's the twist I came up with and I want the question built around it. Call them W1 and W2. W1 is the familiar one: nothing exists except two exactly similar iron spheres — same diameter, same colour, same temperature — a fixed distance apart in an otherwise empty universe, and nothing about them ever changes. W2 is the same, except that after a while one of the spheres picks up a small dent and keeps it forever; otherwise it's just as empty and just as symmetric as W1.

I want three parts, 20 marks total, doable in about 45 minutes (students answer two of the four questions in two hours). A short part on whether the spheres in W2 are discernible and if so by what. A middle part where the paper hands them an argument — since W2 is just W1 with a dent added, and in W2 the spheres are obviously two, Black's world never threatened the principle to begin with — and asks them to evaluate it. And a longer part on what W2 actually licenses you to say about W1, including the awkward thing that you can't even state W2 without saying which sphere got the dent.

Please write: (1) the question exactly as it'd appear on the paper, with one line telling students they don't have to cite or name anybody; (2) a model answer of about 650 words that commits to a view instead of concluding that both sides have a point; and (3) a marking scheme in two bits — a rubric naming the four or five moves a top answer makes and how the marks split across them, plus five specific mistakes that should cost marks, each with one line saying what confusion it reveals. At least two of the mistakes should be cases where the student reaches for a move that looks clever but doesn't work in a world with nothing in it except two spheres: invoking the property of being identical to a, say, or insisting that being-a-fixed-distance-from-the-other-one already does the job. Also worth flagging in the rubric: an answer that treats the dent as showing the principle is the kind of thing you could confirm or disconfirm by looking should lose marks, and i want the rubric to say why.

We never did any formal stuff beyond the second-order statement of the principle, so nothing should require notation — but a good answer should be able to say why "has all the same properties" can't just be cashed out as a very long list, and the model answer needs to handle that rather than assume it away.
