I am the TA for an upper-level formal epistemology seminar (18 students; one term of probability and decision theory assumed, no measure theory). Lectures covered de Finetti's coherence framework, including his position on the additivity requirements, the diachronic Dutch book argument for conditionalization, and Joyce's accuracy-dominance argument. I have to mark the following problem-set essay, which is worth 20% of the course mark and carries a 1,200-word limit. We mark on a 100-point scale: 70 and above for an answer that is precise about the formal claims and identifies at least one substantive limit on the argument's reach; 60–69 for a solid answer with some imprecision; 50–59 for an answer that addresses the question but with gaps or errors; below 50 for one that misses parts of the question.

**The question as set**

De Finetti held that a set of betting quotients is *coherent* just in case no Dutch book can be made against it, and argued that coherence amounts to conformity with the probability calculus.

(i) State the synchronic Dutch book theorem as precisely as you can, and identify the assumptions it needs about the agent's betting dispositions, as distinct from the assumptions it needs about arithmetic.
(ii) Explain why the theorem alone does not yield de Finetti's conclusion, and what the converse theorem adds.
(iii) Describe the diachronic (dynamic) Dutch book argument for conditionalization, and say what exactly it exploits.
(iv) Assess one objection to the claim that Dutch-bookability shows incoherent credences to be epistemically, and not merely practically, defective.

**The submitted answer**

(i) The synchronic Dutch book theorem says that if an agent's betting quotients over a set of propositions fail to satisfy the probability axioms — non-negativity, normalization, and finite additivity over mutually exclusive propositions — then there is a finite set of bets at those quotients which guarantees the agent a net loss, no matter which of the relevant possibilities obtains. De Finetti called a set of quotients immune to such a book *coherent*, so the theorem is in effect the claim that incoherence just is vulnerability to a finite sure-loss book. Ramsey had already gestured at the same idea, though he put the weight on representing an agent's partial beliefs by a probability function recovered from their preferences rather than on the book itself.

The theorem needs assumptions about the agent, not only about arithmetic. First, the agent's quotients must track their credences: the bookie has to be able to price bets at what the agent is disposed to accept, and the agent must be willing to take either side at that price. Second, the stakes must be small enough that utility is linear in money, since if the agent's utility for money is concave a set of bets that is sure-loss in dollars need not be sure-loss in utility and the construction can fail. Third, the bookie must know the quotients and get to choose the direction of each bet. Fourth, the argument relies on what is called the package principle: if the agent would accept each bet separately, they should accept them together, since the joint payoff is the sum of the individual payoffs.

(ii) The theorem as stated runs only from incoherence to exploitability. It does not by itself rule out the possibility that some coherent agents are exploitable anyway, and that is what the converse theorem establishes: if the quotients satisfy the axioms, then no finite book exists against them however the bookie arranges the bets. Taken together the two directions give an exact characterisation — exploitability and incoherence turn out to be the same property — and this is why de Finetti could treat the axioms as articulating what consistency of partial belief consists in, rather than as one further constraint imposed on belief from outside.

(iii) Lewis's diachronic argument extends the strategy to updating. The bookie sells the agent a bet on H priced by the agent's prior credence in H, and separately arranges a bet that pays only if the agent comes to learn E, priced by the agent's posterior credence in H once E has arrived. If that posterior is not the prior conditionalized on E, the two bets can be combined so that the agent loses in every outcome; if the posterior is exactly the conditional, no such combination exists. What matters is that the argument does not rest on any assumption linking the agent's present conditional credences to their later ones, and the bookie does not need to know in advance how the agent will update: because the second bet is priced only after the evidence comes in, any actual departure from conditionalization suffices to generate the book. In that sense the reasoning licenses the reflection principle rather than being licensed by it. It delivers conditionalization only where the agent becomes certain of E; for evidence that arrives short of certainty the argument has to be generalised, as Jeffrey's rule generalises it, and it is a further question how much of its force survives that generalisation.

(iv) The most serious objection is that the argument proves too much about betting and too little about belief. What it establishes is a norm of practical consistency: an agent disposed to accept all these bets loses money, and losing money is bad. But it is a further step from *your betting dispositions are jointly disadvantageous* to *your beliefs are epistemically defective*. Someone might hold incoherent credences and simply decline to bet, or bet only where the stakes are negligible; the loss is then real but purely pragmatic, and it is not obvious that a norm whose violation shows up only in the wallet governs belief as such.

This is why the accuracy-dominance arguments are usually taken to vindicate the same conclusion better. They substitute a measure of distance from the truth for money and show that every incoherent credence function is dominated — some coherent alternative is closer to the truth in every possible world — while no coherent function is dominated. Since accuracy is plausibly what belief as such aims at, the normative force is now epistemic rather than pragmatic and the objection above loses its grip. The cost is a new dependency: the result requires the inaccuracy measure to be strictly proper, and strictly proper measures are chosen rather than derived, so the question of why one should care about coherence is relocated to the question of why one should care about accuracy in that specific sense.

One further point about de Finetti's notion of coherence. It is sometimes said that he demanded only finite additivity, but in fact the book construction reaches failures of countable additivity too: an agent who assigns zero to each member of a countable family of mutually exclusive hypotheses while assigning their disjunction some positive probability can be exploited by a finite set of bets, and de Finetti regarded such merely finitely additive assessments as defective for precisely that reason.
