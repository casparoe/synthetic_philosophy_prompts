For the past fifteen years I have worked on the replication layer of a distributed database: consensus protocols, failure detectors, consistent global snapshots, quorum intersection, linearizability arguments, and the impossibility results that discipline all of them. I can state why common knowledge cannot be established over an unreliable channel, I am fluent in the hierarchy of unreliable failure detectors and in what each class can and cannot be used to solve, and I write safety-versus-liveness arguments for a living. My philosophy background is close to nil. This has recently stopped being an abstract limitation. A few months ago we had a partition incident in which two nodes each behaved as though they held a leadership lease, and the postmortem turned, uncomfortably, on the difference between what was true of a node's state and what the node could be said to know about it. What began as an internal talk on that incident has grown into a paper for a workshop on knowledge and coordination in distributed systems, and in preparing it I fell into the epistemological literature on introspection, where I found two ideas I cannot map onto anything in my own framework.

The first is the KK principle: the claim that if one knows something, one is thereby also able to know that one knows it — a claim that some epistemic logicians took to follow from treating knowledge as a sufficiently strong or robust state, and that others reject. The second is luminosity: a condition is luminous when, whenever it obtains, the subject is able to know that it obtains; and there is an influential argument, associated with Williamson, that no non-trivial condition is luminous. As best I can reconstruct it, that argument runs by considering a slow, incremental series of cases — the usual illustration being something like gradually ceasing to feel cold as the day warms — together with a safety-style premise to the effect that if one knows the condition obtains at one point in the series, the condition must still obtain at the next point, so that the failure of knowledge propagates back down the series. The advertised upshots are that the mind is not transparent to itself and that certain internalist demands on justification cannot be satisfied. I would like you to take these claims one at a time — KK itself, the case for it that turns on knowledge being a strong state, the definition of luminosity, the incremental-series argument, the safety premise, and the conclusion that a subject cannot survey its own knowledge — and restate each in the vocabulary I described above. I have candidate mappings I want you to test rather than adopt: is KK anything like the trivial fact that a process can always read its own local state, or does the analogy break exactly there, since my models stipulate self-access instead of earning it? Is luminosity roughly the existence of a failure detector of adequate strength for a given predicate? Is the safety premise a claim about resolution — that a sensor whose granularity is coarser than the variation it must track cannot certify which side of a threshold it sits on? Is the sorites-shaped series structurally akin to the impossibility results I know, which typically show that no protocol can behave correctly on both sides of a boundary? And is the two-generals result an analogue of anti-luminosity, or is it about a different phenomenon altogether — agreement among several parties rather than one party's access to its own state?

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. For each claim, label it as translating cleanly, translating only with a fudge (saying precisely what the fudge consisted in), or having no expression at all in my framework. Be explicit about what gets left behind: does my vocabulary have any way of describing a subject that is genuinely in a state yet has no means, even in principle and with unlimited local computation, of coming to know that it is in it? Guard against smuggling my assumptions in and handing them back to me as the philosophers' position — in particular, do not quietly suppose that every interesting predicate in my sense is locally decidable, or that whatever is knowable is knowable by reading state. Finally, tell me where the fault lies. Where the translation fails, is that a genuine expressive deficit on my side, one I might close by enriching my models — perhaps with a sharper separation between truth in a global state and detectability from inside one — or is it evidence that some of these philosophical claims were less determinate than they first sounded? And run the translation in reverse where you can: is there anything from my corner, such as the limits of what a failure detector can certify, or the fact that global properties like snapshot consistency can obtain without being locally detectable, that ought to make the philosophers sharpen or abandon part of their argument? I do not want references, quotations, or a survey of the literature; I will chase sources down myself. What I need is the mapping, the residue, and your judgment about which of the two frameworks is losing something in the exchange.
