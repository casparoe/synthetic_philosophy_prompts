Below is the interpretive framework I have settled on for explaining the conduct of the men of Reserve Police Battalion 101, and before I write it up for a workshop on the social psychology of atrocity I want to know whether its parts can stand together. My commitments:

1. Character, in the sense that virtue ethics requires it, does almost no explanatory work. What the classic obedience and prison experiments establish is that conduct in extreme settings is a function of the setting: legitimate authority, routinization, peer pressure, and cumulative brutalization do the work normally assigned to temperament.

2. Browning's account in Ordinary Men is far better supported than Goldhagen's in Hitler's Willing Executioners. The battalion was recruited from Hamburg and its environs, its members averaged close to forty, most were working-class, and only a minority held party membership; these were not committed Nazis. What happened at Józefów and in the actions that followed therefore has to be explained out of the situation, not out of a prior ideological endowment.

3. Explanatory appeals to a monolithic eliminationist antisemitism, or to German political culture as such, are inadmissible: they cannot be falsified, and the social composition of the battalion is precisely the evidence that counts against them.

4. At the same time, I find the re-analysis of Milgram's own archival material persuasive. Participants did not slide into the passive agentic state that Milgram's published account posits; they cooperated because they identified with the scientific purpose of the enterprise and came to see the learner as an obstacle to it. Obedience here is engaged and willing rather than compelled.

5. A dozen or so men took Trapp's offer to step out at Józefów, and others avoided participation in later actions. I accept that those who declined faced no formal sanction, and I also hold that they cannot be distinguished from their comrades by age, class, party affiliation, or anything else recoverable from the record. I therefore refuse to explain the refusers by appeal to character either: refusal was situational as well.

6. In light of the replication problems in experimental social psychology, and of what the archival work has revealed about the gap between Milgram's procedures and his published account, I place little weight on the precise obedience rates those studies report.

7. An adequate explanation of perpetration must account for variation among individuals who shared the same situation, and not only for the aggregate outcome.

Which of these genuinely conflict, and which only appear to? Please order the conflicts you identify by how damaging they are rather than presenting them as a flat list of equal worries, and for each one state what has to be surrendered and what the least expensive revision would cost me in evidential or explanatory terms. Two further questions: in what respects have I been stricter than Browning himself, given what his own list of contributing factors contains; and which single revision would clear the largest amount of trouble without abandoning the situational project altogether?
