I am a retired limnologist — some thirty-five years of measuring lakes — and I have taken up political philosophy seriously only since I stopped working. For two years I have served as the lay member of a county Regulatory Review Panel (population served: roughly four hundred thousand). Twice a month a proposed rule reaches us and we must attach a note to it before it goes to the elected board: two pages, prepared in an afternoon, by a retired school principal, a contractor, a nurse, and me, none of us trained in law or philosophy.

The note is meant to say what the rule does to people's freedom. In practice we flounder, because the word is not doing any work. One member hears freedom as the absence of obstacles and concludes that a rule nobody has yet enforced is costless; another hears it as control over one's own affairs and concludes the same rule is emancipatory. Reading Berlin on the two concepts and Pettit on non-domination, I have come to suspect we are not disagreeing about facts at all, but scoring proposals with different instruments while giving the readings one name.

So: build the instrument. Write a decision procedure our panel can run on any proposed rule in a single afternoon, from the sheet alone, without having read anything. Tell me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, how to record the finding in language a board member can read, and where the procedure must stop and hand the matter to a person because the question is one of values rather than description. Be explicit about which distinctions in the literature must survive the simplification because the verdict turns on them, and which can be dropped. It has to handle three kinds of hard case: rules that restrict choice but end someone's dependence on another's goodwill; rules whose burden consists in a discretionary power that, so far as anyone knows, has never been exercised against anyone; and rules defended on the ground that they make people capable of governing themselves. And since I am by temperament a counter of obstacles who trusts only what he can measure, build in a check for whether the procedure's own wording has quietly pre-selected one conception of freedom.

Then run it on three live proposals: a permit office empowered to waive a setback requirement in its unreviewable judgment, with criteria promised at some future date but none published; a licensing requirement for well drillers, with a written examination and appeal to a panel that includes practitioners; and a rule obliging landlords to renew tenancies absent enumerated cause, enforceable by the tenant directly. At least one of these should come out against my instincts — say which, and do not soften it.

Finally, report your own confidence. Tell me how sure you are that the procedure states what these theories actually say rather than a convenient approximation of them, and single out the one step you would most expect a philosopher to reject.
