I moderate a podcast on democratic theory, and next month's guest is a political theorist who defends a specific institutional design rather than sortition in the abstract. His proposal, as he has laid it out in talks, is this: the upper house of a bicameral legislature is abolished and replaced by a Citizens' Chamber of 240 members, drawn by stratified lot from the electoral register (stratified on sex, age band, region, education level, and income decile), serving a single non-renewable four-year term at full salary with childcare and travel costs covered. Service is compulsory on the model of jury duty, with narrowly drawn hardship exemptions. The Chamber holds an absolute veto over ordinary legislation, exclusive power to initiate constitutional amendment, and no power over supply. It sits in committees whose composition is itself determined by lot; party groups are prohibited; members choose their own expert witnesses by majority vote from a list compiled by a permanent, unelected clerical service. The guest is known for two characteristic moves: he answers every objection about the competence of amateurs by appealing to the transformation of preferences under good deliberative conditions, and he answers every objection about accountability by insisting that authorization and accountability are separable, so a body need not be elected in order to be answerable.

Please draft nine to eleven interview questions for a recorded conversation of about an hour. The questions must be anchored in the design as specified: each one should name the particular feature of the proposal it is pressing (the compulsory-service clause, the clerical list, the four-year term, the absolute veto, the stratification variables, the prohibition on party groups), not sortition as a general ideal. Under each question, give one sentence stating the reply he is most likely to make, and then a follow-up that meets that reply rather than repeating the original point. Please include at least the following lines of attack, distributed across the set and not all bunched at the end:

- The response problem. Compulsion plus hardship exemptions means the people who decline are not a random subset; ask him to distinguish the properties his stratification actually guarantees from those the acceptance process silently undoes, and push him to commit to a threshold of service uptake below which he would concede the Chamber is not a sample at all.
- The capture problem, run through his own clerical service. His answer will be that randomly chosen members have no donors, no careers, and no party whip; the follow-up should note that the absence of repeat play also removes the forward-looking sanction that disciplines an elected chamber, so the only monitor of a four-year amateur body is a permanent bureaucracy that is neither elected nor sorted — and ask whether the clerical service is then the real legislature, and if so why that is better than openly having one.
- The term-limits reductio. His design is the limiting case of the term-limit reformer's program, and the standard complaint about term-limited legislatures is institutional amnesia plus dependence on staff and outside interests. Ask him what in his design prevents the complaint from applying with maximum force, and press the reply that four years is long enough to acquire expertise by asking where that expertise comes from if not from the permanents who brief him.
- The Athenian transfer. Athens paired the lot with entry vetting, post-office audit, one-year non-repeatable tenure, a presidency that rotated by the day, and an assembly of the whole citizen body sitting above the sorted council. Ask whether his evidence is the lot or the lot together with that apparatus, and — if he answers that Athenian accountability was ex post and non-electoral, which is precisely his point — ask him to add the corresponding devices to his own design and say how much of the argument survives once accountability, rather than random selection, is doing the work.
- Presence versus pivotalness for minorities. Stratified sortition delivers a group its population share of seats and nothing more, whereas elections can make a geographically concentrated or strategically positioned minority decisive and therefore worth bargaining with. Ask why proportional presence should be preferred to pivotal power; if he answers that presence changes what counts as a reason in the room, ask for the causal mechanism and for how he would distinguish genuine persuasion from the majority's preference being restated in more presentable language — and whether he would accept a design granting small groups more than their share of seats, which would break the mirroring claim he relies on elsewhere.
- The ratification record. The citizens' assemblies on electoral reform in British Columbia and Ontario were randomly selected, deliberated at length, and produced recommendations that the electorate then rejected, in one case falling short of the supermajority threshold set for it and in the other losing decisively. If he answers that we should judge the output rather than the uptake, and that the failure lay in handing the last word back to voters, note that his own design removes ratification entirely for the veto and makes the amendment-initiative self-executing, and ask him to walk through what happens when the Chamber vetoes an emergency or deeply unpopular bill with no possibility of appeal.
- Framing power. Members vote on the witness list and the committee charge, but both are drafted by someone else, in policy areas where they have four years of exposure and no prior training. Ask what makes a choice from a staff-drafted menu autonomous rather than merely unconstrained.
- The halfway house. If random selection tracks the public's judgment better than election does, why stop at one chamber and leave the central bank, the courts, and the epidemic-response agency to appointment? His answer will be that legislatures settle contested values while technocratic bodies settle means; ask him to take a live case in which the technical parameter just is the moral question — a debt brake, a capital ratio, a coverage threshold — and say which body gets it and by what criterion.
- The exclusion built into the sampling frame. The frame is the electoral register, so the Chamber mirrors the electorate rather than everyone subject to the law: children, resident non-citizens, and the disenfranchised are governed but cannot be drawn. If he answers that the frame should be widened, ask what one stratifies on when no register exists for those groups, and whether mirroring the population rather than the electorate severs the connection to the demos that authorizes law in the first place.

Close the sequence with a question about disconfirmation: ask him to name a comparison that could be run before adoption — the same bill put to the sorted Chamber and to the elected lower house under identical testimony and identical time — and to commit in advance to the criterion on which he would judge the result, and to a result that would make him withdraw the proposal rather than reinterpret it. Do not accept a metric that can only be assessed after the fact by looking at whether he likes the decisions.

After the questions, add a short paragraph explaining the order you have chosen: which objections you open with to establish that you have understood the design, and where you place the questions that depend on answers he has already given.

Two constraints on the deliverable. First, no generic interview material — nothing about what drew him to the subject, how he responds to critics in general, or where he sees the movement going; every question must be one that only this proposal would prompt. Second, do not supply or request references: no titles, no author lists, no suggested further reading, and no quotation marks around anything attributed to a real person, since I will be reading these aloud and cannot verify attributions.
