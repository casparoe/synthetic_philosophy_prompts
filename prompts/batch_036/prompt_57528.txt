I am a second-year philosophy student on a module in the history and philosophy of science, and our tutor has asked each of us to run one slot in the week-eight student-led revision session. I have taken the Lakatos slot: sixteen second-years, ninety minutes, a seminar room with four tables, two whiteboards, and no budget whatsoever, so any payoff scheme has to be in points rather than money.

Please design an in-class game in which the teams actually operate rival research programmes rather than talk about them. Each team should receive a fictional core thesis and then a stream of anomalies distributed round by round. Invent the subject matter yourself, and do not use Ptolemaic epicycles, phlogiston, or continental drift: half the room already knows the punchline to those from lectures. The scoring must render the difference between an adjustment that merely saves the phenomenon and one carrying surplus content mechanically visible. I would like teams to have to commit in writing, before the next round's data are revealed, to whether their new auxiliary hypothesis entails anything not already on the table, using sealed envelopes or some equivalent device, so that the distinction emerges from their own paperwork rather than from a definition. Please also build in a late round in which the programme that looked degenerating at the halfway point overtakes its rival, so that the students feel the retrospective character of the appraisal, and feel the force of the charge that criteria of this kind are permissive enough to license almost anything.

Give me the rules, a round-by-round timeline, a one-page scorecard I can print for each team, a materials list, and a debrief that maps particular moments of play onto the vocabulary of the hard core, the negative and positive heuristic, and the protective belt, with nobody having heard those terms explained beforehand. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this with sixteen people who have skimmed the reading, and be frank about what the game cannot reproduce — I suspect it cannot capture that a genuine anomaly is not a card handed over by a facilitator, and that a bold conjecture may be corroborated only decades after the researcher has died. Say what I must supply in the debrief to cover that gap.
