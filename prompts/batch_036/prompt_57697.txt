So — 40 minute talk at a department that's basically a behavioral ecology / cooperation lab with a philosophy-of-biology reading group bolted onto the side of it. Call it eight evolutionary biologists and psych people who work on cooperation and fairness, two who do cultural evolution and dual-inheritance modeling, one philosopher of biology who works on functions and teleosemantics, and a single moral psychologist who is politely skeptical of everything including her own data. Nobody in the room does metaethics. I've given versions of this to philosophers three times and the Q&A was all relitigating the tracking story and whether the coincidence argument begs the question; I expect literally none of that here, so I need to prep for a different set of questions entirely.

The thesis is an evolutionary debunking argument against moral realism that never uses the words "reliability" or "sensitivity." Sketch, so you have something for the objections to bite on. Call it the inertness test. Take a complete geneology of some moral belief — mutation, selection, ontogeny, transmission, the whole causal story, written out at whatever level you like. Now delete every moral fact from it. If the story explains exactly as much afterwards as it did before, then moral facts were doing no work in producing the belief. So any realist reply has to be either (a) deny inertness — insist that moral facts figure in the causal/explanatory account of how we came to hold the beliefs we hold, which costs the non-naturalist their non-naturalism and turns them into an adaptationist about truth-tracking, a claim that now owes empirical detail they can't supply; or (b) grant inertness and argue it isn't epistemically damning. My claim is that the familiar third-factor replies all quietly take horn (a) while talking as if they were taking horn (b), because a third-factor story either smuggles the moral fact into the causal chain or is just the genealogy with a decorative label stuck on it. And my claim about (b) is that inertness-plus-a-complete-competing-causal-story is precisely the configuration in which, everywhere else, we let pedigree defeat a belief.

Room's commitments as best I can read them: most would call themselves vaguely realist about morality without ever having had to defend it; several are convinced moral judgment is mostly post-hoc rationalization and will think that already wins my argument for me (it doesn't — that's a claim about psychology, not about whether moral facts are inert in the genealogy, and I need to say so without sounding like I'm rejecting an ally); at least two hold that "morality" is a grab bag of separate systems — disgust, reciprocity, coalition psychology, harm avoidance — with different histories, so they'll want to know which one I'm debunking; one of the cultural evolution people thinks genetic timescales are the wrong level of analysis altogether; and everybody has an allergy to arguments that sound like theyre doing philosophy at them.

What I want: the eight-ish objections this room actually raises, ordered by how much damage each would do to the talk rather than by how interesting it is. For each one — who asks it, since the adaptationists won't ask what the modelers ask; how they'd phrase it in their own register (proximate vs ultimate, just-so story, exaptation, phylogenetic signal, WEIRD samples, whatever fits); a reply I can deliver standing up in about two minutes; and a blunt note on where that reply is soft, so I know when to stop talking and concede instead of digging in. Then tell me what to give away in the first five minutes so I'm not spending the Q&A defending positions I don't hold, and which single reply has to land for the talk to count as a success with these people specifically.

And separately, the question I'm dreading. There is one, I can feel it, and I'd rather see it written out with the answer I'd give if I were brave than get ambushed by it in front of a room that thinks philosophers are evasive.

No reading lists or lit reviews please, I know the papers — I want this room's objections, not the journals'.
