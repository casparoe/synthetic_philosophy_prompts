Background: I came at this literature from the rationalist-blog side — the akrasia threads, the disagreement posts, the level-splitting arguments — so I know the terrain but I don't fully trust my own grading instincts in a department setting. Question and answer are below. Grade it for me.

The question I set:

Case: You spent last evening working through a messy dataset and came out highly confident that p — that there's a real effect of the kind you were looking for. This morning a colleague you trust tells you that the sleep aid you'd taken before starting is known, in a minority of the people who take it, to produce one specific reasoning distortion: marked overconfidence in pattern detection. She says nothing about the dataset, the methods, or p. You know you took it. You also know that in the people it affects, the drug leaves them feeling unusually lucid and sharp, so there's no reliable inside view on whether you're in that minority.

(a) Does her testimony bear rationally on your confidence in p? Explain what turns on answering yes versus no.
(b) State the enkratic (or non-akrasia) requirement and say whether a response to this case that keeps high confidence in p while taking the testimony seriously violates it.
(c) Explain the level-splitting response, and give the best argument you can for or against its being a rationally permissible state to be in.

The student's answer, about 600 words:

The first thing to get clear on is what kind of evidence this is. Nothing my colleague says is about the dataset. It is evidence about my cognitive performance — about whether the process that produced my confidence was working properly — and that is a different subject matter from whether the effect is real. So her testimony is not first-order evidence against p, and anyone who treats it that way has misdescribed the case.

The live question is whether evidence of that sort can nonetheless bear on what I ought to believe about p. There's a familiar objection to the idea that it can. My colleague's report is generic: it tells me that the drug distorts reasoning in a minority of users, and I might well be in the majority. Her testimony is therefore quite possibly misleading about me. And rationality is a matter of responding to the evidence you actually have, not of getting things right; a belief can be perfectly rational even when the evidence is misleading. So the fact that her testimony might be misleading means it cannot rationally require any change in my confidence in p. What it does affect is prudence: it would be reckless to act on p, to bet on it or write it up, but recklessness in action is a separate matter from irrationality in belief.

That gets me to (b). The enkratic requirement says you shouldn't have high confidence in p together with the belief that your evidence doesn't support p. Call the combination epistemic akrasia, by analogy with acting against your own view of what you ought to do. Horowitz is the standard source for the claim that the requirement can be rationally violated in exactly these cases: she argues that what's rational at the first-order level and what's rational at the higher level can come apart, so an agent can end up rationally confident in p while rationally believing that her confidence isn't supported. I think that's the wrong conclusion, and for a reason that has nothing to do with drugs or datasets. The akratic combination isn't merely undesirable, it's incoherent in the same way as the Moore-paradoxical belief that it's raining and I don't believe it's raining. In both cases the state undermines itself: believing is transparent, so to hold the belief is to be committed to something incompatible with the rest of what you hold. So the combination isn't a rationally permissible option that we have to weigh against other options. It isn't an option at all.

Which brings me to (c). Level-splitting is the proposal that in a case like mine you keep your first-order confidence where it is and revise only your higher-order assessment: you stay confident that p, and you also take seriously that your confidence may well be unsupported. As a description of what people actually do under self-doubt, that's plausible enough. As a normative proposal it has nothing going for it here. If the higher-order evidence doesn't touch the support relation — and by the argument above it doesn't — then there is no akratic state to describe. I don't come to believe that my evidence fails to support p; I come to doubt my own assessment of that evidence, which is a different attitude toward a different object. So the case never forces a split between the levels, and the apparent tension between staying confident and taking the testimony seriously dissolves: the tension was only ever between confidence and action.
