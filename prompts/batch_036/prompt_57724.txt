I sit on the allocation committee of a community land trust in a town of about eight thousand people. There are nine of us — a retired millwright, two teachers, a nurse, a couple of small-business owners, and me; I fix roofs. None of us has any training in economics or philosophy, though two or three of us have been reading on our own for years, and I suspect that is part of why we are stuck.

This spring the trust sold a parcel it no longer needed and came into $150,000. Our bylaws say a windfall of that kind goes back to resident households and is divided equally among the applicants unless the committee can agree on a reason not to. We have met nine times. Five households applied, and here they are, roughly as they stand:

- The Reyeses: two healthy working adults and a teenager, frugal, no debts beyond the mortgage, planning to bank most of their share against future college costs. Per dollar, they will get more lasting benefit out of this money than anyone else on the list.
- Della: a single parent whose nine-year-old uses a wheelchair. The lift on her van has failed, the bathroom has to be rebuilt around her, and the back steps need a ramp; she prices all of it at about sixty thousand dollars. An equal share of thirty thousand gets her halfway, and only if she spends every cent of it on those things.
- The Haddads: arrived fourteen months ago, two adults and three children, little English, no local references, no American credit history. Their difficulty is not that they need more stuff. It is that a check does not turn into a life for them the way it does for everyone else — landlords will not take them, contractors quote them double, and nobody they know can tell them which offer is real.
- Roy: fifty-two, walked away from steady plant work two years ago to start an orchard and an apiary. It has not gone well, his options are nearly gone, and he wants the money for grafting stock and a used cooler. He chose this and does not pretend otherwise.
- Marguerite: retired, comfortable, and candid about having expensive tastes. She says an equal share would do her very little good, that she would need closer to fifty thousand to enjoy this summer as much as her neighbors enjoy theirs, and that she genuinely cannot get anything out of less.

Two positions on this committee have stopped making contact with each other, and I would rather see them argued properly than settled by a vote. The first — call it the equal-shares position — holds that fairness means giving every household the same share of the stuff and then leaving people to decide what to make of it; if the money buys one household more life than another, or if a household sinks it into a failing orchard or into a taste for fine things, that is the price of treating adults as responsible for their own choices. The second holds that the share is the wrong thing to look at: what should be equalized is what people are actually able to do and be, because the same bundle converts into wildly different lives depending on a body, a language, a credit history, or a set of options already foreclosed.

Please write a dialogue between two committee members who hold these views, each trying to move the other. A few requests:

Both should be at the top of their form. Neither gets to be the sensible one by default, and neither view should be represented by its weakest version.

They should argue from these five households rather than from books. Please keep philosophers and titles out of it entirely — these are volunteers, and the force ought to come from the cases.

Each of them has to commit. At some point each should put actual dollar figures against the five households and defend the resulting allocation as a whole, not merely attack the other's.

Each has to take the case that hurts them: Marguerite's expensive tastes and Roy's chosen risk for the second position, Della and the Haddads for the first. I want to see whether the second can explain why the Haddads' situation generates a claim while Marguerite's does not, and whether the first can explain why an identical check is a fair response to a family that cannot spend it.

Let them locate what they already agree on — my sense is that both reject simply maximizing how much happiness the money produces — so that what remains is the real disagreement.

Each should concede at least one point the other has genuinely earned, and the dialogue should end unresolved but with the disagreement narrowed to something precise: whether the trouble is that we have the wrong measure of fairness, or that a measure nobody can publicly check cannot serve as the measure of justice.

No moderator, no narrator, no closing paragraph telling us who was right. Roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words.
