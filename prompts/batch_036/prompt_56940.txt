The setup, short version: the coin-flip gamble where the payoffs alternate in sign and grow just enough that the expected-value series converges only conditionally — the Pasadena game, I'm pretty sure that's the name. Add the terms in the order the flips happen and you get ln 2, or something near it. But the ordering is doing all the work: shuffle the terms and you can land on any number you like, or make the thing diverge. So unlike St Petersburg, where the expectation is at least a definite infinity, here the expectation just doesn't exist.

Here's the claim I want argued for or against: when an expectation fails to exist that way, the question "should you take the bet?" has no determinate answer, and the right response is not to reach for a repair but to treat the whole case as ill-posed — more like a sentence that looks grammatical and turns out not to say anything than like a hard problem in decision theory. On that reading the paradox isn't a puzzle about how to choose, it's evidence that the concept of expected value has a hidden precondition (absolute convergence, basically) that we only ever notice when it gets violated.

Also say whether making expected utility theory self-limiting like this is a legitimate move or a way of defining the embarrassing cases away.
