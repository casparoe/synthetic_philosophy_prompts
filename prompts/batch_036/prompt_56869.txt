I don't have any philosophy training — business degree, and now I run the people-and-ops side of a fully remote company of about 300 — but I've been reading around this stuff on my own for a couple of months because we're about to try to kill a norm I hate. On a flight last week I got through a chapter of one of those pop social-science books a behavioural economist writes, and it spent several pages on a philosopher named David Lewis and his 1969 book *Convention*. I copied down the part that mattered, roughly word for word from my notes:

"A convention is a stable regularity in how a group behaves that everyone in the group would be better off keeping — driving on the same side of the road, shaking with the right hand, saying your name when you answer the phone. Lewis gave a short list of conditions for when a regularity counts as one: everyone does it, everyone expects everyone else to do it, everyone wants everyone else to do it, and everyone knows all of this and knows that everyone knows it. That last layer is called common knowledge and it's the whole engine of the theory. The other big idea is that conventions are arbitrary — left is as good as right — so there's no principled way to choose between them; whichever one gets going first wins, and after that nobody has any reason to change. Lewis was arguing against people who thought rules had to come from an agreement: he showed that a convention is really just a tacit agreement that's been around so long everyone forgot it was ever made. And because common knowledge does all the work, the practical payoff is enormous — a public announcement from someone credible literally manufactures common knowledge, and a convention along with it. That's also why norms evolve: people copy whoever seems to be doing well. So you change a culture by changing what gets announced, not by training people or appealing to their values."

Some of that felt too smooth, but I can't tell which parts. My sister, who's a lawyer, said "an email doesn't make a norm, that's ridiculous," but she couldn't say why, and I half want the chapter to be right because it would make my job easy. So: can you go through that passage claim by claim and tell me what someone who actually works on Lewis would accept, what's a fair simplification for a general reader, what's subtly off, and what's just wrong? I'm especially suspicious of four spots — the "tacit agreement everyone forgot" line, the slide from "arbitrary" to "nobody has any reason to change," the idea that an announcement manufactures a convention, and the copying-whoever's-winning bit, which I suspect belongs to a different tradition of thinking about norms entirely and got stapled onto Lewis. I'd also like to know whether the conditions he actually gave are the ones the book lists, since I get the feeling "everyone wants everyone else to do it" is doing a lot of dishonest work. Then tell me whether the practical upshot survives the correction, and run the corrected version on our case: when a customer escalation lands in our shared channel, whoever sees it first quietly tags the most junior support person on shift, and it's now simply expected — the juniors resent it, the seniors don't notice it's happening, and nobody ever decided it. Leadership plans to announce on Monday that escalations belong to the senior on call that week, and they think the announcement plus everyone knowing everyone heard it is the whole job. Does Lewis's framework, properly understood, say that will take? If not, what has to be true for it to take, and is our thing even a Lewis-style convention at all given that some people clearly lose out under the current arrangement? No reading list, please — I can't chase down sources, I just want the audit and what to actually do.
