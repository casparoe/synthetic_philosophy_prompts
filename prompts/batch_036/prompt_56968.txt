so I'm the one who looks after our mill museum up here. I worked in the place as a weaver for nineteen years before it shut and I've got no qualifications whatsoever, I just read alot and I've been through every box of the mill's paperwork in the attic.

Next Thursday we open a new exhibition, paid for by the family of the mill's last owner. There's a plaque by the door and the family chose the wording themselves and it says WHY DID THE WORLD CHANGE HERE AND NOT IN CHINA?

I have to say a few words before the ribbon gets cut. Six minutes tops, stood up, no notes really.

The room: the donor's daughter and about eight of her relatives, two councillers, the year nine class from the school down the road, the volunteers who help me, and — this is the tricky bit — two proper economic historians we've invited, one of whom has basically made his whole career out of answering the question on that plaque. He speaks right after me.

Here's my problem. I don't think the question on the plaque is a question. Not that the answer's wrong — I mean it isn't the kind of thing that could have had an answer. It points at one place and calls it here and points at another and calls it not here and assumes there was one single thing that happened which could equally have happened in either. When I go through our own ledgers I cant find that single thing. I find a water-rights argument that got settled in a pub, a set of loom parts copied off a mill forty miles away, three years where we nearly went under, cotton that came from a place nobody in this town ever laid eyes on, and one woman who could fix the cards when nobody else could. If somebody had stood in this yard in 1790 and asked why spinning got cheap they'd have got twelve answers and not one of them would have mentioned China.

The nearest I can get to saying it my own way: down the shed you could ask why this loom stopped and not that one, but it only ever meant anything because both looms were running the same cloth off the same beam. Take that away and the question's just noise.

So write me the speech. It has to get all that over to a room that will hear the plaque is stupid if I'm clumsy about it, and I can't say that, because his daughter is stood three feet from me and we want her money again next year. It also can't take a swing at the historian who's on after me — I'd rather he enjoyed it. Do the work with the objects in the room instead of with arguments: there's a shuttle off the looms, the 1798 ledger, the photograph from the last working day, and the replica spinning frame the kids have been winding for me all week. I want one moment where somebody in that room feels the question go soft underneath them, without me ever announcing that that's what I'm doing.

Don't put any names of famous books or historians in my mouth, I'd get them wrong and it isn't me anyway. And it can't end on a downer — these are my neighbours, they're proud of this place, and they should still be proud of it when they walk out.
