Twenty-five people are signed up for a two-hour Saturday workshop I am running on the franchise: roughly half are sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds studying government and politics at school, and half are members of a local residents' association, several of whom are long-term residents without citizenship and therefore without a vote in national elections. I have a whiteboard and no handouts. I am not assigning anything, so please do not suggest readings.

What I want from you is a plan for how to teach one idea, not the content of the session. The idea is that whether voting should be compulsory, whether the voting age should fall to sixteen, and whether resident non-citizens should be enfranchised are not three independent policy debates but three probes into a single underlying question about what entitles a person to a place in the electorate — and that the candidate grounds for inclusion (being subject to the coercive law, being affected by the decisions, being a participant in the ongoing collective undertaking) diverge from one another, and diverge again from whatever grounds could support compelling people to turn out.

Please set out the following:

- The order in which the pieces must be introduced, and why each must precede the next. In particular, what has to be established before the compulsion question can be posed at all without collapsing into the inclusion question, and whether the competing grounds should be built up one at a time or laid out simultaneously as rivals.

- The single running case that should carry the workshop, together with what it must contain and what it must exclude. I have in mind one household: a long-term resident non-citizen parent, a sixteen-year-old who will be enfranchised in two years, and an adult citizen who has never voted and would not if left to himself. The three are identical in their exposure to the same laws and taxes and differ only in the formally relevant respects. Tell me whether this is the right vehicle, or whether it fails because several people in the room are themselves resident non-citizens and would end up functioning as the exhibit. If it fails, propose a replacement with the same structural properties.

- The misconceptions the room will bring, in the order they are likely to surface, and the point in the sequence at which each should be pre-empted rather than rebutted afterwards. I anticipate at least these: that voting is a duty in the way jury service is; that any age threshold must be arbitrary because any line could be shifted; that excluding children on competence grounds is inconsistent with including adults who have cognitive impairments; that since one's own vote cannot be decisive, compulsion cannot be decisive either; and, from the younger half, that the issue is already settled in their favour. Please also tell me where to place the fact that what the standard compulsory-voting model actually compels is enrolment and attendance rather than the casting of a valid vote, without letting the session dissolve into procedural detail.

- One question I can put at the end, on a case we will not have discussed, that would distinguish those who can apply the grounds from those who can only restate them.

- What I should deliberately leave out at this level, and announce that I am leaving out.
