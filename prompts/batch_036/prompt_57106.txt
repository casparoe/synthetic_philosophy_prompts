I teach an upper-level political theory seminar — sixteen students, a single eighty-minute session — on Rousseau's *Letter to d'Alembert on the Theater*, and I want an in-class activity rather than a discussion of the text. The reading the activity should produce is not the familiar one (Rousseau as a moralist worried about corrupting content). His case is structural: a theater converts a people into an audience — separated, seated, immobile, paying, watching hired substitutes perform what they might have done themselves — whereas the festival he recommends at the end of the Letter converts each spectator into a participant. It is the arrangement of the gathering, not the morality of the scripts, that he takes to determine the effect on public mores.

Please design the activity to those specifications. Three constraints matter to me. First, hold the content constant across both arms: the same material, whether a short scene or a decision the group has to make, delivered once theatrically and once as a festival, so that any difference in what students do or report is attributable to bodies, roles, money, and exit rather than to what was said. Second, students should undergo both forms before Rousseau's argument is explained to them. Third, build in something observable — a record of behavior, not merely how they felt.

Then give me the debrief: specific moments in each arm mapped onto specific claims in the Letter; a second movement in which the festival is made to feel its costs, the impossibility of withdrawing, the conspicuousness of the non-participant, the demand for transparency, so that students leave holding both the insight and the objection rather than a verdict; the failure modes particular to this design and how to handle them; and a frank statement of which feature of Rousseau's argument an eighty-minute exercise cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself. I will provide the text and any references.
