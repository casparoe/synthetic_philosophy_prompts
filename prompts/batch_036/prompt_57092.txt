I am building a personal list of historical junctures that do and do not strike me as pivotal in the sense the hinge-of-history debate cares about: moments at which what happened altered the long-run future in a way that later actors could neither undo nor arrive at by some other route. My difficulty is that the list does not hang together, and I want to know whether a principle can be extracted from it or whether parts of it have to be surrendered.

Three terms I have been using, which you are welcome to sharpen, rename, or throw out:

- **Ratchet**: the change persists; later actors cannot walk it back even if they come to regret it.
- **Substitutability**: whether some other actor, on a somewhat different timetable, would have made the same change anyway.
- **Ceiling**: whether the change alters the best long-run future that is reachable at all, as opposed to the speed or smoothness of the route to it.

Judged pivotal, with my reasons:

1. *The adoption of agriculture in each of its independent centres.* Once populations were committed to stored grain and fixed settlement, there was no route back to foraging at anything like the same density. The ratchet here is demographic rather than political.
2. *The first nuclear weapons programme.* The physics cannot be unlearned, and the weapon converted a category of permanent civilizational loss from speculative to feasible. I flag a tension myself: I also think another state would have produced a bomb within five to ten years, which under a substitutability test ought to disqualify it.
3. *The demographic collapse of the Americas after 1492.* Whole political and intellectual traditions were extinguished, not merely delayed; nothing later could restore them.
4. *The certification of smallpox eradication.* A permanent subtraction of a cause of death, and unlike the others a ratchet with no obvious offsetting hazard channel.
5. *The phase-out of chlorofluorocarbons agreed at Montreal.* It foreclosed a trajectory of severe ozone depletion that later actors would have been unable to reverse for decades, even had they wanted to.

Judged not pivotal, with my reasons:

6. *The Black Death in Europe.* Mortality was enormous, but population and the manorial order substantially recovered. It moved the level, not the ceiling.
7. *The Cuban Missile Crisis.* A near miss is not a juncture. Nothing about it changed what was possible afterwards; the danger recurred in other forms and eventually subsided on its own terms.
8. *The Meiji Restoration.* One polity catching up to a frontier set elsewhere: substitutable in the sense that mattered, and in principle reversible.
9. *The assassination at Sarajevo.* The war's causes were structural; a different trigger would have produced a comparable war within a few years.
10. *Movable-type printing in Europe.* The one I keep going back and forth on. I can imagine a Europe reaching print within a generation by other means, and printing looks to me like an accelerator of everything downstream rather than a switch; but I also cannot picture mass literacy, the speed of the Reformation, or the institutional forms of early modern science arriving on any other timetable.

Two pairs I cannot reconcile. Cases 3 and 6 are both episodes of catastrophic mortality, and I give them opposite verdicts. Cases 2 and 8 both involve a single actor moving quickly on something many others were converging toward, and again I split them.

What I would like from you:

- Determine whether a single principle, or a small ordered set of clauses, reproduces these verdicts. State it precisely enough that it could be applied by someone who had never seen my list, and tie each clause to the case that forces it.
- Where clauses conflict, say how the conflict is resolved, and justify the ordering rather than asserting it.
- Tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot save. I would rather have a spare principle that overturns one of them than a baroque one that accommodates all ten; but if you recommend the spare version, price it — say what is lost by the overturned verdict and why the loss is worth it.
- Apply the principle to case 10 and give me a stable answer on printing.
- Apply it to three prospective junctures: a single laboratory reaching transformative artificial intelligence substantially ahead of everyone else and keeping it proprietary; the open-weight release of a frontier model to the world at large; and a durable international agreement governing heritable human genome editing. Say which of these the principle treats as pivotal, and whether the verdicts are sensitive to how the agreement or release is designed.
- Finally, say what the principle implies about the hinge-of-history hypothesis itself: does it make the present unusually pivotal, and if so, what evidence would count for or against that claim rather than merely restating the intuition?

No references or bibliography, please; I want the reasoning, not the literature.
