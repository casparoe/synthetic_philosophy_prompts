I serve on a seven-member municipal review panel that issues and renews operating permits for sidewalk delivery and inspection robots, and on Thursday we vote on whether to approve a vendor's revised planning stack for a fleet of forty units already operating downtown. The permit has two conditions that the vendor's engineers now tell us cannot both be met. Condition 4(a) requires the device to maintain a current model of the public right-of-way at all times; Condition 4(b), added two years ago after complaints about robots freezing in crowds, forbids a device from impeding pedestrian flow and specifies that it may not halt to re-survey its surroundings more than once every three seconds while on the sidewalk. The revision in front of us resolves the tension by keeping a persistent world-model that is updated chiefly by the robot's own actions, with a learned filter deciding which other features of the scene are worth spending perception cycles on. Our file includes one incident that bears directly on this. In October a unit wedged a service gate open so it could make a second trip; a building porter closed the gate and kicked the wedge aside; the unit's filter had classified gate state as untouched by any action of its own and therefore not worth re-checking, so its model still recorded the gate as open. It approached at speed, stopped thirty centimetres short, and then sat in the courtyard's only accessible route for nine minutes deliberating. A resident who uses a power wheelchair was late to a dialysis appointment. Nothing in the unit's own logs violates 4(a): its model was internally current, just not current about the gate.

I spent most of last night arguing about this with a friend who designs planning systems, and I lost more ground than I'd like to admit. My proposal, which I have already put in the draft motion, is that the panel require a fixed schedule of protected fact classes — anything bearing on a third party's access or egress, anything inside the robot's own footprint, anything the robot has itself displaced or is holding in place — that may never be treated as persistent without fresh perception, and that the learned filter be confined to everything else, with a guaranteed minimum re-check budget. Alongside it I proposed a closure certificate: a filing in which the operator enumerates every fact class the system is licensed to assume unchanged between perception events, on the principle that whatever is not on the list must be re-checked. Her objections were, in order, that a finite list cannot cover a world that is not finite; that the certificate therefore collapses into the status quo with extra paperwork, since enumeration only ever marks exceptions to a default of persistence; and — the one I could not answer — that the filter deciding what deserves re-checking is itself a bounded deliberator that must assume most things persist while it works, so auditing it simply relocates the question a level up, with no bottom to reach. She also said, not unkindly, that our panel is trying to legislate an epistemology for machines and that every version of the rule we write will be either unenforceable or trivially satisfied on paper. She calls the whole thing the frame problem and thinks we have wandered into it by accident; I think there is still a version of my schedule that does real work, and I want the memo to help me find out whether I am right or merely attached.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the panel before Thursday's vote, roughly eight hundred to a thousand words. It should reach a clear bottom line — approve release 4.2, reject it, or approve it subject to conditions we could actually write into the permit — and it should say explicitly what to do about the conflict between 4(a) and 4(b), since one of them will have to be amended and I want to know which and how. Where you recommend conditions, make them the kind an inspector with a clipboard and a data download could verify: what the operator files, what it logs, what triggers a suspension. Take my friend's strongest objection seriously rather than swatting it: if the demand for an auditable filter just pushes the problem up a level, say what a panel is nonetheless entitled to require, and why that requirement is not empty. Tell me plainly whether to keep or drop the closure certificate from the motion, and whether the protected-fact schedule survives her objection in some revised form — for instance as a floor rather than a boundary — or whether I should withdraw it. Two of our commissioners have no technical background and one is hostile to jargon, so whatever you make of the underlying difficulty should be conveyed in the language of gates, wedges, and nine minutes, not in the vocabulary of the planning literature.
