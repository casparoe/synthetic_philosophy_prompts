I'm the QA lead at a small company that sells a packaged-food allergen database. About 12,000 rows a quarter, each row saying whether a specific product contains or may contain one of the major allergens. School districts and two hospital systems run their menus off it. I get six hours of senior reviewer time per release, two junior contractors who are fast but have no stats background at all, and a compliance guy who signs the release note and only trusts things that are written as numbers.

Here's the problem I can't get out of. Every single row we ship, I could defend on its own. Most are way above any threshold you'd want — call it 99.5% that the row is right, from the supplier spec sheet plus our scrape plus how often that particular source has burned us before. Our written rule right now is basically "if every row clears 99%, ship it." But I also know, with total confidence, that a 12,000-row release contains errors, probably dozens. Last year one bad sesame row ended up on a kid's tray. So me, same person, same afternoon, believe each row is correct and believe the release is wrong somewhere. Somebody once told me there's names for this, something about a lottery and something about a book preface, but I never read it and I don't want a lecture about it.

What I want is a procedure. Something my juniors can run per row in a couple of minutes and that I can hand to compliance without him panicking. It needs to do these things:

1. Sort rows into classes by what kind of evidence is behind them — I suspect "we have a base rate for this source and nothing specific about this item" is a different animal from "we looked at this label," and that they shouldn't get treated with the same threshold. Tell me if I'm wrong about that.
2. Say, per class, when a junior is allowed to mark a row accepted, when they have to kick it up, and when the honest move is to not ship the row at all.
3. Tell me where the six hours go. Right now we spend them wherever something looked weird, which I think is mostly luck.
4. Tell me what the release note can honestly claim about the set as a whole, given that we already know it has errors in it. The current wording implies otherwise and I'm tired of it.
5. Mark every place where the procedure is really just a judgment call wearing a number as a costume. I'd rather know which steps are fake-precise.

And test this hunch, because it's the one thing I actually think I've figured out and nobody here agrees with me: whether this is a problem at all depends on where the cost lands. If a bad row costs us a refund and an apology, per-row thresholds are fine and I should stop losing sleep. But if the cost lands on the collection — the whole release gets pulled, a customer's automated menu system chokes, a parent stops trusting any of it, two wrong rows compound into one bad meal — then per-row thresholds are the wrong instrument entirely and no amount of individual 99.5% adds up to anything. Is that a real distinction or am I just making it up to feel better?

Then run it on three things we actually have:

- 3,100 rows auto-imported from a distributor feed. We've measured that feed at about a 0.3% historical error rate. Nothing about those individual rows has ever been looked at by a human.
- A new ingredient-list parser that's genuinely excellent except it quietly mangles rows where the label uses a French or Spanish term. The errors are clustered, not spread out, and we can't tell you which rows are affected — only roughly how many.
- Our flagship "verified" tier: 400 rows, each hand-checked twice by a senior person. Sales advertises it as defect-free. This one's my baby and I know you're going to say something about it I don't want to hear. Don't soften it, just tell me, and tell me what to do about the contract language if the answer is that we can't promise that either.

Finish with a short list of what the procedure can't settle — where it bottoms out and a human has to just decide and own it. Flowchart-in-words and numbered steps, please, no bibliography, I'm never going to read one.
