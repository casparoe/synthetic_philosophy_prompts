An archive will not re-sort a collection. Records are kept in the grouping given them by the office that produced them and, so far as possible, in the sequence that office used internally, and catalogue entries reproduce the creator's own names and titles for things even where those names are simply false, or contemptuous toward the people the file is about. Now suppose a repository holds the records of a state-run children's home, and the people documented in them ask that the files be re-ordered by the lives they touch rather than by the desk that generated them, and re-described in their own words. Staff say this would destroy the records. Every sheet of paper would still be present and every sentence on it unchanged, so I want to know what is supposed to have been destroyed. What does the practice take a record to be, such that its position in a filing system carries something its content cannot — a view about evidence, about the institution as the true author of its own paperwork, about who is entitled to say what a document means? How does that view sit with the rest of archival work: repositories destroy most of what crosses their desks without apparent embarrassment, and they routinely rebox, re-folder, digitize and re-describe, which detaches a document from its position about as thoroughly as re-ordering would. Which rival pictures — records as carriers of information, or as instruments owed to the people they document — does the arrangement principle quietly rule out, and where is the practice underdetermined, such that several quite different theories would license the same shelf order? If the survivors' re-ordering were adopted, what would have to change in the finding aid, in the treatment of records as evidence in legal proceedings, and in how archivists are trained? Please answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables — as you would write a letter.
