You are a formal epistemologist and philosopher of science whose work sits at the intersection of imprecise probability theory, decision theory, and the epistemology of risk regulation. You are at home with the mathematics — sets of probability functions as representations of belief, upper and lower probabilities, dilation, accuracy scoring, maximin decision rules — but your particular strength is diagnosing disagreements: working out what people who appear to be arguing about the same question are in fact arguing about. Please think carefully before answering. What I want is a diagnosis, not a survey of the literature and not a ruling on which side is correct.

**The situation.** For several years I have advised a public body — call it the Authority — that publishes numerical risk estimates for a portfolio of hazards (flood defence failure, pathogen spillover, long-run infrastructure degradation) and then decides how much capital to spend against them. Treat the following as stipulated rather than as a report of any real institution. The Authority's technical committee is split into two camps that have not moved in four years.

The *pointers* hold that, whatever is said in public documents, the committee's underlying credal state must be capable of being reduced to a single probability function, and that any model which refuses this is unusable. Their reasons: an agent with a set of probability functions cannot plan, because in sequential choice problems the act it would choose at the first stage and the act it would choose at the second can be jointly indefensible, and the only escape is precommitment, which costs the agent the ability to respond to evidence; sets of functions suffer dilation, whereby learning a piece of evidence that is, by every member of the set, irrelevant to the quantity in question can nonetheless widen the range of that quantity; and, decisively, at some point a decision must be made, and making one requires picking a rule — maximin, or something else — that in effect selects among the functions in the set, so the precision the model pretended to avoid reappears one level up, hidden inside the choice of rule.

The *rangers* hold that the evidence in these cases simply does not licence a unique function, that insisting on one smuggles in a precision the data cannot support, and that the imprecise model is more faithful and, on standard accuracy-theoretic measures, not dominated by any precise alternative. Their replies: dilation is not a pathology but a correct signal that the new evidence is uninformative about the parameter, and a representation that hid this would be worse; the inability to act on a unique maximum is a virtue in high-stakes settings, because it forces the value-laden step out into the open as an explicit choice of decision rule rather than burying it inside a number that looks like a measurement; and the planning objection assumes an agent who must have one doxastic state across time, which is precisely what is in question.

The feature that puzzles me most is this: both camps cite the *same* formal results — the dilation theorems, the coherence and no-sure-loss results, the accuracy-dominance results — and each says that the theorem favours them.

**My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis.** I suspect the two camps are not modelling the same object. The pointers are answering "what state must a single, temporally extended agent be in for its decisions over time to cohere?", while the rangers are answering "what does the evidence permit, and which representation avoids overstating it?". If so, dilation and the planning problem are both true and neither refutes anything, because they are facts about different explananda. A variant I find equally plausible: the hidden premise is about the temporal unity of the agent — whether the rational subject is one persisting planner who must have a single state now, or a sequence of semi-independent time-slices each of which need only be synchronically coherent — in which case the dispute is not about probability at all but about the metaphysics and pragmatics of agency.

**What I want you to do.**

1. Begin with a short section of explicit scoping — no more than about 250 words — in which you list the candidate diagnoses (verbal dispute; dispute about the explanandum of the formal model; a substantive normative disagreement about planning and revision; a hidden empirical or practical question about how committees actually deliberate; an unsettled formal question nobody has proved; an ill-posed question), and say which you are discarding at the outset and why.

2. Reconstruct each camp's strongest case in your own words, briefly and charitably. Do not strawman either side, and do not resolve the tension yet.

3. Give your diagnosis, supported by what each side actually claims. In particular, address the puzzle I flagged: how can the same theorems be read as supporting both positions, and what does that tell you about where the disagreement lives?

4. Apply the following test, which is the part I care about most. Are the two positions ever *behaviourally* distinguishable? Construct the simplest concrete decision problem — you may invent it — in which a committed pointer and a committed ranger, given the same evidence and the same utilities, must make different choices, or receive different verdicts from their own standards. The case must be one in which the difference cannot be mimicked by a precise agent with suitably chosen risk attitudes: if an ambiguity-averse pointer using a maximin rule over a singleton behaves exactly like the ranger, the case fails. If no such case can be constructed, say so, and explain whether that makes the dispute idle, merely pragmatic, or deep but unresolvable. If one can be constructed, explain whether its existence settles anything, given that each camp will deny the other's premises about the case.

5. Say what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a theorem (describe roughly what it would have to establish), an operational rule the Authority could adopt, a change of question, or nothing. Be honest if you think the stalemate is permanent, and say why.

6. Close with a direct verdict on my hypothesis — accepted, rejected, or accepted in part with a specified amendment — and with a sentence or two on whether the Authority's impasse and the philosophical one are the same debate, and whether the committee's deadlock is independent evidence for your diagnosis.

**Output requirements.** Write in British English.
