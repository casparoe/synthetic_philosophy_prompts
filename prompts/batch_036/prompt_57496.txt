I chair a seven-person grants panel at a small private foundation — we fund about eight projects a year out of roughly 200 applications, $150–200k each. Our guidelines tell reviewers to weigh three things: whether the research design is sound, whether the question is worth the money, and whether it can actually be done in the time and budget asked for. Then we vote on whether to recommend funding. The guidelines dont say which of those two things decides. Nobody noticed that was a gap until yesterday.

Here are the votes on the application in question (an early-career PI, $180k over two years). Format is [design sound / question worth funding / feasible as costed] and then their vote on the funding recommendation:

- me, Dan, Priya: yes / yes / yes → fund
- Ines: no / yes / yes → don't fund
- Marcus: yes / no / yes → don't fund
- Tom: yes / yes / no → don't fund
- Ruth: no / no / no → don't fund

So five of us think the design is sound, five think the question is worth funding, five think it's feasible — and four of us vote no. Add up the criteria and it gets funded; count the funding votes and it dies. Dan says we've walked into a paradox with an actual name but couldn't remember it, and I don't want to spend Tuesday's meeting on trivia.

Three things make it worse then the tidy version:

- Ines is one of the two "design isn't sound" votes, but reading her written comments, what she's actually objecting to is a paper this PI published three years ago that she thinks was sloppy. Her premise vote is doing something other than assessing the premise.
- Ruth voted no on all three and then said out loud that even if she'd said yes to all three she still wouldn't fund it, because we've already made two grants in this area this cycle and the portfolio's lopsided. That consideration is nowhere in the guidelines.
- Last round we did the opposite. On application 11 a majority of us thought it wasn't feasible, a majority voted to fund it anyway, we funded it, and it's going fine. So if I now insist the criteria control, I'm choosing the rule that produces the answer I want, in the one case where I want it — and Ines is going to point that out within about ninety seconds.

What I need is a written opinion, roughly 800 words, that I can circulate Monday so people read it before the meeting rather than hearing it cold. It has to:

(a) say what we do with this application — fund it, reject it, or something else — with the reasoning, not a hedge;
(b) say what rule the panel adopts for the rest of the cycle, and give the other six something they can apply to the next application mechanically, without relitigating this one;
(c) say what goes in the feedback letter, since we can't write "the panel found your design sound, your question important and your budget realistic" and "we are not able to fund you" in the same paragraph with nothing in between; and
(d) state the strongest version of the "just count the funding votes, the criteria are advice" position and answer it, because Marcus will make that argument and he isn't being obtuse.

Please be committed. I'd much rather you land on a recommendation I can push back on than lay out the options and hand the choice back to me — I've already read the options. If your answer is that we fund against a 4–3 vote, say so plainly and tell me what I say to the four. And give me a definite line on Ruth's portfolio point: I can't work out whether that's a reason to reject this application, a reason to amend the guidelines, or a reason to change how we schedule the cycle, and I have a feeling those three have different right answers and I'm conflating them.
