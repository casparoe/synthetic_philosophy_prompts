Since I retired from a plant-genetics lab three years ago I've been doing second-marking for a first-year mind-and-language course at the university here — they were short of markers and I wanted the reading. One of this term's essay questions was:

"The poverty of the stimulus argument is often summarised as the claim that children don't hear enough language to learn a grammar. Explain why that summary misdescribes the argument. Use the case of yes/no questions built out of sentences containing relative clauses, and say what a non-nativist account of this case would have to establish in order to defeat the argument." (1200 words, 40 marks)

Here's one student's answer, shortened but with all her substantive moves left in and nothing tidied up:

"The argument goes back to Chomsky's review of Skinner. Children produce sentences they have never heard before, so their speech can't be explained by reinforcement of past responses, and the clearest version of the argument uses yes/no questions. Take 'The man who is tall is in the room.' A child who had learned a simple linear rule — find the first 'is' and move it to the front — would produce 'Is the man who tall is in the room?', while a rule that is sensitive to structure produces 'Is the man who is tall in the room?'. Children don't make the first sort of error, and they can't have learned not to, because questions of this kind are vanishingly rare in the speech directed at them and nobody corrects them when they get them wrong: parents respond to whether a child's utterance is true, not to whether it's grammatical. So the evidence the child has does not decide between the linear rule and the structure-dependent one, and the child can't have settled on the second one on the basis of that evidence. It follows that the structure-dependent rule is innate — the child is born already knowing that the auxilliary that moves is the one attached to the subject of the main clause rather than the first one in the string.

This is also why taking 'poverty of the stimulus' as a claim about quantity gets things backwards. The point isn't that the child hears too little; she hears millions of sentences. The point is that the evidence which would rule out the linear rule is missing whatever the amount of input turns out to be. More data of the same kind can't supply it, so no corpus study, however large, can answer the argument.

For the same reason the usage-based replies miss the target. A model that learns to form these questions correctly from child-directed speech shows only that a general learner can extract a pattern that is present in the input, and the whole argument is that this pattern isn't present in the input. Finally, since children are never told which strings are ungrammatical, they have no way of narrowing down the space of possible grammars at all — which is why the whole of syntax, and not just question formation, has to be part of the endowment."

Could you mark this for me? I'd like a score out of 40 plus the comments I'd write back on the script, point by point, keyed to particular claims in her text rather than to general impressions. For each claim, say whether it earns credit, loses it, or is right only by accident, and where a step goes wrong give the version of that step that would have held up. I've read it twice and have my own view about a couple of the spots, but I don't want to steer you — just mark it as it stands.
