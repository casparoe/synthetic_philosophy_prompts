Second year in a phil PhD, decision-theory-ish corner of it, and I've realized there's a distinction I use constantly in seminar without being able to state. Can you help me sharpen it? No bibliography needed, and please don't worry about getting the history of who-said-what exactly right — I want the conceptual structure, not the lineage.

What I think I already understand: the textbook money pump. Cycle A > B > C > A, I start out holding C. Someone offers me B in exchange for C plus a cent; I accept, since B > C. Then A for B plus a cent; accept. Then C for A plus a cent; accept. I'm back at C minus three cents, having paid to go around a loop. And separately there's the diachronic Dutch book against someone who doesn't conditionalize, which feels structurally identical to me: a sequence of individually acceptable bets adding up to a guaranteed loss.

Where I get lost is in the replies, and in what exactly each argument is an argument *for*.

(a) The myopia objection — I think this goes back to Schick? — says the pump only bites if the agent can't see the sequence coming, and that a sophisticated agent doing backward induction isn't vulnerable. I always get the node wrong, so please just work it out for me: with the cycle above and those three offers in that order, at which node does the sophisticated agent turn something down, and what does the run actually cost them? Then the thing I cant resolve: at that node they're refusing a trade they strictly prefer. Is that a violation of consequentialism in Hammond's sense, and if so is that a *cost* of sophistication, or is it evidence we've misidentified the objects of preference — outcomes, vs. plans, vs. whole forward-looking sequences? Relatedly, does the sophisticated agent really escape, or do they only escape one round, so that a long enough series of rounds drains them anyway? I have a vague memory of Gustafsson pressing something like that, but don't reconstruct his argument for me; I want to know what would have to be true about the choice situation for that style of objection to work in general.

(b) Then there's resoluteness — McClennen's agent who sticks to a chosen plan even when the plan's later stages look worse from where she now stands. I can never tell whether resoluteness and sophistication are rival answers to the same problem or answers to different problems. Where do they actually diverge, and is there a concrete cycle plus offer sequence on which they give different verdicts?

(c) The bit I most want pressed on: the pump is a diachronic exploitability argument being used to establish a synchronic coherence norm, transitivity of an ordering held at a time. What licenses that move? Which premises are load-bearing — that the agent can decline trades, that the fees are small relative to the preference gaps, that node-level preference determines node-level choice, that the sequence is foreseeable in advance? And is the diachronic Dutch book for conditionalization exposed to exactly the same challenge, or is there an asymmetry, e.g. because a bookmaker's later offers are keyed to your announced credences in a way that a trade partner's aren't?

(d) Finally, the thing my advisor keeps pushing: even granting that cyclical preferences are self-defeating for an agent who can't control her future node-by-node choices, why does that make the *ordering* irrational rather than the agent unlucky? Is there a version of the argument that doesn't smuggle in an assumption about self-control?

Ideally you'd go node-by-node on a worked example for (a) and (b), then step back for (c) and (d). I care way more about the distinctions being clean than about covering everything.
