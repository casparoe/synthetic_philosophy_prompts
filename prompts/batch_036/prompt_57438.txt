My parish reading group did Augustine on lying this month — *On Lying* and the later one he wrote to Consentius about whether you may pretend to be a Priscillianist in order to catch Priscillianists — and afterwards two people got into a heated arguement about a real thing that happened, they picked me as tiebreaker, and I completely failed at it.

The case: Nadia's brother Marc was on a night bus. A young woman came and sat down next to him, whispered "please pretend you know me, that guy's following me," and then the guy leaned over and asked Marc, "you know her?" Marc said, "yeah, she's my niece, she's coming home with me." The guy got off at the next stop. Both parts of what Marc said were false and Marc knew it.

Nadia and Peter (he's a seminarian) weren't arguing about whether it was the right thing to do — they both thought it was. They were arguing about what Augustine's own machinery delivers. The exchange, as best I can remember it:

1. **Nadia:** On his own ranking this is the bottom rung — a lie that injures nobody and protects someone from rape — so his system basically sides with Marc. When the mildest category on your list is this one, the absolutism is decorative.
2. **Peter:** Ranking isn't permission. He keeps even the mildest ones inside the list of sins, and he gives a reason that has nothing to do with the scale: a lie damages the liar's own soul, and your soul is worth more than somebody else's body, so Marc would be trading a greater good for a lesser one. Grading severity and permitting the act are different moves.
3. **Nadia:** Then that *is* a weighing. "Your soul outweighs her body" is a claim about which goods are bigger, and once he's comparing goods, his conclusion is contingent on the comparison. It only holds if the damage a lie does to the soul is a fixed amount regardless of stakes — which is the one premise he never argues for. If the damage scales with why you lied, Marc's lie costs him nearly nothing and I win on his own arithmetic.
4. **Peter:** He's not doing arithmetic. His reason is that words are signs, and a sign's whole job is to carry what's in your mind into someone else's; uttering what you don't hold is using the instrument against its purpose, wrong the way it'd be wrong to use your eyes to make darkness. Outcomes never enter, so there's no comparison left to reverse.
5. **Nadia:** Fine, but the escape hatches wreck him. He allows you to keep silent, to refuse to answer, or — as he seems to think — to say something true you fully expect will be taken the wrong way. And his own definition says a liar is someone who holds "one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind." On a night bus silence is itself a sign: Marc saying nothing right after she'd whispered to him reads as "no" and gets her hurt. So either the loophole is real and the operative distinction is about what the hearer ends up believing, which is outcomes after all, or the loophole is closed and he has to say Marc couldn't have stayed quiet either. Pick one.
6. **Peter:** Silence isn't a sign of anything determinate. It puts no content into the guy's head, so it can't put a false content there; the inference is the pursuer's own act, and Augustine is explicit that you judge lying by the speaker's heart, not by what the hearer concludes. Misleading-but-true speech is the same case: the words did their job correctly. No dilemma — the rule is about your will and it stays deontological all the way down.
