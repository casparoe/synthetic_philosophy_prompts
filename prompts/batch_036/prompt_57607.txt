I'm teaching a seminar where students reflexively slide from "two people found it at the same time" to "it was in the air, it was inevitable," and I want a case that jams that slide. Build me a thought experiment to spec:

- Set it in present-day science, no sci-fi, and the scenario itself should be about one paragraph.
- It has to be a genuine Merton-style multiple: two groups with no contact and no leakage produce substantially the same result inside about a year of each other.
- But the counterfactuals have to be assymetric. Without group A, the result doesn't arrive for decades or maybe never; without group B, it arrives on schedule anyway. So the multiplicity is real and yet it licenses no inevitability inference — and the asymmetry has to be generated by something structural about the problem and the field, not by a lucky accident, not by A having a once-in-a-generation genius, not by A having some private advantage B lacks.
- And you can't just assert the asymmetry in the last sentence. The machinery has to be in the scenario so a reader could run the counterfactuals themselves. Stipulating the verdict is cheating.

Then do the work: run both counterfactuals explicitly and say what you're holding fixed across the nearby worlds; add one wrinkle where the field ends up citing and building almost exclusively on B's version, and tell me what that does to the question of whether it matters who discovered it — is the answer about credit, about the content, or about uptake? Name the single feature of the case that's doing the work, and tell me what happens if I delete it — does the case collapse into a singleton, or into a symmetric multiple the inevitabilist is happy with? Finish with the inevitabilist's best attack on your case, letting them redescribe it however they like, and say honestly whether the attack lands.
