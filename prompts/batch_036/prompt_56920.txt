I hold a version of the suffering-risk thesis, and I want a systematic account of what would count against it.

The version is not the familiar one. It does not rest on negative utilitarianism, nor on any asymmetry between suffering and happiness, nor on a claim about the lexical badness of pain. It rests instead on three premises about the structure of large-scale optimization, which I will call the entrenchment argument:

1. Outcomes involving astronomically many sentient beings are reachable only through processes that are self-optimizing and self-replicating. No such outcome could be produced by deliberate, welfare-tracking stewardship, because stewardship does not scale.
2. Such processes are far easier to aim at instrumental proxies — throughput, persistence, resource acquisition, legible indicators of success — than at the diffuse, context-sensitive, and hard-to-specify conditions under which sentient life goes well. On this picture, badness is cheap to specify and goodness is expensive.
3. Once a proxy-directed process controls the relevant resources, its outcomes are locked in, because the very features that make it efficient at scale also make it resistant to correction from within.

So suffering risks dominate extinction risks not because suffering outweighs happiness unit for unit, but because bad outcomes are cheaper to specify and harder to reverse than good ones. Extinction is a one-off event; the entrenchment of a badly specified optimizer is a standing condition.

What I am looking for:

- A map of the view's exposure. For each premise, state what would have to be true for it to fail, and identify which premise is load-bearing and which could be given up without abandoning the conclusion.
- A distinction between truth-defeaters and significance-defeaters. It is possible that the argument is sound and yet recommends the same research agenda as its rivals, in which case it would fail not as philosophy but as a basis for allocating attention. Treat that possibility seriously rather than as a rhetorical complaint, and say what would show that the entrenchment argument changes what anyone should do.
- An assessment of the empirical evidence already on hand: the historical record of how efficiently optimizing institutions have treated the welfare of those inside their scope; whether entrenched harmful arrangements tend to be reformed from within or only displaced by outside competition; and what the trajectory of industrial animal agriculture suggests about how easily a bad outcome can be specified and locked in. Say how much weight each of these can bear, and where the analogy to engineered optimizers breaks down.
- What the view predicts that its main rivals do not. The rivals I have in mind are the view that capabilities generalize across aims, so that a sufficiently capable system converges on broadly human values, and the view that suffering and extinction are comparable in magnitude with neither dominating. If the entrenchment argument makes no prediction that distinguishes it from these, say so plainly, and say whether that is fatal or an acceptable feature of a claim about the far future.
- An honest judgment about whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability — for instance by retreating from the claim that suffering risks dominate to the weaker claim that they are non-negligible, which no possible evidence could rule out. If it has, decide whether the retreat is a legitimate response to genuine uncertainty or an evasion, and whether a view of this kind is entitled to the retreat.
- A separation of what a sympathetic defender would themselves concede as counting against the view from what they would dismiss as beside the point. I want the defender's own standards of defeat, not only the objections critics press.

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and close with your own assessment of how exposed the position actually is. I would find most persuasive an argument showing that one of the three premises is false for structural reasons, and least persuasive an argument whose content amounts to the far future being unknowable.
