I cover science and ideas for a mid-sized magazine, and somewhere along the line I became the unofficial person who decides how we handle a readership that's gone sideways on a factual question. Nobody appointed me. It just kept landing on my desk, and now my editor wants a written recommendation on a new one by Thursday, and I'd like to be consistent with myself, which means I first have to figure out what I've actually been doing.

Here's the track record, more or less in order.

When our circulation in a couple of counties collapsed after the local papers shut down, readers there had simply never seen the state's own groundwater monitoring data. I wrote a plain explainer with the numbers in it. It worked — letters to the editor, and one school board reversed itself within a month.

Then there was a large Facebook group running a claim about a childhood vaccine. I published a correction, polite, sourced, links straight to the primary studies. Nothing happened. Six weeks later the same claim came back stronger, and members were citing my piece as evidence that we were part of the problem. When my editor asked what to do next, I told her to stop aiming corrections at them entirely, and instead to go find the one pediatrician the group still listened to and get her on the record about something adjacent — no debunking, no mention of the claim. She agreed it felt dishonest. We did it, and eight months later the group's position had shifted on its own.

A subreddit that banned all links to mainstream outlets: I said we should keep publishing into it anyway, because it had no moderators worth the name, no money behind it, no dues or status attached to belonging, and anyone who drifted away simply wasn't missed. Its members had been told to ignore us, not taught to hate us, and one of them emailed me after reading four of our pieces to say he'd changed his mind. I counted that as a win and used it as precedent twice.

A national network of alternative-health practitioners, with their own conference, their own journal, their own celebrity faces, and a standing line about the medical boards being captured by pharmaceutical money: I argued hard against debating any of them in public, on the grounds that a debate is precisely the format that lets them show off how thoroughly they've inoculated themselves against outsiders. We ran a reported feature about a family that had left the network instead, told from the inside.

A rumor about a new school curriculum that spread purely by word of mouth, with no organizing body and parents who were mostly embarrassed about it once asked: straight explainer, done, dead in a week.

And a single well-funded account manufacturing a claim from nothing, with an audience that had no structure of its own: I said go after the funder and the account, and don't waste a word on the audience, because the audience was only following whoever spoke last.

Here's the new one. There's a private platform, somewhere around forty thousand members, almost all clinicians — ER physicians, nurse practitioners, ICU staff. Over two years they've converged on a treatment protocol for a certain kind of acute presentation that the outcome data says is doing real harm. Three things make it weird. They are extremely fluent in the primary literature and cite it accurately; the protocol grew up bottom-out from bedside experience, not from any guru, and there's no leader, no conference, no journal, no dues, no money, and no cost at all to leaving. But they have a fixed, contemptuous account of the specialty society and the federal regulators as captured by industry funding, and they use it to dismiss anything those bodies put out. They don't distrust us. They read the magazine, several have written for us, and they like our coverage.

The science desk wants to run a data explainer on the protocol's outcomes. I've floated instead a reported piece about clinicians who used it and stopped, in their own words, with the numbers appearing only incidentally.

So: what rule have I been running, and are there other rules that fit everything above just as well? Tell me which of them this case splits apart and what each one predicts I'd say. Then give me your best guess at what I should actually recommend Thursday, with the reason for preferring it, and tell me which of my earlier decisions is the closest analog — or say plainly that they underdetermine this and that either choice commits me to a principle I haven't been operating with, and name that principle. If one of my past verdicts was just wrong and inconsistent with the rest, I'd rather hear it now; its not going to be the first time. And finally, describe a hypothetical future case that would let me tell the candidate rules apart from each other, because I want to be able to test whichever one I adopt.

Please answer in plain continuous prose, the way you'd write me an email. No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — if you need to set the candidate rules side by side, do it in sentences. And a first draft is fine, I just need to get something down before Thursday, even if you end up recieving half of it wrong.
