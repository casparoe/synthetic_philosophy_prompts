I audit a reading group on risk regulation that spends most of its time on the precautionary principle, and last week one of the members — a policy analyst considerably sharper than I am — presented what he takes to be a proof that the principle is not merely misguided but empty, and that it drags the doing/allowing distinction down with it. I could not find the exit during the session and I have not found it since. He states the principle himself, in his own formulation:

(P) For any proposed course of action A that carries a credible risk of serious and irreversible harm, and where the causal evidence bearing on that risk is unsettled, the burden falls on the proponents of A to establish that A is safe; unless that burden is discharged, A is not to be undertaken.

Then the argument. (1) Declining to undertake A is itself a course of action — call it not-A: a decision, made by identifiable people, with foreseeable consequences for identifiable others. (2) By (1), not-A carries its own credible risks of serious and irreversible harm, what people in this field call foregone risk. (3) So (P) applies to not-A exactly as it applies to A: the burden falls on the proponents of not-A to establish that not-A is safe, and unless they discharge it, not-A is not to be undertaken. (4) Therefore (P) requires both that A not be undertaken and that not-A not be undertaken. (5) A principle that, in every case to which it applies, forbids each of the available options is not a decision procedure; it is either a contradiction or an instruction to do nothing while insisting that nothing is forbidden. (6) And this is no quirk of pathological cases: there is no risk-free baseline, since a status quo is itself an allocation, and every real regulatory decision has victims on both sides of it. (7) So (P) is unusable in every real case — PP is not a false principle but an empty one. (8) Note, though, what did the work: (1) and (6). Those premises say that an asymmetry between acting and not acting is an artifact of where the baseline happens to be drawn. (9) The asymmetry between doing harm and allowing harm is an asymmetry of exactly that kind. (10) So if the argument against (P) succeeds, the doing/allowing distinction is an artifact too.

His running example is the 1976 swine flu decision, and the facts he gave look right to me: a novel H1N1 strain turned up at Fort Dix that winter, one young recruit collapsed and died after a forced march, the CDC read the strain as a relative of the 1918 virus, and Ford announced a program to vaccinate the entire country. Roughly 43 million people received the shot before the program was suspended that December amid reports of Guillain-Barré syndrome, at an excess of about one case per hundred thousand vaccinated and something on the order of two dozen deaths attributed to it. The pandemic never arrived. He says the case is a textbook instance of (2): the precautionary measure produced harm of the kind it was meant to forestall, and the option of waiting had a catastrophic tail of its own.

I can recite the standard complaint — that PP is paralyzing, or self-defeating — in general form, but I cannot locate the step in his version, and I suspect the standard complaint is too blunt to reach it. Two constraints of my own, which you may treat as fixed: I am confident (6) is true, because I do not believe there is any such thing as a risk-free status quo, and I want to keep the doing/allowing distinction. So whatever is wrong lies either earlier than (6) or in the passage from (4) to (5) — unless you think one of those two commitments has to go, in which case tell me which and why.

What I want is this. Name the step you would reject and say precisely why it looked acceptable where it stands, since I want the diagnosis of the temptation and not just the verdict. Then price the alternatives: what the argument costs if I reject (2), or (3), or (9) instead, and whether any of those rejections leaves a version of PP worth keeping — or whether the surviving version has quietly become a comparative risk-assessment rule that no longer deserves the name. Finally, give me your verdict on the spillover: does (10) really follow from the premises that drive the refutation, or does it borrow from them something the doing/allowing distinction never needed? Please open with one sentence naming the step you reject, keep the entire response under 600 words, and organize it in at most three numbered blocks — the rejection, the pricing, the spillover.
