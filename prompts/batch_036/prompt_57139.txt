I'm six books in and I've noticed I keep writing the same scene over and over, but I cant say what makes some versions of it land and others feel like a cheat to the reader. So here's my gut, going by my own drafts — tell me what principle sits underneath it, if there is one.

- Mother in a burning house, can carry one of her two kids out. Real dilemma. Afterwards she isn't blameworthy, but "you did the right thing" would be an insult, and I want her to feel she owes the one she left something.
- Field hospital, surgeon lets three die to save seven. Doesn't feel like a dilemma at all. Grim arithmetic, there's a right answer, nothing left over except tiredness. Structurally the same shape as the last one and I treat them completely differently, which bugs me tho.
- Guy skips a promised lunch to stop a mugging. Obviously correct, nothing outstanding — except I keep wanting him to apologise, and if nothing was actually violated, apologise for what?
- Two kids drowning, neither of them his, he saves one. Here I want it to feel like the burning house and not the hospital, and I honestly don't know why.
- Woman forced to pick which of her own children lives. Real dilemma, there was no right thing to do, and she's still not to blame for having picked. But if she freezes and lets both go, that's worse — she IS to blame.
- Man takes a job that puts him in two cities at once and then has to break one of two promises. Fake dilemma, he did this to himself. Still needs the grief in the scene.

My working rule when I'm drafting: it's a real dilemma only if you couldn't afterwards say to the character "you did the right thing" and mean it. Test that against the six, say which verdicts it can't deliver and which single verdict I should probably give up, and then state whatever principle survives as precisely as you can manage — including what it predicts about a case I haven't got yet: a character who can either keep a promise to a friend who just died or stop a stranger from being badly hurt, where the two are exactly as bad as each other.
