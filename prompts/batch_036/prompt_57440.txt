**Purpose.** I am preparing a session for a first-year graduate seminar in population and infinite ethics. The intuition I want to break is this: students tend to assume that whether we discount the future is a merely technical parameter — one that changes the *magnitude* of an option's value but never the *direction* of a comparison, so long as the two options are otherwise held fixed. I need a single decision problem, worked out numerically, in which three standard ways of aggregating well-being across an endless future are applied to the *same* pair of options and return three different verdicts: strictly better, strictly worse, and exactly indifferent. The verdicts must be generated by the numbers. If the construction looks like the verdicts were fixed in advance and the arithmetic dressed up to match them, it is useless to me.

Please do not cite anything, do not offer a reading list, and do not put any passage in quotation marks. I want the construction and the computations, not the literature.

**Setting constraints.**

1. One civilization with an unbounded future; generations are indexed 1, 2, 3, … .
2. Exactly the same number of people exists in every generation under both options, and the same generations exist under both. There is to be no non-identity problem and no different-people choice: the only thing that varies is the level of well-being.
3. Well-being per generation is a real number in [0, 10] on a common cardinal scale. No unbounded utilities within any period, no St. Petersburg structure, no infinite value in any finite stretch of time.
4. The only infinity is temporal. No Hilbert-Hotel spatial infinities, no branching timelines, no multiverse, no exotic physics. Indefinite survival is a stipulation of the case, and should be flagged as such.
5. The choice is made once, now, and is irreversible: an institution with binding commitment power over the whole future (or a steward with an unalterable mandate) selects an entire profile. Sequential-rationality escapes are therefore unavailable.
6. Shape of the pair: option A imposes a severe but bounded cost on a finite initial stretch of generations, and is better than option B at every generation thereafter, with A's per-generation advantage shrinking as time goes on. The exact form of the initial block and of the shrinking advantage is yours to choose and to justify.

**The three rules and the required verdicts.** State each rule in a sentence or two that someone with one term of consequentialism and no measure theory can follow, then apply it to your numbers:

- (a) Undiscounted summation, evaluated by the overtaking or catch-up limit of the partial sums. Verdict required: **A strictly better**.
- (b) Exponential discounting at a discount factor you select and defend as one a real policy body might actually adopt. Verdict required: **B strictly better**.
- (c) Comparison of the limits of average well-being per generation across the first N generations. Verdict required: **exactly indifferent**. Add one sentence saying what a shift-invariant finitely additive measure applied to the difference sequence yields here, and why it coincides with (c).

**Arithmetic requirements.** Give the profiles explicitly as formulas with concrete parameter values — not as a verbal description. Then compute rather than assert:

- The generation N\* at which the undiscounted partial sums flip from favoring B to favoring A.
- A demonstration that, at your chosen discount factor, B wins at *every* finite truncation of the case, however far out. Explain why this must be so; I want it on the record that (b) cannot be dismissed as an artifact of taking a limit.
- A proof that, given your other constraints, there must exist some δ\* < 1 at which the discounted verdict flips in A's favor, together with the equation that determines δ\* and one sentence on what fixes its magnitude.
- A demonstration that both average limits in (c) exist and are equal.

**Diagnostics.** Identify the single structural feature of your construction that does the work — the thing that makes the three rules peel apart — and state the constraint on it: if the shrinking advantage is a power law, for instance, say which range of exponents preserves all three verdicts and what breaks outside it. Then perform two removals. Truncate the future at a large finite N and report what happens to each verdict. Change the decay rate of A's advantage and report the same.

**Adversarial section.** Give the strongest objection a defender of discounting can press against *the case itself*, rather than a general defense of discounting, and say whether it succeeds. Then give the strongest objection from someone who finds (c)'s indifference unacceptable — A is better at every generation past a certain point and never stops being better — and say whether that objection defeats (c) or merely shows it incomplete. Finally, list any other familiar aggregation rule your pair leaves without a verdict, and say why.

**Honesty clause.** Close with a short paragraph naming any point at which your construction could fairly be accused of rigging the result, and any idealization a reader should discount when generalizing from it. If some pair of specifications turns out to be unsatisfiable, say so and identify which two conflict, rather than quietly weakening one of them.

**Format.** 1,100–1,400 words, with headers for the scenario (prose only, no more than 350 words, and it must come before any mathematics), the numerical specification, the three verdicts, the diagnostics, and the adversarial section.
