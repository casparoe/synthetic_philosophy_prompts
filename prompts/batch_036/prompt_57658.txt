I am a third-year PhD student in metaphysics, and for two terms I have co-convened a reading group on identity and modality in which two members have argued past each other with more energy than progress. One defends a Kripkean necessity-of-origin thesis: no organism could have come into being from a different origin than the one it actually has. The other is convinced the thesis is either false or unintelligible, on grounds that shift from week to week — sometimes it is that twinning and embryo-splitting break the link between an organism and any particular origin, sometimes that origin claims smuggle in a notion of coming from that carries no modal content, sometimes that the thesis survives only by being weakened into something no one would have signed up for. Before our closing session I want to circulate, as a pre-read, the document the two of them ought to have produced back in month one. Please draft it. You should approach this as a specialist in the metaphysics of identity, origin, and de re modality who has worked through the arguments on both sides, and before writing anything you should spend some effort brainstorming privately: enumerate the candidate referents an essentialist might mean by an organism's origin — a gamete pair, a zygote, a fertilization event, a genome-bearing cell, a portion of matter — and note carefully how the shape of the disagreement changes depending on which one is selected. I suspect a good deal of the group's stalemate is an artifact of the two sides having different referents in view, and I want that suspicion tested rather than assumed.

The deliverable is a joint statement both camps could put their names to, and it should contain five parts. First, a numbered section of claims both sides genuinely accept — about what identity over time for organisms requires, about what the biology of reproduction actually involves, about which arguments each side concedes are not decisive — with the discipline that a claim belongs here only if it would survive each side's own scrutiny, not merely if it is vague enough to be unobjectionable. Second, a disagreements table with columns for the topic, the essentialist's formulation of the dispute in wording the essentialist would accept, the contingentist's formulation in wording the contingentist would accept, and a judgment on whether the row records a substantive disagreement or a verbal one. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one party, as distinct from the questions the group has been litigating. Fourth, for each crux, a concrete case or argument that both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance and an explicit statement of what result would count as a concession. Fifth, a short closing section in which you commit to a view about which parts of the dispute you judge to be merely verbal, and say why — I would rather have a contested diagnosis here than a diplomatically empty one.

Two cases should figure prominently, because our group kept failing to make headway on them and I want them handled rather than waved at. The first is mitochondrial donation: a child whose nuclear DNA comes from two people and whose mitochondrial DNA comes from a third. What is her origin on each candidate construal, and does the essentialist owe a single answer, or is the thesis tacitly indexed to a reproductive technology it was never designed for? The second is somatic-cell nuclear transfer of the kind that produced Dolly — a clone whose nuclear genome was taken from an already-existing adult organism and placed in an enucleated egg. Does she share an origin with her genetic donor, and if not, what does that reveal about whether origin tracks matter, biological information, or the event of an organism's coming to be? Please also treat the sorites pressure directly: if you could not have come from a different sperm, could you have come from a sperm differing from the actual one by a single molecule, and what does the essentialist's answer commit her to about the sharpness or vagueness of the origin relation? Throughout, keep the wording neutral, resist declaring a winner, and do not pad the agreement section with platitudes that both sides would accept only because they say nothing. Around fifteen hundred words is right. You need not supply citations or a bibliography — I will add those myself — but the arguments should be recognizable to someone who has read the literature, and where you are reconstructing a position rather than quoting it, say plainly that you are reconstructing.
