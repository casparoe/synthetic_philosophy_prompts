I sit in on a Tuesday reading group at someone's flat — mostly non-philosophers, we work through old texts badly. Last week we did the sea battle bit in Aristotle's On Interpretation (chapter 9, I think, the one everyone argues about), and afterwards two of the regulars, Ines and Marcus, started up on the walk to the bus and didn't stop until the pub closed. Ines is a mathematician and Marcus is a lawyer, which he's convinced makes him good at this and it definately doesn't. I was mostly trying to eat, so I couldn't follow who was ahead. Here's roughly how it went, in order:

(1) Ines said the sea battle argument forces a choice: if 'there will be a sea battle tomorrow' is already true, then it isn't up to anyone whether there is one — deliberate all you want, the outcome's written. So either the future is fixed and deliberating is pointless, or that sentence just isn't true or false yet. She takes the second horn. (2) Marcus said that's a modal fallacy: from "it's true that p" you get "p", not "necessarily p" — calling something true isn't calling it unavoidable, so you can keep every truth value and keep free will too. (3) Ines said the fatalist never needed necessity. Try the past instead: if it's true now that there's a battle tomorrow, it was true yesterday as well, and nobody can now act so that yesterday's truths would have been different — that's just what "past" means. So there's nothing I can do that's compossible with there being no battle, hence nothing I can do. No modal operator anywhere. (4) Marcus said she's equivocating on which facts about yesterday are fixed: some are hard — the coin landed, the kettle boiled — but 'yesterday it was true that there'd be a battle' isn't a fact about yesterday at all, it's just tomorrow's fact with yesterday's date stamp on it. Fixity covers hard facts only, so her argument assumes what it needs to prove. (5) Ines said then he's given up bivalence without admitting it — if it wasn't settled yesterday, it wasn't true yesterday, and "soft fact" is a polite word for a truth value gap. Plus the date-stamp picture does no work: if soft facts aren't fixed, then it's in my power that yesterday's sentence was false, and false isn't true, so you get a difference in the past anyway, just one nobody could detect. (6) Marcus said nothing gets revised — the value doesn't shift, what varies is which history you evaluate in. Two histories can match exactly up to tonight and diverge tomorrow, and my choice is part of what determines which one we're in, so yesterday is untouched. He added that she needs the same flexibility for any claim linking yesterday to tomorrow, like 'yesterday it was true that you'd be wearing that jumper' — if fixity bit on those, then everything about tomorrow would already be out of my hands, which is her fatalism over again. (7) Ines said: fine, then say it out loud — on your view the sentence has no determinate value until the choice is made. Keep the word 'true' if it comforts you, the substance is mine. (8) Marcus said it isn't, and his costs less: he keeps excluded middle and every tautology, 'there'll be a battle or there won't' is true full stop, whereas she has to say that disjunction isn't true while refusing to say which disjunct fails — that's logic-breaking, not modest. (9) Ines said a disjunction that's true when neither disjunct is true is exactly as bizarre as a sentence with no value, and he only gets his tautologies by treating 'true' as shorthand for "comes out true on whichever history we end up in," which is her gap with the word 'true' glued over the top. So they both broke something, he just broke his quietly. Neither budged and they split the bill.

Can you go through this move by move and say who's right at each one, not just who wins overall — a rating per move would be great, and say when someone's half right. Then: who's more right on the whole, and what do you think both of them kept skating past? Two specific things I couldn't work out on my own. First, is Ines's move in (3) genuinely a different argument from the one Marcus shot down in (2), or did she just dress the same argument up again? Second, Marcus says in (4) that a truth about tomorrow isn't a fact about yesterday, talks in (6) about histories we might be in, and claims in (8) that he's kept all the truth values intact — is that one coherent position, or is he sliding between two different ones without noticing?
