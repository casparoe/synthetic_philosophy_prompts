So — third-year philosophy of art seminar, 22 students, and I've got one 90-minute slot on forgery and the value of originals. Room has a projector, movable tables (so groups of four are fine), I can print stuff, budget is basically whatever's in my pocket, call it twenty bucks. What I do NOT want is another session where I walk them through the standard moves — Goodman's line that the mere possibility of a visually indistinguishable fake should change how you look at the thing in front of you, the appearance-based pushback that if you genuinely can't tell them apart then the difference can't be aesthetically relevant, and the achievement reply that says the value sits in what somebody actually did rather than in how the surface looks — because they can memorise all three, regurgitate them on the essay, and still think forgery is a victimless crime. I want them to be caught in the contradiction first and only then get handed the vocabulary. Also worth knowing: I vaguely remember there's psych experiments where the exact same picture gets rated higher once people are told a human made it instead of a machine, or that the artist laboured over it more. If that's real, it's a nice thing to have them unknowingly replicate.

Design the activity. My rough idea, override it if you've got something better: they spend the first stretch comparing/ranking a set of works presented with no provenance at all and write down commitments they can't quietly walk back from; then provenance gets revealed in a way that makes some of those commitments uncomfortable; then a third phase where they have to stare hard at a pair trying to spot which one is the fake — without being told whether a fake is even present in the set, or which side it'd be on — because that's where the "train your eye" intuition either earns its keep or dies in front of them. I'd like at least one pair in the set to involve an AI-generated image, since that's the version of this the students will actually argue about, and maybe one where the provenance reversal is the other way round (the "original" turns out to be a later copy of something lost, or the ugly one turns out to be the real Van Meegeren-era fake that fooled experts).

Give me: the exact materials list and how to source it for free or nearly free, a minute-by-minute run sheet, the reveal mechanic including who knows what and when — and I suspect I'm going to have to actively mislead them about which items are originals, so tell me whether that's defensible in a class, how to set it up so it doesn't blow up trust, and how to disclose it afterwards. Also what each group should write down during phase one so the debrief has actual data to point at rather than vibes. Then the debrief itself: map the moments they had onto the three positions above and force them to say which premise they'd give up, not just "it's complicated." And be straight with me about three things — what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it (I'd bet one confident kid says "so it's all just status and money" and takes the room with him — how do I head that off without shutting him down), which feature of real forgery the activity structurally can't reproduce (real fakes deceive a market and a buyer, and my printer is not going to give me genuine visual indistinguishability, so I want to know what part of the puzzle I'll have to supply by talking rather than by them doing), and how to handle the student who says the AI image also took enormous effort and skill, just distributed differently across people and time. That last one is where I think the achievement account either cracks open or gets a lot more interesting, and I'd like the session to arrive there on purpose rather than by luck.
