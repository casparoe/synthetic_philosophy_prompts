I'm teaching an upper-level seminar on the philosophy of punishment, and for the past three weeks we've been working through two bodies of material that don't obviously belong together. On one side: the retributivist–rehabilitative debate, including the Kantian worry that a system which incarcerates people in order to change their characters treats them as objects to be worked on rather than as persons to be answered to, and the related problem that any treatment regime whose length is set by progress rather than by the offence makes the prisoner's release hostage to his own assessed compliance. On the other side: the Nordic penological literature and its two showcase institutions, Halden and Bastøy, with their principle of normality (that life inside should differ as little as possible from life outside), their import model (schooling, healthcare and social work delivered inside the walls by the same public agencies that provide them in the community), their preference for dynamic security — officers and inmates in ordinary conversation, unarmed, sharing meals and activities — over static security, and their very long maximum determinate sentence. We also spent a session on the two standing objections: that a comfortable prison fails the less-eligibility constraint, since the condition of the prisoner must remain worse than that of the poorest free labourer, or the system loses its deterrent and its political legitimacy; and that Nordic exceptionalism is an artefact of small, wealthy, high-trust welfare states rather than a moral discovery, and so cannot simply be transplanted. One of my students, who has clearly read more Hugo than I have, insisted that Les Misérables is essentially a tract for the Halden model avant la lettre: the bagne at Toulon hardens Valjean, and the Bishop's house at Digne remakes him, so the novel already knows that conditions and human relations, not severity, are what reform a person. Before I either endorse or demolish that claim in front of a room of twelve undergraduates and three visiting law students, I want it properly tested.

So: read Les Misérables against this debate and tell me how much of the student's thesis survives. I'd like the reading to be specific about the mechanisms the novel actually proposes. What does Hugo identify as the operative cause of Valjean's change — a designed environment, a normalized routine, meaningful activity, trained staff — or something the correctional-service vocabulary has no slot for, namely an unearned act of generosity by a person who owes him nothing and is not trying to reduce anyone's recidivism? If it is the latter, say plainly whether Hugo's transform is rehabilitative in the modern administrative sense at all, or whether it is something a state cannot deliberately produce, because it depends on the moral character of a particular benefactor rather than on a protocol. Then take seriously the parts of the novel that are not about custody but about what happens at the gate: the yellow passport that brands Valjean as an ex-convict, the innkeepers who turn him away, the police surveillance he is required to report under, the fact that he cannot hold a job, a name, or a place to sleep. Is the novel's real institutional diagnosis a normality argument about release rather than about confinement — that the punishment is not the nineteen years but the permanent status that outlives them — and does that make Hugo a better ally for the collateral-consequences and reentry literature than for anyone designing a prison? And note the proportionality problem the student's framing skips over: five years for a loaf of bread and fourteen more for the escape attempts is presented as monstrous in magnitude, which suggests Hugo's first target is desert and sentencing scale, something Halden-style design leaves untouched, since a humane prison still gives you twenty-one years.

Finally, I want the reading to be honest in both directions, so please give me the places where the book resists. Test the thesis against the Arras sequence, where Champmathieu is about to be condemned as Valjean and three of Valjean's old galley companions testify against him — does the novel there supply its own evidence that the bagne reliably produces recidivists and that Valjean is the statistical exception created outside the system? Test it against Valjean's own later conduct — concealment, a staged death, years of evasion, deception of Cosette and Marius — and ask whether a parole board applying any ordinary risk or supervision-assessment metric would classify him as reformed or as an absconder. Test it against Javert, and tell me whether he is best read as a retributivist, a legal positivist, or a man whose entire practical identity is built out of rules, and whether his suicide is meant to show that retributivism cannot incorporate mercy or only that legalism collapses when it meets a good person. And test the whole thing against Fantine, whose free life in Montreuil-sur-Mer is arguably worse than anything the galleys do to Valjean — which would make Hugo, perhaps inadvertently, an exponent of exactly the less-eligibility observation his reformist readers want to deny, since the punishment of the free poor exceeds the punishment of the convict. Concede where you have to that reading a mid-nineteenth-century French Romantic epic, much of which is digression on Waterloo, the sewers, the convent and the argot of the criminal underworld, through twenty-first-century Scandinavian prison architecture is an anachronism that distorts both the book and the policy. Then close by telling me what the novel contributes to the punishment-versus-rehabilitation debate that argument alone cannot: what does it show about the temporality of a sentence, about being addressed as a person rather than managed as a risk, or about the difference between a state that wants you not to reoffend and a stranger who simply trusts you, that my seminar would lose if we stuck to the papers? Aim for something around fifteen hundred words, organized around a clear verdict on the student's claim, with the scenes named but paraphrased rather than quoted, since the class will have various different translations in front of them and I don't want paraphrase dressed up as quotation.
