I've been a 911 dispatcher in a rural county for six years and I'm halfway through a part-time bioethics certificate at the community college, mostly because my wife wanted me to have a thing. This week's unit is the doing/allowing distinction — killing versus letting die — and I assumed I'd breeze through it, since I've basically been living inside the distinction since 2019. Instead I sat down on my days off and wrote out my actual verdicts on a bunch of cases, real and hypothetical, and they don't hang together at all. I don't want a summary of the literature or a survey of positions. I want the principle behind my verdicts, if there is one.

Here's the rule of thumb I've been defending in class, which I actually believe: an act *does* harm if things end up worse than they would have if I'd never been involved at all; it merely *allows* harm if the bad thing was already on its way and I just failed to intercept it. Now the cases. (1) Washed-out road, unconscious diabetic, no rig can reach him for forty minutes; I don't coach his wife through the glucagon kit in the house because our medical director's protocol says don't coach on medications. He dies. Verdict: allowing, and I feel bad but it's allowing. (2) Same call, but I do coach her, and I describe the wrong product — I give her the steps for a vial and syringe when what they have is something else — and what she ends up injecting him with is his insulin pen, which was sitting right next to it. He dies. Verdict: doing. I did that with my mouth, from thirty miles away, through her hands. (3) Man choking, alone, twelve minutes out. I put him on speaker and walk him through abdominal thrusts against a chair back. He does it wrong and dies sooner than he otherwise would have. Verdict: doing — except my rule says allowing, because I didn't start anything, the death was already on track. (4) Same man, same twelve minutes, I say nothing at all, just send the rig and stay on the line so he isn't alone. He dies at about the same moment. Verdict: allowing. So (3) and (4) differ only in whether I made noises, and I think that difference is real and not just a feeling, but I can't say what it is. (5) We used to staff a warm line for a valley with no cell coverage, a landline the county paid to keep up; the county cancels the program for budget reasons, and six months later a man out there has a heart attack, can't reach anyone, dies. Verdict: allowing. (6) Same valley, same line, still funded; a woman calls and starts describing symptoms and my shift ends at seven and I hang up without logging it because I don't want to eat into overtime, and she dies. Verdict: doing. Note that (5) and (6) differ only in where the off switch got flipped, in a budget office or in my hand, and my rule does get the right answers, but it feels like it gets them for the wrong reason. (7) Hospice patient's daughter calls and asks us not to send anyone; he's actively dying; I log it and send nothing. Verdict: allowing, and completely fine. (8) There's a standing no-heroics agreement on file for one particular nursing home — genuine, signed, but nobody at the scene can confirm it and I can't reach the administrator at three in the morning — so I don't send. A resident dies. Same shape as (7), minus anybody actually saying so at the time. Verdict: doing, or at least way closer to it. Which makes me suspect that what's carrying the weight in (7) isn't doing versus allowing at all, it's whose choice it was. (9) Our only ALS rig is on a call forty minutes the other direction; a farmer with a crushed leg bleeds out before anything can reach him. Verdict: allowing, obviously, I didn't crush his leg. Except I'm the one on the only line with the only map, and if I hadn't answered, his wife might have driven to the next county's station and gotten somebody, and I know how insane that sounds out loud but my rule of thumb seems to require taking it seriously.

So: is there a principle, or a small set of them, that delivers these verdicts? State it precisely, clause by clause, and tell me which case each clause is there to handle — if a clause isn't earning its place by fixing one of my cases, cut it. If you need two competing principles rather than one, give me both and say which case forces the split. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts you can't save, and which one I should give up; I'd much rather lose one verdict than recieve a principle with eleven clauses and a footnote. And please answer this directly, because it's the part that's actually keeping me up: what is "if I'd never been involved" supposed to mean — never taken the job, never answered that phone, or never spoken after answering? My verdicts seem to shift depending on which one I pick and I don't know which is right, or whether the question is even well-posed. Finally, give me two cases I haven't listed that would discriminate between whatever candidates you end up with, and say what each candidate predicts about them — I want to be able to check the principle against something new rather than just watch it fit the stuff I handed you. And once it's all written down, say whether doing versus allowing survives as its own axis at all, or whether it turns out to be a proxy for something else entirely: permissibility, whose choice it was, who has to explain it afterwards. My classmate thinks its a distinction without a difference and I want to know if he's right, because if he is I have to go back and think about a lot of nights on shift.
