I am recording secretary for a federation of roughly forty worker cooperatives, and I have been asked to prepare an internal review of our governance documents. Three features of the federation's practice puzzle me, and I want to know what they presuppose before I attempt to describe them.

First, the three federation officers are elected by secret ranked ballot under an instant-runoff rule. The bylaw commentary on that election provides that if the final round is a tie the winner is drawn by lot, and it defends the lot on the ground that at that point no counting rule can be shown to be fairer than any other.

Second, all substantive decisions are taken in open assembly by formal consensus. A facilitator states the proposal and tests the meeting; any single member may block, and a block halts the proposal and returns it to a working group with the blocker's reasons attached. A block is never put to a count and never overridden. Members are asked to say whether they can live with a proposal, not how strongly they prefer it, and the official record notes only the sense of the meeting — no tallies are recorded for consensus decisions.

Third, the bylaws contain a fallback that they themselves treat as a failure: if a proposal has been through two rounds of revision and still cannot reach consensus, the assembly may adopt it on a seventy-five percent vote. In the federation's four decades this has happened four times, and on each occasion the officers issued a statement regretting that the assembly had been reduced to counting.

So we count preferences and then pronounce the counting rule arbitrary at the margin; and elsewhere we refuse to count at all, treating a single dissent as decisive.

What theory of the group's decision — of what a collective choice is, and of what makes one legitimate — does the consensus procedure presuppose? Does it presuppose that there is a single right answer to be discovered by deliberation, that members' preferences are material to be transformed rather than data to be aggregated, that legitimacy requires that every member could accept the outcome, or some combination of these? Which rival accounts does it thereby rule out, and does it rule them out or merely decline to engage them?

Are the three practices consistent with one another? In particular, is the lot-drawing bylaw an admission that aggregation is underdetermined — and if so, does that admission undercut the assembly's confidence that consensus finds something rather than merely produces something? Does Arrow's impossibility theorem actually bear on the assembly's procedure, or does it fail to apply because the assembly never fixes a rule mapping profiles of individual preference onto outcomes? Be explicit about which of Arrow's conditions the consensus practice evades, and by what means.

Finally, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with; mark the places where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license exactly the same procedure; and say concretely what would have to change in the bylaws and in the conduct of the assembly if the federation adopted the opposite view — that a group's decision is nothing over and above the output of a stated rule applied to members' expressed preferences.

Lead with your bottom line in no more than three sentences, stating what you take these practices jointly to presuppose and whether they are consistent; then give the reconstruction. Keep the whole answer under 700 words, and do not append references or suggested reading.
