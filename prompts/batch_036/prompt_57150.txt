I'm writing up a dissertation chapter on criminal trials as memory-making and I've got properly stuck on one case, so I thought I'd throw it at you. Take the Eichmann trial in Jerusalem — Mossad grabbing him off a street in Buenos Aires in May 1960, the diplomatic row with Argentina at the UN afterwards, then the fifteen counts under Israel's 1950 Nazis and Nazi Collaborators law, the three-judge district court with Landau presiding, Hausner running the prosecution and Servatius the defence, hearings from April through the summer, verdict in December, appeal knocked back, hanged on 1 June 1962. I've got all of that, so don't spend words on it. What I want is the whole episode run through Bauman's Modernity and the Holocaust, properly — not as a nod to Arendt's banality-of-evil line that everyone reaches for and that Bauman himself thought was only half the story. So: which of Bauman's actual mechanisms does the courtroom's architecture presuppose, and which does it contradict? The instrumental rationality of the bureaucratic apparatus, the stretching of distance between act and consequence, the adiaphorisation business (his term, as I recall, for whole tracts of conduct being made morally indifferent rather than morally condemned), the gardening state, the Milgram-derived agentic state. The tension I can't get past is this: to convict, the court had to locate a continuous, intentional moral agent inside a man whose entire defence was that he had been a logistics function in a machine, and Bauman's thesis is precisely that the machine's modernity consisted in dissolving that agent. So either the court's moral ontology is wrong and it convicted on a fiction, or it reached the right result for reasons Bauman's account cannot license. Work that out rather than picking a side by default, and be disciplined about the mapping — say who the relevant actor is at each stage, what the court took as evidence of a state of mind, and what Bauman would count as the real locus of responsibility.

Then fold in the witness problem. Hausner called a great many survivors whose testimony had little to do with Eichmann personally, which is the bit Arendt attacked as stage management, and Buber and Scholem were among those who signed the open letter to Ben-Gurion arguing the trial shouldn't be held in Israel at all. What does Bauman's framework make of that — is the survivor testimony an attempt to reverse adiaphorisation by re-supplying the moral content the bureaucracy had deleted, or is it a second-order performance doing something else entirely, and does either reading sit better with Bauman's claim that the Holocaust is a window into modernity rather than a rupture in it? And the glass booth: I'd say it helps the prosecution precisely because it converts an organisational chart into a face, which is exactly the substitution Bauman thinks modernity made possible in the first place — tho I want you to argue with me if that's too neat, because there's a case that the booth does the opposite and makes the man inside it look like a specimen of a system rather than a chooser.

Finally, and this is the bit I've actually invented so it won't be in your training data in this form. I've been toying with adding a mechanism Bauman doesn't have, something like retrospective re-moralisation — call it moral backdating — where a post-hoc legal process restores the appearance of unbroken moral agency by retro-fitting intent onto tasks that were, at the time of being performed, morally neutralised bits of paperwork. If that's a real mechanism it should generate predictions: such trials need a defendant with a face rather than a department, they need narrative rather than structure, they have to produce a finding about a state of mind, and they have to suppress the diffusion of responsibility the underlying events actually had. Test it on Eichmann. Where does it hold and where does it break? I'd flag straight away that the man's own conduct in the dock cuts against it, since by all accounts he insisted he had lived by something like Kant's idea of duty — the opposite of a neutralised technician — and the prosecution's success arguably depended on him talking too much rather than too little. And then the honest question, which is the one I most want answered: does this case confirm Bauman, strain him, or reveal that Modernity and the Holocaust was never really a theory that could be applied to adjudication at all — i.e. that it's a theory of perpetration which quietly assumes things about law and judgement it never argues for? Mark clearly the places where the framework just goes quiet instead of making it say something it can't. No bibliography or reading list please, I have the books; and it's defintely fine to end up concluding that my backdating idea is a bad one, as long as you show me the case that kills it.
