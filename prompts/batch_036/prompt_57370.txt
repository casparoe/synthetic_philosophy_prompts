so I just got off a three hour argument with a friend of mine — she does governance work for an NGO, I'm halfway through a political anthropology MA — and we ended up talking past each other so hard that I've decided to write my actual position down in one place and get a second pair of eyes on it, because I can't tell anymore whether I have a view or just a pile of sympathies. The argument started over a case she brought back from fieldwork: a district education officer who is expected to hire his cousin's wife's brother, and where refusing would be read not as integrity but as a public announcement that he'd withdrawn from the kin network entirely, with consequences for everyone downstream of him. She called it a textbook conflict of interest; I said the phrase "conflict of interest" already assumes there are two separable interest-spheres for it to be a conflict between. And then I realised I don't know if that last move is available to me given the rest of what I believe.

Here's the package, as honestly as I can state it. (a) I think the workhorse definition of corruption is basically right — the abuse of a position you've been entrusted with for private benefit — mainly because it's the only one that makes cross-country comparison possible at all. (b) I accept the anthropological objection that the gift/bribe line is not universal: in my own tradition the difference between a gift and a bribe is whether there was already an impersonal rule in place that the transfer bends, and in plenty of places people live inside circuits of obligation where no such prior rule exists to be bent, so calling a whole society corrupt for drawing the line somewhere else is a category error. (c) At the same time I'm not a relativist about consequences — I think patronage networks are genuinely bad for the people at the bottom of them, because they entrench whoever already has something to give, and I want to be able to say that on the practice's own terms rather than by importing my folk concepts. (d) I think laws that criminalise gift-giving to officials do real damage, because in a lot of places the relationship is the only channel a poor person has for getting anything out of the state, and if you outlaw the channel you don't get an impartial bureaucracy, you get the poor shut out while the wealthy buy lawyers. (e) I think there's a universal core regardless of all that: whoever ends up unable to pay, or unable to call in a favour, is harmed, and describing that harm as just-a-different-gift-culture doesn't make it go away. (f) And methodologically I've come around to thinking the unit of analysis should be the relationship over time rather than the transaction — a single envelope means nothing on its own, what matters is whether two people are in an ongoing circuit of reciprocal obligation.

Can you audit this? I want the tensions ordered by severity, not listed as if they're all equally bad, and for each one I want to know what specifically has to give and roughly what I lose by giving it up. Please also tell me which pairs only look like they're in tension and dissolve once you separate the levels they're operating at, because I suspect I've been flagging those in my head and wasting the argument on them. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most damage. I'm deliberately not saying which of these I'm most attached to, on the theory that the one I'd defend hardest is probably the one that's wrong. And lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence — name the commitment that has to go — before you give me the reasoning. No preamble, don't read my list back to me, and I don't want a bibliography or a reading list, I want the diagnosis.
