I write fiction — call it literary but with actual plots in it — and for six years I've been in the same workshop, and honestly its the only place I get feedback that isn't my agent suggesting the book be shorter. Over that time I've watched the group enforce two craft rules like they were laws of nature, and lately I've started to suspect they're smuggling in a whole philosophy of mind that nobody's ever argued for. I'd like you to tell me whether I'm right and what the philosophy is.

Rule one: cut the filters. If your sentence says "she heard the gate slam" or "he noticed the milk had gone off" or "she felt a wave of dread coming on," you get told to delete the perceiving verb and write "the gate slammed." "The milk had gone off." The stated reason is that the reader stops watching a narrator report and starts having the experience directly, unmediated.

Rule two: interior monologue in italics, no quote marks — so you get a paragraph of ordinary third-person narration and then *God, not again, not today* dropped into the middle of it. The italics are treated as transcribing something: a complete sentence that occurred, word for word, at a determinate moment inside the character's head, which the character had privileged and immediate access to.

Third thing, related: the workshop considers "he was angry" a failure and "his knuckles went white around the glass" a success, even though strictly speaking the second one is a description of a hand. Someone once said put it in the body and everybody nodded like it was settled.

So what does this package presuppose about what experience is? Does it need something like the view that there are determinate phenomenal properties, that the subject is directly and reliably acquainted with them, and that the right prose can transfer them intact into another head? Which positions does it quietly rule out — I've read a bit of the illusionists, Frankish and Dennett, who seem to be saying the felt raw character is a kind of user-illusion generated by the brain's self-modeling, and also that introspection stuff suggesting people are bad at reporting their own streams of experience even when they're sure. If that's right, is the workshop teaching a technique for faithfully reproducing an illusion, and does that make the technique wrong or just a different species of right?

Are the two rules even consistent with each other? The italics imply there's a place where the sentence gets displayed and read by someone; the filter-cutting implies there's nobody home watching the display. And does put-it-in-the-body commit us to that James-Lange line where the emotion just is the perception of the bodily change?

I'd like you to separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to fit comfortably — my suspicion is that several mutually hostile theories of mind license the exact same prose rules, which would mean the workshop has no idea what it believes — and then tell me what would have to change in the actual sentences if the workshop converted. Is there a prose style that's the honest one under illusionism? Would it be worse art, or just weirder?
