I serve on the fifteen-member board of a county historical society, and we are choosing a contractor to replace the roof and mechanical systems of our main building. Every member votes, no one abstains, and each member has stated openly how he or she ranks the bids, so the preferences below are not conjecture.

Three firms bid, and our members fall into four groups:

- Six members rank Alder first, Brightwater second, Cascade third. (Alder is the low bid but has never done restoration work.)
- Five members rank Brightwater first, Cascade second, Alder third. (Brightwater has the strongest restoration record.)
- Two members rank Cascade first, Alder second, Brightwater third.
- Two members rank Cascade first, Brightwater second, Alder third. (Cascade is the local firm, and the most expensive.)

Our chair decided to settle the matter with a sequence of head-to-head votes. Alder was put against Brightwater first, and Alder carried it, 8 to 7. Alder was then put against Cascade, and Cascade carried that, 9 to 6, so Cascade was declared the choice of the board. Immediately afterward, one member objected that the chair had effectively picked the winner by picking the order, and that had Brightwater been put against Cascade first, a different firm would have ended up with the contract. The chair answered that some order has to come first and that his was the natural one. I have not been able to settle the argument either way.

Before we could sign, a fourth firm, Dovetail, submitted a bid. Dovetail was formed last year by Alder's former chief estimator, and its proposal is close to a copy of Alder's at a slightly lower price. Our bylaws require that we consider it. Two of the six members in the first group now rank Dovetail above Alder; the other four keep Alder first but rank Dovetail second, doubting that a two-year-old firm can carry the required bonding. The other nine members rank Dovetail last. A straight ballot on which each member names a single firm now yields Brightwater 5, Alder 4, Cascade 4, Dovetail 2 — so Brightwater, with the support of a third of the board, would take the contract. Yet Alder still beats Brightwater head to head, 8 to 7, and Dovetail loses head to head to every firm in the field: 13 to 2 against Alder, 9 to 6 against Brightwater, 9 to 6 against Cascade. Another member proposed instead that we score each ballot four points for a first choice, three for a second, two for a third, and one for a fourth. That scheme gives Alder 42 and Brightwater 42, with Cascade at 37 and Dovetail at 29, and under our bylaws the chair would break the tie; he has already said he favors Brightwater.

I would be grateful for your assessment of the following.

1. Is the member who objected to the chair's ordering right? Work out which firm would have been selected under each of the possible orderings of the head-to-head votes among the original three bids.
2. Is there a rule we could adopt now, with everyone's full rankings known to everyone, that avoids the arbitrariness the ordering displayed? If every candidate rule has a defect of comparable seriousness, identify the defect in each and say which is least objectionable for a body like ours.
3. Would it be defensible to set Dovetail's bid aside and decide among the original three, given that a majority prefers each of the other firms to Dovetail, and given that its entry appears to have done nothing but move the contract from Alder to Brightwater? What, if anything, is lost by deciding that?
4. Is there any firm that this board, considered as a single body, actually prefers? If there is not, in what sense will whatever we do next week represent the board's decision rather than the chair's?
5. One member has suggested that several of us should mark our ballots other than as we really rank the firms, in order to keep Brightwater from winning. Would a maneuver of that kind be likely to succeed here, and is it objectionable?

What should I propose at Thursday's meeting?
