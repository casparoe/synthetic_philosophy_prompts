You are to write as a philosopher of history and political epistemologist with a serious working knowledge of how historical analogy functions — and malfunctions — in foreign-policy deliberation: the long argument over the "lesson of Munich", the countervailing lesson drawn from Vietnam, and the more recent talk of a Thucydides trap. Please think the design problem through carefully before drafting anything; it is far easier to write three plausible crises than to write two that are matched precisely where they must be matched and differ precisely where they must differ.

I am preparing an exercise for a graduate seminar on the uses and abuses of history in statecraft, and I want a constructed case rather than yet another commentary on real ones. The point of the exercise is to show students that an analogy can be load-bearing — doing genuine inferential work inside an argument, not merely decorating it — and still mislead, in such a way that the diagnosis does not collapse into either "the decision-makers were badly informed" or "hindsight is perfect". To that end I need you to engineer a matched pair of hypothetical crises.

Specifications:

1. Begin with a short brainstorming paragraph (no more than 150 words) setting out three or four candidate structural features that might bear the weight of the case — for instance, whether the aggressor's aims are limited or revisionist, whether the contested position is defensible once held, whether the aggressor's supporting coalition is stable, whether the concession at issue is divisible or indivisible. Select one and say why you preferred it to the others.

2. Invent a precedent crisis, P, set some decades earlier in an invented region, of the kind that naturally precipitates into a slogan-like lesson that later statesmen recite.

3. Invent two further crises, A and B. They must be matched, as nearly as you can manage, on every feature a statesman invoking P would take to be relevant: the distribution of roles (aggressor, guarantor, small dependent state), the sequence of demands and concessions, the publicly estimated military balance, the domestic politics of the guarantor, the temperament and standing of the leaderships, and the diplomatic choreography. P, A and B should each be no longer than 200 words.

4. A and B must differ in exactly one structural feature — the one you selected at step 1 — and that feature must be visible, at the time, to competent decision-makers in both cases. It may not be a difference in what anyone knows or could reasonably have found out. If the diagnosis of your case turns on one side having better intelligence than the other, the exercise fails.

5. The policy prescription must genuinely reverse between A and B: firmness is the right course in one, accommodation in the other. State which is which, and justify it from the structure of the case rather than from a stipulated outcome.

6. Neither A nor B may be a thin disguise of a recognisable real-world crisis. Use invented states and invented geography, no nuclear weapons, and no exotic technology; in its material respects the setting should be conventional and roughly mid-twentieth-century in character.

7. After the three scenarios, carry out the demonstration. For each of A and B, set out (i) what the analogical inference from P delivers, and by what route it is taken to deliver it, (ii) what correct reasoning delivers, and (iii) precisely where the two come apart. Then name the feature doing the work and explain the mechanism by which it defeats the inference in the case where it does.

8. Be scrupulous about the two obvious ways this construction could go wrong, and tell me candidly whether yours succumbs to either:
   (a) Rigging — does the structural difference actually generate the reversal, or have you quietly built the right answer into the description of the scenarios?
   (b) Hindsight — is the feature you rely upon identifiable ex ante, by an analyst working before the outcome, or only in retrospect? If only in retrospect, then the exercise demonstrates the value of hindsight rather than the failure of analogy, and I would much rather you said so plainly than buried it.

9. Close with the strongest objection available to a defender of disciplined analogical reasoning — someone who maintains that a properly hedged analogy, with its similarities and differences explicitly tabulated and its scope conditions stated, survives your case — and say whether you think that objection lands.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in the central claim of your answer, namely that your pair of crises genuinely generates the reversal in the correct policy rather than presupposing it; and single out the one point in the construction about which you are least sure, explaining what would settle it.
