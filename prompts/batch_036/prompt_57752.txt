I have followed the health-and-disease debate on and off for the better part of a decade, and I am tired of reading the same two paragraphs rehearsed on both sides. I should like you to draft the document that a serious adversarial collaboration between the two camps ought to have produced years ago, and to do it against a case neither camp has been writing about, so that neither can fall back on stock moves.

The two parties are to be characterised as follows, and I want you to treat these descriptions as authoritative rather than trying to be faithful to any particular author's texts. Do not cite literature, name exegetical authorities, or recommend reading; the document should stand on its own.

**Party N, the naturalist.** Whether a condition is pathological is settled entirely by value-free facts: by whether some internal state contributes less than is statistically typical, for the relevant reference class in the relevant environment, to goals that biology rather than our approval fixes — paradigmatically survival and reproduction. N holds that this value-freedom does real work: it explains how clinicians can agree about what is diseased while disagreeing vehemently about what ought to be treated, and it preserves the possibility that a whole society is simply wrong about which of its members are ill.

**Party V, the normativist.** Disease attribution is ineliminably value-laden. To call a condition a disease is already to say that someone's functioning falls below a standard we have reason to care about, where the standard is set by our practical interests, our institutions, and our moral commitments. V holds that even a theory erected on statistical typicality must smuggle in a choice of which functions count, which reference class to fix, and which environment to hold constant, and that these choices are ours, made for reasons that are not biological.

Neither party is a strawman. N must be permitted the sharpest version of the charge that V collapses disease into mere disvaluation and so cannot say anything to a society that pathologises homosexuality or refuses to pathologise hypertension. V must be permitted the sharpest version of the charge that N cannot explain why anyone bothers with the category at all, and that N's appeal to statistical typicality makes disease status flip with the shape of the population.

Now the case, which I stipulate and which you are to take as given. Call it the Shift. In a population of some four million, a somatic gene therapy has been administered to roughly ninety-seven per cent of newborns for forty consecutive years. The therapy installs a blood variant markedly better at carrying oxygen at low partial pressures; as a side effect it abolishes endogenous synthesis of one micronutrient, so carriers need a cheap daily supplement. Uptake being age-stratified, nearly everyone under forty is a carrier and nearly everyone over seventy is not. In the present environment the supplement is distributed free at schools and workplaces, compliance is near-universal, and carriers and non-carriers are on every measure anyone has taken equally healthy and equally long-lived. Then the sole manufacturer issues a recall lasting eighteen months, and carriers of working age begin presenting with severe deficiency illness while non-carriers do not. Note two further stipulations: before the recall, the non-carrier minority is statistically rare within its own age band; after it, carriers are the ones requiring rescue.

The document must settle, so far as it can, what each party says about this case: whether the carrier state is a disease before the recall and after it; whether the non-carrier state becomes a disease in the under-forties band merely by being rare; and whether the question of what a diagnostic manual ought to list is downstream of the classification question, identical to it, or something else again.

Deliver the following, in this order:

1. A numbered section of claims both parties can sign — about the empirical facts of the Shift, about what each theory is committed to in principle, and about which parts of the dispute you judge to be merely verbal. Flag the verbal parts explicitly, name the word doing the sliding, and do not quietly drop them.
2. A table of the residual disagreements: the question at issue; N's formulation of the disagreement in wording N would accept; V's formulation in wording V would accept; and a note on whether the two formulations genuinely contradict one another or only sound as though they do.
3. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one party. I suspect there are at least two: whether fixing survival and reproduction as the relevant goals is a value-free stipulation or an evaluative one; and whether disease status can be interest-relative while the underlying biological facts remain fully determinate, so that the parties disagree about what our practices should track rather than about the world. Test my guesses rather than assuming them, and propose others if mine are wrong.
4. For each crux, the case, experiment, or argument that both parties would agree bears on it, and each party's prediction recorded in advance in a form that could be scored later. Where a prediction concerns how clinicians or laypeople would classify the Shift, specify the vignette or instrument you have in mind and state the two predictions numerically or at least ordinally, not vaguely.
5. A short closing section: if the collaboration stalls, which hybrid account — requiring both a functional deviation and a harm — would each party retreat to, and would the other regard that retreat as consistent with everything they had already signed?

Begin with the document itself. No preface, no restatement of what I have asked for, no closing commentary about how you went about it, and no summary after the fact.
