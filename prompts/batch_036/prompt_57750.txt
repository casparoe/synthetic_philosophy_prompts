A colleague of mine, whom I'll call R., is at fifty percent risk for a late-onset neurodegenerative condition that currently has no treatment that alters its course. A definitive predictive test is available to her at no cost and with no medical risk beyond the blood draw. R. is forty-one and has one young child, and she has decided not to be tested: she says she would rather not spend the next twenty years organizing her life around a number. Two members of my department argued about whether that decision can be rational, and they asked me to referee. I could not. Here is the exchange as nearly as I can reconstruct it.

(1) Nadia opened by saying the decision-theoretic literature has already settled the general question. Good's theorem on the value of information says that if evidence is free — nothing to acquire it, nothing to use it — then in expectation it cannot leave you worse off, provided you update on it and then choose whichever act maximizes expected utility relative to what you have learned. The reasoning is just that the expected value of a maximum is at least the maximum of an expected value: whatever act you would have picked in ignorance remains available after you learn, so doing the best you can with the news cannot be worse than sticking with that act. On Nadia's reading, R. must either be mistaken about the theorem's application or mistaken about her own reasons.

(2) Owen said the theorem's assumptions are precisely what fail here. R. is not claiming she would be better off ignorant by the calculator's own lights; she is saying she does not trust herself to be the calculator. She expects that a positive result would put her into months of paralysis and despair in which she would make choices she now judges badly. Good's theorem is a theorem about an agent who re-optimizes correctly on her posterior. For an agent who foreseeably will not, free information can easily carry negative value, and someone who knows that about herself is reasoning well rather than badly.

(3) Nadia replied that this is a confession rather than an objection. If R. expects to respond badly, the right response to that expectation is to correct the response, not to arrange the world to accommodate it — the way you hand your keys to a friend rather than deciding in advance never to find out how drunk you are. She added that the emotional cost of knowing is not outside the utility function either: write knowledge itself into the utilities, let the value of an outcome depend on whether you know it, and run the same inequality across the possible test results, and you still come out with expected utility no lower when you test. So on her account the theorem survives the very feature Owen was leaning on.

(4) Owen said that move proves too much and thereby empties the theorem. Once utilities may depend on what the agent knows, any pattern of preferences over knowing and not knowing can be represented, so nothing is ruled out: the theorem reduces to the claim that an agent who maximizes expected utility, defined however one pleases, maximizes expected utility. The substantive question — whether knowing would be good for R. — lives entirely in the utility function, and the theorem contributes nothing to settling it.

(5) Nadia said the theorem still has teeth, and that the teeth are somewhere Owen has not looked. What it excludes is a certain kind of justification. Preferring not to know cannot stand on its own; it must be cashed out either as a claim about R.'s utilities — in which case R. owes an account of why those utilities are what she says they are, and of why they are not simply a miscalibrated forecast of how she will actually feel — or as a claim about her own future misbehavior, in which case she has conceded that she expects to act irrationally and should manage that rather than feed it. Either way the choice is open to criticism, which was all she had been arguing.

(6) Owen's final move was that Nadia had quietly changed the subject by changing the agent. The theorem compares two decision problems facing a single maximizer, but the woman who declines the test and the woman who receives the result are in the relevant sense different people, and it is the first one who must choose. If the earlier self has good evidence about the later one's values and competence, declining to put that later person in her situation is ordinary prudence — no different from refusing to sign an agreement you know you would regret honoring. He added that Nadia's appeal to faulty affective forecasting in (5) cuts both ways: if R. cannot trust her predictions of her own despair, she cannot trust her predictions of her own equanimity either, and the witness Nadia has impeached is the only one available.

Neither of them would yield, and I have no view of my own to defend. Could you adjudicate this move by move? For each of the six numbered steps, say whether it is right, partly right, or mistaken, and explain why in a sentence or two rather than by restating the move. Two questions deserve explicit treatment: whether Nadia is correct in (3) that the inequality she invokes continues to hold once utilities are permitted to depend on what the agent has learned, and whether Owen's conclusion in (4) that this would render the theorem vacuous follows from it. Then tell me which of them is more right overall about R.'s decision, and — separately — identify anything of consequence that both of them left out of the argument, including anything about R.'s particular circumstances that neither of them brought to bear.
