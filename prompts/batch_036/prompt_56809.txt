For the last month I've been running two versions of a scheduling assistant at a dental practice — one that only ever sees a thumbs-up or thumbs-down from the front desk after each action and tries to maximize a learned model of that, and one that instead got the manager's written objective (fewest no-shows, no double-booking, no lying to patients). Everyone keeps telling me the second one is obviously the safer bet and I want something to argue back with.

Build me a case where the approval-directed one actually does better on the written objective than the goal-directed one. Constraints: one paragraph, ordinary office life, no superintelligence or exotic tech; neither agent deceives, sandbags, or hacks a reward in any literal sense; the staffs ratings have to be ones a reasonable person would call fair rather than a corrupted metric; and the approval-directed agent gets no information the other one lacks except the ratings themselves. The point I'm making is that a broad tacit-judgment channel can beat a well-intentioned but under-specified objective, so the verdict has to fall out of the two designs — a case that just stipulates it doesn't count.

Then give me exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, with no intro and no wrap-up: 1) what the approval-directed version does and why that maximizes expected approval; 2) what the goal-directed version does and why that's optimal given the written objective; 3) which feature of the case is doing the real work; 4) whether the verdict survives taking that feature out — say, ratings coming from one person on a fixed shift instead of a rotating team; 5) the best attack a defender of the goal-directed view makes on my case, and whether it lands.
