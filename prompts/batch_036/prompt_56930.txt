For the past three years I have been in a departmental reading group on aggregation and population ethics — six of us, mostly philosophy, plus a postdoc named Ines who works on climate-risk cost-benefit analysis and who keeps an informal ledger, in a spreadsheet, of where each of us lands on the canonical cases. We are drafting a joint paper, and the section that will do the real work is a reconstruction of Ines's position, because she is the only one of us who denies unrestricted aggregation without denying that numbers ever count, and she has never written the view down. I would like your reconstruction before we put a new case to her next week. Here is her column in the ledger, verdict by verdict. One person endures fifty years of the worst torture that can be devised, versus a number of people each getting a momentary speck of dust in the eye, where the number has to be written as a power tower to fit on the page: the torture is worse, and she adds that the number could be anything at all and the verdict would not move. One person loses both legs, versus ten million people each losing a finger: the ten million win, decisively, and she is visibly annoyed when people bring the torture case up here. One person is tortured for a single day, versus ten billion people each suffering an uncomplicated broken wrist that heals cleanly in six weeks: the day of torture is worse; this, she says, is where she loses the room every year. One person endures a year of maximal torture, versus a hundred trillion people each enduring a year of moderate chronic back pain — pain she concedes is a genuine bad and makes each of those lives worse: the year of torture is still worse. One person is tortured for ten minutes, versus a hundred trillion people each tortured for nine minutes: the many win, overwhelmingly, and she says anyone who denies that has misunderstood her. Pressed for a specification of the line — for what has to be true of a harm in order for it to fall on the far side of it — she refuses, saying only that harms differ in kind and not merely in degree, that some harms are the sort of thing that can be summed with others of their kind and some are not, and that demanding a precise boundary is a way of dodging the argument rather than answering it.

The case we are putting to her next week is this. A political prisoner is held for eleven days in a windowless cell. He is not struck, starved, or sleep-deprived, and there is nothing a physician could later document: he is fed, kept warm, and allowed to sleep. He is hooded whenever he is moved, he cannot tell day from night, he is told credibly and repeatedly that he will be executed, and he is never told why he is there. He is released unharmed on the twelfth day and goes on to live a full life, though he describes those eleven days as the worst thing that ever happened to him. Against that: a hundred trillion people each suffer a single cluster headache — pain at the extreme end of what pain gets rated, ninety minutes, no fear, no humiliation, no lasting damage, and no memory of it afterwards beyond the ordinary. She has agreed to answer two questions. First, which side is worse. Second, either to name the pair of adjacent cases, on a stipulated spectrum running by degrees from the eleven days in the cell down to the ninety-minute headache with the number of people held fixed throughout, at which her verdict flips — or to say that there is no such pair, and to tell us what that concession costs her.

What I want from you: a prediction of both of her answers, with a rough confidence for each; an explicit reconstruction of the candidate rules that fit all six ledger entries — at least three, and stated so precisely that we could check each one against every verdict and see exactly which entry, if any, would falsify it; your bet on which rule she is actually running and why you prefer it to the others; the single further case that would discriminate between your top two candidates, together with an account of why none of the six entries already does that work; and, if you think the ledger is not consistent at all, a statement of which verdict she ought to retract and what the revision would cost her elsewhere. Please do not give me a tour of the literature or a list of who has held what — we have all read it, and citations are of no use to us here. What we need is the reconstruction and the bet, in something like nine hundred words, organised under those five headings.
