I'm a third year doing philosophy and CS, and we're partway through a seminar on minds and machines that's spent a few weeks on Dennett. He's the one who coined "sphexishness," right — after the digger wasp that hauls its paralyzed prey to the mouth of the burrow, leaves it there, goes inside to check that everything's ready, and then comes back out and drags the prey in. If you shift the prey a few inches while it's in there doing its check, the wasp comes out, puts the prey back at the entrance, and runs the whole routine again from the top, forever.

Last week I said in seminar that the chatbot we were discussing sometimes looks genuinely non-sphexish to me: it occasionally notices that what it was working toward has stopped making sense and backs out and tries something else. Our professor cut me off with "it's all sphexishness underneath, don't be fooled," and half the class nodded like that settled it. I walked out feeling like one word had done an enormous amount of work that nobody checked.

So I want the menu. What are the distinct claims that could actually be packed into calling something sphexish, or into saying sphexishness is what's "underneath" some behavior? Roughly ordered from the trivial to the loaded, and for each one: what does it rule out, and is it a platitude nobody disputes, a substantive claim that might be true, something flatly false of anything that learns, or just too vague to test? My sense is there's at least a version that's merely "it's implemented by a physical process," which a thermostat and a rock both satisfy; a version about not re-checking whether the conditions your routine assumes still hold; a version about having a procedure versus having the capacity to revise the procedure; and maybe a version that's really about where you'd expect breakdowns to show up if you perturbed the system. But I want them stated sharply, not gestured at.

Two things I want you to weigh in on rather than just catalogue. First, my own tentative gloss, which I can't get to work: sphexish behavior is behavior that's keyed to the trigger conditions of a routine rather than to the reason the routine exists. That feels like it captures the wasp, but it also seems to make a Roomba non-sphexish, since bumping-into-a-chair-leg is a perfectly good reason to turn around — and it makes me sphexish too on the drive home when I pull into my driveway having thought about nothing. Is that gloss a reading the slogan could bear, a rival to it, or just confused? Second, where should the line get drawn in cases that pull opposite ways: the chatbot that happily abandons a doomed plan in one conversation but, when you paste in a fresh error message, apologizes and re-emits the exact same broken plan it just got told failed. Perturbation-sensitive in one place, stone sphexish in another — does that make the property come in degrees, be relativized to situations, or not be a real property at all?

Then be committal about the two questions I actually care about. Which reading, if any, was my professor entitled to, given all he said was that it's underneath — and if none of them licenses dismissing my point, say so plainly. And pick a side on this one: is "not sphexish" even the right thing to be arguing about, or is the real question whether the breakdowns happen in the situations we care about rather than in the situations we deliberately build to expose them? If you think the concept earns no keep beyond some plainer description of a failure mode, say that and say what to use in its place. I'd much rather get a definite answer with reasons than a tour of the options and a "but it depends."
