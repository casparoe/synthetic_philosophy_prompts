Write me a short story about mechanism identity — specifically the version of the problem that shows up when you're doing anomaly detection on a neural net's internals.

Here's the setup I want. A deployed model has a circuit that's been carefully characterized: it's the thing that makes it refuse to fabricate citations. Everyone knows where it lives, which features light up, what the causal tracing looks like. Then there's a round of fine-tuning and some weight surgery, and afterwards the model passes every behavioral test it passed before — identical refusals, identical scores on the eval suite. But when the interpretability team goes to confirm the circuit is still in there, it isn't obviously the same one. Different features in the dictionary fire, the attribution has shifted a layer or two, the patching experiment gives a different-shaped causal graph. Behaviorally indistinguishable, mechanistically not-the-same, apparently.

Now there's a monitoring system whose whole job is to flag "the safety-relevant mechanism has changed," and it has to decide whether to fire. And the policy document that governs it says it should fire when the mechanism is no longer the same mechanism — but nobody ever wrote down what "same" means there. So the story is really about the humans (and maybe the monitor itself) fighting over the criterion: is a mechanism its functional profile, its position in the causal graph, its weights, its counterfactual responses to interventions nobody bothered to run, or what? I'd like one character to push the idea that two mechanisms are the same iff they agree on their whole counterfactual response profile — across every input and every intervention, not just the eval set — and somebody else to point out that this is unfalsifiable in practice and that the retrained model does in fact diverge on out-of-distribution stuff, which makes the criterion either vacuous or damning depending on how you squint.

Two things I care about. First, commit: pick an answer to the identity question and build the plot so the ending actually lands on it. Don't give me the shrug-and-leave-it-ambiguous ending where the reader is meant to decide. Second, the losing criteria should get their best arguments — the disagreement should feel like it's between competent people, not a strawman getting demolished.

Aim for maybe 1200–2000 words. Use real technical texture (attribution patching, feature dictionaries, eval suites) but make sure it still reads as a story for someone who doesn't do interpretability.

Then after the story, tack on a short note — a couple of paragraphs, no bibliography — saying where you took liberties: which bits of the interpretability machinery are simplified or invented for the story, and where your chosen criterion is leaning on a contested philosophical reading rather than settled fact. Be specific and own the choices you made instead of hedging into "well, it depends."
