I have spent eleven years as a backend engineer, most of them leading a team whose entire job is porting a large legacy service onto a new platform, and I have no formal training in philosophy or literary theory. Last month my book group took up Douglas Hofstadter's *Le Ton beau de Marot: In Praise of the Music of Language* (1997), the book built around dozens of competing English versions of Clément Marot's short poem to a sick young lady — the one Hofstadter refers to as "Ma mignonne" — with its very short lines, strict syllable counts, and relentless rhyme. Halfway through the discussion I made a claim that has not let go of me since: that translating a fixed-form poem is structurally the same activity my team performs. You cannot carry the implementation across; the target environment imposes hard, non-negotiable constraints — in my case an API surface, a latency budget, a memory ceiling, in the translator's case English phonology, the scarcity of usable rhymes, and line length; so you redesign from the ground up and call the result faithful if observable behavior is preserved. I then pushed it further, and this is the part I most want pressure-tested: if the formal constraints are mechanically checkable — a syllable counter and a rhyme validator are, in effect, a linter and a test suite — and semantic fidelity is the part modern language models have become decent at, then fixed-form translation starts to look like a tractable engineering problem, solvable by a generate-many-candidates-then-filter-and-rank loop. At work our acceptance criterion is concrete: replay a week of production traffic against both systems and require matching responses. I said I saw no reason in principle why the same shape of procedure should not apply here.

One member of the group, who translates literary prose for a living, told me my analogy was not merely imprecise but load-bearing in exactly the wrong direction. Her objection, which she did not have time to develop and which I could not answer, was that the analogy smuggles in two things a port has and a poem does not: a specification that can be written down independently of the implementation, and an oracle that tells you when behavior has been preserved. I suspect she is onto something, but I also suspect my position can be rebuilt rather than abandoned, and I would rather find out which.

So please audit the analogy instead of just telling me it is imperfect. Specifically:

- Give me the correspondence table explicitly. What plays the role of the source codebase, the specification, the observable behavior, the target platform's constraints, the test suite, the refactor, the regression, the end user, and the acceptance criterion? Mark every row where there is no honest counterpart, or where the counterpart exists but is doing something different from what it does in software.
- Lay out where the mapping breaks, and sort the disanalogies into those that genuinely threaten the automation conclusion and those that are cosmetic — the kind of objection a translator might make for rhetorical effect that I would be entitled to wave off.
- Say what the source domain quietly imports that the target lacks. I have in mind candidates like the idea that form is a wrapper around a separable payload, that a poem's constraints are incidental to its purpose rather than constitutive of it, that "same behavior" is an equivalence relation at all, and that checking necessary conditions amounts to verifying sufficiency. Tell me which of these are real problems and which are not.
- Then give a verdict on the conclusion: does the generate-and-filter picture survive, and if only in part, for which part of the territory? I would like a sense of where along the spectrum from technical documentation through free verse to strict fixed form the porting analogy holds up and where it snaps, and why those boundaries fall where they do.
- Finally, tell me whether a better analogy exists. If some other engineering comparison does the work more honestly — a reimplementation with no spec, a lossy transcode, a cover version of a song, an optimizer whose objective function cannot be measured, a compiler with no correctness proof — say which one and what it licenses that porting does not. And state the strongest defensible version of my original claim, with the parts that cannot be defended removed.

I am not looking for references or a reading list; I want the reasoning, and I would rather be told bluntly that the analogy was carrying the argument the whole time than be reassured.
