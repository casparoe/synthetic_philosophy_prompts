I host a small podcast about the practical side of AI safety. Next week's guest is a researcher who thinks the field has its priorities backwards: rather than building systems that want the right things, he argues, we should build systems that cannot act on wanting the wrong things. His proposal is to treat a deployed model the way a security engineer treats a component assumed to be hostile — supervised by a separate, weaker overseer model, filtered at the output, kept away from the tools it would need in order to do damage, and switched off by tripwires that fire on detected intent rather than on harm already done. He insists this is no second-best fallback, because unlike alignment work it requires nobody to have settled what the right things are.

Write eight to ten interview questions, ordered so that the first few pin the view down and the last few press it, each with a follow-up aimed at the answer he is most likely to give. I do not want questions he could discharge with a generality. Please include at least one on each of the following:

- the regress: his overseer is a trained model too, so what makes it reliable in a way the one it watches is not, and whether securing the overseer simply hands him back the problem he claims to have bypassed;
- the tripwires are built, tuned, and can be disabled by the same company that is under pressure to ship the product: what his scheme demands of that company, and whether the demand is any lighter than the one he says he avoids;
- detection of intent assumes the model does not know it is being watched: what survives of the architecture if it does know, and if it is trying to pass the very tests meant to catch it;
- control limits what a system can do but not what it can persuade people to want: whether that is a gap in the view or a reason to abandon the ambition.

Close with a question asking what would count for him as evidence that control cannot work in principle, not merely that one protocol failed. He deflects criticism with some version of "nothing is airtight; the question is whether it beats the alternative," so make at least one question that leaves him nowhere to put that sentence.
