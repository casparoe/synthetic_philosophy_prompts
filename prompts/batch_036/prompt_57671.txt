I am drafting a chapter on causal explanation in history, working from a single case, and I have produced an argument whose conclusion I cannot defend. I want a diagnosis of the argument itself, not a survey of positions.

The case, stripped of names: a besieged garrison capitulates in the sixth week of a siege. Four factors are documented, and historians argue separately over each: the food stores were exhausted in the fifth week; a relief column was scattered by a storm and never arrived; the commander died of fever in the fourth week and his successor was more cautious; the defenders had by then learned that no terms would be granted. From this I abstracted a general claim about causal density and gave myself a rough measure of it. For an event E, let d(E) be the number of distinct minimally sufficient sets of factors that a competent reconstruction of the sources can support. In my case d is at least four; the four sets overlap one another, but none contains any other. In a controlled physical experiment d is one, or close to it.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) To explain E by citing C, C must have made a difference to E: had C been absent, E would not have occurred, or would not have occurred as it did.
(2) Where d(E) > 1 and the sufficient sets are independently present, then for any candidate C drawn from any one of those sets there is some other set that would have produced E in C's absence.
(3) Therefore in any such case no candidate factor made a difference to E.
(4) Therefore historical explanations that cite causes do not explain; at best they record sequence, and the choice among competing factors is fixed by the narrator's interests rather than by how things were.
(5) Nothing in (1) through (4) is specific to history. The reasoning applies verbatim to a patient dying under multi-organ failure, to a bridge with several independently sufficient design faults, and, arguably, to any event in a world as densely antecedent as ours.
(6) Either those are not explanations either, or (1) is false.
(7) I will not say that they are not explanations, so (1) is false, and difference-making has nothing to do with causation.

(7) is not something I can live with. If difference-making drops out entirely I no longer know what I mean by cause, and the whole chapter is about how historians choose among causes. But I also cannot see how to deny (2), because density is precisely what my source work shows, and denying it looks like refusing to admit that my case is a case.

The obvious repair I attempted was to read (1) as difference relative to a contrast, so that the storm made a difference to capitulation in the sixth week rather than in the ninth. The objection I got was that this quietly changes the explanandum, since what historians dispute is the capitulation and not its date. I do not know whether that objection is any good.

So: which step do you reject? Take (1), (2), and (4) in turn, and say what giving up each of them costs, and whether there is a repair that keeps difference-making in some form without licensing (5). Tell me also whether my measure d(E) is doing real work in the argument or is decoration that makes it look quantitative. And if you think (2) is the step that has to go, I want an account of why it looked unassailable from inside the case, where every factor genuinely does seem replaceable.

Two further requests. Say how confident you are in the diagnosis you offer, and single out the one point in it that you are least sure of. And write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter or a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italic emphasis, no tables, and no numbered lists in your reply even though my argument is numbered.
