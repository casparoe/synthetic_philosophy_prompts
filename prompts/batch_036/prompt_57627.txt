I am presenting a paper at a two-day workshop on the political economy of famine hosted by an economic history department, and what I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense of the paper. My thesis is that the familiar question — is a famine a natural disaster or a political crime? — is not a question about moral tone but about the explanatory target, and that it can be settled by two tests rather than one. The first is avertability: was there a set of measures available to the ruling authority, at costs it was realistically able to bear, that would have held excess mortality near baseline? The second is epistemic: did the authority know, or culpably fail to know, that people were dying and that those measures were available? A famine is a disaster when the first test fails; it is a crime when both are satisfied and the authority nonetheless declined to act; and it falls into a third category, which I call culpable opacity, when the authority's ignorance was itself produced by its own choices — censored reporting, purged internal critics, production statistics supplied by the very officials being evaluated against them. My cases are Bengal in 1943, Ireland between 1845 and 1849, and China between 1959 and 1961, and I argue that all three belong in the third category rather than the second, which is a claim I expect to have to defend hard: the wartime denial policies over rice and boats and the absence of any formal declaration of famine in Bengal, the local eligibility conditions attached to relief under the amended poor law in Ireland, and in China the way the 1959 purge of the leadership's internal critics destroyed the reporting channel on which procurement quotas were subsequently set. On Sen I take a line that is not original but draw a conclusion he does not: the entitlement analysis explains convincingly who starved and by what mechanism, yet it says nothing about why nothing stopped it, and his much-cited claim that famines do not occur in functioning democracies with a free press is really a claim about information channels — which makes it an institutional, partial version of my third category rather than a rival to it.

The audience will be about thirty people, almost all quantitative economic historians and development economists who work with identification strategies and who are, as a group, allergic to moral vocabulary inside causal explanation; they read Sen as an economist and are impatient with him as a moralist. Also in the room: one legal scholar working on international criminal law, who cares about mens rea and about whether "crime" can be predicated of anything other than persons; one economic historian of the Irish famine of the revisionist persuasion, who considers the genocide framing polemical and thinks Victorian fiscal ideology and the hard limits of state capacity do more explanatory work than individual culpability; and two specialists in Chinese demographic history who know far more than I do about the competing estimates of excess deaths and about how much of the 1959–61 output collapse the econometric decompositions assign to weather. They will also press on the fact that the Chinese state's own initial designation of the episode attributed it to natural disaster, and ask what that does to my epistemic test if the authorities may have partly believed their own framing.

What I need, then: the eight or so objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to the paper, and phrased the way the person most likely to raise it would phrase it — the identification question from the economists, the agency and mens rea question from the lawyer, the state-capacity question from the Irish historian — not the way a moral philosopher or an advocacy audience would put them. For each, a reply I could deliver in about two minutes, plus an honest note on where that reply is soft, especially wherever it depends on a counterfactual I cannot estimate or on treating a bureaucracy as an agent. Then tell me what to concede in the first five minutes of the talk so as to disarm the two most predictable objections before anyone asks them, and identify the single reply that has to land if the paper is to survive the session. Finally, give me the question I am hoping nobody asks — the one that would expose the weakest joint in the argument — and say briefly why it is dangerous and what my best available answer is, however unsatisfying.
