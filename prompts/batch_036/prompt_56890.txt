For several years now I have read history as an amateur, mostly the kind of book that promises to explain a century by way of a harvest, a microbe, or a mountain, and I have reached the point where I can no longer tell when such a book is actually saying something and when it has merely changed the subject in a confident tone. Before I pick up another one, I would like to know what I should be demanding of the account. So: write out the requirements that any attempt to treat non-human things — pathogens, climate, volcanoes, soil, livestock, ocean currents — as causes in history rather than as scenery has to satisfy in order to count as adequate. I am not looking for a survey of who has written what, and I do not want a reading list; I want the standard itself, the thing I could hold a book up against.

For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that motivates it, and then mark it plainly: is it non-negotiable for any account that deserves the name, or does it turn out to be the local taste of one school of historians dressed up as a criterion? I suspect a fair number of the demands people make in this area are of the second kind, and identifying those is worth as much to me as listing the first.

To give you something to work with, here are the puzzles I have not been able to resolve on my own. I would like the list to speak to each of them, whether by turning it into a requirement, by dissolving it, or by telling me it rests on a confusion:

- What I have been calling the difference between backdrop and co-author. A dry decade that raises the odds of unrest, and a dry decade that determines what the unrest is about, seem to be different claims, and I cannot say what would settle which one a book is making.
- The alibi problem. A famine that turned out to be less about how much food existed than about who could get at it makes me wary of any account in which the weather arrives just in time to relieve the people in charge of responsibility. Is there a requirement that an environmental explanation must not function as an excuse, and if so, how could that ever be more than my own politics?
- The invisible-cause problem. Suppose a region grew drier and its farmers quietly switched crops, so that nothing at all shows up in the record. The cause was real and the effect was nil. An account that only counts visible effects will systematically under-rate non-human causes; an account that counts absorbed ones seems unable to be checked against anything.
- The double-telling test. If the same event can be narrated twice, once with institutions as the protagonists and once with the climate, and both versions read as true and neither contradicts the other, I am not sure either has explained anything. What should a theory owe me here?
- Proportion. It is easy to move from "the eruption made things worse" to "the eruption made this happen," and I cannot find the seam.

Then, and this is the part I most want, tell me whether some subset of these requirements cannot all be satisfied at once. My own hunch is that insisting the account preserve human choice and responsibility, insisting that a non-human factor sometimes be the thing that makes the difference, and insisting that the account never launder the decisions of the people in power form a triangle that cannot be closed — but I have no way of knowing whether that hunch survives contact with a careful statement of each demand. If the list is itself a result in this way, say so and show the conflict.

Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling candidates out, and give me a short verdict on whether the word actor is earning its place in this literature at all, or whether it is decoration laid over an ordinary causal claim that could be stated without it. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; if you mention how the familiar kinds of account fare against the list, a paragraph will do, and I will draw my own conclusions from it.
