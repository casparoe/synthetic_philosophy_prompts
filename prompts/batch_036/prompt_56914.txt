Ok so, third-year archaeology PhD here, and the second chapter of my dissertation is basically about the word 'collapse' — when are we entitled to say a society collapsed rather than changed. My supervisor trained as a philosopher of science before she moved over, and she's made me do the exercise where you write down your gut verdicts on cases and see whether there's a principle underneath them. It is not going well. I want you to be the philosophy half of this: act as someone who knows the collapse literature (Tainter's complexity account, Diamond, the whole resilience/'reorganization' reaction against him) but who actually does conceptual analysis for a living and can tell me what my verdicts commit me to. Please no bibliography, no reading list, don't cite anything — I have the literature, I want the reasoning. Think carefully, and do some open brainstorming before you commit to a view.

Here are my verdicts, as honestly as I can state them:

1. The Hittite state around 1200 BCE: collapse. Gone, capital abandoned, the scribal tradition dies with it.
2. Egypt hit by the same drought and the same Sea Peoples at the same time, sliding into the Third Intermediate Period: not a collapse. Fragmented, weaker, but kingship, temples, script and cities all continue. Same shock, same date, opposite verdict from me.
3. Ugarit: collapse. Burned, never rebuilt, and its alphabetic cuneiform goes extinct with the city.
4. Mycenaean palatial Greece: collapse. Palaces gone, Linear B gone, big population drop, the world that built the tholos tombs never comes back.
5. Indus de-urbanization: NOT a collapse. Cities empty out, the script disappears and never gets deciphered because nothing continues it, people drift east toward the Ganges — but no mass death, no invasion, and I want to call that a reorganization. This is the pair that keeps me up at night: on paper 4 and 5 look like the same event to me. Lost script, emptied cities, elites gone, villagers left standing. I can't say what I'm tracking in one and not the other.
6. Classic Maya southern lowlands: collapse. Monument carving stops, the k'uhul ajaw institution goes, cities depopulate — even though Maya people and Maya languages are obviously still around and the northern Yucatan is flourishing at the time.
7. Angkor: not a collapse. The hydraulic city stops working and the capital is abandoned, but the polity carries on downstream and the people and the religion are continuous.
8. Rapa Nui before Europeans: not a collapse. The palm forest goes (a lot of it the rats), moai carving stops, but the population looks broadly steady to me and they adapt with rock gardens. Rapa Nui after contact: collapse. A couple thousand people down to about a hundred inside a century and a half, and the rongorongo tradition goes with the people who could read it.
9. Cahokia: collapse. Moundbuilding stops, the American Bottom empties out.
10. Chaco and Mesa Verde: not collapse, diaspora. Great houses abandoned, but people move to the Rio Grande and the Hopi mesas and their descendants are identifiable today.
11. Norse Greenland: collapse — the settlements are simply gone, nothing continues. Thule Inuit on the same island through the same cold snap: not a collapse, they dropped some practices and kept going.
12. The Western Roman Empire: collapse. But I refuse to say 'Rome collapsed', full stop, because the East goes on for another thousand years. So apparently my verdicts are unit-relative, and I don't know if that's principled or just me picking whichever unit makes the story I want to tell.
13. As a control, not archaeology: the USSR dissolving. I want to say that's the collapse of a state but not of a society, and I genuinely cannot say what's making me say that.

My tentative principle, which I know is a mess: a society collapses when it loses a big chunk of its institutional complexity and doesn't get it back within a couple of generations; the loss has to be in the polity, not just in people's standard of living; and 'didn't get it back' shouldn't count if the same people just moved down the road and rebuilt it somewhere else.

What I want out of you:

(a) First brainstorm. Give me four to six candidate principles or dimensions — demography, loss of complexity, loss of stored information, persistence of the unit, speed, reversibility, whether actual persons were harmed, whatever else you think is lurking in my list — and for each say which of my thirteen verdicts it gets right and which it wrecks. Be specific about the wrecks.

(b) Then commit. Give me the principle (or at most two competing ones) you think I'm converging on, precise enough that I could apply it to a site I've never heard of without phoning you. Break it into clauses and say which case motivated each clause — I want to see the load-bearing structure, not a slogan. Also tell me explicitly how it handles the unit problem in 12, since that's where I think the whole thing could fall apart.

(c) Run it on cases I didn't list, and say how confident you are: the Ur III / southern Mesopotamian end of the third millennium, Great Zimbabwe, the fall of the Western Zhou, and then two non-archaeological ones — Detroit, and the USSR again, this time under your principle rather than mine.

(d) The honest part. Which of my verdicts does your principle fail to save? Pick the one verdict you think I'm flat wrong about and argue against me properly. And please don't dissolve the problem by saying collapse is a cluster concept and everyone wins — if you think it's a cluster concept, tell me exactly which cluster members do the work in which of my cases and what happens when two of them conflict, which I assume is 4 versus 5.

(e) One meta question at the end: does my list look like it's tracking a real property societies have, or a narrative convention about which stories we're allowed to tell? If some of it is convention, point at the specific clauses that are doing the conventional work.
