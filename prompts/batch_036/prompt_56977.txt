I co-teach a historiography seminar — philosophy grads and history grads mixed — and one of my students turned in a paper arguing that the Rankean ideal isn't just unattainable but incoherent. Her argument is good. Too good, and I cant find the joint. Here's the spine of it, compressed:

1. To write history at all is to impose a narrative order on events: a beginning, a middle, an ending, saliences, causal links.
2. No set of events, considered just as events, determines which narrative order to impose. The same material admits a tragic, a comic and a satirical telling, and nothing in the material itself ranks them.
3. So the narrative order comes from the historian — her interests, her politics, her genre habits, her audience.
4. Where a representation's form comes from the representer rather than from the thing represented, the representation isn't answerable to how things were. It's a construction.
5. A construction is answerable only to internal standards — coherence, elegance, persuasiveness — not to truth about its subject.
6. Therefore historical writing is not answerable to the truth about the past, and the aim of showing things as they actually were isn't a regulative ideal but a category mistake: there's nothing for a narrative to correspond to.
7. Objection: historians get things wrong and get corrected — a date, a casualty figure, an attribution of motive. Reply: every correction is itself a narrative move made from inside some emplotment, so appealing to correction just relocates the construction one level up.
8. Hence the honest options are to stop writing history or to admit it's a branch of literature.

She won't accept (8) — nobody sane will — so at one point she concedes that "answerable to" is doing more work than she can cash out. But she never says which step she'd drop, and neither can I. She also thinks she's got Carr behind her, which I doubt: Carr wanted historians to be less naive about their own standpoint, not to stop believing there was a past.

Two things I want from you.

First, the diagnosis. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Be specific about the joint — why it looked seamless when I read it the first two times — and price at least two alternatives: what you give up if you keep (2) but deny (3), versus what you give up if you deny (2) outright. There's also a generalization worry I can't shake, which is that (4) as stated should sweep in a lot more than history, and if it does then either it's false or a great deal of ordinary factual discourse goes with it. Tell me whether that's a reductio of (4) or an unwelcome truth I have to live with. And for what it's worth I'd rather lose (6) than (2): I don't want to stand up in front of my students and claim the events pick the plot, beacuse I don't believe it. But tell me what I'm paying.

Second, when you write this back, mark clearly which parts of the terrain are settled among people who actually work on it and which are live fights, and don't hand me a contested position wearing a consensus costume. I want to give her something honest about where the field stands rather than a textbook summary of it.
