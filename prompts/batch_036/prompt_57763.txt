I'm putting together a six-week reading group on Hofstadter-style accounts of the self — the idea that the 'I' is just a symbol in a system that loops back around and reads itself, and that this strange loop is all there is to someone being home. I'd rather get the spec nailed down before we argue about it: what does a theory of the self *as* a self-referential symbol have to deliver before I'd call it adequate? Not a plot summary of the book — an actual requirements list, with the case or consideration behind each item.

The wrinkle I care about most, and I want it built in as a requirement rather than handled as an aside: in normal representation talk you can ask whether a model is accurate, map matching territory. But if the self-model partly constitutes the self it models — what the system takes itself to be feeds back and changes what it is — then there's no independent territory to check the map against. So a serious theory has to say what a correct versus incorrect self-model even means in that situation, or concede the distinction collapses. Tell me which way you'd go and what that requirement costs you.

Then the rest of the list. Phenomena it has to explain: the felt unity of the first-person view, why that unity keeps feeling real even after you're told it's an illusion, the sense of persisting through total turnover of parts, and the fact that introspection is somehow both privileged and wildly unreliable — split-brain patients confidently narrating reasons for actions they never chose, the left-hemisphere interpreter business, confabulation generally. Distinctions it can't collapse: self-model versus self, indexical versus description, there-is-no-self versus the-self-is-an-illusion-of-something-real. And the formal limits: if the theory wants to lean on anything Gödelian or Tarskian about a system representing its own states, it needs to say whether a self-model can be both complete and used from the inside, because I suspect it can't. Flag any subset of these requirements you think is jointly unsatisfiable — that's the part I most want to see, since then the list itself is the result.

Also mark which items are non-negotiable and wich are just the preferences of a camp. My guess is that "there must be a self at the end of the analysis" is a prejudice of the non-eliminativists and belongs in the second pile, so argue with me if you think that's wrong.

Close with the single requirement that knocks out the most candidate theories, and then a couple of sentences each on how a narrative/self-interpretation view and a Buddhist-flavored eliminativist view do against the list. A table is fine for that last bit.
