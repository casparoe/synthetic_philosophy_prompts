I want a spec before I read another defense of karma. Set the purpose: the theory has to work as a genuine account of moral desert — the kind of thing you could actually run a ethics of praise, blame and punishment on — not as an edifying story a tradition tells itself. So: lay out everything such an account has to deliver. The phenomena it has to explain, the cases it has to get the right verdict on, the distinctions it can't collapse, the constraints it can't violate.

For each item, give me the case that motivates it, and mark whether it's non-negotiable or really just a taste of one camp. Then the part I care about most: tell me which subset of the list can't all be satisfied at once. My hunch is that (a) not blaming the victim for what they're suffering, (b) actually explaining why the undeserved stuff lands where it does, and (c) leaving the agent able to keep track of where they stand, are in tension — sort that out, and tell me which single requirement knocks out the most otherwise attractive theories.

Two things to build in explicitly: whether there's any room for amnesty in the picture at all — forgiveness, expiation, wiping the slate — or whether a theory that makes room for those has quietly stopped being a theory of desert; and whether karma is being run as a law-of-nature affair or as a cosmic bookkeeping one with recorders and judges, since I think those give different verdicts on the Gyges-style case of the man who does awful things in perfect secrecy and dies prosperous and unrepentant.

Close with a short scorecard, three lines max, for the hard cross-lifetime retributive view, the secularized character-formation reading, and whatever hybrid tries to have both. No citations, I'll track the literature down myself.
