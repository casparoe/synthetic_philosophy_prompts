Got into it with a friend till like 2am about how Japan screens school history textbooks and I still haven't settled the part that was actually bugging me. The setup as I understand it: the books are written by private publishers, not the ministry; MEXT's authorization council reads the drafts against the national curriculum guidelines and can send them back for revision when something isn't objective or impartial or has errors in it; the cycle runs every four years and local boards of education then pick from the authorized list. One historian litigated the whole thing for three decades arguing it amounted to censorship. And one of the screening criteria, added in the early 80s after an international row, asks whether the book shows consideration for the feelings of neighbouring Asian countries when it deals with modern history. (Korea has flirted with going the other way entirely, one state-written book for everybody, then dropped it.)

So what theory of historical knowledge does this practice presuppose? My friend says it presupposes that contested claims — the scale of the Nanjing killings, say — have a determinate answer that a panel is entitled to certify, and that's the scandal. I said no, the ministry isn't pronouncing on what happened, it's deciding what's suitable for a classroom, and suitability isn't a truth question. But I couldn't then explain why a criterion about other countries' feelings sits in the same list of reasons as a criterion about factual error, when one of those is about whether the sentence is true and the other isn't about truth at all.

Reconstruct what the practice actually commits you to, and seperate out what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with. Specifically: can the whole thing be cashed out in purely pedagogical and diplomatic terms without smuggling in a claim about who gets to say what the past was, and if not, at which exact step does the epistemic claim get in? Then press the inconsistency — the same curriculum guidelines want students doing historical thinking, working from sources and forming their own judgement, which looks like the opposite of certification. Can both live in one system or does one of them have to be a polite fiction? And finally, what would have to change about the screening if you swapped in a view where a contested claim is settled by evidence available to anyone rather than by an authority's say-so — is there any version of the current practice that survives that swap?
