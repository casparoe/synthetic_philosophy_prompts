I'm hanging next week's session on Hegel's Lordship and Bondage off Kazuo Ishiguro's Remains of the Day instead of the usual examples, and I want a real reading, not a gesture at one. Stevens and Lord Darlington look like a master/bondsman pair, but I'm not convinced the dialectic ever gets started. So: is there any life-and-death struggle in the book at all, or does Stevens foreclose it in advance by defining his dignity as the thing a professional never lets be shaken in front of anyone? Does Darlington even count as a Hegelian master, given that he seems to have no need whatever of Stevens's recognition — my memory is that Fanon presses something like this against Hegel, that in the colonial situation the master wants work rather than recogntion, so the whole mechanism stalls, and I'd like you to correct me if that's not what he actually argues. Then: do Stevens's decades of labour get him the independence-in-the-thing-he-formed that Hegel promises the bondsman, or is his dignity simply the master's standpoint installed in his own head and mistaken for a self? Treat the Miss Kenton thread seriously too — she offers him recognition and he refuses it, more than once — and read the pier ending against Hegel: is learning to banter with Mr. Farraday a genuine moment of recognition between equals, a surrender, or something Hegel's scheme has no slot for? Finally, tell me where all this is unfair to the book. Ishiguro is writing about complicity and self-deception as much as about servitude, and if Hegel turns out to be a Procrustean bed here I'd much rather know before I stand up in front of them. Close with what the novel shows about being a servant that the argument in the Phenomonology can't.
