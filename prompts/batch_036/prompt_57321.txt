I have been reading the rationalist blogs and their comment sections for something like fifteen years, which means I came at inference to the best explanation from the Bayesian end and never quite left it. The house position, as I absorbed it, is a disjunction: either explanatory reasoning is redundant with ordinary conditioning plus sensible priors, dressed up in nicer language, or it is an unreliable leap. The standard exhibit for the second horn is van Fraassen's bad-lot objection — that picking the best of the hypotheses you happened to think of licenses nothing, since you have no grounds for supposing the true one is even in your sample. The realist camp answers that this makes the track record of science a miracle, that the objection proves too much because it would equally discredit the empirical reasoning the objection itself runs on, and that the best explanation of the reliability of explanatory reasoning is that it tracks something real. Lipton's contribution, as I understand it, is to drive a wedge between how lovely an explanation is and how likely it makes the evidence, and to insist that loveliness is what guides likeliness in actual practice.

The exchange has now gone around the same loop for four decades with the same four moves, and nobody appears to have budged. I do not want a survey of positions and I do not want you to adjudicate whether IBE is legitimate. I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement.

Here is my working hypothesis, which you should feel free to demolish rather than flatter. First, I suspect the two camps are not arguing about the same slot in the inference machinery. For want of better labels, explanatory considerations can be located in at least three distinct slots: the prior slot, where explanatory coherence shapes the anteced probability you assign to a hypothesis before the data arrive; the likelihood slot, where a hypothesis's explanatory fit with the evidence is what raises the probability of the evidence given that hypothesis; and the acceptance-rule slot, where explanatoriness licenses a categorical belief that outruns the probabilities entirely. My guess is that the empiricists are attacking the third slot while the realists are defending the first two, and that neither side announces which slot it occupies, which is why nothing ever resolves.

Second half of the hypothesis: the bad-lot objection is a claim about the hypothesis generator, whereas the defenses are claims about the verifier, and generator quality and verifier quality are separate parameters that co-vary in some domains and not in others. Sampling-based search, trained predictors, and evolutionary search all involve generators whose output distributions are demonstrably informative about truth, cheaply checkable afterward, while a generator drawing uniformly from a space of conceivables is not. If that is right, then buried under the philosophical standoff is an empirical question nobody has operationalized: whether the process by which scientists generate candidate hypotheses over-represents truth, and whether the reliability of such a generator can be estimated without already granting some of its outputs.

Tell me whether this diagnosis holds, and if not, what better one looks like. Consider at least these alternatives before settling: that the dispute is partly verbal, turning on what counts as a distinct rule rather than a description of one; that the camps are answering different questions under a shared heading — one a normative question about what warrants belief, the other a methodological question about how inquiry is actually conducted; that a hidden formal issue is doing the work, such as whether a non-conditionalizing update rule can be coherent over time without exposing the agent to exploitation; or that the question is simply ill-posed, because the best explanation is only best relative to a comparison class that the theory never fixes. If you think my three-slot taxonomy is a false trichotomy, say so and replace it.

Format, please:

2. What each camp is actually committed to (400–500 words). The load-bearing commitments of each side, plus the two or three moves each has made in response to the other's best argument. Argue for your characterizations rather than listing them.
3. Locating the fault line (500–600 words). Test my hypothesis against the arguments from section 2. Which slot or slots is each side really contesting, and does the generator-versus-verifier split explain the non-engagement, or does it just relabel the problem?
4. Verbal, substantive, empirical, or ill-posed (350–450 words). Pick one as primary, defend it, and say what work the others are doing if any.
5. What would count as progress (300–400 words). Be specific: a formal result, a distinction, a change of question, an experiment, or a case study that would discriminate. If your conclusion is that the stalemate is stable and should be expected to persist, argue for that instead and say what a rational inquirer should do with the question.
