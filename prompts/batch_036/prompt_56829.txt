For thirty-eight years I worked as a physiological ecologist, chiefly on the energetics of small invertebrate communities in disturbed soils, and since retiring I have taken up the philosophical literature on rationality and cooperation with more seriousness than I expected of myself. I should like a careful comparison of two explanatory frameworks, and I hope you will indulge a concrete case of my own that has been nagging at me.

The case is this. I have built a modest simulation of an indefinitely repeated prisoner's dilemma played by a finite population of a few hundred agents, with a small per-interaction error rate, random re-matching each round, and a discount parameter I can vary at will. With the discount parameter set comfortably above the familiar threshold at which the shadow of the future outweighs the one-shot gain from defection, the population settles into sustained mutual cooperation, much as the textbooks lead one to expect. What puzzles me is what happens when I step that parameter down below the threshold: cooperation does not collapse promptly. It persists and decays only slowly over many hundreds of generations, and the decay is markedly slower when the population has previously spent a long time above the threshold than when it has barely crossed it. History matters here, in a way I have come to call cooperative inertia.

Now the comparison I want. On one side stands the equilibrium reasoning inherited from rational choice theory: payoffs, best responses, Nash equilibria of the repeated game, the embarrassment of riches delivered by the folk theorem, and the equilibrium-selection problem that this embarrassment creates. On the other stands evolutionary game theory: strategy frequencies moving under a replication dynamic, evolutionary stability, mutation–selection balance, and the stochastic-stability results obtained for finite populations with small mutation rates.

Please compare these two as accounts of my phenomenon. In particular: first, in what sense, if any, are they answers to the same question, and where do their notions of a stable outcome genuinely diverge — what does each make of the contrast between the one-shot game, in which defection alone is stable, and the indefinitely repeated game? Second, which framework possesses the conceptual resources to represent path dependence and slow post-change decay, and which must import additional machinery in order to do so? Third, would either framework regard cooperative inertia as an artifact of my parameter choices rather than a feature of the dynamics, and how could I distinguish these possibilities from inside the simulation rather than by appeal to theory? I would also value your judgment on whether the two frameworks are rivals competing for the same explanandum or complementary descriptions pitched at different levels, and on what a philosopher of biology would identify as doing the real explanatory work in the case above.

I am comfortable with modest formalism, but please define any technical term you introduce, and argue your way through the comparison rather than merely enumerating points.
