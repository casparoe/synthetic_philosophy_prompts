I work at a small health charity and every year we split a fixed pot between an expensive rescue program that saves one child we've actually met, and a cheap prevention program that saves about five we never will. Our director ends every meeting with the same line: its just like choosing between losing one finger and losing five -- nobody in their right mind takes five if they can take one, and that's the end of the argument. He also says coin-flipping, which is roughly where my friend from grad school (basically a Taurek) ends up, is just refusing to choose in a fancy voice. My friend's counter is that the finger case only works because one person owns all the fingers, and once the losses fall on different people there's no one who owns the total, so there's nothing to add up in the first place.

Can you audit the finger analogy properly? Tell me what maps onto what -- the fingers, the hand, the owner, the moment of choice, the loss itself -- then sort the disanalogies into the ones that actually threaten the director's conclusion and the ones that are just noise. I suspect the fact that fingers are much less bad than deaths cuts in a direction neither of them has noticed, so say which way it goes. Then the main thing: does the idea that the numbers count survive the audit, or does my friend win, or does neither follow just from the analogy breaking? And if there's a better analogy for this allocation problem, give it to me and tell me how far that one holds before it starts doing the same smuggling.
