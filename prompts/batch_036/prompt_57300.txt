So my partner is a philosophy PhD student and I've been getting dragged along to her reading group for about a month now. This week the whole thing was about thought experiments in science — specifically the puzzle of how thinking hard about something that never actually happened can tell you anything about the world. I do infra for a living, zero formal training, but I found it weirdly familiar: its basically an argument about whether a test that can't be run in CI still counts as a test. I want to write something for their group chat (and honestly for my team's #random channel too), and I think the right format is a code review thread, because that's the one genre where four people can be completely deadlocked and still all sound polite.

Here's the conceit. A pull request, something like WIP: eliminate absolute simultaneity (do not merge yet), filed by a very young contributor who says the whole change came to him from imagining riding alongside a beam of light — if you could catch up to a light wave you'd be looking at a frozen, oscillating electromagnetic field just hanging there at rest, and nothing in the existing framework permits such an object to exist, so either you can never catch up to light or there's a hole in the framework. Tiny diff, enormous blast radius, no way to run it. Around that, write the review: PR description, commit message, inline diff comments, a CI bot, and four reviewers with their approve/request-changes statuses. The four should map onto the positions the reading group was fighting about, which I've probably mangled, so correct me where I'm wrong. One is the hard-nosed reconstructivist (Norton, I think?) who holds that a thought experiment is really just an argument in costume, that you can always rewrite it as explicit premises and inferences without losing anything, and that it only convinces you exactly as far as the reconstructed argument is sound — so his review is him demanding the cute narrative be replaced with a numbered derivation, and then finding one step that's quietly doing all the work. One is the Platonist (Brown?) who thinks the reconstruction misses the entire point, that Galileo-style cases deliver a priori insight into how nature works that outruns anything sitting in the premises, and who is completely unbothered by the fact that nothing gets executed — he'd actually like to know what the test environment even is, since on his view you know it by contemplating it, not by running it. One insists (Sorensen is the name I remember) that these are literal experiments, just carried out without the usual materials, which means they can be badly designed like any other experiment: unrepresentative idealizations, misleading variations, a setup that doesn't warrant the generalization being drawn from it. His comments should read like an experimentalist's — sample size of one, confounds, whether the case is typical of the class you're generalizing over. And the fourth is a Kuhn-flavored principal engineer who thinks the whole does-it-run question is confused, because the value is precisely that the scenario shows the existing conceptual scheme can't even describe the case, and that's the deliverable, not a defect — this is the reviewer who should cite the tied-cannonballs thing as precedent: if heavy bodies really did fall faster, then strapping a heavy ball to a light one gives you a composite that should fall both slower (the light one dragging it back) and faster (the pair being heavier than either alone), and that precedent shipped with no equipment whatsoever. Add a CI bot that tries to auto-reconstruct the thought experiment into premises plus an inference and posts a coverage report nobody asked for.

Then one genuinely blocking comment, and I want this one to not be funny, because it should land the actual problem instead of the joke: where is the new information coming from if nobody measured anything, and why should an intuition about a situation we can't run get any weight just because it feels compelling? Don't resolve it, and don't have anyone get talked around — end on whatever merge decision makes that unresolvedness sting a little. Keep it deadpan in engineering register the whole way through, no winking, and a reader who knows this literature should come away thinking that's fair rather than these people clearly don't get it. Roughly 900 words, formatted like a real review thread. Invented handles for the reviewers; name-checking the actual philosophers behind the positions is fine but the satire is aimed at the views, not at people. And no bibliography or citations or anything like that, I definitely don't want a references section stuck on the end of a bit about a pull request.
