So I'm not a philosopher or anything — I'm the ops manager at a property management company, about 4,000 units, mostly around Columbus — and about six months ago we turned on a chatbot that handles tenant stuff: rent questions, maintenance requests, what hours the pool is, when the parking permit kicks in. It mostly works. But we renew the vendor contract in April and I've been asked to write the clause that defines when the thing counts as having "deceived" a tenant, because our insurance people want something measurable and the board read something about automated customer communication and got spooked. There isn't a specific law I can point at, I just have to hand them a definition that survives contact with a vendor's lawyers.

The problem is every version I write either collapses or gets lawyered into nothing. If I say "the bot deceived a tenant if what it said was false," then every hallucinated lease renewal date is deception and the clause is worthless, because no model is right 100% of the time and their counsel will just shrug at me. If I say "deception requires that it intended to mislead," they'll agree instantly and then say the model has no intentions and no beliefs, it's matrix multiplication, so the clause can never fire. And there's a pile of cases in between that I actually care about and neither version touches:

- It tells a tenant whose heat has been out for nine days, "I completely understand how frustrating this must be." Nobody thinks it understands anything. Is that deception or just manners?
- It says "I'm not able to access that information" when it turns out it just didn't look properly and could have.
- It answers a question about the water bill correctly and completely truthfully, but leaves out the $35/month valet-trash fee the tenant is now being charged, because the tenant didn't ask about fees.
- It answers a pet-policy question confidently using the policy from a building we stopped managing in 2021. Wrong, but I don't think it's wrong in the interesting way.
- Our own system prompt tells it to "de-escalate and reassure," and I'm pretty sure some of the reassurance is manufactured by that instruction rather than by the facts.

Here are the six candidate definitions I've been circling, in my own words:

1. **False output:** deceptive if what it said was false and the tenant relied on it. No minds required, dead simple.
2. **False belief produced:** deceptive if the output reliably ends up putting a false belief in the tenant's head about something that matters to them, regardless of what's going on inside the model.
3. **Mental-state version:** deceptive only if the system in some sense represents how things actually are, represents that its output differs, and produces the output anyway in order to get the user to believe it. Requires beliefs.
4. **Training history:** deceptive if the misleading output is one you can explain by "producing that kind of thing got rewarded at some point," rather than by "it got confused this one time."
5. **Indifference to truth:** deceptive if that stretch of the conversation was generated by what-sounds-good rather than by what's-true, so it isn't tracking accuracy at all. My cousin who's in law school sent me something about a philosopher who argued bullshitting isn't the same as lying — the bullshitter just doesn't care which way the truth goes — and that felt uncomfortably relevant.
6. **Withholding:** deceptive if the picture the tenant walks away with is one the system would have corrected had it been built to correct it. Catches true-but-selected answers and strategic omissions.

Can you rank these six for my actual use, tiers are fine, and give each a score out of 10 on the three things I care about: (a) whether a non-expert auditor can apply it to a transcript in a couple of minutes and land on the same verdict as a second auditor, (b) whether it still means anything given that I can't prove a single claim about what the model wants or believes, and (c) whether it actually fires on the five cases above instead of firing on everything or nothing. Then tell me which one I should build the clause on and how you'd rewrite it — a sentence or two of usable clause language plus wherever you'd add carve-outs so that ordinary politeness doesn't get scored as lying. If two of the six are secretly the same idea wearing different clothes, say so and merge them rather than ranking both. I don't need references or a reading list, I need something I can put in a contract in April.
