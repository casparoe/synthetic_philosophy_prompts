So I'm putting together a batch of hard cases for a seminar on reference, and I want one that's engineered to a spec rather than the morning-star/evening-star thing everyone has seen a hundred times. The case I'm after should feel a bit wierd once it's built, but it has to be buildable.

Here's the spec. I want a realistic, everyday scenario — an archive, a field station, a hospital records office, a small-town registry, whatever — containing two names and one competent speaker who uses both of them. Constraints:

1. The two names have to reach her through introductions of exactly the same kind: same sort of source, same sort of baptismal event, so there's no descriptive difference she could articulate between them. If your case secretly leans on something like "one came down from the sailors and the other from the astronomers," it doesn't count.
2. She knows the complete naming history of both names. Her ignorance can't be doing any of the work — she's as well informed about where each name came from as it's possible to be.
3. Even so, the identity between them is a real empirical discovery for her, while the self-identity stays trivial and a priori. Both of those have to hold in the same case, for the same speaker.
4. The object is an ordinary concrete particular, not a natural kind — I don't want the discovery to be an essence-uncovering one.
5. She's already been making true singular claims with each name before the discovery lands, so we can see what happens to her existing beliefs when it does.

Scenario in one to three paragraphs, and keep it self-contained. Then the walkthrough, which is most of the work:

- Say exactly what sense a Fregean is forced to assign to each name. If the honest answer is that the only difference available is something like the item tagged in the first naming event versus the item tagged in the second, then say whether a metalinguistic sense of that kind is actually available to her, and whether it can still explain the triviality of the self-identity or whether it wrecks it.
- Say what a direct-reference theorist claims she learned, given that on that view the two identities express the same proposition. Where exactly does the informativeness go, and is that a semantic story or a psychological one?
- If you want to reach for the two-separate-mental-files or dossier idea, be explicit about whether that's a claim about her mind or about the language, and whether using it concedes the point to Frege.
- Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing all the work, and delete it: show me that once it's gone, the thing collapses back into a bog-standard identity with a descriptive difference after all.
- Last, give the best objection a Fregean who thinks your case is a dud would press. Not a strawman — the move that would actually make me doubt it.

So you know what counts as cheating here: the point of the exercise is to find out whether the sense/reference apparatus can be individuated finely enough to explain why some identities are informative without being individuated so finely that every true identity comes out informative and the trivial ones lose their triviality. A case that stipulates its verdicts instead of generating them from the machinery fails, and so does one that quietly reintroduces a descriptive difference through the back door. No reading list or citations — I'm not after scholarship, just the case and the demonstration that it meets each of the specs.
