i write literary fiction and i've been circling the same story for about a year now. it's supposed to be built on simpson's paradox, but not as decoration — i want the reversal to work the way a reveal works in a mystery, where the reader has to re-understand everything they were already shown.

the seed: a county is closing one of its two hospitals. the consultants' table shows the small rural one wins in every category they broke out — maternity, trauma, long-term care — and loses badly overall, and the overall number is the one in the press release. the person who finds the breakdown works at the small one. the person who made the pooled table isn't corrupt, she's careful, and she has a genuine answer for why her number is the one that matters for a patient who walks in off the street not knowing which category she belongs to.

write it as a complete short story, roughly 2,000–3,500 words, and stage that argument for real — i want both numbers to come out looking defensible, not a gotcha where the good guys win. nobody says the phrase simpson's paradox. no charts, no whiteboard scene where somebody explains the math over coffee; it has to be carried entirely by what people are doing and saying to each other. and dont let the small hospital be saved at the end, that's the version i keep accidentally writing.

if it's useful, the point of view could be somebody who never sees either table — a porter, a patient in the corridor, the driver who takes the merged records across town.
