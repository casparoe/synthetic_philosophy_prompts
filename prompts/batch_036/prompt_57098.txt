I am a novelist, and I have reached the point in a manuscript where a formal device I have been relying on is beginning to look less like a technique than like an unargued metaphysics. I would like your help making that metaphysics explicit, not in order to abandon it but in order to know what I am committed to.

The book concerns one woman, whom I will call Vera, and it consists entirely of four documentary registers. The reader is never told which to trust. They are: her private diary, kept unevenly over twenty years and addressed to no one; the transcript of her testimony under cross-examination in a civil action, in which she was, by her own admission, a witness hostile to her own interests; her younger sister's memoir of her, written after her death and composed in an unmistakable register of tribute; and, in the final movement, a dossier assembled without her knowledge out of her correspondence, her calendar, her purchases, and her clinic's notes, then summarised by a system that no human being has ever read in its entirety. There is no narrator and no scene in which Vera is simply present to us.

What I need from you is a diagnostic instrument I can use on myself, on my editors, and eventually on readers: a sorting quiz of nine questions, each with two or three options, that establishes what a person actually believes about selves of this kind before they are handed any vocabulary in which to announce it.

The questions must satisfy the following. Each must be concrete and drawn from Vera or her documents — never abstract, and never a request for assent to a thesis. No option may contain a term of art, a philosopher's name, or any word that a trained reader would recognise as a flag. A reader with no philosophy whatsoever must be able to answer every question on the strength of intuition about the case alone.

The scoring key must be given as a table, question by question, showing which answer is diagnostic of which position and, just as important, which pairs of positions each question does no work separating. I would far rather you flag the indistinguishable pairs than pad the instrument to a round number. I want the key expressed as diagnostic structure rather than as a tally of points, since different questions should be testing different distinctions.

The positions that must appear as leaves include: the view on which the self is a useful abstraction posited to organise the stories told by and about a person, in the sense Dennett gives to the phrase a center of narrative gravity; the stronger view on which a person exists as a person only insofar as she integrates her life into a self-telling, so that the diary does constitutive work and not merely evidential work; the view that denies narrativity any such role and holds that some of us live, quite contentedly, as a series of episodes; the view on which Vera simply is the human organism, and the documents bear on her not at all; the view on which personhood is a status conferred by practices of holding someone to account, which would make the deposition rather than the diary the constitutive document; the reductionist view that accepts psychological continuity while denying that identity is what matters; the eliminativist line on which no one ever had a self and the appearance of one is the illusion to be explained; and any view on which the self is fixed by the attributions of others rather than by the subject's own account, which my sister-document seems designed to raise. If two of these collapse into a single leaf given the questions you write, say so and explain the collapse rather than hiding it.

Two further demands. First, at least one question must be engineered specifically to separate the abstraction view from the eliminativist one; tell me which question does that work and how, because I suspect most readers will treat them as a single position. Second, once your questions are fixed, the space of possible answer patterns will be larger than the space of positions actually held. Give me at least three patterns that look coherent but are occupied by nobody, and for each say whether the vacancy reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. I am especially curious whether the combination narrative constitutes the self but the subject's own narrative carries no privileged authority is genuinely empty, since the sister-document and the dossier together suggest to me that it need not be.

Finally, a short coda. My novel withholds all adjudication among the four registers and ends with the dossier — a pattern of covariation with no narrative shape, which is why I am unsure whether your tree has a leaf for it, or whether the absence of such a leaf is a gap in the tree or a sign that no one can hold it. Tell me which leaf the book's form commits me to, whether that commitment is stable across the whole instrument or only across some questions, and whether readers who answer your quiz in the ordinary way will experience the ending as an illumination or as a betrayal.

Please do not append a bibliography or reading suggestions; names belong in the key and nowhere else.
