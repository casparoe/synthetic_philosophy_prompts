**Question 4 (25 points).** Explain what, on Elster's account, the Sirens episode shows about the rationality of binding oneself. Then assess the suggestion that an entrenched constitution is the political analogue of a Ulysses contract: what would have to be true for the analogy to hold, and where does it come under strain?

**Candidate 43's answer:**

Elster's example comes from Book 12 of the Odyssey. Ulysses wants to hear the Sirens, and knows that if he hears them he will want to steer toward them, which would wreck the ship and kill everyone aboard. Circe tells him how to handle it: wax in the crew's ears, ropes for himself. The important feature of the case, for Elster, is that nothing goes wrong with Ulysses' reasoning at the moment of choice. He isn't weak-willed in the ordinary sense — he correctly predicts that his preferences will reverse, and he acts now, while calm and able to see clearly, to control what he will do later when he can't. That's what makes the binding rational rather than merely compulsive.

How does the binding actually work? The ropes obviously matter, but they don't do the real work. What makes this a Ulysses contract rather than a man tied up by other people is that the earlier and the later Ulysses remain the same practical agent: his higher-order desire to get home safely survives the encounter with the song, and it is that desire, not the hemp, that keeps the ship on its course. The ropes are only the outward expression of a preference that never in fact reversed. This is essentially Frankfurt's point about second-order volitions, relocated from a single moment to a stretch of time — which is also why Elster's account was so readily picked up and formalised in Thaler and Shefrin's planner-doer model.

The same logic is supposed to carry over into politics. A founding generation, or a people acting through a supermajority, knows that it will later be tempted — by panic, by a charismatic leader, by a temporary majority — into doing things it will regret: suspending habeas corpus, restricting speech, spending down the future. Entrenchment, plus judicial review to police it, is the rope.

The analogy strains in at least two places. First, the crew. The sailors plugged their own ears and were lashed to the mast alongside Ulysses, which shows that Elster's device can operate without any third-party enforcement at all — and a constitution obviously cannot, since somebody has to apply it against people who want it overturned. Second, and more seriously, there is no single continuing agent available to do the binding. The people who end up bound by a constitution are mostly not the people who made it, and they never consented to it; Jefferson made something like this point when he argued in his 1789 letter to Madison that one generation cannot bind the next, and proposed that constitutions expire every nineteen years. So the political Ulysses contract is at best an agreement between the dead and the living, enforced by courts that have preferences of their own and no ropes whatever.

Which leaves the analogy weaker than Elster wants it to be. The individual case works because the agent's better self stays in charge throughout; the political case has no better self, only a procedure.
