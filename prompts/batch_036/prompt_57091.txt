I work on the infra side of an LLM agent product — checkpointing, eval harnesses, tool sandboxing, nothing research-y — and for the last couple of months I've been having a running argument with a coworker about whether the stuff inside our models is actually *about* anything, or whether aboutness is just us projecting onto matrix math. I have no philosophy training at all, but I fell down the rabbit hole on naturalizing mental content over a bunch of weekends (indicator semantics, teleosemantics, the disjunction problem, some of the Fodor asymmetric-dependence stuff, the swampman case) and I've landed somewhere I want sanity-checked before I write it up in an internal doc, becuase I have a nagging feeling I stitched together pieces from different toolboxes. Here's where I am:

1. I want a genuinely naturalistic account: what a state is about has to be fixed by facts about the system itself — its causal and functional organization, stuff you could in principle reconstruct from the training setup, the weights, and the logs. Not by what some interpreter charitably decides to attribute to it. If content just is "whatever makes the best predictive story about the system," I don't see how it's a real property rather than a bookkeeping convention.
2. Pure covariance / information theories can't work, because of the disjunction problem. A state that fires both for "user wants a refund" and for "user is frustrated" can't mean refund just because it correlates with both — something has to make one of those firings a mistake instead of an equally good candidate for the content.
3. That something is function, and function comes from selection history. A state is about what it got recruited to carry or do: by evolution in animals, and in our case by gradient descent against a reward signal plus the choices we make when we design the architecture, the data mix and the objective. Misrepresentation is the state failing at the job it was selected for.
4. But history can only matter through what it left behind in the current system. Two agents instantiated from byte-identical weights in identical containers have identical contents, full stop — including the one we spun up fresh from a checkpoint this morning, which has no past of its own beyond whatever is baked into the file. So content supervenes on the current intrinsic/functional state, not on the causal history running up to it.
5. Content needs to be determinate at the grain I actually work at. I'm fine with there being no fact about whether some activation means "refund" or "refund-shaped grievance," but when our eval harness reports a result, there has better be a fact about whether the model represents the user as asking for money back versus asking for store credit. Radical inscrutability would make the whole exercise useless to me.
6. When I can cleanly decode a feature out of the residual stream with a linear probe, I treat that as good evidence about what the state is about, and I write evals on the basis of it.

Something in there feels wobbly, but I can't tell whether it's one big structural problem or three small local ones, and I've gone around in circles twice already talking to myself about it. So: audit the set. Tell me which pairs genuinely conflict and give me the mechanism of the conflict, not just a label — I want to be able to see *why* the two can't both be true, in terms an engineer would recognize. Tell me which pairs only look like they conflict and what distinction dissolves them, because I'd rather not revise a view that was never in trouble. Rank the real conflicts by how badly they bite rather than listing them all as equally bad, and for each one say what the cheapest revision is and what I actually lose by making it — including whether the loss hurts me on the day job or only hurts philosophically. Also flag anywhere I've used one term in two different senses without noticing, since that's my usual failure mode. And please run the audit on a concrete case instead of leaving it abstract: a state in our agent that fires both when the user genuinely wants a refund and when the user is merely annoyed, where both patterns were rewarded during training — under my own commitments, what does that state represent, and do I have any resources left for calling one of the firings an error?

Then end with a single last line in exactly this form: \answer{N — short phrase} where N is the number of the one commitment I should revise to clean up the most, and the phrase is six words or fewer naming the revision.
