So I got volunteered to run our team's monthly paper-reading thing and I picked Turing's 1950 "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" — the imitation game one. I'm an evals/backend engineer with zero philosophy background, so my summary of his rulings below is probably rough in spots; feel free to tell me I've garbled one.

Here's the list of objections he works through and what he does with each, as I read it:

1. The religious one (thinking belongs to the soul, God doesn't hand souls to machines) — waved off, he says it puts limits on God and he isn't impressed.
2. The "that would simply be dreadful" one — he explicitly refuses to treat it as an argument at all.
3. The Gödel-flavored one (every machine has true statements it can't prove, humans don't) — he engages properly, then turns it around: how do we know humans aren't in the same boat?
4. The consciousness one (it can't really feel, or write a poem because it feels) — calls it the deepest objection but declines to argue it, on the grounds that we can't check this in other people either.
5. Lady Lovelace's (machines can never originate anything) — he answers with an observation, basically that machines surprise him all the time.
6. The list of disabilities (can't enjoy strawberries and cream, can't fall in love) — dismissed as things nobody has proved impossible.
7. The ESP one — this is the one he says impresses him most. He takes the statistics on telepathy to be overwhelming, and proposes running the game in a room that blocks it so nothing can leak between interrogator and machine.

So: six objections he argues down or refuses to engage, one he concedes and then neutralizes by changing the setup of the experiment.

Now the actual problem, which is a real one at work. We score model outputs with an LLM judge. Someone on our team raised the objection that the judge and the model under test were trained on alot of the same text, so there's a statistical channel between them — the judge recognizes its own kind and passes it for reasons that have nothing to do with the answers being good. We have numbers for it: identical answers score higher when the judge comes from the same model family. And there's an obvious patch — use a judge from a seperate family, or human raters, or blind the judge to which system produced the answer. But there's also the comeback that two human raters from the same culture share priors too, so this isn't machine-specific.
