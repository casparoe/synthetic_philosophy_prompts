I'm a litigator in federal civil practice, fifteen years in; I know the rules of evidence and Rule 11 backwards, and I have no philosophy training. The debate I want translated is the epistemologists' argument over the norm of assertion — that one should assert only what one knows (the view associated with Williamson), against the rivals holding that assertion requires truth, or reasonable belief, or nothing more than sincerity.

Render each candidate in the vocabulary I work in: personal knowledge as a foundation requirement for a witness, the certification that factual contentions have evidentiary support after an inquiry reasonable under the circumstances, the perjury standard's preoccupation with what the declarant believed rather than with what was so, materiality, willfulness, sanctions, and the allocation of risk between the one who speaks and the one who relies. Translate faithfully; don't smuggle my framework's assumptions back to me as conclusions about theirs.

Then give me the residue report. Which candidate has an exact legal counterpart and which only an approximate one? Where does the translation force distortion — for instance, because every standard I know is imposed from outside on an activity that would exist anyway, whereas the philosophers hold that at least one of these norms is constitutive of the activity itself? What in their debate is inexpressible in my vocabulary at all? And where translation fails, is that a limitation of the legal framework, or a sign that the philosophical claims were less determinate than they sound?
