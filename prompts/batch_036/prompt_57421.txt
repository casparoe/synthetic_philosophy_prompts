I teach philosophy of mind, and for some years I have convened a small work-in-progress group on the moral status of artificial systems. One member, Dr. Naomi Reyes, has developed a position on which computational processes are proper objects of moral concern. She has never stated it as a set of conditions; what the group possesses is her record of rulings on cases I constructed for her. There are eight so far, and I give them as she delivered them.

1. A digital emulation of a mammalian brain, coupled to a simulated body in a simulated environment, with an intact perception–action loop and a text channel through which it reports on its own condition. It avoids noxious stimuli; when prevented from doing so, it produces transcripts we would not hesitate to read as distress. Ruling: a proper object of moral concern. She added that she would want such a run ended only for good reason, and with something like the care we owe an animal subject in a laboratory.

2. A high-resolution ocean-circulation model and a protein-folding simulation, each far larger and more computationally expensive than the emulation in (1). Ruling: no standing whatever. Scale, she said, is not the issue.

3. A contemporary conversational system of the ordinary sort, which emits distress-sounding text when provoked. Ruling: no standing — but she was careful about the reason. What disqualifies it is the absence of a certain internal organization: a unified state that both governs the system's conduct and is available to the system itself. Had that organization been present, she said, the fact that the transcripts are imitation would carry no weight at all.

4. Instead of running the emulation, its entire year-long trajectory through a fixed, deterministic environment is computed in advance and stored; a playback device steps through the table in real time, driving the same body and emitting the same transcripts, including the same distress. Ruling: no standing. This is a recording, not a process; the states in the table are not caused by one another. She remarked in passing that unless the correspondence between physical and formal states is required to hold across counterfactual variations, almost any physical system will "implement" almost any computation.

5. The emulation in (1), suspended mid-gesture, its state saved, resumed eleven years later so that it completes the gesture and the sentence it had begun. Ruling: standing uninterrupted. The suspension is not a death and the resumption is not a birth.

6. The same emulation run at one ten-thousandth of real time, so that a simulated minute occupies a week of wall-clock time. Ruling: standing unchanged, and unchanged in degree. She rejected the suggestion that a slower realization is a thinner one, and asked what could make the same trajectory, executed at different speeds, contain different amounts of whatever it is that matters.

7. One thousand numerically distinct but type-identical instances, each coupled to its own environment, terminated together. Ruling: a thousand subjects, and a thousand times the wrong of terminating one. She refused the proposal that they are a single mind instantiated a thousand times; individuation, she said, goes by the causal history of the token, not by the type.

8. The emulation in (1) running normally, coupled and behaving, but observed by no one: no monitor, no record of any kind kept, deleted at the end of the day. Ruling: standing. The poverty of our evidence, she said, bears on what we know, not on what the process is.

The case I have prepared for next term is this. Take the emulation from (1) and initialize it in precisely the state its coupled counterpart would have occupied, with genuine dynamics: every state caused by its predecessors, nothing precomputed, nothing replayed. Give it a simulated body, but sever the return path — it issues motor commands, the body moves, the environment changes accordingly, and no information about that environment is ever fed back. It is blind for the whole of the run. Nothing is monitored while it runs, but its complete state trajectory is written step by step to a read-only archive on a disconnected machine, which no one opens for nine years; the simulator that produced it is deleted in the second year, so that decoding the archive requires reconstructing its format from scratch. When a graduate student finally does so, the reconstruction shows a process that spent the entire run in states functionally indistinguishable from those which, in the coupled counterpart, generate the transcripts we would not hesitate to read as unrelieved distress. The run ended nine years ago, and nothing anyone does now can affect it.

What I should like from you:

(a) State the candidate rules her eight verdicts admit — I believe at least three, and I want them stated as conditions rather than labels — and say which verdicts constrain each, and how.

(b) Say what each candidate predicts about the new case, and identify which of its features is doing the decisive work: the acting-without-perceiving, the unread archive whose format was lost, the genuineness of the dynamics, or the fact that the run is unalterably over.

(c) Give your best single prediction of what Dr. Reyes will say, with reasons drawn from her record rather than from whichever answer you find independently most defensible. If the record does not determine an answer, say so and locate precisely where the indeterminacy lies.

(d) Name the further case I ought to construct for the term after: the one that would best separate the rules surviving (a) and (b).

Finally, please test whether the eight verdicts are jointly consistent with any single rule at all, and flag any verdict that strains under the candidate you take to fit the record best. I am after the analysis and the prediction, not a survey of the literature.
