Okay so a little background first. I'm not an academic, never took a philosophy course in my life, I've just been reading about consciousness on my own for a few years now. I also run a monthly "big questions" meetup in the back room of my town's public library. Usually 15 to 20 people, ages anywhere from early 20s to late 70s, nobody's studied this formally, two or three are surprisingly sharp. I get 90 minutes and my budget is basically zero: index cards, envelopes, pens, a whiteboard, and I can print handouts at work.

Next month I want to do the epiphenomenalism problem about our own reports — the one where, if the feeling itself doesn't cause anything, then the words I use to describe the feeling were produced entirely by the physical machinery in my head, and its suspiciously convenient that the machinery gets it right every time. And there's a second layer that bothers me even more: the reasoning that got me to epiphenomenalism in the first place is itself downstream of those same reports, so the experience never gets to be a cause anywhere in the chain, not even of my arguing about it.

Here's what I need from you, and please be honest if I'm wrong about this. I think there are two seperate problems that get mashed together, and I've noticed people only ever demo the first one:

1. The report problem. Someone's speech about their inner life could in principle have been generated with nothing in there. Feels like a gotcha, and I think its a weak one, because obviously a liar or a confabulator can report without the experience, and that doesn't show the experience is inert when its really there.

2. The argument problem. Even if my reports are perfectly honest and perfectly accurate, and even if I grant that they're about something real, the whole structure of my knowing-and-then-arguing never has a place where the feeling does any work. The feeling is only ever the topic, never a link in the causal chain. This is the one that actually bites me and I want the room to feel it.

So: design an activity for this group that lands on (2), not just (1). Something they do with their hands and to each other, not something I narrate at them. I want the realization to come out of what happens in the room — from the rules, the setup, whatever they end up saying to each other — and I don't want anyone to hear the words epiphenomenalism or phenomenal judgment until the debrief.

One more thing to work with, because I know someone will raise it. Last time, a guy in the group said the feeling and the report can both be caused by the same brain event, so the report can still be accurate even though the feeling didn't cause it — like two clocks that were set by the same person, they agree without talking to each other. Build that objection into the activity somehow, so the room gets to try it out and see for themselves whether it rescues things or just moves the lump around. I want a verdict on it, not a shrug.

Please include: rules and materials, a timeline that fits in 90 minutes with a real debrief, what people should come away seeing, the debrief itself mapping specific moments in the activity onto specific parts of the problem, what will predictably break the first time I run this with amateurs and how to recover, and a frank note on which feature of the problem the activity just cannot reproduce so I know what I have to supply by talking.

Also, if your design requires me to deceive people — I'm not opposed to a little of that, it can be the whole point — tell me how much is too much, and how to do the reveal so nobody leaves feeling like a mark.

Write it in American English. And no reading lists or citations, please, I can't chase those down and I don't want you guessing at them. Just give me something I can actually run.
