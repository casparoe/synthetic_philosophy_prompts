I cover science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about what biography can actually do for a reader morally — not the sentimental version, but whether there is a coherent mechanism behind the claim. The clearest statement of Plutarch's own is at the opening of the Life of Aemilius Paulinus (Loeb edition, trans. Perrin):

"I began the writing of my 'Lives' for the sake of others, but I find that I am continuing the work and delighting in it now for my own sake also, using history as a mirror and endeavouring in a manner to fashion and adorn my life in conformity with the virtues therein depicted. For the result is like nothing else than daily living and associating together, when I receive and welcome each subject of my history in turn as my guest, so to speak, and observe carefully 'how large he was and of what mien,' and select from his career what is most important and most beautiful to know."

A few sections later he describes the effect quite differently: keeping the records of the noblest characters in his soul lets him repel whatever base or ignoble suggestion his enforced associations intrude upon him, turning his thoughts away to the fairest of his examples. And in the opening of the Life of Alexander he makes his methodological claim — that he writes lives rather than histories because the most glorious deeds do not always disclose virtue or vice, whereas a trifling act, a phrase, or a jest often reveals character better than battles in which thousands fall.

My difficulty: does the mirror image commit him to imitation? If it does, it sits badly with the small-signs method, since a jest or an unguarded remark is diagnostic precisely because it was not chosen, and what is not chosen cannot be the thing the reader copies. Tell me what the mirror passage actually commits him to, whether the guest-and-associating language and the repelling language point to a mechanism other than imitation, and whether that dissolves the conflict with Alexander or merely relocates it. I want a definite answer with your reasons, not a survey of the options — and please flag anywhere you are leaning on a reading of the Greek or of Plutarch's project that specialists would contest. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning; no preamble and no restating the question.
