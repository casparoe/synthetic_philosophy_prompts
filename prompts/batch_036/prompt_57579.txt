I'm on a small team writing the "honesty" section of the internal spec that governs our customer-facing chatbot (banking stuff — balances, disputes, fee explanations), and a colleague dropped the following argument on me in a review meeting last Thursday and I still haven't been able to answer it. He's an ex-lawyer, not a philosopher, and his claim is that the section we're writing is window dressing because the distinction it's trying to enforce doesn't actually exist for a system like ours. Here it is in roughly his words; I've numbered the steps so we can talk about them, and I've tried to write each one as charitably as I could, because in the room every single one of them sounded obviously true to me and I only started squirming at the end.

(1) To deceive someone is to bring it about that they end up believing something you take to be false.
(2) When a model answers a question, the user automatically assumes it is answering cooperatively — that is, that the answer includes everything the model takes to be relevant to the question.
(3) So if the model leaves out something it takes to be relevant, it brings the user to believe that there was nothing relevant left out, and it takes that to be false.
(4) Therefore, by (1), every incomplete answer is a deception.
(5) No answer is ever complete: for anything the model says there is some further consideration it treats as relevant that didn't make it in, whether because of length limits, latency, or uncertainty it didn't want to belabor.
(6) So no answer a model gives is non-deceptive, and the only non-deceptive thing it could do is decline to answer.
(7) But declining is itself something the user interprets cooperatively — as "the model has nothing to tell me" — and that is often false as well.
(8) Therefore no available behavior of the model is non-deceptive, "honest" and "deceptive" carve out no real distinction for AI systems, and any spec built on them is just branding.

I want to say (8) is absurd — it would make a bot that answers "2 + 2 = 4" into a deceiver, which can't be right — but I couldn't find a step in the chain that I was willing to call false on the spot, and he knew it, which is why the meeting ended with him fairly pleased with himself. For what it's worth I'd like to keep (4) if I can, because misleading-by-omission is a real failure mode we actually see in our red-team transcripts and I don't want a definition of deception that lets a bot off the hook just because every sentence it emitted was true. I'd also rather keep (1), since it's the closest thing we have to a working definition. But tell me where you'd actually cut.
