Last night I lost three hours arguing with a friend about whether the far future can ground duties at all, and I came away dissatisfied with how the argument went — so I want to apply the same pressure to next month's guest in our department's public lecture series. She defends the non-identity line: because any climate policy changes which particular people come to exist, nobody in the remote future can complain that our emissions wronged them. Her signature move is that our obligations attach not to future individuals but to the shape of the future population — its size and the distribution of wellbeing within it — and that a shape can be made worse without anyone being wronged.

Write me seven questions, each paired with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. I want at least one on whether the shape of a population can carry any moral weight at all if no one is ever in a position to complain about it; one on what the view commits her to elsewhere in population ethics — total versus average aggregation, the repugnant conclusion, and whether a policy that halves the future population while raising the quality of every life within it counts on her view as an improvement; one on the asymmetry her reasoning seems to require, since the same argument would appear to rule out harmless wrongdoing whenever an act affects who exists; and one on the time lag, given that the first decades of warming fall on people who are already here — does the wrongness of emissions decay with temporal distance on her account? Close with a question about what a future person would have to say to defeat her position, and one that forces her to distinguish her view from the claim that the far future simply does not matter. Nothing about her influences or how she came to the topic.
