I am a second-year law student, and for the past three weeks my jurisprudence seminar has been working through the exchange between Patrick Devlin and H. L. A. Hart that followed the Wolfenden Report — Devlin's Maccabaean Lecture and *The Enforcement of Morals* on one side, Hart's *Law, Liberty, and Morality* on the other, with Feinberg's offense principle hovering in the background as a third position nobody quite wants to sign up for. Last Thursday we had moved from the general argument to applications — the continuing criminalization of consensual adult incest, the permissibility of paying kidney donors — when one of the strongest students in the room said, mildly, that the whole debate was beside the point, "because you can't legislate morality," and the seminar, our professor included, nodded and changed the subject. I said at the time that I didn't think that was an answer to Devlin so much as a restatement of the question, but I could not explain myself, and the upshot is that I have been asked to open this week's session with a short framing statement and a one-page handout. The seminar has fifteen people, roughly half of whom have done the reading and half of whom have not, and I would like the handout to be usable by both groups.

Please write as a legal philosopher who has taught this debate for thirty years and is impatient with slogans. Before you answer, spend some time brainstorming privately about how many distinct propositions that one sentence can bear, and then give me the results as a numbered menu rather than as a defense of whichever reading you personally find most interesting. For each candidate reading, I would like: (a) the proposition stated in a single crisp sentence, with the ambiguity resolved rather than gestured at; (b) an account of what is doing the work in it — which sense of "can't" is in play (conceptual impossibility, causal inefficacy, practical infeasibility, or a normative claim wearing the grammar of an impossibility), what "legislate" is being taken to cover (criminal prohibition only, or the whole apparatus of licensing, taxation, and public education), and what "morality" is being taken to mean (outward conduct, inner motive and character, moral belief, or shared moral sentiment); (c) a verdict on whether the reading is a platitude, a substantive and contestable empirical thesis, or simply false; and (d) an assessment of whether it engages Devlin's actual position, which I take to be a pro tanto claim — that immorality gives society a prima facie right to legislate, defeasible by considerations of principle and expediency — supported by something like a disintegration thesis about the shared morality that holds a society together and by a standard drawn from the right-minded person in the jury box. Say, reading by reading, what Devlin could concede without cost, and separately what Hart could and could not borrow from it, given that Hart's own objections run through harm, the fragility of the disintegration thesis as an empirical matter, and the distinction between the positive morality a community happens to hold and the critical morality by which it might be judged.

Two further things. First, please pay particular attention to whether any reading is self-undermining: it strikes me that on at least one construal the slogan cannot be true, because every body of law already enforces somebody's morality, including the morality of restraint that would have the law stand back — and I would like to know whether that objection is as decisive as it feels to me or whether a Devlinian can absorb it. Relatedly, consider the Kantian strand on which coerced conformity with duty has no moral worth, and tell me whether that reading is fatal to Devlin or simply irrelevant to him, since he appears to care about social cohesion rather than the virtue of individual souls. Second, close with a short section telling me which reading, if any, would actually have earned my classmate that nod, what the argument would have had to look like to get there, and how I should open the discussion on Thursday so that asking what the slogan means does not come across as pedantry from someone who has not done the reading.
