I'm the warden of a mid-size state prison and next month we're dedicating a new housing unit we built on Scandinavian lines — private rooms with real windows, keys the residents carry themselves, staff who sit down and eat with them, no long-term solitary. The ribbon cutting will have two state legislators in the front row who voted for the funding because somebody showed them projections about recidivism and cost per bed, a victims' advocacy group that's genuinely ambivalent about being there, about sixty officers who think I've gone soft, and forty of the men who'll actually live in the unit standing behind me.

Here's my problem: I don't think the recidivism case is the right case. If this unit is worth building only because it produces fewer future victims, then the day the numbers come in flat we've all agreed the way we treated these men was a mistake — and I don't want to hang their dignity on a statistic that hasn't arrived yet. Write the remarks. They have to thank the legislators in a vocabulary they'll recognize without conceding that their reason was the right reason, and they can't lecture the officers. Hard constraints: no statistics, no cost figures, and don't mention Norway or anywhere else in Europe — the argument should stand on its own. I also need one section that faces the victims' advocates without turning into either a call for forgiveness or a claim about what anyone deserved, since im not asking anybody to forgive and im not saying anyone got their due.
