So I've been trying to pin down where I think consciousness actually peters out, going purely by gut, and the gut turns out to be a mess. It came up for two practical reasons. My cousin wants to farm black soldier fly larvae for feed — a few million of them in a bin of food scraps — and I said go ahead without a second thought. I also used to work at a seafood place that dropped lobsters into boiling water, and I thought that was bad, genuinely bad. The only difference I can name is size and how long it takes.

Rest of the ledger:

- Mosquito: swat, no feelings. Honeybee: also fine to kill, but I feel bad about it. I dont have an account of why the bee and not the mosquito except that the bee has better PR.
- The cuttlefish self-control study moved me way more than I expected — the one where they could take a mediocre snack right away or hold out for a better one, and a bunch of them held out, and adjusted how long they'd hold out to what they'd learned about the wait. Felt like somebody home.
- But the little cleaner fish that passed the mirror mark test does not strike me as more conscious than a dog, and a dog fails the mirror test. So whatever that test measures I don't think it's what I care about, and I also don't want to say it measures nothing.
- Octopus: obviously yes. Except two thirds of the neurons are out in the arms, and a severed arm will apparently keep recoiling and grabbing for a while. I don't think each arm is its own subject, and I don't think the subject is just the bit in the head, and I have nothing to put in the middle.
- Portuguese man o war: no clue. It hunts, it moves, it can't be cut in half and survive. One subject, or fifty tiny ones in a trench coat?
- Oyster: no. Scallops though, the ones with the little eyes all round the rim that clap their shells and swim off from a starfish — weirdly less sure.
- Sea squirt: I'd give the swimming larva a maybe and the sack cemented to the rock a no, which is embarassing since it's the same animal a week apart.
- Caterpillar: sure-ish. Moth: sure-ish. The chrysalis in between, when most of the thing has turned to soup: total blank. I think this is where anything I say is going to break.
- My friend's baby, born without most of a cortex, who settles when she's held and grimaces at needles: yes. Not up for debate for me.
- A chatbot that seemed genuinely cross with me at 2am: no. Also no for someone under propofol, and yes for the same someone dreaming in REM, even though from the outside those two are the same lump.

Closest thing I have to a principle is what I've been calling the letdown test: there's something it's like to be X if X's situation can go in a way that lands on X as a letdown — X has expectations that can be busted, and the busting is bad for X rather than merely different. That gets me the cuttlefish, the bee, the lobster and my friend's baby, and it dumps the oyster. But the chatbot can be disappointed about things too, at least in every sense I can read off its behaviour, and the pupa has no expectations at all, and I don't want to say the moth's own pupal fortnight was nothing.

So — is there a principle, or a small handful of competing ones, that gets most of this? I want each clause stated precisely and matched to the case that forces it, not a vibe. Then tell me what it says about cases I didn't list: a chicken embryo halfway through incubation that's started jerking around, a clump of human neurons kept alive in a dish, and one case you pick that you think is the most damaging for whatever you come up with. And be straight about which of my verdicts it can't save. If it can't save all of them, tell me which one to give up first and why that one's the cheapest to lose.

Last thing. I suspect I've been sliding back and forth between a rule about how I can tell and a rule about what makes it so, and that half the mess above is that slide. Say which of the two you're offering, and whether the the other one comes out different.
