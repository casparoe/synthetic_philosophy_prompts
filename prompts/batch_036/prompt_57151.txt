Our working group is drafting ethical guidance for clinicians who will predictably find themselves unable to discharge every obligation they are under: beds and ventilators that run out, a duty of care owed to more patients than one pair of hands can serve, commitments made to families that a change of shift quietly breaks. Before we select a normative framework to build that guidance on, we need to know what we are entitled to demand of one. Please write the specification: the requirements that any adequate account of moral conflict must satisfy, with the case or consideration motivating each, stated so that we could actually determine whether a proposed framework meets it rather than merely finding it congenial.

The list should cover at least the following, and you should add what I have missed.

1. **Phenomena to be explained, not merely accommodated.** Agents who violate an obligation they could not have kept typically take themselves to have done something wrong, not merely something regrettable; apology, compensation, and acts of reparation seem apt afterward even where no fault attaches; and the distress that clinicians report survives being told that their choice was, on reflection, the correct one. A theory that dismisses all of this as confusion should be marked as paying a cost, and we should know how large.

2. **Distinctions that must not be collapsed.** Conflicts among obligations versus conflicts among reasons, values, or desires; pro tanto verdicts versus all-things-considered verdicts, and at which of those levels the theory locates the conflict; incompatibility within what a single agent can do at a single time versus incompatibility only across time, together with an account of which time-slice the ability condition is assessed over; conflicts an agent brought on herself by earlier wrongdoing versus conflicts manufactured by someone else's wrongdoing versus conflicts produced by brute contingency, with an explicit statement of whether the theory's verdict differs across the three; and tragedy, where every option is permissible and all are bad, versus dilemma proper, versus hard choice among options that are incomparable rather than conflicted.

3. **The fate of the unfulfilled obligation.** The theory must say whether it persists as an undischargable debt, converts into a duty of compensation, transfers to the institution that generated the constraint, or simply lapses. Whatever the answer, it must not leave the agent with a residual duty she cannot perform, on pain of reproducing the original conflict one level up; please treat that regress as a requirement in its own right if you think it bites.

4. **Formal consistency.** Under the usual assumptions, obligations agglomerate under conjunction and obligation entails permission. An account that posits two obligations an agent cannot jointly discharge therefore owes us either the rejection of agglomeration, the rejection of the link between obligation and ability, or an explanation of why the appearance of conflict misleads. For each available escape, say what it costs and whether the cost falls on the theory or on ordinary moral thought.

5. **Distribution across agents.** Most of our cases involve no single person who can be told to do both, because the binding constraint is systemic. The account must say whose obligation it is when system-level scarcity generates the conflict, whether obligations can be distributed over an institution in such a way that no individual falls under any of them, and how any residue is allocated.

6. **Action-guidance and publicity.** The theory must issue directives an agent can follow, which rules out any verdict that amounts to doing both; and since our guidance will be published and read by the people it binds, we should know whether a theory's correct verdicts can survive being told to the agent, or whether it depends on their being kept from her.

7. **Non-ubiquity.** It must leave the overwhelming majority of ordinary cases non-dilemmatic. A framework on which every hard choice turns out to be a genuine conflict of obligations is useless to us, whatever its other merits.

For each requirement, state whether it is a genuine constraint on adequacy or a substantive moral thesis that one camp has smuggled into the specification; my suspicion is that several of the demands currently fashionable are of the second kind, and I would rather be told which. Identify any subset that cannot be jointly satisfied and say what has to go — where the impossibility is itself the interesting result, present it as such rather than papering over it. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most work in eliminating candidate theories, since that is the one we intend to test first.

Close with no more than a paragraph each on how the two broad strategies fare against the list: dissolving apparent conflict into outweighed pro tanto duties, and accepting genuine conflicts of obligation at the price of the logic. Skip a full scorecard; the requirements are what we need.
