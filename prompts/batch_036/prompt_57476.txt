I help run a little evening series in our department — half reading group, half podcast, we record it and about forty people show up, mostly second-years plus a retired book club from town who are honestly the most ruthless question-askers in the room. Next month's guest is a philosopher who's spent the last several years arguing that supererogation is a fake category. She's very good and very charming, and I've now read the paper twice and I still can't pin down where the disagreement with the rest of the field actually bites — she keeps sounding like she's just redescribing everyone else's view, and I don't think she is, but I can't say why not.

Here's her position as I understand it, so the questions can be aimed at the real thing rather than at my paraphrase. She thinks the supererogatory gets manufactured by fusing two questions that come apart: whether omitting an act is morally wrong, and whether the omission is blameworthy. On demandingness she's unabashed — not giving a kidney to a stranger is wrong, not throwing yourself on the grenade is wrong, and she'll happily grant that most of us are doing wrong more or less continuously. But she holds that blame is its own practice with its own entry conditions: it's fair to hold someone to a standard only if it's reasonable to expect people generally, with our actual psychologies and material situations, to meet it. So the non-donor acts wrongly, blamelessly. Her stock move when you press the grenade case is that "wrong but blameless" isn't a contradiction, it's just a distinction the other side finds uncomfortable. And she likes to say the quarrel with people who believe in supererogation is largely verbal, two ways of carving the same normative territory. That last bit is the one I want to make expensive for her. The room will have read Urmson's saints-and-heroes paper and Wolf on moral saints, and one of the second-years won't shut up about Scheffler's agent-centered prerogative, so hooking onto those is fine — but I don't want to quiz her on the literature, I want questions about her view.

So: draft me nine to eleven questions, with at least one on each of these — the grenade case and what "wrong" is even doing once blame is off the table; whether guilt is fitting for the non-donor and whether the person who needed the kidney may resent her; whether her fairness constraint on blame is just the agent-centered prerogative wearing a different hat, and if she says no, making her name a single concrete case where her view and the supererogationist's come out with different practical upshots; Kant's wide or imperfect duties as a home for latitude that already exists, and how she stops latitude from sliding into anything-goes; what becomes of praise if the donor merely did her duty; the mirror-image cases — needlessly petty, unkind-but-not-forbidden behaviour — and whether she'll accept the symmetric category of right-but-blameworthy; whether wrongness licenses legal compulsion, Good Samaritan statutes and the like, or whether she has a third layer of fairness constraints for the state too; what makes the omission wrong at all if she isn't a consequentialist, and if she is, why the rest of the interview shouldn't just be about consequentialism; and the drift over time, where things that used to be listed as saintly — teaching your own children to read, pulling a stranger out of shallow water, giving away a big chunk of your income — now get described as basic duties, so is that her view winning or is it evidence the category tracks something real that moves. End with a question about what the strongest thing a supererogationist can say against her is, and what would actually get her to give the view up.

Two more things. I want one of the questions phrased as though I'm agreeing with her and handing her a compliment, but built so that accepting the compliment concedes the point — she has this habit of taking an objection and turning it into praise for her own framework, and I want at least one question she can't do that to. And format it for someone reading off a page live: number them, and under each one give a single line for the reply she's most likely to give followed by the follow-up keyed to that reply, not to some other reply. Each question needs to be sayable out loud in about twenty seconds, with any term of art glossed inside the question itself since half the room is undergrads. Star two of them as droppable if we're running long. No warm-up questions, nothing about what drew her to this stuff, and nothing she could answer by summarising the paper we already circulated. Also — and I know this sounds like a weird thing to ask — try to make sure at least one of the questions is one where I'd be unhappy with the answer she gives, because if every question has a comfortable reply then I've written fluff.
