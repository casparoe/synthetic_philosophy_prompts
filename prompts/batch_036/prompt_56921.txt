I am a high school junior. My uncle, who runs a small bookkeeping firm, offers me an even-money bet every Sunday on a coin he flips from a jar on his desk. After two years of keeping a record, I am fairly confident it comes up heads 55 percent of the time, so I have a small but genuine edge. When I asked a friend how much of my savings to stake, he handed me the following argument, which he says settles the question and also shows that talk of "risk tolerance" is confused. The conclusion disturbs me and I suspect a mistake, but I cannot locate it.

(1) A decision rule is rational only if applying it repeatedly across a long life maximizes the long-run growth rate of your wealth.
(2) For any bet at fixed odds with a positive edge, offered again and again, the growth rate is maximized by staking the Kelly fraction of your current bankroll — 10 percent in my Sunday case.
(3) So I should stake 10 percent every Sunday.
(4) Neither (1) nor (2) says anything about how I feel about risk; the 10 percent is pure compounding arithmetic, not a preference.
(5) Since (1) governs every bet I face and (2) governs every bet with a positive edge, the same arithmetic fixes the right stake for one-shot bets as well, such as whether to put my college savings into a venture my mentor rates at 55 percent to double my stake and 45 percent to lose it.
(6) Hence for each bet there is exactly one rational stake, and someone who stakes less is not being cautious but accepting slower growth for no reason at all.
(7) Yet my aunt staked well under that fraction on a comparable one-shot bet decades ago and retired comfortably, and people who bet for a living are commonly said to prefer half Kelly because it surrenders only a quarter of the growth while cutting the swings roughly in half.
(8) Therefore (1) is false: long-run growth rate cannot be the standard of rationality.

Tell me which premise or step you reject and why, explain what made that step look acceptable when I first read it, and then price the alternatives — what I would have to give up elsewhere to keep the steps I would rather not lose. I should say that I would most like to hold on to (2) and (3), since the arithmetic seems to check out when I run the numbers myself. Please state your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question. Write in American English.
