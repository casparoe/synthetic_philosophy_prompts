I report on machine-learning research for a general-interest outlet, and for the past year I have been covering systems that try to work out what people want by watching what they do: a car that infers your intentions from how you edge toward a merge, a service that infers your tastes from what you click, a lab assistant that infers the task you had in mind from the way you handle it. Interviewing the people who build these systems, I keep encountering the same maneuver, and I have never found a term for it.

Here is the setup as I understand it. When the goal a system infers for a person looks wrong, the builder has several places to put the discrepancy. The person can be read as wanting something other than what was inferred; as wanting the right thing but being mistaken about what the action would bring about; as wanting the right thing and knowing it, but being shortsighted or weak-willed in the moment; or as acting under a pressure that was never really theirs. The behavior itself rarely settles which of these is correct — the same stretch of conduct can usually be reproduced under more than one of them. What I notice is that the choice among them gets made on other grounds entirely. In one conversation, a researcher told me that a person who repeatedly reached for the sugary option plainly valued something other than health, and that their model had simply been wrong about the goal. In another, describing near-identical behavior in a different product, the same group's framing was that people do value health, they merely discount the future, so the system's job is to help them against themselves. Same evidential pattern, opposite allocation, and in each case the allocation happened to support the story the team wanted to tell about its own product.

The asymmetry is the part I cannot name. Deference to behavior is switched on and off rather than held constant: when the inferred goal flatters the thing being built, the behavior is treated as authoritative evidence of what people want; when the inferred goal is embarrassing, the same kind of behavior is treated as corrupted by confusion, haste, or design, and the real preference is supplied from somewhere else. Neither move is foolish in isolation — people genuinely are sometimes confused about what will satisfy them, and choices genuinely are sometimes evidence of what someone values — which is exactly what makes the pattern hard to pin down and easy to miss in a press release. It also runs the other way around outside the lab: a company that has shaped which options appear on a screen will insist the resulting clicks are pure expression of taste, while an outside analyst will insist the same clicks are mostly residue of a bad menu. Underneath all of it sits a circularity, since the people whose behavior is being interpreted are busy adapting to the interpretation, so the confirming data can be partly manufactured.

So: does this have a name? I want the carving more than the label. I am aware that there are established ideas in the neighborhood — the old dispute over whether picking something is evidence of wanting it; the general point that what someone wants cannot be separated from what they believe if all you have is their actions; work on wants that take shape under pressure or that people come to hold because the alternatives were quietly closed off; and a term from the recommender-systems world for loops in which a system's own output generates the data that appears to confirm it. Tell me which of these, or which others, comes closest, and state precisely where each falls short of what I have described. In particular, I want to know whether any existing concept captures the two-sidedness: the selective, direction-dependent appeal to behavior as evidence, together with the fact that the discretion to redistribute the discrepancy among goal, belief, and self-control is precisely the place where a builder's own values enter the system without ever showing up among its stated assumptions. If nothing captures that, then coin it — give me a name and a definition tight enough to be usable in an argument, two or three boundary cases that resemble it but are not instances of it, with the reasons, and a test for distinguishing a genuine case from an ordinary and defensible revision of a model in light of new evidence.
