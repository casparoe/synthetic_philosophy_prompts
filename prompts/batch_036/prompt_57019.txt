I write distributed systems for a living and have no philosophy training, so please treat me as someone who wants to be corrected rather than flattered. I'm giving a talk to a small reading group at work on Christopher Nolan's The Prestige, and I want to build it around Parfit's teletransporter. I'd like a critical reading, not a plot summary or a survey.

First, my gloss on Parfit, which I expect is sloppy. As I understand Reasons and Persons, he asks you to imagine a scanner that destroys your brain and body while transmitting enough information for an exact duplicate to be built elsewhere, and then argues that what matters in survival is not identity but a relation of psychological connectedness and continuity holding in virtue of the right kind of cause. In branching cases, where more than one continuer results, he thinks identity fails while what matters is preserved, and possibly preserved twice over. Please tell me where that misstates him before you build anything on top of it, and supply the proper name for the relation, which I've forgotten.

The film hands Angier a machine that duplicates rather than transports. He tests it on objects and an animal, finds that the copies pile up, and then builds an act out of it: a duplicate appears at the back of the house while the original drops through a trapdoor into a sealed water tank and drowns, every night of the run. The warehouse at the end is full of those tanks. His rival Borden achieves the same illusion with no machine at all, by being one of two identical twins who share a single public identity, each giving up half of his own life to sustain it.

What I want from the reading:

1. Does the film commit itself to the view that identity is what matters? Angier's stated dread — performing without knowing whether he will be the man in the balcony or the man in the tank — seems to require that being the original is what matters, since on Parfit's account both continuers have everything that matters. Is that dread a philosophical claim the film endorses, or a character's confusion the film is careful not to ratify?

2. A counting question. Does Angier die once, at the first performance, with everything afterward being a succession of heirs? Or does he die every night? Which answer does the film's structure support, and does it matter to Parfit's analysis that the branching recurs nightly instead of happening once?

3. My own principle, which I can't yet defend. I keep wanting to say that what matters is that the obligations get discharged: the run of shows continues, the audience and the theater are not let down, the daughter is provided for. Whether the person who made the promise is numerically identical to the one who keeps it strikes me as bookkeeping. My colleagues and I apply something like this to deployments — what matters is that the state persists and that nothing outside the process can detect the handover. Is this a distinct position, Relation R in disguise, or precisely the view Parfit thinks the teletransporter refutes? And does the film reward it, given that Borden's shared life looks like a triumph of continuity-of-commitment, or does it punish it?

4. The bird-cage illusion, in which the cage collapses and the bird is crushed and a live one produced, works only if nobody asks about the first bird. Does the film use that as a figure for what an audience will accept — and if so, is there an analogue in Parfit's own method, where the reader's willingness to accept his verdict about the scanner case is doing comparable work?

5. Where the film resists the reading. The horror is bodily: rows of drowned bodies, not interrupted psychologies. And the twins are not psychologically continuous with one another, so the relation Parfit cares about may not describe Borden's arrangement at all. Tell me honestly whether the twin case is a counterexample to my framing or simply a different kind of case that my framing should never have been applied to.

6. What the film adds that argument alone does not. My guess is the first-person perspective: Parfit analyzes the teletransporter from outside, while the film supplies what it is like to face branching from the inside, and attaches a price to it that his version never carries. Sharpen or refute that.

Finally, if you think the spine of the talk is wrong — that the film is really about obsession, sacrifice, or the ethics of art, and that I am forcing it into a personal-identity frame — say so plainly and tell me what I should argue instead. Point me to specific scenes for each claim. No citations, no bibliography, no verbatim quotation: paraphrase both the film and Parfit, and flag anything you're unsure of.
