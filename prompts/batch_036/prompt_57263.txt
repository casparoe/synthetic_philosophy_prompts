Third-year political theory PhD student, and my DGS has this tic where he ends any proposal with 'if it ain't broke, don't fix it.' Last week he used it to squash a student push to kill the oral qualifying exam — we'd argued it doesnt predict anything downstream and just burns people out — and he said we were naive rationalists, that this is Chesterton's fence, go find out why the exam exists before you touch it. Which is wierd, because we *had* offered a story about why it exists (faculty like watching people perform under pressure, it sorts students into subfields, it's a hazing ritual everyone's now attached to). He treated that as beside the point.

I'm drafting something on conservative epistemology and I want to know how much weight that one sentence can actually bear. Can you lay out the menu of precise claims it might be making, from the near-tautological to the genuinely radical, with a verdict on each — trivially true, defensible, false — plus whether it would license what he did? The candidates I can half-see: something about the reformer's ignorance; something about where the burden of proof sits; an asymmetry between the cost of wrongly removing and the cost of wrongly keeping; a selection-style inference from 'this practice has survived' to 'this practice does something'; and a stronger Burkean thing about the reason not being articulable by anyone at all.

Specifically I want you to deal with:

- whether 'don't fix it' and 'don't remove it until you know why it was put up' are even the same principle — one is about how the thing is performing now, the other about how it originated, and those come apart: a fence built to keep cattle in can end up load-bearing for reasons nobody at installation could foresee, and a practice whose original purpose is long dead can look perfectly unbroken from the outside
- what 'know why' has to amount to: knowing that there is a reason, knowing what it is, knowing whether it still applies, or knowing the counterfactual about what happens without it — those are wildly different bars, and which one he has in mind decides whether we already cleared it
- 'broke' for whom and on what metric, since the exam seems to work fine from the faculty side and badly from ours, and the slogan hides that aggregation step
- whether any reading of it is really Burkean, given I thought his point was that a polity which can't change can't conserve itself

Then rank the readings by how much work they can do in an argument like his, and flag the ones that collapse into general paralysis — i.e. that would equally have forbidden anyone from setting the exam up in the first place.
