Right — I retired four years ago after thirty-odd years in comparative neuroanatomy (mostly fishes and birds, a fair bit of museum work), and I've fallen into philosophy rather hard since. A reading group at the university has asked everyone to write down their actual positions so the others can attack them, instead of writing an essay. So here's mine, on whether brains — and how big they are — have any bearing on moral standing. I'd like a proper audit of whether the lot hangs together.

1. Absolute brain size means nothing morally and very little scientifically. Most of what makes a brain big is that there's more body to service. A sperm whale's brain is roughly six times mine and I don't think it's six times anything that matters.

2. The encephalisation quotient is worse; I regard it as basically dead. The allometric exponent underneath it is contested (two-thirds versus three-quarters, and it shifts with the sample), the baseline is whatever taxa you happened to measure, and the whole device treats a big body as a kind of cognitive debt the brain must pay off. I don't want it doing any work in ethics at all.

3. What I do think tracks morally relevant capacity, on the best comparative evidence we have, is neuron numbers — in the pallium/cortex specifically — rather than volume or mass. This is why I like the counting work. Birds have tiny brains and do things we used to reserve for apes, and it turns out their pallia pack far more neurons per gram than a monkey's cortex. Meanwhile the elephant has a colossal total count but the overwhelming majority of it is cerebellar, running a trunk, and its cortical count is much less grand. The counting also keeps handing out embarrassments: one study found a whale — a pilot whale — with more neocortical neurons than any mammal anyone had looked at, us included. I do not think that makes it the cleverest thing in the sea.

4. Related to that: I'm a mosaic-evolution person — Barton and Harvey, not Finlay and Darlington. Structures evolve seperately under selection, so any single lumped number hides exactly the bit you care about. I want region by region, not one scalar.

5. I don't trust behavioural tests to rank species. They're confounded by motivation, sensory ecology, domestication history, and by the fact that we design them around primate problems. So I can't just go with observed performance — which is part of what pushes me towards anatomy in the first place.

6. On the ethics side: moral standing isn't a threshold thing, it comes in degrees, and the degree tracks how much of a subjective life an animal has — how much it can suffer, and how much there is to loose for it. Not cleverness as such. Being smart isn't what makes you matter.

7. And the affective machinery is old and deep — brainstem, midbrain, hypothalamus, the amygdala-ish circuits — and broadly shared across vertebrates and probably well beyond them. So the capacity I say matters in (6) is distributed far more widely than the capacity I rank animals by in (3).

8. I lean towards octopuses having some standing, which is awkward for a pallium-based metric since they haven't got one, and most of their neurons are out in the arms. I'd sooner count neurons in odd places than measure a lump of tissue, but I concede the metric doesn't extend there naturally.

9. Practically: whatever we end up with has to be usable by a committee. Legislation, farming, lab animal schedules — somebody must be able to put species in an order without a decade of fieldwork. So it needs to be measurable in a museum or a scanner, not inferred from twenty years of ethology.

10. And one case verdict so you can check me against myself: I think a chicken and a crow should get much the same legal protection. I wouldn't sign up to a scheme that gave corvids a higher welfare status than poultry.

So — where's the trouble? I genuinely don't know. I've been sat with this list for a month and it feels fine to me, which is presumably a bad sign. I'm deliberately not telling you which of them I'd give up, because I don't know that either, and I'd rather you weren't steered into the easy target.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture. Which of these pull against each other, ranked by how bad it is — fatal, merely expensive, or just embarrassing? For each real tension, say what has to give, what the cheapest revision is, and what that revision then costs me further down the list. Some of these will look like contradictions and won't be; I'd like those named and dismissed explicitly rather than padded into the tally. And if one single change would clear up most of the mess at once, tell me which.

Three house rules. Don't give me a reading list or citations — I'm not chasing anything down, and half of it I've read already. If you think I've got a fact wrong above, correct me in passing rather than quietly building on my error. And British English throughout, please, spellings and idiom both.
