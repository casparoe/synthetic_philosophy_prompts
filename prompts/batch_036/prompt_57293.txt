I serve on the eleven-member board of a community land trust, and two seats come up this spring. For the past decade those seats have gone to whichever members stood for election, and the same four people have held them continuously; the rest of the board mostly defers to them. One member has proposed that we instead draw the two seats by lottery from our 400-person membership roll. Her argument is that an election doesn't select for good judgment but for wanting to win, and the members who most want to win are the ones who have already been winning for years.

Three responses came up at the meeting. (1) Our bylaws can't compel anyone to serve, so a drawn member may decline and we draw again — and someone pointed out that those who accept will overwhelmingly be the members with free time, meaning we'd land on the same retired professionals we have now, merely by a different route. (2) An elected member who performs badly can be turned out at the next election, whereas a drawn member answers to no one and can't be recalled, so nothing disciplines them. (3) A third member said the lottery is the only method that treats every member's claim to a seat as equal, and that objection (1) proves too much, since the retirees are also the ones who keep winning elections.

I've been asked to write the recommendation. Is the equal-claim point genuinely an argument for drawing lots here, and can the decline-and-redraw problem be fixed without giving up the lottery altogether?
