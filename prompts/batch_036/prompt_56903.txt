So my grandmother died in March and it has turned into a whole thing. She was a sculptor — mostly small bronzes — but the piece the family actually cared about was the one she never fired and never cast: a life-size clay bust of my grandfather, sitting on a board in her damp basement studio for like forty-five years. Unfired clay. It cracked constantly. Every few years she'd bring a conservator in, or do it herself, injecting consolidant, rebuilding a shoulder, re-wetting and reworking the surface. There's a binder — she called it the ledger — where every intervention is logged, some of them down to the gram of material added and removed, with dates and humidity readings.

The will splits things in a way that turned out to be a landmine. To me: the bust of her late husband, as it stands in the studio. To my aunt: all the clay remaining in the studio, together with its offcuts, spoil and sweepings. My aunt's argument is that the ledger shows the object currently on the board is made almost entirely of material added after 1994, so the bust I was left has stopped existing and she inherited everything. My argument is... I dont know what my argument is, honestly, which is part of why I want this.

Write a short story, somewhere between 1400 and 1800 words, built around that situation. A few constraints I actually care about:

- No one in it gives a philosophy lecture, and no one says the phrases "Ship of Theseus," "material constitution," "numerical identity" or "ontologically." The metaphysics gets carried entirely by objects, paperwork, and people bickering about mundane stuff — moisture meters, dust sheets, the quote for a plinth, who pays for the dehumidifier.
- Include at least one scene where two characters agree on every single physical fact — same ledger, same numbers, same object in front of them — and still flatly contradict each other about what's in the room.
- The bust has to be taken apart and put back together at one point, and at another point essentially all of its surface matter has to be replaced. Treat those as genuinely different situations with different stakes, not the same puzzle recycled.
- My aunt wants a lock of my grandfather's ashes kneaded into the base before the probate hearing, and she's already done it. Use that.
- Don't resolve it. But the final paragraph should leave the reader feeling that one of the two claims is worse than the other, not merely different from it.

Then, after the story, a separate short note in your own voice: how confident are you that you've represented the real options in this debate fairly rather than a folk version of them? And single out the one thing you're least sure of — the place you think you've taken a liberty, leaned on a reading that's contested, or smoothed over a distinction that actually does the work. If you think the ledger framing quietly rigs the story toward one answer, say that too.
