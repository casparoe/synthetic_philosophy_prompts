I'm opening the last session of my philosophy-of-biology seminar — the one on heritability — with a poem instead of a definition, because I want the shape to do the teaching rather than the exposition. Could you write one, twenty to twenty-eight lines, loose metre, no fixed rhyme?

The conceit: two stanzas of equal length, near-mirror images of each other. The same genetically varied batch of seed, sown into two plots — one tended so uniformly that nearly all the variation left among the plants is variation between the seeds, one sown into patchy ground where the soil does most of the talking. Each stanza should end on it's plot's heritability figure, an actual number, and the two numbers should be far apart, so that the reader watches the same seeds pick up two different scores with nothing about them changed. Then a short coda, three or four lines, turning on what those figures can't see: the gap between the two plots themselves, one lush and one stunted, which the within-plot numbers say nothing whatever about.

Please use H² rather than h² — the seed story gets you total genetic variance, not the additive component, and I'd like the poem honest about that. And don't reach for phenylketonuria or twins-reared-apart; my students have had both served up twice already. If you can slip in, without sermonising, that a figure near one is perfectly compatible with abolishing the trait outright by changing the environment, I'd be delighted, but dont cram.

American English throughout, please.

Afterwards, a short note on where you've taken liberties: would your two plots really yield the numbers you give them, and is there anything a quantitative geneticist would wince at? And more importantly — if you think the conceit itself is a bad one, if mirrored plots can't help suggesting that each seed carries its own fixed share of the outcome, which is precisely the confusion I'm trying to break, say so plainly rather than writing me something agreeable and wrong. I'd rather hear it now than in week nine.
