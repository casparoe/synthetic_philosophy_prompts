A friend of mine adjudicates hard cases strictly by Augustine's two treatises on lying, and I want to know where her reading lands on a case she has not yet met. Her verdicts so far: the Egyptian midwives who misreported to Pharaoh, and Rahab with the king's men — sinful, but the mildest sort of lie, and what God rewarded in them was their mercy rather than their deceit; a storyteller who invents an episode for the amusement of listeners who plainly do not take it as an assertion — not a lie at all, since no one is meant to be misled; a man who feigns agreement with the Priscillianists in order to get inside the sect and expose it — flatly forbidden, and not excused by the good it would accomplish; a lie that destroys an innocent person's reputation while gaining nothing for anyone — worse than any of the protective lies above.

The new case: a priest in a country where the police are hunting a fugitive is asked point-blank whether he has heard that man's confession. He has, and he says he has not — thereby shielding both the man's life and the secrecy of the confessional.

Which principle is she running: that what makes speech a lie is the will to have the hearer take it falsely, with gravity then set by the balance of harm and benefit; that every deliberate false assertion is a sin whose grade is fixed by a ranking in which lies touching sacred things sit near the top; that the decisive question is which good is being shielded, so that spiritual goods may license what bodily goods never can; or something else that fits her verdicts just as well? Give your best guess at her ruling, with the reasons for preferring that reading; list the rival readings her four precedents leave open, including any that her verdict on the feigned heretic appears to refute and would need refinement to survive; and name the single further case that would most sharply separate the surviving candidates.
