I write a monthly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I've been reporting on cultural heritage policy — not the cathedrals-and-old-buildings kind, but the "living heritage" kind, where governments keep registers of practices instead of monuments. Before I file anything I want to get straight on what this whole apparatus actually assumes, philosophically. So: play the part of a philosopher of culture who has also sat through heritage-committee meetings, think carefully, and start with some brainstorming — list the candidate assumptions the practice might rest on before you commit to which ones it really needs.

Here's the practice, roughly as I found it across several countries (it's the shape the UNESCO-style convention on intangible heritage encourages, and a lot of national registers copy it). A ministry keeps a list of intangible heritage items: a craft, a festival, a style of singing, a foodway, a healing rite. To get an item listed, the applicants have to show something like this — that it's been handed down across generations, that it's still alive and practiced today, that some community or group recognises it as theirs and as part of what makes them who they are, that it's in danger of disappearing, and that there's a safeguarding plan. Listing brings funding, prestige, school curriculum slots, sometimes tourism.

And here's the bit I keep tripping over in interviews. Once something is listed, the group tends to write down rules that used to be improvised, settle on a canonical version, accredit teachers, put the festival on a fixed date with a program, and start calling variants "not authentic" that nobody would have blinked at fifteen years ago. The application form also forces them to name the bearers, so groups whose membership was fuzzy, optional, and overlapping suddenly need a boundary and a spokesperson. Yet the register's own publicity describes all this as *protecting* the tradition from change, and the officials I talked to genuinely see standardisation as success, not damage. So a machine built to stop traditions drifting seems to make them drift faster, in one particular direction, and everyone inside it is telling the truth about their intentions.

What I want from you:

1. Reconstruct the theory of tradition this form presupposes. Does it have to assume that a tradition is a persisting thing with an identity, such that there's a fact of the matter about which changes conserve it and which destroy it? That authenticity is a matter of genealogy — unbroken transmission — rather than of function, or meaning, or just being what people actually do? That practices belong to communities the way a language or a landscape does, so that a group can own, speak for, and license one?

2. Which rival pictures does the paperwork quietly rule out? In particular, the view that a great many "ancient" customs are recent inventions wearing old clothes, and the view that a national or ethnic culture is a unity that exists mainly because people are repeatedly told it does. If those views are roughly right, is the register merely philosophically confused, or is it actively manufacturing the thing it claims to be discovering — and would its defenders have any answer?

3. Tell me where the practice is underdetermined. I care a lot about the difference between what the listing procedure strictly requires and what merely sits comfortably with it. Could a thoroughgoing constructionist sign the same forms for purely instrumental reasons and lose nothing? Is there any reading of "safeguarding" on which freezing a canonical version is defensible, i.e. where preservation-by-standardisation isn't a contradiction?

4. Finally, what would have to change — the criteria, the form, the funding rule, who gets to sign — if we swapped the presupposed theory for one where traditions are ongoing processes rather than objects, or one where there is no bearer community in the position to represent anybody? Give me at least one concrete edit to the machinery that follows from the swap, so I can tell whether the philosophy has any grip on the practice.

One more thing: I'm going to be quoting your reasoning in spirit, so tell me how confident you are in the main claims, and single out the point you're least sure of — the place you'd most want a specialist to check. I'd rather know where you're guessing than get a smooth answer. And if any of what I described above is not how these registers actually work, say so before you build on it; my summary is from reporting, not from policy documents, and its probably lossy somewhere.
