I am a third-year doctoral student in political science working on religion and politics, and next week I lead a fifty-minute discussion section attached to a first-year undergraduate seminar. The students are nearly all American, have read the Establishment Clause and a page or two of Supreme Court excerpts, and have never had to think about how other democracies organize their relations with religious bodies. My assigned topic is the comparative structure of church–state arrangements. The single idea I want them to leave with is that separation is not one variable that different countries possess in different degrees, but a bundle of distinct state functions — collecting revenue for religious bodies, recognizing particular bodies in public law, registering individual membership, participating in the appointment of personnel, and enforcing or deferring to a body's internal jurisdiction — which countries combine in patterns that no left-to-right ordering can capture.

Below is the material I have assembled and intend to use. Please treat it as given: do not verify it, expand it, or correct it, and build the plan entirely on it.

England: the Church of England is established; up to twenty-six of its bishops sit in the House of Lords; the sovereign formally appoints bishops on ministerial advice. Yet there is no church tax — the church runs on endowment income, donations, and fees. Establishment also does not travel within the United Kingdom: the church in Ireland was disestablished by statute in the nineteenth century, the church in Wales in the twentieth, and Scotland's national church is Presbyterian rather than episcopal.

Germany: no established church, but the state collects a church tax on behalf of recognized religious bodies, set at roughly eight or nine percent of a member's assessed income tax, gathered by the ordinary revenue authorities, which retain a collection fee. Employers withhold it at source because membership is recorded in a federal tax database. The only way to stop paying is to file a formal declaration of exit with a state office. In 2012 the Federal Administrative Court considered the declaration of a retired professor of canon law who added the qualification that he was leaving only the church in its capacity as a public-law corporation while remaining a Catholic in matters of faith; the court held the qualification legally inoperative, because state law cannot register a split membership.

France: the 1905 statute ends state recognition and funding of religion. Yet in Alsace-Moselle, which was under German rule in 1905, a pre-1905 regime still operates: Catholic, Lutheran, Reformed, and Jewish clergy are on the state payroll, Catholic bishops are named by the President of the Republic on papal proposal, the heads of the Protestant churches are appointed by the prime minister after election by their own bodies, and religious instruction is offered in public schools with a parental opt-out.

Against that material, plan the section for me; I do not want the idea explained to me. I want the following, with the fifty minutes allocated in rough blocks:

1. The order of introduction, with the dependency stated at each step — what cannot be grasped until something else is in place. In particular, tell me when the function-grid should appear: before the three profiles, as an empty schema the cases fill in, or afterward, as a generalization the students assemble themselves. Also tell me which profile comes first and why.

2. The one case that should carry the session, chosen among the German exit ruling, the Alsatian state payroll, and the bishops in the Lords. Say what your chosen case must contain to do the work (an individual with a motive? a clerk? a bishop? a sum of money?) and what must be stripped out of it so that it does not consume the hour. If a two-case structure is genuinely better, say so and specify the division of labor.

3. The misconceptions. I predict at least these: that a church tax means Germany has a state religion; that France must be the most separated country because it has an explicit separation statute; that establishment entails government control of clergy and therefore makes England the least free; and that membership is a matter of private belief rather than of registration. Tell me the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off, and whether any of them is better conceded or bracketed than refuted in fifty minutes.

4. Your judgment on my grid. Are five functions the right scaffold for first-years, or should it be cut down, and to what? Which function is load-bearing for the distinction I care about? I suspect it is the registration of individual membership, precisely because students cannot see record-keeping as a church–state question at all; tell me if you disagree and what should carry that weight instead.

5. A closing check that discriminates a student who has grasped the bundle from one who has memorized three country descriptions. Ideally this is a classification task on a fourth arrangement they have not seen, solvable using only the functions — if so, draft the fourth arrangement yourself.

6. What I should openly decline to cover and announce as omitted. I am thinking of American funding jurisprudence, the canon-law consequences of the German ruling, and the history of the concordat that Alsace-Moselle preserves; give me one sentence I can actually say aloud to that effect.

Please do not propose readings, a bibliography, or further sources — everything the students need should come from the material above and their own reasoning. Write the plan in American English.
