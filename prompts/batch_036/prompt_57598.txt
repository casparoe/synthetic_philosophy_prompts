I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training, and once a month I run an evening session for about fifteen colleagues — mostly backend and machine-learning engineers, none of them philosophers. I have ninety minutes, no lecture, and I'd like a hands-on activity rather than a discussion.

The distinction I want them to run into is between two ways of making a powerful system safe: building something that genuinely shares your aims, versus building something you refuse to trust and then boxing it in — sandboxes, monitors, tripwires, kill switches, verification of its outputs. My engineers talk as though these are points on a single spectrum of caution. I want them to discover that they are different projects with different failure modes, and specifically to feel this structural bind: the more thoroughly you constrain and observe a system you don't trust, the less useful work you extract from it and the less you learn about what it would do when unconstrained; the less you constrain it, the more work and the more evidence you get, but you have silently staked everything on good faith, which is precisely the assumption you declined to make. A team that opts for heavy control finishes safe, nearly useless, and with no data about the system's dispositions at all. A team that opts for light control finishes productive and unable to say why.

Mechanics I'd like you to build in, though I'm open to better ones. The checks themselves must draw on the same scarce pool of resources as the work does, so that vigilance is literally paid for out of capability. Each team must commit to its control regime before it sees any evidence about how the system will behave. The system should be a person, or a paper-and-card mechanism — no computers. And at some point teams should end up operating a system they did not build, inheriting somebody else's control choices without knowing what they were. Crucially, the session must close in a state where the participants cannot establish, even in principle from what they observed, whether the system was trustworthy or merely well behaved while watched.

Please give me the complete rules, a materials list, roles, a timeline for the ninety minutes, and a scoring scheme that needs no real money. Then a debrief that maps particular moments of the play onto features of the real trade-off, an honest list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it in a room of confident engineers who will try to game it, and — the part I care about most — a frank statement of which feature of the situation the activity cannot reproduce and which I will therefore have to supply in words afterwards.
