I've talked myself into a fairly tidy reading of Aquinas on tyrannicide and I want it stress-tested: on my view he never licenses a private person killing a ruler. Everything that looks like permission is really about one of two things — either a usurper, who lacks a valid title in the first place, or action taken by public authority, the community or a superior. So the De Regno stretch about tyrant-killers being praised and rewarded is, as I read it, Aquinas reporting what people have thought and then hedging it with the danger that removing one tyrant breeds worse ones, not endorsing the killing. And the sedition article in the Summa fits the same pattern: disturbing a tyrant's rule isn't strictly sedition, but only where the subjects don't end up worse off from the disturbance than from the tyranny.

What would actually count against this? Rank the defeaters for me — which one should make me give the reading up, and which are merely awkward and absorbable. A few I keep circling: does that harm-balancing clause quietely license killing in extremis, so the "never" is doing no work? Does the usurper route smuggle in private action, since anyone may resist someone with no title at all? What about the scriptural cases of private men killing oppressive rulers — does his handling of them dispose of the problem or just relocate it? And does reception evidence carry any weight here, given that both later Thomists and their enemies read him as a tyrannicide theorist?

Finally, is my reading insulated to the point where no passage could falsify it, and if it is, is that a real defect for an interpretive claim or just how these readings normally work? Don't hand me a menu — commit to which single consideration you'd bet on breaking the view, and say whether you think the view survives it.
