I am in the final year of a philosophy degree and currently taking a module on moral thought in eighteenth-century France. Last week's seminar was built around Émilie du Châtelet's Discourse on Happiness — the private, semi-autobiographical text she wrote in the mid-1740s and which appeared in print only in 1779, after her death — and afterwards two people on the module, Yusuf and Clara, carried the argument into the café for the better part of an hour. I took notes as they went, because neither conceded anything and I could not work out which of them was making the better case. Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it.

1. Yusuf began from what he called the central tension of the text: du Châtelet lists among the conditions of happiness both that one should have got rid of one's prejudices and that one should remain susceptible to illusions, and she later urges her reader to preserve their illusions carefully. For him the two collapse into one another. An illusion, he said, is simply a prejudice that happens to please; and since she recommends keeping illusions on the grounds of the agreeable feelings they produce, the standard she applies to belief is hedonic throughout. Her anti-prejudice rule is therefore not a rule about truth at all but a rule about which falsehoods pay — which makes her a fictionalist about belief and a consistent, if unusual, hedonist about happiness.

2. Clara said this cannot be right, because du Châtelet does not treat prejudice and illusion as the same sort of thing. Prejudices, on her account, are what make us vicious, and she holds that we cannot be both vicious and happy; illusions by contrast add colour to experience — she gives the case of being carried away at a comedy — without determining what we do. So the distinction is not truth against falsehood, but belief that governs conduct against imagination that enriches feeling. On this reading du Châtelet is a selective realist: belief proper is answerable to truth, and imagination is licensed to embellish.

3. Yusuf replied that love destroys the division of labour. Du Châtelet counts love among the passions that have filled her own life, treats it as suffused with illusion, and plainly thinks it governs conduct — she warns against letting the heart love someone who will bring it misery. So illusion does determine action, and Clara's allocation of domains has no textual basis.

4. Clara conceded the love case but said it cuts against Yusuf too: du Châtelet regulates that passion by its consequences, which is just what someone managing a whole life would do, and it shows the illusion doctrine is subordinate to a broader programme rather than a theory of belief. She added that Yusuf cannot explain the prominence of study in the text. Study is recommended as a resource that cannot be exhausted or taken away, to be relied on above all when youth and its amusements have gone — a point she presses, Clara said, with particular urgency for women, who are otherwise left with nothing once charm fades. The object of study is truth; if truth were merely a useful fiction, the recommendation to spend a life in it would be incoherent.

5. Yusuf answered that study is not privileged by its object but by its durability. Du Châtelet sets gambling, love and study side by side as the passions of her life, and she says of gambling that it is a fortunate passion to have provided one can moderate it and keep it in reserve for the years when one will need such a resource — that is, for old age. Study is recommended on the same terms: it occupies the mind agreeably and survives the loss of everything else. So it is one agreeable activity among others, not evidence that truth has non-instrumental value.

6. Clara's last move was that uniform hedonism cannot accommodate the way du Châtelet ties happiness to virtue and to the inner satisfaction and self-esteem virtue brings, nor the fact that she separates prejudice from mere social convention, which she thinks retains something of truth and decency. Were she measuring beliefs by pleasure alone, she would have no reason to insist that the vicious cannot be happy, and none to distinguish prejudice from convention at all.

They asked me afterwards who had won, and I said I could not tell. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who is right at each stage, treating each of the six as a separate claim to be assessed rather than simply tallying an overall winner? I would also like to know whether the underlying disagreement is substantive or partly verbal: is 'illusion' against 'prejudice' a real distinction in du Châtelet, or two names for one thing? And is there anything important that both of them are missing? I have a suspicion that each is assuming the text must yield a single consistent theory of belief, which may be the wrong stance towards something she wrote for herself and never intended to publish — but I should like that suspicion tested rather than confirmed.
