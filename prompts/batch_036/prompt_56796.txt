Write a dialogue between two moral philosophers, Nadia and Owen, who disagree about whether everyday social conventions generate genuine moral obligations.

The case before them: in the small seaside town where both are holidaying, nobody forms a physical queue at the pharmacy counter. The local practice is that you ask who is last, note the answer, and wait wherever you please, with that person serving as your marker. A tourist who does not know this walks straight up to the counter and is served without a word. Nobody objects; the woman whose turn it would have been says nothing, though she now waits five minutes longer. The tourist will never return to the town.

Nadia holds that practices of this kind can create real duties, and that the tourist did something worse than bungle a local custom. Owen holds that conventions as such are morally inert — the only duties in play are rule-independent ones, such as not harming, not deceiving, and not breaking undertakings — and that since none of these was breached, the tourist was at worst inelegant, like wearing the wrong shoes to a wedding.

Both are sharp and neither may be made to look foolish. Owen must answer for why the tourist would rightly resent the same treatment if the roles were reversed, and for the fact that she took the benefit of a cooperative scheme she did nothing to uphold. Nadia must answer for how a duty can bind someone who never undertook anything, did not know the rule, and could not have complied without putting a question to a stranger that she had no way of anticipating. Have them press each other on the gap between doing wrong and being properly told off for it, and on whether the five lost minutes are what matters or whether the wrong lies elsewhere. Let them finish without agreement, but have each state exactly where the dispute now rests and what, if anything, could move them.
