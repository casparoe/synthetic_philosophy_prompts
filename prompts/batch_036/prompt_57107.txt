I am a novelist, and for the past year my sister — who teaches philosophy of action — has been reading my drafts to check that my groups behave the way groups actually do. Her verdicts so far: two neighbours who each plant their own half of a hedge without ever discussing it, simply assuming the other is doing the same, do not jointly plant it; a flash mob whose dancers were emailed identical instructions, and who know the others were too but meet only at the performance, does dance together; a board that resolves by its own voting procedure to sell a subsidiary does sell it, though a majority of directors opposed the sale and now disavow it; a crowd that spontaneously forms a bucket brigade does fight the fire together; a household in which the one member who stopped doing dishes was met with a pointed note on the fridge was jointly running that household.

The scene I cannot settle is this. Five siblings care for their dying mother under a rota maintained by an app they never discuss aloud: each checks the app, works the assigned shifts, and knows the others do likewise. When one sister quietly drops out, the remaining four absorb her shifts without a word to her or about her. Afterwards all five insist they were never doing anything together — each was only discharging a private duty to his or her own mother.

Predict my sister's verdict on whether the siblings cared for her jointly, and identify the rule she is implicitly running — meshing subplans and mutual responsiveness, a joint commitment that generates obligations and standing to rebuke, we-intentions distributed across individual heads, or a group agent with its own decision procedure. Say which rival rules fit her earlier verdicts about as well, which of them she would rule out first on the siblings and why, and propose the single further case I could put to her that would settle which rule she holds.

Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
