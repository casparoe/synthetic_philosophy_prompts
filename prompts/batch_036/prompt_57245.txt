I teach a second-year philosophy of mind module at a mid-sized British university, and for four years Moore's paradox has occupied twenty unremarkable minutes of it. It stopped being unremarkable in October, when I realised that the state I have been in since the spring is a first-person instance of the very form I teach in week five: I have known for months that the project my last two papers were built on is a dead end, and I have gone on writing, supervising and applying for money to continue it. I have a fifty-minute tutorial for fourteen students in a fortnight, a whiteboard, and a growing conviction that the way I have handled this has been far too fast. I also intend to open by telling them my own case, which I suspect is a mistake, and I would like that suspicion examined rather than indulged.

What I want is a plan for the fifty minutes, not an explanation of the paradox to me.

First, the order the components must go in, and why that order rather than another: what has to be secured before what. Tell me explicitly which of the following are load-bearing and which can be moved or dropped — that the conjunction is perfectly satisfiable; the difference between its being absurd to assert and its being absurd to believe; the asymmetry between the first-person and the third-person version; and the disappearance of the absurdity when the tense shifts into the past.

Second, the single example that should carry the session. Rain is what everyone reaches for and I suspect it is the wrong choice. Tell me what the carrying example must have and must lack. My own sense of the design problem is this: the example must give the speaker some standing to assert the first conjunct at all, otherwise the utterance is merely odd rather than paradoxical; yet any case in which the evidence is sitting right in front of the speaker invites the students to hear weakness of will. Either resolve that tension or tell me plainly that it cannot be resolved at this level.

Third, the misconceptions. I expect at least three: that the sentence is a contradiction in disguise, so there is no paradox to solve; that the speaker is lying, or self-deceived, or simply unwilling to admit something; and that belief is a feeling, so that the second conjunct is an honest report of the absence of the feeling and the whole thing dissolves. For each, say where in the sequence it should be headed off, which one I should let the class walk into deliberately and why, and what to do with the student who insists they have uttered the sentence themselves, sincerely, and that it was therefore perfectly all right. A related one I have met before: a student who wrote in an essay that they do not believe in free will but do believe they chose their modules, and took that to settle the matter.

Fourth, a sorting step. My students keep bringing me sentences that look like instances and mostly are not, and I want the plan to include a point at which the class sorts a set of them. Here are seven, drawn from essays and from office hours over the years: it isn't raining, but I believe it is; the bridge is safe, but she doesn't believe it is; it was raining, but I didn't believe it was; the bridge is safe, but I don't believe it is; I don't believe the bridge is safe, but it is; I know the funding isn't coming, but I don't believe it; and I don't believe in free will, but I believe I chose to take this module. Give me the sorting, the principle that does the sorting, and where in the fifty minutes it belongs — my instinct is that it cannot come at the end, but I do not know where it can come.

Fifth, one question I can put in the last five minutes whose answers will distinguish the students who have grasped the paradox from those who have merely followed me politely, together with what a good answer and a bad answer actually sound like.

Sixth, what to leave out entirely and what to say if asked. Whether the paradox is properly about assertion or about belief, the competing diagnoses of why such a belief is impossible, and the later Wittgenstein's interest in the matter all strike me as belonging on another week, but I would rather you argued that than assumed it. I am not asking for sources or further reading; my students will not read any.

One last thing, and I mean it. I shall be handing this plan to fourteen people who will trust it. Separate clearly what is settled here from what is genuinely contested among those who work on this material, and do not present a contested diagnosis as though it were the consensus view. Where my framing above reflects one side of a live dispute rather than the settled state of play — including my hunch that the assertion reading and the belief reading pull apart, and my assumption that the akrasia objection is a confusion rather than a real competitor — say so, and say which side you are taking and why.
