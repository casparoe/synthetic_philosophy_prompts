Two years in our department's free will reading group have convinced me that the fight between the Strawsonians and the responsibility skeptics can't be settled by argument, and what I want is a diagnosis of the deadlock rather than a ruling on who is right. The two sides share the architecture: reactive attitudes are internal to participatory life, the objective attitude is available toward particular agents on particular occasions but — so the Strawsonians say — not wholesale, and therefore the truth of determinism never gets a grip. The skeptics accept that picture and deny its normative force: basic desert is indefensible, suspension can be sustained far more broadly than Strawsonians allow, and blame should give way to forward-looking practices of protection, incapacitation and moral education.

They grind to a halt on identical evidence. Our resident skeptic reports feeling no resentment at all, only resolve to prevent harm, while endorsing censure, demanded apology and exclusion; the Strawsonians call this resentment in costume, which supposedly proves wholesale suspension impossible, and the skeptics call the very same conduct proof of how far the attitudes can be reshaped. My hunch is that the two camps fix the extension of 'resentment' by different criteria — phenomenology in one case, functional role in the other — but I may be wrong about that.

Is it the right diagnosis, or is something else doing the work: a suppressed empirical question about whether suspension can be held steady, a clash over what counts as evidence — first-person report versus third-party behaviour — or two distinct questions, the justification of a practice and the criticizability of a single attitude, being pursued under one name? Argue for the diagnosis you favour, and say what would count as progress on it: a distinction, a study, a reframing, or nothing at all.
