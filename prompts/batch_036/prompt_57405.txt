I cover science and research for a living, so basically my whole job is deciding which claims to check and which to just take. Lately I've been reading Wittgenstein's late notes on certainty — the ones where certain propositions aren't the sort of thing you can know or doubt at all, they're just what your doubts and your checking swing on — and I started writing down my own verdicts, and they don't line up.

Ones I'd call framework, untouchable from the inside:
- other people have minds. never comes up, never could be tested.
- the past happened, roughly as recorded — the broad one, not any particular record.
- the laws of physics will be the same tomorrow as they were yesterday.
- the number I copy out of a spreadsheet into my notebook is the number I'm looking at. I'm totally certain of this one and I can't say why.

Ones I'd call ordinary evidence territory:
- there's a laptop in front of me. I look, I know.
- water is H2O — its the model of a claim that got settled by evidence and could have come out otherwise.
- the Earth is roughly 4.5 billion years old, because dating methods genuinely bear on that number and genuinely moved it.

And now the ones that break my brain:
- "the Earth existed long before I was born" I want to call untouchable. But that looks like the same claim as the 4.5 billion years one, just blurrier. How can the blurry version be outside evidence and the sharp version inside it?
- "published results mostly replicate." For my first ten years on the job that functioned exactly like a framework proposition — I never once checked it, I checked things against it. Then it came lose, over a few years, by ordinary evidence. Same with "the literature isn't being farmed by paper mills." So if a hinge can come loose, was it ever a hinge?
- "reviewers are competent and acting in good faith." I can imagine this being overturned. I flatly cannot imagine that for other-minds. But when I'm writing, both feel the same way — load-bearing, invisible.
- "my editor's summary of what legal said is accurate." I do check this one, sometimes. But only when I already suspect something. Does suspicion have to arrive first, and if so from where?

My tentative principle is: a claim is framework for me iff any evidence you brought against it would have to be less secure than it is — I'd have to use the claim itself to weigh the evidence against it. Trouble is, that trick seems to work for the laptop too, and I just said the laptop is knowable. So the principle is already in trouble before I've started.

Give me the best you can do: one or two candidate principles, stated precisely, clause by clause, with which of my cases each clause is there to handle. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts each one forces me to abandon — if the only way to keep all of them is a baroque mess, say that and pick the simpler principle and name the casualty. Then run your winner on three I haven't listed: "climate models are broadly trustworthy," "the archive I'm working from isn't systematically forged," and "I'm not misremembering what my source told me last week." And finally, tell me what would change your mind — which argument or which case would make you drop your principle for the rival one, and where in your own reasoning you think you're most likely to be wrong. I don't want a tour of who said what, just your verdict and its soft spots.
