**Leave-one-out confirmation: a proposal about old evidence (draft, ~1 page of a longer paper)**

Glymour's problem of old evidence is normally stated as a problem about updating. If Pr(E) = 1, conditionalizing on E leaves everything exactly where it was, so there's no room in the Bayesian machinery for the fact that general relativity got a real boost from explaining the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion — a boost Einstein and his contemporaries plainly took to be a boost, even though the perihelion data was already on the books and already known to everybody.

I want to suggest the problem isn't about updating at all. It's about which credence function the confirmation measure gets to look at. Confirmation is not a two-place relation between H and E; it's a three-place relation between H, E, and a credence function, and the live question in the old-evidence case is which function is the right third relatum. The standard answer — the one Glymour's problem quietly assumes — is "the agent's current one." That's what generates the puzzle: the current function has already absorbed E, so there's nothing left for E to do.

My proposal: the third relatum should be the agent's **leave-one-out function** for E, written L_E — the function they would have had if E had never entered their evidence base. Confirmation of H by E is then Pr_{L_E}(H | E) − Pr_{L_E}(H), computed with a function that is by construction not saturated by E. On the Mercury case this gives the verdict we want: relative to L_E, GR's entailment of the observed perihelion advance is a genuine boost, and the boost is larger the less L_E expected it, so the predictivist asymmetry between novel prediction and accommodation falls out for free rather than being built in.

The obvious objection is that L_E is a fiction — a prior that never was, an idealization with no claim on the actual agent. This is where I think the account improves on the counterfactual-prior views, which as far as I can tell just stipulate the relevant function and then hope it's reasonable. L_E is supposed to be *recoverable* rather than stipulated. An agent who learned E by Jeffrey-shifting a partition containing E can run the update backwards: the shift preserves the old conditional probabilities on the partition cells, so if the agent still has the old cell probabilities on hand, they can reconstruct exactly the function they had before the shift. And in the limiting case where the agent just conditionalized outright and went to Pr(E) = 1, the same trick works — Jeffrey-shift Pr(E) back down to whatever L_E assigns to it, and you have your old function again. On this picture L_E isn't a philosopher's construction, it's a record-keeping achievement, and the difference between an agent for whom E is old and an agent for whom E is new is a difference in their records, not in the world.

Part of why this feels natural to me is that I did ML for a few years before switching. What I'm describing is basically the train/test discipline. Scoring a model on data you used to select it is leakage, and the fix is to hold that data out. L_E is a held-out credence function. The Bayesian's unease about old evidence is, I want to say, the same unease a practitioner has about reporting in-sample accuracy, and both are relieved the same way: don't score against the saturated function.
