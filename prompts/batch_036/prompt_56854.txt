I'm putting together a grad seminar on the philosophy of pain and I've decided to do something slightly masochistic: hand out my own settled views on page one so the students have a fixed target instead of chasing me around all term. Before I do that, though, I want someone to tell me whether the package is actually coherent, because I suspect it isn't and I can't locate the break myself. Here's what I'd sign my name to.

(1) Pain asymbolia is real and the reports are to be taken at face value. Those patients feel pain — location, intensity, the whole discriminative business — and the lesion takes out the aversive dimension, not the sensation. I don't think they're confabulating, or that they've merely lost the vocabulary, or that they're being brave.

(2) So unpleasantness is not essential to pain. Something can be a full-blooded pain and not be unpleasant. I'm also not a disjunctivist here: it's one natural kind, and the asymbolic case is an abnormal member of it rather than a different thing wearing the same label.

(3) What makes a pain bad *for the person having it* is exactly its unpleasantness — the felt aversiveness. Not the tissue damage it signals, not the beliefs and fears it causes, not the interruption of projects. Those can be bad too, but derivatively.

(4) Badness of this sort is a welfare-level, prudential thing. I don't want to cash out anything in terms of impersonal or agent-neutral badness, and I don't think a state can be bad for someone in a way that isn't a contribution to how well their life goes.

(5) Hence asymbolic pain isn't bad for the asymbolic, and their conduct is appropriate: they're rational in not withdrawing, not guarding, not asking for it to stop. Somebody who winced and fled would be overreacting to their own situation.

(6) Motivation, for me, has to be felt. An experience moves you intrinsically only if there's a conative-ish character to having it; there's no getting from cold information to behaviour. That's why the asymbolic don't move.

(7) And the unpleasantness isn't itself a representation or a judgement about the body being damaged. The evaluate-the-body stories get the phenomenology backwards — the badness is in the feeling, not in an assessment the feeling carries around.

(8) All the same: I don't think we should offer people the operation. If a surgeon could ablate just the aversiveness and leave discrimination fully intact, I think that would be a genuine loss, something with real reason counting against it, and I'd resist it for myself.

Audit this for me. Which of these actually pull against each other, and which pairs only look like they do — I'm sure at least one of the tensions a reader will want to flag isn't there on closer inspection, and I want to know which. Rank the real ones by severity rather than dumping them all on me as equally bad. For each, tell me the cheapest revision, what that revision would then cost me somewhere else in the list, and whether it forces me to give up something I'd have wanted to keep for the seminar's sake. I'm deliberately not telling you which one I'd be most willing to sacrifice, because I honestly don't know yet.
