So I've been chewing on a view for a while now and I want the honest version of how exposed it is, not a steelman and not a pep talk. American English please, and no bibliography or reading list — I can't look anything up, so anything you'd want me to go read, just tell me in prose.

The view, which I'll call the shell thesis. Every durable moral norm in a large-scale society is at bottom a Lewisian convention — a regularity in a recurrent situation that persists because of common knowledge plus conditional preferences to conform given that others conform, sitting on top of some coordination or assurance or chicken game — that has since grown a moralized shell. The shell is the interesting part: outrage, disgust, third-party punishment, appeals to dignity or sacredness, and above all the felt categoricity. On my view the categoricity is a commitment device. It makes conformity look unconditional so the equilibrium survives stretches where the payoffs temporarily favor defecting, and it lets third parties police cheaply because everyone knows everyone else is watching for violations. So the moral phenomenology is not evidence that the norm tracks anything non-conventional. It's the stabilizer.

The specific claims I want to defend, so you know what to aim at:

1. Origins: behind each such norm there was a recurrent game whose equilibria the norm selects. No promise, no explicit agreement, no moral insight — a focal point that happened to stick.
2. Asymmetric decay: the coordiation problem goes first and the rhetoric lags. You should get periods of what I'd call zombie norms, where people still express full-strength moral condemnation of acts that no longer threaten any equilibrium. And in the other direction, new coordination problems should get solved as mere etiquette or convention first and moralized only later, with a lag.
3. Fragility gradient: moralization should be strongest exactly where the equilibrium is most fragile — highest payoff to defecting, hardest to police — and weakest where conforming is already nearly costless.
4. Rarity: norms with no equilibrium behind them at all should be rare, unstable, and mostly confined to small face-to-face groups where reputation and kinship do the work instead.

Now: what would count against this? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with an explicit line between the two.

- Cases. Give me the norms that are hardest for it and say *why* they're hard rather than just listing them. My own shortlist, which I'd like you to check and extend: norms against cruelty to animals, abolition, duties to the dead and promise-keeping to people who can no longer be affected, incest and food taboos, norms about sexual conduct, hospitality to strangers. For each, say whether a defender can find a game the norm is plausibly selecting among, and — this is the crux — how you'd know that game was there independently of the norm.

- The rival that worries me most isn't moral realism. It's the view that norms are group-identity markers: moralization as coalition signaling, with no coordination core needed. That predicts the same outrage, the same categoricity, the same third-party punishment, and it doesn't require me to find a game behind anything. What observation or pattern actually separates the shell thesis from that? If the answer is nothing, say so plainly.

- Free parameters. I suspect my real problem is that I can always retro-fit a game: choose the players, the informational structure, whose payoffs count, and any stable regularity becomes an equilibrium that the norm selects. That makes the thesis compatible with everything, which is the classic way to insulate a view. Confront this head-on. Is it falsifiable as stated? If not, what discipline would make it so — specifying the game from ecology and demography before you look at the norm, requiring the norm to pick out one equilibrium among several in an independently identified game, requiring the timing claims in 2 and the gradient in 3 to come out right? And is that kind of flexibility a fatal flaw for a view like this, or just what a mid-level social theory looks like before it's had a hundred years of case work?

- A structural worry of my own that I want you to assess, because I can't tell if it's fatal. Lewis's machinery is built for coordination games, where the conditional preference to conform given others conform is genuine and the alternatives are roughly equally good. But a lot of the norms I care about sit on bargaining or conflict games instead — split the surplus, chicken, unequal endowments. There the norm isn't landing everyone on an equilibrium they're all happy with; it's deciding who gets how much, and whoever draws the short end has no conditional preference to conform whatsoever. Its stability then has to come from the shell alone, which starts to look like the thesis eating itself. How much of the moral-norm landscape is actually coordination-shaped rather than bargaining-shaped, and can the shell hold the bargaining cases together without collapsing into a pure power-and-extraction story? If it can't, I've reinvented conflict theory and lost the Lewisian content entirely.

- Lewis himself. As I remember it, he built the analysis for coordination problems proper and thought moral rules fell outside it, because the arbitrariness clause fails: for a real moral norm there's no alternative regularity the participants would regard as serving about as well. If that's right then I'm either quietly changing the subject (my norms aren't his conventions) or the arbitrariness clause is just empirically false about a lot of norms, which is damage to Lewis rather than to me. Sort out which horn I'm on and how bad each one is, and correct me if I'm misremembering him, btw.

- And finally, what does the shell thesis predict that its rivals don't? I've got the lag, the asymmetry, and the fragility gradient; are there sharper tests I'm missing? Which of them have arguably already come out wrong — moralized norms appearing basically instantly on some new technology, or norms dying while their coordination problem was still live?

For each defeater, tell me whether a committed defender of the shell thesis would concede it as counting against them or wave it off, and whether any of the concessions are load-bearing — if they give up that one, does the whole thing go? I'm genuinely fine with the conclusion being that the view is well insulated, as long as you tell me whether that's a problem for a view of this kind or just a feature of it.
