I teach a grad seminar on chance and time, and every year for the last session I put one deliberately bad argument on the board and make the students find the crack in it. This year the argument backfired: I can't find the crack myself, and one of the students has started defending the conclusion. Here it is, cleaned up but with the structure I actually used. Let C be a rational credence function, let X be the proposition that the chance of A at time t is x, and let E be whatever else the agent knows:

1. The Principal Principle says: if E is admissible, then C(A | X ∧ E) = x.
2. Admissible evidence, as I gloss it for the class, is evidence that tells you nothing about A over and above what the chance already tells you. The paradigms are evidence about the physical set-up of the chance process, and evidence about the past.
3. The outcomes of a chance process up to time t are evidence about the past.
4. So the observed relative frequency up to t is admissible. Take a coin you're certain is fair, chance of heads 0.5, and suppose you've just watched a run of 100 heads. Then C(heads on flip 101 | X ∧ F) = 0.5, where F is the proposition recording the run.
5. Generalize: so long as you're certain of X, no finite body of frequency data from that very process can move your credence about the next trial off x. The Principle plus certainty about the chance is frequency-blind by construction.
6. But a principle that makes it rationally required to disregard all frequency evidence from the process it governs is not a guide to life, which is precisely what Lewis wanted chance to be. And (5) doesn't even look like a fact about chance: if I'm certain the die is loaded 0.6 toward six, no run of non-sixes should move me either, and nobody thinks that's a deep discovery about dice. So the Principle isn't doing the work its fans claim for it.
7. Therefore either the Principle is false, or certainty about chances is never available to us, or chance isn't a guide to life after all.

My student's line, and the part I couldn't answer at the board: you wrote the certainty into (1) and (4) yourself, so you can't turn around and complain about what certainty licenses. If the Principle is a constraint on credence conditional on a chance hypothesis, its job was never to tell you how to respond to evidence that bears on which chance hypothesis is right, and (5) is a true theorem about a conditional you chose to set up rather than an indictment of it. Another student, who comes over from the statistics department, said it was just the old point that a likelihood is not a posterior, and I said that's a slogan and not an argument, and we went around in circles for twenty minutes. My own instinct is that (2) or (3) is the weak link — surely a long run of heads isn't merely evidence about the past, it's evidence about the chance — but I can't articulate why that isn't just begging the question against the Principle, since the whole point of (2) is to sort admissible from inadmissible evidence. So I'm not sure whether the argument is invalid, and if so exactly which step does the work, or valid with a conclusion I have to swallow.

Two follow-ups I'd want folded into the diagnosis. First, what happens if you swap in the version Lewis moved to later, where the constraint lands on your unconditional credence in A and it's supposed to equal your expected chance — a weighted average over the competing chance hypotheses, weighted by your credence in each? Does that dissolve (5), or just relocate it one level up? Second, is there a leftover problem about chance functions that get shifted by the very evidence that would move your credence in them, and does the Humean part of the story end up load-bearing for whichever diagnosis you give, or can a non-Humean about chance dodge it?

Please don't split the difference and don't hedge at me. Tell me which step you'd reject, how confident you are that it's the load-bearing one rather than a merely dispensable one, and what rejecting it costs — including whether it forces you to give up something attractive about the past being admissible, or to concede that admissibility is defined circularly (E is admissible just in case conditionalizing on it leaves your chance-conditional credence where the Principle puts it, which makes the Principle true by stipulation and unable to fail). Flag any place where you're leaning on a contested reading of Lewis rather than on anything he clearly says. And if you think my framing is broken — if there's no single thing worth calling the Principal Principle, or (2) is a bad enough gloss that (3) through (7) never get off the ground, or (6) is doing something illegitimate I'm not seeing — say so plainly instead of giving me a diplomatic walkthrough. I'd much rather be told the seminar exercise is broken in advance than watch it blow up in front of the room a second time.
