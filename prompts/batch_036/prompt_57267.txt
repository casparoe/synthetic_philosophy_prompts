Before retiring I spent thirty-odd years fitting stock–recruitment and habitat-selection models in marine ecology, and in retirement I have been reading the algorithmic-information literature — Solomonoff induction, Kolmogorov complexity, minimum description length — in which simplicity is defended not as a convenience for the computationally limited but as a guide to truth. That reading has left me dissatisfied with a reporting practice I followed for decades without reflection.

The practice: one fits a set of candidate models to the same data, computes for each the fitted deviance plus twice the number of estimated parameters, ranks the candidates by that quantity, and reports both the gaps between them and weights proportional to exp(−Δ/2), which the standard texts call the "relative likelihood" of each model and which are used to discard unpromising candidates and to average over the survivors. In the same literature, sometimes in the same paper, one finds the Bayesian-flavoured criterion whose penalty instead grows with the logarithm of the sample size and which rests on the quite different assumption that one of the candidates is true and will be recovered as data accumulate. Practitioners treat the two penalties as interchangeable knobs.

What does this bundle presuppose about the aim of modelling? Does ranking by an estimate of predictive loss commit one to the view that every candidate is a false approximation, so that no question of truth arises — or can the ranking be read epistemically after all? Does the weighting step, which has the algebraic form of a posterior probability while being derived from an estimate of information loss, import a doxastic interpretation its derivation cannot support; and is the model averaging it licenses consistent with the same papers' habit of announcing which factor really drives the pattern? And does any short-description-length penalty presuppose that the data-generating process is itself simple, or only that a finite investigator must settle for simplicity? I would like the implicit commitments reconstructed, a clear separation between what the practice strictly requires and what it merely sits comfortably with, a judgement on whether the two penalty strands can be held together or must be chosen between, and a sketch of how our reporting conventions would have to change if simplicity were treated as truth-conducive because of how the world is built.
