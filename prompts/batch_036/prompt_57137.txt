I'm teaching an upper-level epistemology seminar in the spring — 18 students, we meet once a week for 75 minutes, and they've all had a semester of formal epistemology, so they know what a credence function is and they've seen a Dutch book argument before. I want to spend one whole session on the problem of logical omniscience, and I want them to run into it before they ever hear the name.

Design me a betting game they can play in the room — maybe 40 minutes of play, rest for debrief — that makes them feel it. Specifically: I want them to come out incoherent, exposed, or Dutch-booked purely because of logical connections they failed to notice, not because they had bad evidence or weren't paying attention. That's the whole point, so the design has to guarantee it rather than hope for it.

Things I care about:

- The sentences they bet on should be your invention. Give me some self-contained, slightly silly little world — named characters, made-up predicates, a made-up card game or an invented weather system, whatever — so nobody has prior beliefs about the subject matter to lean on. I want the actual sentence list, plus a key showing which ones entail or contradict which, and which pairs are equivalent but disguised by surface form.
- I want two seperate groups getting the same content with different packaging: one group gets a short glossary that makes some of the equivalences visible up front, the other doesn't. The idea is for the class to see how much of the incoherence lived in the notation rather than in the reasoning. Build that comparison into the scoring so it actually produces a number at the end, not just an impression.
- No money. Token budget, and I play the house. Tell me exactly how I quote prices, how many trades per round, and how to keep the arithmetic from swallowing the period — 18 people doing conditional bets by hand is how you loose 25 minutes to bookkeeping and never get to the interesting part.
- At least a couple of the traps should be non-trivial: a longer chain, or a pair that's only equivalent after two steps. If every exploit is a disguised contradiction, my fastest students will spot it in round one and the exercise collapses into a contest over who's quickest at logic. That's exactly the wrong lesson and I want to avoid it.

Then the debrief. Map moments in the game onto pieces of the problem: what the play showed about the demand for coherence, what it showed about bounded computation, and where those two come apart. I'd like it to gesture at — without turning into a lit review — at least three families of response: admitting impossible worlds, treating the discovery as growth in awareness rather than revision of belief, and putting an explicit price on reasoning steps. Say what each one costs.

Be honest with me about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. I'm braced for one student to be genuinely fast and just win, which would blow up the point; tell me how to handle that and anything else likely to jam (dead rounds, token hoarding, the group that refuses to bet).

And tell me straight which feature of the problem the game cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply by talking. My guess is that it's the fact that real agents suspect they might be incoherent and hedge accordingly, plus the fact that the game hands them a fixed finite menu of sentences while actual belief isn't like that at all — but tell me if I'm wrong, or if there's something bigger I'm missing.

No reading list, no citations, please — I'll supply my own. American English throughout.
