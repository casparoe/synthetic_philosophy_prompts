So, I help run a very small research outfit — two of us, plus the occasional contractor — and a funder has offered us about 18 months to do conceptual (explicitly not wet-lab) work on insect welfare. One condition they were pretty blunt about: they don't want another version of the standard scale argument, the "even if there's only a 1% chance insects are sentient, there are like 10^18 of them so the expected value dominates" piece. They've read that one, everyone has. Fine. But now I actually have to tell them what the 18 months goes on, and I want to pressure-test my own list against someone who's thought about this more than I have.

Here's the hunch I keep coming back to, and I'd like you to say whether it's load-bearing or a distraction. People in this space talk about the probability of insect sentience as if it were a single number you nudge up or down with new evidence. But our credences across taxa aren't independent — they're all downstream of a small handful of shared commitments about what kind of neural organisation is enough for there to be something it's like to be. If you think a brain with on the order of a million neurons plus mushroom-body-style integration can support experience, then you probably think that about flies, bees and ants, and quite possibly about spiders and decapod crustaceans too (the UK's sentience legislation already covers cephalopods and decapods, on the strength of a review that leaned hard on exactly this shared-feature reasoning). If you don't, you probably don't for any of them. So maybe the real question isn't "what is P(sentience) for crickets" but "what's the distribution over the latent parameter that fixes P for a whole clade at once" — and the conclusion of the scale argument is far more sensitive to that latent variable than to any per-species estimate. If that's right it changes what's worth researching: you'd go hunting for the one assumption that, if it flipped, flips forty taxa simultaneously, and argue about that, instead of accumulating marginal evidence taxon by taxon.

Given all that, rank the open questions we should spend the 18 months on. Candidates so far, plus anything I'm missing:
- the latent-variable / correlated-credence thing above;
- whether the right denominator is individuals, insect-days, or something else — a huge fraction of an insect's life is spent as a larva, and larval experience is barely discussed anywhere;
- moral weight ratios: if insect experience is much less rich than a mouse's, how much does the arithmetic actually shrink, and is "richness" even the right dimension to scale by;
- whether behavioural evidence (nociception, opioid modulation, learned avoidance, grooming-vs-feeding tradeoffs) is the bottleneck, or whether the bottleneck is that we have no account of what would count as evidence;
- whether mass mortality in larval stages means most insect lives are net negative, and whether that claim survives scrutiny at all.

For each: why it's upstream of the others, whether two people with no lab can make real progress on it in a year and a half, and what a usable output looks like for people who fund interventions rather than papers. Then give me the ordering you'd actually defend and the criteria you're using to generate it — I care much more about that than about the list. And be honest about where your reasoning is shakiest: which of these rankings would you reverse if I pushed back, and what argument or piece of information would change your mind?
