I retired four years ago from a national laboratory, where I spent the better part of four decades doing neutron scattering on magnetic materials, and I have since been auditing a philosophy-of-mathematics reading group at the university in our town — less, I confess, for the metaphysics than for the chance to understand properly some of the machinery I used instrumentally for most of my working life. I relied on the Hahn–Banach theorem, for instance, in exactly the way one relies on a well-calibrated torque wrench: I knew what it delivered, and I never once looked inside it. A trade book on the infinite that our group read last month devotes a short chapter to Zorn's lemma, and the passage that introduces it has left me uneasy. I should be grateful for a careful audit of it.

The book says the following:

"Zorn's lemma is the least glamorous and most load-bearing result in mathematics, and it says something almost trivially plausible. If, in some collection of objects, every ascending line of elements eventually tops out — if there is no chain that keeps climbing forever without arriving at a last element — then the collection must contain a largest element, one greater than everything else in it. Once that is granted, a great deal of algebra falls out for free: that every vector space has a basis, that every proper ideal in a ring with identity is contained in a maximal ideal, that every field has an algebraic closure, and that the real line can be well-ordered. Best of all, none of this requires the axiom of choice. Zorn proved his lemma outright from the axioms of set theory in 1935, which is why working mathematicians, who mostly distrust choice, are happy to use it. The proof is a diagonal construction in Cantor's sense: one lists the chains, defines a new element that outruns each of them, and reads off the maximum. The Hahn–Banach theorem comes out of the same lemma, though it is the one application that genuinely needs the full force of choice, which is why analysts often prove it separately."

Could you take this sentence by sentence and tell me how much of it a working set theorist would sign? I should like the claims sorted into what is accurate, what is a defensible simplification for a general reader, what is subtly wrong in a way that would matter to someone actually trying to apply the lemma, and what is simply false — and, for the last two categories, the correct statement put in terms a physicist could hold onto. I am particularly interested in the dependencies: which of the book's later claims rest on which of its earlier ones, and whether anything in its list of applications survives once the framing paragraphs are corrected, or whether the whole edifice comes down with them.

Two further matters. First, when I tried the lemma as the book states it, on the collection of proper ideals of the integers ordered by inclusion, I ran into trouble. The union of any chain of proper ideals is again a proper ideal, since an ideal containing 1 is not proper at all; so the hypothesis appears to be satisfied, and the conclusion should hand me a largest proper ideal. Yet the maximal ideals of the integers are the principal ideals generated by the primes, and no one of them contains another, so I could see no way to single out the largest. I assumed I had botched the union step; please tell me where the difficulty actually lies, since it seems to me now that this small experiment may have more to teach than the chapter did. Second, I am inclined to accept the book's central reassurance — that the lemma is a matter of pure order structure and therefore escapes the axiom of choice — because that is how it struck me when I first met it, and the description of the proof as a diagonal argument was congenial to a man who has spent a lifetime outflanking lists. I suspect that this inclination is precisely what a good audit ought to interrogate, and I would rather be corrected here than comforted.
