I'm putting together a talk for a small workshop on literature and philosophy of language and I've got Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day in my sights. My angle is that it's basically a novel about speech acts — not primarily about repression, or Englishness, or a servant's complicity with a fascist sympathiser, which is what everyone says — and I want to know whether that holds up or whether I'm just imposing a framework on it. The obvious hooks: Stevens's whole ideal of dignity seems to be that a great butler's utterances carry nothing but the locution — he reports, he confirms arrangements, he defers — while Miss Kenton spends decades trying to get him to actually do something with words, and every time he recasts her move as a request for information or a staffing question. The night she tries to tell him about the marriage proposal she's had, during that big conference at the hall with the German ambassador and all the important gentlemen, he answers it, in effect, as a personnel matter. And when his father dies upstairs he says he can't leave his post. Then there's the bantering business with the new American owner, which bracketts the whole thing, and the pier at the end.

Here's the part I really want pushed on, because it's my own coinage and I have no idea wether it survives contact with Austin. I keep wanting to call Stevens infelicitous, but as far as I can tell every cell in Austin's list — the misinvocations, the botched executions, the insincerities where you go through the procedure without the requisite thoughts or intentions — is a way an *attempted* act can go wrong. My claim is that Stevens never launches the act in the first place. So I've been calling it pre-emptive reclassification: a standing social role that converts every available illocutionary move, whether it's coming from you or aimed at you, into a bare statement of how things stand before it can ever acquire a force, so nothing gets performed, nothing misfires, and the typology has nowhere to put it. Is that a genuine gap in the doctrine of infelicities, a species of something Austin already has, or just an ornate description of someone who is silent? And what about the hearer side — Austin does make uptake a condition, so if Miss Kenton's overtures fail to secure uptake from a man who has trained himself out of hearing illocutionary force at all, is that a distinct kind of failure, and whose failure is it morally? I keep flip-flopping on whether she's the one doing something infelicitous, since half the book is her issuing things that are shaped like assertions but meant as proposals, and him responding to the shape.

Then the ending, which I think is the most interesting bit philosophically. He finally says something to a stranger on the pier — that Lord Darlington at least made his own mistakes, whereas he trusted him so completely that he can't even claim that much — and then he resolves to go away and work on his bantering. But banter is exactly the register Austin brackets off early on as non-serious and parasitic, along with actors' lines and jokes and poetry, and then never really comes back to; I think that exclusion is the thing the Derrideans later made a meal of, though I'm hazy on it and I'm not asking you to reconstruct that debate. So: does the novel end with its hero committing himself to become fluent in precisely the mode the theory threw in the bin, and if so is that a criticism of the theory, a criticism of Stevens, or a coincidence I'm over-reading? Tell me honestly where the speech-act lens lights something up and where it's a Procrustean bed — the book is also plainly about class, and about a man who outsourced his moral judgement to his employer, and I don't want to reduce that to a story about illoctionary force, so if you think my framing flattens the novel, say so and say how. One ground rule: don't quote anything. I can't expect you to have the text in front of you and I'll be checking scenes against the book myself, so paraphrase freely — but do flag any scene you think I've got wrong or misremembered. What I most want at the end is your answer to the question I can't settle on my own: is there something the novel shows about doing things with words — specifically about the difference between failing at an act and never attempting one — that the how-to-do-things apparatus simply has no vocabulary for?
