For the independent study I am doing this year on the Scientific Revolution, I have been reading a general-audience book from our library, and one of its chapters gives a brisk account of Newton's religious beliefs. The book has no notes and no bibliography, and I have no way of checking what it says, so I have copied the relevant passage below and would be grateful for your judgment on how much of it I should carry into my paper.

Here is the passage: "Newton is remembered as the man who turned the universe into a machine, and it is usually supposed that his God was simply the machine's builder — a supreme craftsman who constructed the clock, wound it, and thereafter left it to run on its own laws without further interference. That is roughly what he believed, and it makes him a deist well before deism had a name. What is less often mentioned is how much of his writing had nothing to do with physics at all: he left far more pages on alchemy, biblical chronology and prophecy than on motion and gravitation, and most of it he had to keep hidden, because his reading of scripture had convinced him that the doctrine of the Trinity was a fourth-century fabrication, forced into the text of the New Testament by the party of Athanasius. Admitting that publicly would have cost him his position at Cambridge, and he survived only because a special exemption freed him from the requirement to take holy orders. When he did bring God into print, it was as an afterthought: the concluding note added to the second edition of the Principia, in which he says that he frames no hypotheses — meaning that he declined to speculate beyond what he could measure — and then adds that so elegant a system of sun, planets and comets could only have proceeded from the counsel of an intelligent being. The note was a defensive gesture, written to answer critics who had accused his physics of atheism, not the expression of a conviction that shaped the science itself."

I cannot tell whether that recollection is reliable.
