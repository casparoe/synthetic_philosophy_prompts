I am a fifth-year political theory PhD student, and in three weeks I am presenting a dissertation chapter at a closed interdisciplinary workshop jointly run by our political science department and a neighbouring psychology department. The chapter is called "Looseness as a Positional Good," and it engages directly with the cultural tightness–looseness literature — the thirty-three-nation comparative work, the later study of the fifty American states, and the trade-book synthesis of the past decade — which I take to be the most useful thing empirical social science has produced for the normative question of how much rule-following a society may demand of its members. My argument, in brief, is what I call the substitution thesis: where third-party enforcement is weak, dense norms backed by severe informal punishment are not a cultural pathology but a functional stand-in for institutions, and looseness is therefore a positional good, purchasable only by societies that have already bought effective enforcement and physical security, with its benefits — tolerance of deviation, creativity, personal autonomy — accruing mainly to those who can absorb the risk that normlessness creates. From this I draw a deliberately uncomfortable conclusion: what I call looseness-first policy, pressuring a tight society to relax its norms without changing the conditions that make tightness load-bearing, is not a moral improvement but a transfer of risk onto the least protected members, and theorists who treat tightness as a standing moral failure are smuggling a fact about their own societies' security into a universal claim. My supervisor wants the positional-good framing cut; I think it is the load-bearing part, so the Q&A matters a great deal to me.

The room will be about fifteen people and I want to prepare for it as it actually is rather than as my department imagines it. Roughly eight are cultural or cross-cultural psychologists, two of whom have been authors on the principal national-level measurement work and who will know the item wording, the effect sizes, and the standing criticisms of the country indices far better than I do; three are methodologists who work on measurement invariance and cross-national survey comparability, and who are in my experience hostile on principle to any argument that leans on comparisons of country means; two are institutional economists broadly sympathetic to functionalist framing; and one is a political theorist from my own department who has never read the empirical literature and will want to work through the is/ought question at length. The session is twenty-five minutes of talk followed by forty of discussion, and the chair calls on the two most senior people first. Please draft my Q&A preparation sheet: the eight or so objections this particular audience will actually raise — not the ones I would get in a philosophy seminar, and not the ones a journalist would ask — ordered by how much damage each would do if it landed cleanly. Tag each with which subset of the room raises it, phrase it in that objector's own idiom the way a field cross-cultural psychologist or a measurement psychometrician would actually put it, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and add an honest note on where that reply is soft, including which of my own premises it costs me to give it. Tell me also what I should concede in the first five minutes before anyone has to ask, what I must refuse to concede if the talk is to be worth giving, and which single reply has to land for the substitution thesis to survive the afternoon. Finally, include the question I am hoping nobody asks: I suspect it is some version of whether my view commits me to tolerating norms that restrict women or sexual minorities in tight societies, since those are precisely the cases where informal enforcement substitutes for absent institutions — but I may be wrong about which soft spot is most exposed, and I would rather be told.

I do not want a reference list, a bibliography, or a recapitulation of the tightness–looseness literature; I have read it, and several people in the room wrote parts of it. I want the objections, the replies, and the diagnostics. Where you would normally cite a study, describe the finding instead, in the vocabulary the objector would use. End your answer with a single line of the form "Verdict: <objection number> — <label of three words or fewer>", identifying the objection from your list that would do the most damage if it landed and I answered it badly.
