I build and evaluate ML systems, so I'm comfortable with strictly proper scoring rules — Brier score, log loss — as ways of measuring how good a probabilistic forecast is. Recently I've been reading about accuracy-first epistemology, the program that takes a measure of inaccuracy to be the only thing that matters epistemically and then tries to derive the usual norms (be probabilistically coherent, update by conditionalizing on what you learn) as theorems about minimizing expected inaccuracy.

The claim I want to stress is a familiar one in that area: free, truthful evidence can never be expected to make you less accurate, provided you respond to it by conditionalizing. Looking is never epistemically bad.

What I'm actually after is whether this framework can give usable advice to someone who knows she isn't going to update well. So: build me a thought experiment that breaks the claim, to the specifications below. I'll be checking the arithmetic, so the case has to generate its verdict from the numbers rather than from stipulation.

**Setting.** Present-day, realistic, an engineering or operations context. One named person making one decision about whether to look at one piece of evidence. No exotic apparatus: no omniscient predictors, no memory-altering drugs, no brain modifications. The scenario itself should be a single paragraph.

**The evidence must be genuinely informative.** Give me a small algebra — a few hypotheses, two or three possible observations, all checkable by hand — with a prior and likelihoods such that the evidence really does bear on the question, and such that someone who looks and conditionalizes properly is strictly more accurate in expectation than someone who never looks. I don't want a case that works by making the evidence useless noise, and I don't want one that works by making the observation costly.

**The agent doesn't conditionalize.** She follows a specific, statable response rule — write it out as an explicit function from what she observes to what she ends up believing — that is biased in a way she knows about and cannot switch off in the moment. The bias should be an ordinary human flaw (overreading a signal, treating a correlation as a diagnosis, anchoring on a first impression), not a cartoon. Also tell me what her evidence is for thinking she has it; I don't want "she's miscalibrated" as a bare assumption.

**Looking is otherwise free.** No time cost, no risk that the instrument lies, no practical stakes riding on what she finds. The only reason available for declining has to be the accuracy math.

Then demonstrate that the design works:

- Compute expected inaccuracy, using one scoring rule, for the two options genuinely open to her — look and respond with her actual rule, versus decline and keep her prior — and show that declining wins.
- Compute the third number: look and conditionalize properly. That should beat both. The ordering across the three is the whole point of the case.
- Lay out the "free true evidence can't hurt" argument step by step, and name exactly which step your case violates.
- Say which feature is doing the work and what happens when you remove it. In particular: does the case collapse if she can bind herself the night before to a written procedure she then follows mechanically? What if she is less certain about her own bias?
- Tell me how an accuracy-first theorist resists calling this a counterexample at all. My guess is that the claim was only ever meant for agents who conditionalize, which would mean it never applied to her. Say whether that retreat is legitimate or whether it costs the view something — whether the claim stops functioning as advice, and what the view then tells her to do given that she can't do the thing it recommends.
- Finish with two or three sentences on what I should take this to mean for a dashboard that surfaces a true, relevant, alarming metric to an on-call engineer who is known to overreact to it.
