So a bit of context: I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training at all beyond some LessWrong rabbit holes and the occasional blog post, so please treat me like a smart but clueless reader. My team is building an agent that talks to customers' procurement bots, and it sometimes has to make commitments that it will not want to honour later — "we'll have that bug fixed in two weeks", and then in two weeks the cheapest move is to quietly not fix it. Trying to work out how the agent should reason about its own future promises, I fell into what I now learn is called Parfit's hitchhiker: you're dying of thirst in the desert, a driver will take you into town only if you promise to hand over $100 at an ATM once you arrive, and the driver is extremely good at spotting whether you'd actually follow through. Follow through and you're out $100 for a ride you already got; say yes without meaning it and you get spotted and left to die. Which means a perfectly rational agent, by the obvious dominance reasoning, can't get a ride out of the desert — and that reads to me exactly like the bug in my design, so I want to understand where the reasoning actually forks.

What I want back is a decision tree, or a sorting quiz if you'd rather build it that way, that takes someone who understands the case but knows none of the labels and lands them on a named position about what the hitchhiker should do and, more importantly, on what grounds. Call it eight to ten nodes. Every node has to be a question about the case or about how you evaluate choices that a person can answer without knowing which view it leads toward — nothing like "are you a causal decision theorist", no theory names anywhere in the questions. The forks I care most about, because I think they're doing the real work: what exactly the driver is detecting (your current intention, a settled disposition, a run of your own future decision procedure, or just a shrewd guess about what you'll do); at which point in time you're allowed to ask whether a choice was rational (the moment in the desert, the moment at the ATM, or the whole policy you adopted before either); whether precommitment is available in the case, and whether it matters that you could have bound yourself earlier and didn't; whether one-shot versus repeated changes the answer; and whether "rational" here means "maximizes expected payoff" or "can't be criticized / is what you ought to do". Those last two especially feel like they get conflated a lot and I'd like the tree to seperate them cleanly if it can.

For leaves, at minimum I want these, though you pick the names and where they sit: (1) don't pay, and the fact that a good predictor foresaw this is just bad luck rather than a reason to pay; (2) pay, on grounds having to do with the case already fixing what you'll do; (3) pay because it's rational to be the kind of agent who pays even though this particular act of paying is against your interest; (4) both of the last two, as two levels of assessment that don't collapse into one; (5) the case is underdescribed and has no determinate answer until you pin down the detection mechanism. For each leaf, one sentence on the commitment its defender would rather not own — and then the part I actually need: what that leaf flips to under a variant I made up, where the driver can't read you at all but you can sign something before she pulls up that transfers the $100 automatically and irrevocably the second you're dropped off in town. Some leaves should give a different verdict on the two versions and some shouldn't, and which ones do which is a big part of the point. If you can, build the thing from the logical space first rather than from a list of schools: enumerate the answer combinations your nodes permit, mark the empty cells, and for each say whether it's empty because it's incoherent, because nobody finds it attractive, or because nobody's bothered. Also flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really tell apart — my suspicion is that two of the "pay" leaves agree on every version of the case and differ only in vocabulary, and if so I'd rather you said that outright than pretend the instrument sorts them. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations please; I have no way to check them and I don't need them.
