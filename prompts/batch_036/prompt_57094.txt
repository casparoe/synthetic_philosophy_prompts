So I've been on this essay prize committee for three years now and this year's round has me genuinely stuck — not in a fun seminar way, more in a "I've rewritten my verdict four times" way. Setup: five readers, forty-odd submissions, we each get the same six finalists and post a two-paragraph verdict into a shared Google Doc in a fixed order (seniority, don't ask). The doc is live as we go, so whoever writes second has already read whoever wrote first. I'm fourth this year, which I normally don't think about at all.

The paper in question — call it the M paper, it's about self-locating belief and a bunch of stuff I'll spare you — got three clearly positive verdicts ahead of mine. My own honest reaction after two slow readings is that it's technically clean, beautifully signposted, and almost entirely derivative of two papers it cites but doesn't engage with. It reads like a great paper on a first pass and like nothing at all on a second. But the three ahead of me are not interchangeable: the first is a former student of mine who has never met a submission he didn't like, the second is a real specialist in exactly this literature and wrote something specific and substantive that taught me things I'd flatly missed, and the third admitted in her note that she found the formalism hard going but was impressed by how the paper landed at a workshop last spring. So I don't think those verdicts are worth the same amount. What I can't tell is whether I'm doing careful weighting of evidence or just dressing up my own contrarianism — I have a reputation in the department, entirely deserved, for being the one who sinks things.

What I want isn't "trust your gut" or "defer to your colleagues," it's the actual reasoning. How much evidential weight should the unanimity sitting in front of me carry, given that I formed my view after seeing all of it? And does the answer shift depending on what I think each of them was reacting to — the paper, or what was already in the doc when they sat down to write? Second, if I file the dissent my guess is it kills the paper outright, because reader five is a junior colleague who will not want to be the tiebreaker against a 3-1. Is that a reason to soften my write-up, a reason to sharpen it so it can't be skimmed past, or a reason to say nothing in the record and raise it over dinner instead, where it doesn't get frozen into the file? Third, I'm tempted to propose we go to simultaneous blind verdicts next year, nothing visible until everyone has submitted. Some part of me thinks that would throw away real information, since the specialist's note genuinely improved my reading of the paper — so is independence actually the thing to optimize for in a process like this, or am I about to fix a small problem by manufacturing a bigger one?

Deadline's Friday and I've been avoiding it by grading midterms, which probably tells you something. Small prize, small stakes, and yet here we are.
