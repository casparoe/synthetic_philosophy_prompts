Ok so I'm doing my EPQ on social norms and I've got stuck on an analogy my economics teacher keeps reaching for. He says norms are basically like the rules of grammar: nobody ever wrote them down or voted for them, everyone follows them without being able to state them, they drift over centuries without anyone deciding anything, and above all you can't be *wrong* in a moral sense for breaking one — if I say "me and him went" I haven't done anything bad, I've just marked myself out as not one of you. He uses this for two claims. First, that it's confused to blame people for norm violations that were completely normal when they were young, like my grandad saying something at work in 1975 that would get him sacked today. Second, that you can't change a norm on purpose, by arguing people round or by making rules, any more than a committee could decide English will start putting its adjectives after the noun — you just have to wait for the drift.

I half buy it and half dont. The bits that don't sit right: when I broke the unwritten rule about which table you're allowed to sit at in the common room, people were properly annoyed with me and someone actually told me off, which never happens with grammar — a solecism doesn't get you sanctioned, it gets you a raised eyebrow. And I think I read somewhere that Lewis analysed language itself as resting on conventions of truthfulness and trust, which would make the grammar comparison his own example rather than an objection to it. Also some norms feel nothing like grammar: which side of the road we drive on did get changed on purpose (Sweden managed it), whereas "don't freeloader off the group project" comes with real indignation and people policing the policing.

Act as a philosopher who works on social ontology and conventions, and think this through carefully — maybe start by brainstorming two or three candidate ways to line the two things up before you settle on one, since I want to see the alternatives you rejected. Then give me: an explicit correspondence table (what plays the role of the sentence, the native speaker, the solecism, the slow drift, the inability to articulate the rule); the places the mapping breaks, sorted into the disanalogies that actually threaten his two claims and the ones that are beside the point; what features of grammar the analogy is quietly importing that norms turn out not to have; and whether each of the two claims survives once that's all on the table. If there's a better analogy for norms — one that keeps the no-author, no-design point without smuggling in the rest — say what it is and why it's better.

Please actually commit on this one. Don't hand me a balanced "there are considerations on both sides" write-up where I have to do the deciding: pick a position on whether the two claims survive, tell me which single disanalogy you think is doing the most work, and argue for it.
