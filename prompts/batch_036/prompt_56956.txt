I've been reading Bayesian epistemology on and off for about fifteen years — mostly through the old rationalist sequences, van Fraassen, and whatever the arguments of the month on LessWrong happened to be chewing on — and I'm now in a slightly embarrassing position: a small reading group I belong to has asked each member to write down their actual commitments about uncertain evidence so the others can attack them, and once I tried to write mine down cleanly, I found that I couldn't tell whether they fit together. I'd rather find out from you than from them. What follows is my best attempt at a faithful statement; I've tried not to smooth over the parts I suspect are shaky, but I genuinely don't know which of those parts are shaky.

My commitments are these. (1) Radical probabilism about the empirical: no contingent proposition ever gets credence exactly 1, so I don't think perceptual learning can ever be modeled as conditioning on some proposition I have become certain of; the certainty-based rule simply never gets off the ground in the cases it was designed for. (2) What actually happens when I look at something is that the experience hands me new probabilities directly over a partition of propositions — the swatch is teal, the swatch is blue, the swatch is green — and I then propagate that shift through my whole credence function by probability kinematics: my posterior on any hypothesis is the weighted sum of my old conditionals on the cells, weighted by the new cell probabilities. (3) I take the rigidity (invariance) condition seriously as a constraint, not as a convenience: the experience shifts the probabilities of the cells but leaves my conditional credences on them untouched, so that my conditional on each cell after the experience equals what it was before. Without that, I've been told, the rule would be arbitrary rather than determinate, and I think that's right. (4) My reason for believing (3) is dynamic coherence in the Skyrmsian line: an agent who expects to violate rigidity can be led into bets she would now decline, and rigidity looks to me like a special case of the more general norm that I should now defer to my own anticipated future opinions. (5) On partition choice, I use coarse, salient cells rather than maximally fine phenomenal ones, because I don't trust my introspective discrimination at fine grain — teal/blue/green, not the several hundred shades I might in principle distinguish. (6) At the same time I think the real normative story is total-evidence conditionalization: there is some single proposition capturing everything the experience told me, and ideally my posterior is just my old credence conditional on that proposition, with the kinematics of (2) serving as my fallible implementation of it. (7) I also hold that experiences can be known in advance to be distorting — I know I'm sleep-deprived, I know the lighting is bad, I know I've just been primed by an advertisement I recognize as manipulative, I know I'll have had a drink — and in such cases I should not simply hand my credences over to whatever the experience moves me to; I should discount the shift beforehand or correct for it afterward. (8) I believe that when two pieces of evidence bear on unrelated matters, the order in which I receive them should make no difference to where I end up. (9) Finally, I keep van Fraassen's Judy Benjamin case in the back of my mind as a reason to be nervous about this whole apparatus, since there the evidence seems to underdetermine the posterior over the cells and the kinematics looks like it picks one out of many equally eligible answers.

What I want from you is an audit, not a lecture on how probability kinematics works — I know the mechanics fine, and I can do the arithmetic. Please go through the set pairwise and tell me which commitments genuinely pull against each other and which only look as though they do. For each real tension, say how severe it is, identify precisely where the contradiction or incoherence bites (I'd like the reasoning, including the relevant conditional-probability calculations where they matter, not just a verdict), and say what would have to be given up or restricted to resolve it and what that revision costs me elsewhere. Then rank the tensions by severity and tell me which single change to the package would clean up the most, and what I'd be left believing afterward. I'd especially like to know whether I'm entitled to hold (4) and (7) together, whether (5) and (6) can both be the normative story at once, and whether (8) survives the rule I've endorsed in (2) — but please don't take my word for which of these is the interesting one; if you think the real trouble is somewhere I haven't pointed, tell me where. And where you think a worry of mine simply dissolves on inspection — I suspect (9) may be one, and possibly (1) too — say so explicitly and say why, because I don't want to spend the reading group's time defending a problem I don't have.
