I own and run a twenty-two-person home remodeling company in central Ohio — kitchens, baths, the occasional whole-house job — and every December since I bought the business I have carved a share of the year's profit into a bonus pool and divided it by instinct: a large check for my lead carpenter, something for the woman who schedules and orders everything, smaller amounts for the apprentices, nothing for the two guys I was quietly unhappy with. Last December two men quit within a month of each other. Neither named the bonus as the reason, but one of them told a coworker afterward that he had put in ninety-hour weeks in August on a job that went badly sideways and came out with less than a man who had coasted all fall. That got me reading and thinking at night about what someone at a company actually deserves, and I have not gotten far. What I want from you is a procedure I can run once a year in a single afternoon, with no economics or philosophy training behind me: an ordered list of questions to ask about the pool and then about each employee, what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, the points at which the procedure runs out and hands the decision back to me because it is a matter of values rather than facts, and a frank note wherever a step is really a judgment call dressed up as a step. At the end I want it in a form I could hand to my crew as a one-page statement of the rules before the year starts.

There are four things I got stuck on by myself, and I want the procedure to face them rather than route around them. First, the work is joint. Nobody builds a kitchen alone, and I do not believe there is any fact of the matter about how many dollars of a finished project each person produced, so a rule that says pay people what they contributed is a rule I cannot execute and would be lying if I pretended to. Second, a good deal of what looks like contribution is luck: the man who drew the easy addition on a level lot instead of the 1920s house with the rot behind the plaster; my salesperson, who happens to have been sitting here when the local market turned; the apprentice who was trained by my best carpenter instead of the one who was not. Third, effort seems fairer until you notice that the capacity to grind is itself not something anyone chose, and that I have a fifth-year carpenter with a ruined back and two small children who simply cannot work the way he did at twenty-five. Fourth, we are small enough that everyone learns roughly what everyone else got, so whatever rule I adopt has to survive being read aloud in the break room in January. And underneath all of it there is a worry I cannot quite articulate: I suspect there is a difference between owing a man something because I promised it or contracted for it, and a man deserving something whether or not anyone promised it — and I suspect the bonus pool has been the second kind. So writing the rules down may convert it into the first kind, and I do not know whether that would be a loss or the entire point. Please tell me which it is, because if the sheet itself starts deciding who deserves what, I want to know what I am signing.

Here is what I can and cannot supply. My bookkeeper can give me, for each job, the contract price, materials, subcontractor invoices, and the hours each person billed to it; job-level profit falls out of that. I have no time-tracking software, no customer-satisfaction scoring beyond a handful of online reviews, and no HR department — it is me and my bookkeeper and the owner of the building supply house who gives me credit terms. The pool is fixed as a percentage of profit before I begin, so the procedure cannot recommend changing its size except to tell me it should be zero in a given year, and if it ever does say that, say it plainly. Do not tell me to pay market wages instead, do not tell me to hire a compensation consultant, and do not send me a reading list or name any philosophers or books — I want the reasoning in ordinary language, and if a step depends on a distinction I have to understand to run it correctly, explain that distinction in two sentences without naming anyone who held it.

Then run the procedure on three of my people, using these descriptions. Ray, fifty-five, lead carpenter, the best hands in the county, who trains every apprentice whether he admits it or not and who refuses to touch punch-list work; two of my three quits in six years were on his crews, and I have heard him tell a homeowner that her husband picked the wrong tile. Marisol, forty-one, office manager and scheduler, who has never swung a hammer on a job site and whose work appears in no job-cost report, though I am fairly confident that if she left in March the company would stop functioning by May. Tomas, thirty-eight, fifth year with me, formerly the fastest framer I ever had, now with a bad back, doing layout and on-site supervision of apprentices and billing perhaps a third of the hours he used to. Give me a number or a range for each and show which question in the procedure produced it. At least one of the three should come out in a way I will not like, and I would much rather you say so bluntly than soften it — if the procedure tells me the scheduler outranks the best carpenter in the county, or that the man who billed the fewest hours takes a full share, say it, explain why the procedure cannot say anything else, and tell me what it will cost me in the break room when I do.
