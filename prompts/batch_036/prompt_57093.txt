I have spent the better part of a year reading on the cognitive penetrability of perception, and I have come away with the conviction that the disagreement has not moved appreciably in three decades despite a very large volume of careful experimental work. I want to understand why, and I want a diagnosis rather than a survey of the literature.

Here is the shape of things as I see them. On one side stand the inheritors of the thesis that input systems are informationally encapsulated and mandatory, the defence of early vision as cognitively impenetrable, and a widely influential review of the mid-2010s arguing that essentially the whole body of putative top-down effects — value and bodily-state effects on perceived size and distance, the action-specific perception findings, the ambiguous-figure labelling studies, motivation effects — fail to rule out demand characteristics, response bias, memory, attention, and long-run perceptual learning. On the other side stand the late-1990s attack on encapsulation from within vision science, the programme of work on the perceptual effects of names and of concurrent verbal interference, the finding that speakers of a language with two basic terms for shades of blue discriminate across that boundary faster than speakers of a language with only one term, with the advantage attenuated under verbal load, and the philosophical arguments that penetration has consequences for the epistemology of experience and for theory-ladenness in science regardless of the precise mechanism involved.

My hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than adopt, is that this is not one disagreement but three wearing a single name: (a) a boundary-placement dispute about what counts as cognitive at all, since nobody has supplied a principled criterion separating concepts, expectations, attention, perceptual set, and learned association; (b) a genuinely methodological dispute about what would count as a change in perception as opposed to a change in what subjects say about perception; and (c) a real but tractable empirical question about locus and time-course, namely whether top-down influence reaches early visual processing or only post-perceptual read-out. I further suspect that (b) is doing most of the actual work while nearly all the published ink is spent on (a), and that the two camps are not in fact disagreeing about (c) so much as declining to treat it as the question.

Is that right? Please diagnose the impasse by reference to what each side actually says, and tell me in rough terms which of the three carries the most weight and where the two camps are talking past one another.

Then consider a stipulation. Suppose we ran the experiment I would ideally want: no verbal labels, no suggestive instructions, a manipulation consisting of a belief about an invisible causal mechanism rather than about any visible property of the stimulus, responses measured by eye movements and discrimination thresholds rather than by verbal report, and a localised neural measure of the processing stage affected. Suppose it came out robustly positive. Would that settle the matter, or would the encapsulation camp simply relocate the boundary between early and late processing, as I suspect they have done repeatedly over the decades? If the latter, say what feature of the debate makes the boundary mobile in this way.

Finally, given your diagnosis, state plainly what would count as progress here: a new distinction, a formal criterion for the phenomenon, a particular experimental design, or an abandonment of the question in favour of a better-posed one. If your conclusion is that no progress should be expected, argue for that explicitly rather than leaving it as an implication.
