I'm in a kitchen-table reading group that's been working through some Marxist stuff, and one of the regulars — Dan, structural engineer by trade, came to all this in his fifties — has a way of ending basically every argument we have. He says a society is a building: the economy is the foundation, and law, politics, religion, art, the whole rest of it, is the structure sitting on top. What's bugging me is that he uses the same picture two ways and they don't obviously pull in the same direction. Way one: whenever someone floats a reform — a carbon tax, workplace democracy inside otherwise completely ordinary firms, some ESG disclosure rule — he says you're rearranging furniture on the upper floors and it'll settle back, because the load path runs down to the foundation and nothing above it can carry load the foundation isn't transferring. Hence climate policy is theater until ownership of the energy sector changes, full stop. Way two, the part I can't get a grip on: he also reads the upper floors as an instrument panel for the foundation. Somebody pointed out last month how fast attitudes have moved on gay marriage and on remote work, and he said that's precisely how you know the base has already shifted — culture is just the base showing up where you can see it, the way settlement cracks tell you the soil underneath has moved. So the top floors can't cause anything, but they reliably report what the bottom floor did.

Both uses feel intuitive to me and I think atleast one of them is in trouble, and I suspect the building is what's making them feel like they fit together. Can you audit it for me properly? I want the correspondence laid out explicitly — what in the building plays the role of the mode of production, the relations of production, the state, ideology, a revolution, a reform — and then the places the mapping breaks, sorted into the breaks that actually bite on Dan's two arguments versus the ones that are just "well, societies aren't literally made of concrete" and can be waved off. Two specific things I want pressed on. First, does a foundation really have the one-way, non-responsive kind of priority over what sits on it that way one needs, or does the source domain itself already give Dan two-way traffic — and if it does, what's left of the claim that nothing above can carry load? Second, for way two, what would have to be true in the source domain for visible damage to function as a gauge of hidden movement, and does the analogy actually supply that or is Dan smuggling it in? Are the two uses even compatible with one another, or does granting one cost him the other?

Then tell me whether either conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table: is climate-policy-as-theater still a defensible claim on grounds that don't need the building, and is reading cultural change for economic change still a legitimate inference rather than a leap? And if you think there's a better picture for the relation he's reaching for — or, if it turns out he's reaching for two different relations and doesn't notice, a better picture for each — give me that with the same treatment, where it holds and where it fails. Don't bother pointing me at books or telling me who said what; I can chase that down myself. I want the reasoning.
