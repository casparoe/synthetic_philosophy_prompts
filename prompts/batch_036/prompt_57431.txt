I spent thirty-five years in seismic hazard assessment, most of it estimating recurrence intervals for large earthquakes from catalog data, and since retiring I have been reading seriously in the philosophy of probability and rational choice. My daughter, now a graduate student in statistics, has asked me to set down precisely what I believe about the St. Petersburg game so that she can look for the cracks; I would much rather have them found by someone competent than stumble onto them myself mid-conversation. My position, as I currently hold it, is this:

1. I am a frequentist about probability in the sense I was trained in: a probability claim is a claim about limiting relative frequencies in a long run of comparable trials, and I remain unpersuaded by single-case subjective credence. This is a working habit rather than a slogan — a magnitude estimate earned its keep only insofar as it eventually showed up in the catalog.

2. In decision theory, however, I am an expected utility maximizer in the cardinal sense: I rank prospects by expected utility, and I hold that differences of utility between outcomes are genuine measurable quantities, not merely the shadow of an ordering.

3. I accept Bernoulli's resolution of the game: utility is concave in total wealth, roughly logarithmic, so the expected utility of the gamble is finite and modest. For a person of comfortable but ordinary means the fair entry fee comes out somewhere near twenty dollars, which is also what I would actually pay.

4. I think concavity is not the deep point; boundedness is. Utility must be bounded above, because no finite creature can have unbounded utility, and it is boundedness that blocks the escalation Menger identified, in which a payoff schedule growing fast enough reproduces the divergence for any unbounded utility function whatsoever.

5. At the same time I refuse what boundedness appears to imply about the top of the scale. I hold that there is a real and non-negligible difference in utility between gaining a billion dollars and gaining a googol dollars; I am not prepared to say that sufficiently large sums become indistinguishable to me.

6. I also endorse the finite-resources reply: the expectation diverges only because we idealize the house as infinitely wealthy, whereas any actual house has a bankroll that truncates the payoff schedule, so every real version of the game has finite expected value. I observe that this reply, Bernoulli's concavity, and the appeal to boundedness all land at roughly the same modest fee for the standard figures, and I have been treating that agreement as mutual corroboration of all three.

7. I hold that the infinite expectation is an objective feature of the idealized game, and that an adequate account must explain it rather than explain it away. I also recall from a probability text I read years ago that when the game is played repeatedly the running average payoff per play grows roughly like the logarithm of the number of plays — a million plays would still show an average near twenty dollars — so that no finite run ever exhibits the divergence.

8. Finally, although I maintain that no house could ever actually offer this game, I do not think the problem is thereby idle: I take it to be a live and unresolved puzzle about rationality, and one with consequences for how we price rare but enormous outcomes.

What I want is an audit of this set, not a survey of the literature on the paradox and not a defense of any particular resolution. Tell me which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another and which merely appear to, and for each genuine tension state what has to give, what the cheapest revision is, and what that revision costs me elsewhere — including whether it leaves the paradox unsolved on my own terms. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as an undifferentiated list, and close by identifying the single change that would clean up the most. Please be concrete in your reasoning: where two of my commitments seem to me to support each other, I suspect I may be reading corroboration out of a coincidence in the numbers, and I want that suspicion either confirmed or dispelled with an argument. State your bottom line in your first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
