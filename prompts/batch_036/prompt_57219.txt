Been going back and forth on the famine material lately — Sen on Bengal, the Irish catastrophe of the 1840s, the Great Leap years — and the take everyone defaults to is that famines aren't natural disasters but political crimes. I agree with it. Its also, I think, too weak, and it points at the wrong part of the machine.

Here's the thesis I want you to argue for: in the big twentieth-century famines the operative crime was epistemic rather than distributive. What killed people wasn't chiefly that food was taken away or that exchange entitlements collapsed; it was that the information system which would have told the people in charge that anyone was dying got destroyed, corrupted, or — and this is the bit I find harder — got fed perfectly accurate reports which the recipients discounted out of ideological priors. Food policy sits downstream of that break. Note this goes further than the entitlement analysis, which relocates the causal story from harvests to markets but still leaves it sitting in the economy; I'm claiming the interesting failure is upstream of both, in the knowing.

Make the case properly, and specifically:

1. Split the thesis into separable claims before you defend it. At minimum, keep "the signal never got sent" (censorship, killed messengers, cadres inflating output figures to hit quotas, an administration that never formally declares a famine) distinct from "the signal was sent, received, and then not believed or not treated as decision-relevant" (officials who read the reports and concluded the market would sort it out, or that the deaths were an acceptable price for industrialisation). Say which of the two is load-bearing for you and why. If you genuinely need both, say so and explain what the conjunction buys.

2. Use the three cases as evidence rather than decoration. Bengal 1943: the wartime denial policies that stripped boats and rice stocks from the coastal districts, price controls that pushed grain into hoards and then into hyperinflation, a war cabinet unwilling to divert shipping, and a provincial government that declined to call the thing a famine. Ireland 1845–52: grain and livestock kept leaving the country while people died, and a Treasury operating inside a state with a newspaper press and Irish members at Westminster. China 1959–61: over-reported harvests feeding procurement quotas, grain going out for export, and a political climate in which telling the truth about a bad harvest was career-ending and worse. For each, state what the epistemic account predicts you should find, then check whether it's there.

3. Ireland is your hardest case and I want you to treat it as such rather than smuggle it past. Free press, parliamentary representation, food in the country, plenty of accurate reporting — that looks like a refutation of the whole information story, and it's also the standard counterexample to the claim that famines don't happen in functioning democracies with a free press. Either argue that the ideological-filtering variant handles it, or concede something and say how much.

4. Take the strongest objection head on: that this is motivation, not information. Rulers often had the facts and chose otherwise because acting was politically expensive; and if you stretch "epistemic failure" far enough to cover motivated disbelief, your thesis can't fail, which means it stops being a thesis. Answer that without retreating to the truism that politics caused the famine.

5. Tell me what's genuinely new here relative to the familiar democracy-and-free-press claim. If your position is just that claim with extra steps, say so plainly — though I suspect it isn't, since a free press can be read and ignored, and since the Great Leap had plenty of information flowing upward through party channels that didn't translate into relief.

6. Give me the observation that would falsify the thesis, plus one implication for famine prevention that follows if it's true and that would be awkward for the standard send-food-and-fix-entitlements position.

British English please. Somewhere around 1,200–1,500 words, prose over bullet points wherever it can be. No bibliography, no reading list, no suggested further reading — I'm not chasing references and you'd only be guessing at them. If you're unsure of a date or a death toll, say you're unsure rather than rounding a guess into a confident number.
