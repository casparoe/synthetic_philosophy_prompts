I have been asked to lead a seventy-five-minute session on hinge epistemology — the work that Wittgenstein's hinge propositions do in On Certainty — for a reading group of roughly twenty machine-learning doctoral students. None of them has read any Wittgenstein; all of them are fluent in Bayesian reasoning, and they will arrive intending to assimilate hinges to priors, hyperparameters, or structural assumptions of a model. I have a whiteboard and am willing to write down Bayes' rule once, if it earns its place.

Give me the plan for the session, not the explanation itself. I need the order in which the components must be introduced, with the dependency structure that dictates it: what has to be secured before the anti-skeptical point can even be posed, and whether opening with Moore's held-up-hands performance is the right way in or a trap that will make the whole thing look like a reply to a skepticism nobody in the room feels. Tell me the one case I should build the session around, and what that case must exhibit and must conspicuously lack in order to do the work. Tell me how I should settle — and announce early — whether hinges are to be treated as propositions that are true yet not known, or as practical stances that merely look propositional, since the two readings license different payoffs and I cannot fudge between them in front of this audience. Then give me the misconception map: I expect the hinge-as-prior identification and the relativist inference that incompatible hinges leave rival communities equally entitled, but tell me what else this audience will produce, and the precise point in the sequence at which each one should be headed off rather than corrected afterwards. Finally, tell me what I should decline to take up at this level and say so openly, and give me one question I can put at the end that would distinguish a student who understands why a hinge is not a badly evidenced belief from a student who has merely acquired the vocabulary.
