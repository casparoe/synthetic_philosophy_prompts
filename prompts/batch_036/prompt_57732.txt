Last night a friend and I argued for three hours about what Marx's framework can actually tell us about large-scale automation, and at two different points he reached for the same sentence — the much-quoted line from *The Poverty of Philosophy* to the effect that the hand-mill gives you a society of feudal lords and the steam-mill a society of industrial capitalists. I am paraphrasing, since I do not have the wording in front of me, but you will know the passage.

My difficulty is that he deployed it twice, in ways that strike me as incompatible. Early on he used it to dismiss Marx as a crude technological determinist: the artifact produces the social order, so the whole edifice comes down with the first steam-driven economy that remained feudal. Later, pressed on why general-purpose automation should be any different from the mechanization that produced the factory system, he invoked the very same sentence in the opposite direction, as though it guaranteed that a sufficiently general machine technology carries a post-capitalist social form along with it.

What I want is a menu of the distinct claims that sentence could be making, not a defence of whichever one you find most attractive. For each candidate reading, state it precisely enough to be falsifiable: what "gives you" is supposed to denote, what the mill stands in for, whether the relation runs one way or both, whether it is a claim about artifacts, about labor processes, or about whole productive systems, and whether it asserts causation, persistence, or merely explanatory priority. Then say of each whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or simply false.

My own suspicion is that the most defensible reading is a selection claim — social forms that cannot be made to work with the dominant technique do not survive — which is far weaker than determination and yet fully compatible with the deterministic rhetoric. Tell me whether that reading really does the work I want it to. Then settle the question I could not: is there any single reading that supports both of his moves, or does the second use oblige him to withdraw the first? Finally, say what would have to be true of current machine technology, as distinct from the steam engine, for the slogan on its best reading to license any inference whatsoever about where automation leads. Please give me the readings as a numbered list I can bring to the next round.
