Ok, I need a referee, because my friend Nadia and I went at this for like two and a half hours last night and neither of us budged. We were both rereading Pride and Prejudice for my sister's book club (I'm leading the discussion in three weeks, which is the only reason I'm taking this seriously), and somewhere around the second bottle we ended up arguing about Charlotte Lucas. Nadia's line is that Charlotte accepting Mr. Collins is the bleakest thing in the whole novel — a portrait of a woman who has given up on having a life — and that Austen is quietly horrified by it, and that when I defend Charlotte I'm doing exactly the thing she always accuses me of, which is "optimizing your life into a spreadsheet." My line is that Charlotte is the only character in the book who actually reasons well about her own situation: she's twenty-seven, she's never been handsome, she has no fortune, and there's an entail coming that puts her family out of Longbourn, so she takes the first option that clears a reasonable bar instead of gambling on a better one that probably isn't coming. Elizabeth is the one being irrational — she turns down Collins, then turns down Darcy's first proposal on the strength of a story she got from a man she'd known for a few weeks, and she only comes out fine because Austen rigged the draw. Nadia says a novel isn't a decision problem and Charlotte's contentment at Hunsford is a managed decline, not a success story. I don't think she's wrong about the managed part, I just think that's the point.

So here's what I want: a real reading of the Charlotte plot through bounded rationality, with the actual machinery, not the vibe. Where does her aspiration level sit and what in the text tells you it was set there rather than chosen — is "however uncertain of giving happiness, must be their pleasantest preservative from want" a floor under a maximization problem or the whole of the objective function? Is accepting Collins a rational response to search costs, or a settled disposition of character that she'd have had regardless of her circumstances? And how much of her life at Hunsford is second-order satisficing — adopting a policy of not acquiring information that would raise the comparison set? I'm thinking of the scene where Collins is being insufferable about the furniture and the narrator tells us that "in general Charlotte wisely did not hear," of Charlotte owning that she encourages his gardening "as much as possible," of Elizabeth concluding that when Collins could be forgotten the house was genuinely comfortable and "by Charlotte's evident enjoyment of it, Elizabeth supposed he must be often forgotten." That reads to me like deliberate attention management, which in the maximizer/satisficer literature is supposed to be the thing that buys satisficers their lower regret. Then there's chapter six, which is honestly just a fight about sample size and the value of information: Elizabeth itemizes Jane's evidence — "She has known him only a fortnight," four dances, one morning at his house, four dinners in company — and says that isn't enough to know a character, and Charlotte answers that a twelvemonth of studying him wouldn't improve the odds at all, because "Happiness in marriage is entirely a matter of chance" and "it is better to know as little as possible of the defects of the person with whom you are to pass your life." That's not a shrug, that's two claims: that more information has close to zero expected value, and that gathering it has costs, since a woman who plays it too cool "may lose the opportunity of fixing him." I want someone to take that argument seriously and say whether it survives contact with the rest of the book, given that Elizabeth and Darcy do end up knowing each other extremely well and end up happy, and Jane and Bingley end up happy too despite being mutually clueless for most of the year.

The other half, and the half Nadia wouldn't touch: the normative layer. Is there a version of the "good enough isn't good enough when you could have done better" objection that actually bites on Charlotte — the way it bites in the satisficing consequentialism debate, except here it has to be prudential rather than moral, since she's choosing for herself? Can that objection get off the ground at all once you grant that she has no reliable way to rank the offers in front of her, or does Elizabeth's ending prove it can, since Elizabeth at least had decent evidence about her own preferences? And what would a defensible stopping rule even look like for someone in Charlotte's position — is her error (if it is one) that she stopped at the first acceptable option instead of at the point where the expected gain from waiting drops below the risk of the market closing, or is there no such point and she got it exactly right? Two things I want you to be honest about, because I know they cut against me. First, does Austen give Charlotte any interior life after the wedding, or just a handful of summary judgments — the narrator telling us "her reflections were in general satisfactory" and that she felt all the good luck of it — such that the satisficing reading is mostly me filling in a silhouette?
