Our philosophy-of-mind reading group has been arguing about eliminative materialism on and off for about four months and we've gotten absolutely nowhere, which is strange because we all agree on the empirical facts and nobody has produced a new argument since week two. One camp says beliefs and desires are posits of a bad theory, the way phlogiston or demon-possession were posits of bad theories, and that neuroscience will eventually retire the vocabulary rather than reduce it. The other camp says the old self-refutation charge isn't that easy to dodge — you can't even state the thesis without helping yourself to intentional content — and also that the theory-theory picture of folk psychology is itself shaky, since maybe belief-talk isn't a theory at all but something more like a practice or a way of narrating people, in which case 'false' is the wrong predicate to apply to it. Then a third person keeps insisting predictive processing settles the whole thing, except both camps claim predictive processing for themselves, which I find suspicious.

My working theory about why it's stalled, which I want you to either defend or deflate: the disagreement isn't really about beliefs. It's about what it would take to show that a concept has failed, and underneath that, about whether 'does X exist' questions get their content from the explanatory role X plays in a theory or from something else entirely. So the two sides might be answering different questions — one about the fate of a concept, one about the furniture of the world — and using the same sentence to do it.

One clue I haven't been able to use: nobody in the group ever tries to eliminate pain, or seeing, or wanting. It's always belief and desire, and always together, as if they were one package. Is the position term-relative in a way we've been treating as global, and would that change the diagnosis?

So — is my story fair, or is there a genuine substantive disagreement here that I'm dissolving too cheaply? How much of it is verbal? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the work, and could any experiment (a confabulation study, an imaging result, whatever) actually break the tie? I want the structure of the disagreement with the strongest thing each side actually says, and then an honest answer on what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, a new kind of evidence, a change of question — or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. Please write in American English and skip the bibliography entirely; I don't want a reading list, I want the diagnosis.
