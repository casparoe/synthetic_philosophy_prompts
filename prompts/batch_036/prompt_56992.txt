I am co-writing a paper on the metaphysics of measurement standards with a philosopher whose classificatory practice I know well. She sorts declarative sentences into the four boxes formed by the modal and the epistemic axes — necessary or contingent, knowable a priori or a posteriori — and she will rule on sentences drawn from the history of the International System of Units as readily as on the textbook examples.

Her verdicts so far, in her own order:

1. "Hesperus is Phosphorus": necessary, knowable a posteriori.
2. "Water is H₂O": necessary, knowable a posteriori.
3. "Aristotle taught Alexander": contingent, a posteriori.
4. Under the definition in force from 1889, on which the metre was the length of a particular platinum-iridium bar kept near Paris: "That bar is one metre long" — contingent, but knowable a priori by whoever introduced the definition.
5. Under the definition in force until 2019, on which the kilogram was the mass of a particular platinum-iridium cylinder: "That cylinder has a mass of exactly one kilogram" — contingent, a priori. She ruled on this one in 2011 and has not revisited it.
6. The original revolutionary definition, on which the metre was to be one ten-millionth of the distance from the equator to the North Pole along the meridian through Paris: that sentence is contingent, a posteriori, and not in any case exactly true, since the bar and not the meridian became the standard.
7. "One yard is exactly 0.9144 metres," as fixed by international agreement in 1959: necessary, a priori.

The sentence she has now been asked to classify, for a footnote, is:

"Light travels in vacuum at exactly 299,792,458 metres per second."

Background she would take as given, and which you should as well: since 1983 the metre has been defined by fixing the numerical value of the speed of light in vacuum at exactly 299,792,458 when expressed in metres per second, and the 2019 revision of the SI restated that definition while defining the second by fixing the caesium-133 hyperfine transition frequency at exactly 9,192,631,770 hertz. The number adopted in 1983 was the best measured value of the speed of light then available, chosen precisely so that the length of the metre would not appreciably change; before 1983 the metre had been defined by a specified number of wavelengths of krypton-86 radiation, and before that by the bar. In the same 2019 revision the kilogram ceased to be defined by the cylinder and was defined instead by fixing the Planck constant at exactly 6.62607015 × 10⁻³⁴ joule-seconds.

Please supply the following. (a) Two or three candidate rules that account for all seven of her verdicts, each stated compactly and explicitly enough that its content is testable against the list. (b) The classification each rule yields for the footnote sentence, and the point at which the rules come apart. (c) Your best guess at her actual verdict, together with the reason for preferring that rule over the others given the evidence in the list. (d) One further sentence, drawn from the same metrological material but not already on the list, whose classification would separate your two leading rules — stating what each of them predicts for it. If you think the footnote sentence admits of more than one reading, for instance of "metre" or of "the speed of light," on which its classification would differ, say which reading her rule selects and why, rather than offering the ambiguity itself as your answer.

Begin with a single sentence giving the classification you predict. Keep the entire response within 700 words, with part (a) as a short labelled list.
