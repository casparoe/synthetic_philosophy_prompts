Last night a friend of mine and I argued for nearly three hours about Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go, and neither of us budged, so I want a reading from someone outside the quarrel. We are both market ethicists by training — I work on organ allocation, she works on the philosophy of emotion — and our disagreement is about whether the novel belongs in the repugnant-markets literature at all, or whether readers like us have simply conscripted it. Her position is that the book vindicates Leon Kass: the donation program survives only for as long as the wider public is permitted not to look, and once the Morningdale scandal forces society to confront what its medicine rests upon, the whole enterprise collapses, so the revulsion is doing genuine moral work and Ishiguro endorses that work. My position is that it vindicates Alvin Roth instead: the disgust that destroys Hailsham is aimed at genetic enhancement, at the kind of children scientists might manufacture, rather than at the taking of organs, and the clones end up worse off precisely because the public's feeling fastened onto the wrong object. Repugnance, on my reading, is shown to be a poor moral tracker. What neither of us could settle is that the novel never discusses selling organs at all — no price, no broker, no market in anything the donors possess — which makes it strange evidence for either side.

I would like the criticism our argument kept failing to produce. Please work through four things. First, the internal economy of Hailsham: the students are rewarded for creative work, they trade pieces with one another at the Exchanges, and they spend tokens at the Sales, while the best work is removed by Madame for a gallery whose purpose nobody understands. Is the students' art commodified there, and does the token system corrupt what the art is for, or is it the only medium through which these children can register that their work matters to someone? Satz's criteria for noxious markets, Sandel's distinction between the corruption objection and the fairness objection, and Anderson's insistence that different goods call for different modes of valuation all seem to cut differently on that scene, and I want you to say which of them actually fits. Second, the Norfolk trip: the students half-believe that Norfolk is where everything lost in England washes up, and Kathy's lost Judy Bridgewater cassette turns up in a secondhand shop where money changes hands. Treat that as a small case in the ethics of resale — someone charging for the return of a good that was never his to keep — and say what the characters' reaction to it commits the novel to. Third, and most important, the absence of a market exactly where the real-world debate puts one: the organs are neither sold nor freely given but simply expected, embedded in a vocabulary of duty and completion that leaves the donors no way to name an injustice. Would pricing the clones' organs be more or less repugnant than that gift-and-obligation structure, and does Ishiguro's refusal to price anything amount to a position rather than an evasion? Fourth, the deferral rumor — a couple who are truly in love may postpone donation, supposedly — as a non-market rationing device keyed to an emotion rather than to willingness to pay: what does the fact that it turns out to be false imply about inalienability, and about who gets to decide what a life is worth?

Ground rules: American English, continuous prose rather than bullet points, roughly fifteen hundred words, and no quotations from the novel, since I have the book in front of me and want your reading rather than your recall. If you think I have misremembered a scene or an emphasis, say so plainly and then work from the text as you understand it. No references and no reading list. Above all, be faithful in both directions — do not flatten the novel into an illustration of a market-ethics thesis, and do not bend Satz or Anderson or Roth to make them speak to it — and where the market framing is something readers like us bring rather than something the book licenses, say that explicitly and say what the book is doing in its place. Close by telling me which of the two positions from our argument the better reading favors, or whether the honest verdict is that the novel defeats both; and then tell me what Ishiguro adds to the repugnance debate that a journal article on kidney sales could not have said. My own suspicion is that it has to do with who bears the moral phenomenology of a repugnant transaction and what vocabulary they are given for objecting to it, but I want your version of that thought, not mine.
