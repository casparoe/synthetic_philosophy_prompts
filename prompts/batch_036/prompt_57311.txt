I'm presenting at a law school's human rights colloquium next month. The thesis: that conditional amnesty is not a second-best substitute for criminal prosecution but a distinct form of accountability, one that in some circumstances does more for victims than trials would. The argument turns on a distinction I have coined for the paper, between vertical amnesty — granted from above, class-wide, unconditioned, extinguishing the state's claim while requiring nothing of the perpetrator — and horizontal amnesty, which is individualized, sought by the perpetrator, and conditional on full disclosure before a public body, so that the state's claim is not waived but satisfied by a performance. I argue that the duty to prosecute is owed to victims rather than being a duty to imprison, and that horizontal amnesty can discharge it. I also argue that the international norm against amnesty is publicly justified on the claim that amnesty prolongs conflicts, a claim most of its proponents do not actually rely on and could not evidence, and that the mismatch between the stated and the real justification does damage of its own.

The room will be mostly international criminal lawyers and human rights litigators who treat the duty to prosecute as near-absolute, two quantitative civil-war scholars who will go after my empirical claim on identification grounds, and one former staff member of a truth commission speaking from institutional memory.

Give me the question period: the eight or so objections this audience will actually raise, in roughly the order they will come and weighted by how much damage each does, phrased in the vocabulary of whoever raises it, each with a reply I can deliver in ninety seconds, what I should concede before delivering it, and where the reply is soft. Close with the question I am hoping no one asks, and why it is the dangerous one. Where you refer to actual commissions or judgments, stay with what you are confident of; I want the argument, not a list of precedents. Please write it as continuous prose, the way a colleague would in a long email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
