So my roommate is writing his undergrad thesis on free will and I've been shamelessly looting his reading pile, which is how I ended up loosing most of last weekend to Galen Strawson's Basic Argument. He tried to explain it to me first but I think I got the actual version: what you do in any given situation you do because of the way you are, so to be really responsible for the action you'd have to be responsible for the way you are, at least in the mental respects that matter. But you didn't make yourself, so then you'd have to be responsible for whatever made you that way, and then for that, all the way back — basically you'd have to be your own cause. Nothing is. So nobody's ever morally responsible in the sense that would make it fair to reward or punish them for what they do. I found that pretty devastating, in a satisfying kind of way.

Here's what I want to check before I bring it up in his seminar (I'm auditing, unofficially). It has to be a determinism argument in disguise, right? The only reason actions "flow from" character is that the past plus the laws of nature fix everything downstream. So if there's genuine indeterminism in the world — quantum noise bubbling up into the brain, or some agent-causal power where I can just make an uncaused choice — doesn't the regress never get going? I keep seeing people insist Strawson says the argument is neutral about determinism and I honestly can't see how. If my choice isn't settled by anything about me then it isn't flowing from my character at all, and if it is settled by something about me then we're back to needing responsibility for that thing. Where exactly does the argument bite in an indeterministic world, as opposed to just being the old determinism problem with extra steps?

Second thing, and this is what my roommate and I actually fought about at 2am. I've started taking the argument seriously in my life: I don't resent my ex anymore, I've stopped feeling like gratitude and praise mean anything beyond "that worked, do it again," and I basically treat blaming people as a training technique, like with a dog. He says I'm overshooting, that Strawson is only making a point about "true" or desert-entailing responsibility and isn't telling anyone to give up resentment, and that his dad's Freedom and Resentment paper is exactly the reason to keep the attitudes around. But if the Basic Argument is right, how can resentment not be irrational? It seems to presuppose precisely the thing the argument says is impossible — that the person could have been otherwise in a way that was ultimately up to them. Is my roommate reading a retreat into Strawson that isnt there, or am I the one who went too far?
