You are a methodologist of philosophical thought experiments: your job is to audit argumentative devices rather than to defend or attack any of the substantive positions they are deployed for. Set aside, as far as you can, your own view on the questions these cases are meant to settle; where you suspect your substantive commitments are pushing a score in one direction, say so explicitly and give the direction of the push. Think carefully before writing, and begin with a short brainstorming pass rather than going straight to the table.

**The rubric.** Score each case below on five dimensions, each from 0.0 to 1.0, using the following definitions.

1. **Load-bearing stipulation (L).** The share of the elicited verdict that rests on stipulated details no one would independently accept — physically impossible machinery, stipulations inserted precisely to foreclose an obvious reply, or idealizations (exact duplication, exact knowledge, perfect isolation) that do the work rather than merely tidy the case. High L means the verdict would evaporate under a more realistic description.
2. **Paraphrase fragility (F).** Here score the *surviving fraction*, so that 1.0 means robust. Perform the de-loading operation: rewrite the case with all evaluative and affective vocabulary replaced by neutral functional description, strip any detail that cues a social role or an identity, and neutralize order-of-presentation effects. How much of the target intuition survives the rewrite?
3. **Convergence (C).** Your estimate of the fraction of informed readers who would report the target intuition, discounting variation you have reason to expect across backgrounds and framings.
4. **Reach (R).** The gap between what the intuition actually licenses and what the case is standardly taken to establish — between "this described case has feature X" and "no case of kind K has feature X," or "principle P is false." High R means the case is being asked to carry a general conclusion it cannot support.
5. **Yield (Y).** The durability of what the case leaves behind *even when its author's conclusion is rejected*: does the distinction or concept it introduced stay in use among people who deny the argument?

Also tag each case **D** or **G**: is it standardly used *diagnostically* (to test a principle against a case) or *generatively* (to introduce a distinction or a piece of vocabulary)? Compute Y under the use the case is actually put to, and note wherever you think the standard use is the wrong one — a case that fails as a diagnostic may be entirely legitimate as a generative device, and your tier placement must reflect the use, not the ambition.

Then produce a composite score, **epistemic yield per unit of distortion**, from L, F, C, R, and Y. You choose the weighting, but you must state the weights before you score anything, justify the scheme in no more than 150 words, and use the same weights throughout.

**The cases.** They are described rather than named or attributed, so that nothing turns on recall of who said what; if you think one of them is a poor exemplar for this exercise, you may replace it with a better one, provided you say why and gloss it in the same way.

a. Pushing a very heavy man off a footbridge into the path of a runaway trolley, stopping it and saving five people on the track ahead.
b. Waking up surgically connected to an unconscious stranger whose bloodstream yours alone can filter; disconnecting him kills him, staying connected for nine months saves him.
c. A machine that would give you any sequence of experiences you choose while you float in a tank.
d. A person in a room who follows a rulebook to manipulate symbols in a language he does not understand, well enough that outsiders judge the room to understand it.
e. A molecule-for-molecule duplicate of you that behaves exactly as you do but in which nothing is experienced.
f. A neuroscientist who knows every physical fact about color vision but has lived her whole life in a black-and-white room, and now steps out and sees red.
g. Glancing at a clock that reads 2:00 and forming the true belief that it is 2:00, when in fact the clock stopped some hours earlier and has read 2:00 ever since.
h. Driving through a county in which most structures that look like barns from the road are façades, and looking at the one real barn.
i. A scanner that destroys your body while building a duplicate elsewhere; and the variant in which the original survives and two duplicates are made.
j. Wading into a shallow pond to save a drowning child at the cost of ruining your clothes.

**Deliverables, in this order.**

1. The brainstorming pass: which of the five dimensions you expect to be most diagnostic of whether a case's reputation is deserved, and which pairs you expect to be redundant with one another — that is, likely to move together so that one of them adds nothing.
2. A single table: case, D/G tag, L, F, C, R, Y, composite, and tier (Tier 1 = the case still does the work people think it does; Tier 4 = it should be retired or explicitly quarantined).
3. Per-case notes of two or three sentences each, saying what specifically earns the case its placement. Do not pad these.
4. The two placements most sensitive to your weighting choice: name them, and exhibit an alternative weighting, defended in a sentence or two, under which each moves a tier.
5. A rubric audit: identify the one dimension you now think is the weakest measure, explain the failure by reference to at least two of the cases above, and propose a replacement dimension, scoring those two cases under it.
6. A closing note on direction of bias: for each case where you think your own substantive view inflated or deflated a score, flag it.

No citations, bibliographies, or attributions are wanted or needed; work entirely from the descriptions given and from your own analysis of how the cases operate.
