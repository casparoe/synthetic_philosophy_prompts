So — I write a weekly science-and-ideas column for a general-interest magazine, mostly neuroscience and AI plus whatever philosophers are arguing about that leaks into the lab down the hall. In about five weeks I'm giving the closing talk at a science-communication conference that a university philosophy department is co-hosting, so the room is going to be a weird mix: something like sixty working journalists and magazine editors, eight or nine academic philosophers (a couple of whom work on thought experiments and think people like me butcher their material), and two comms psychologists who study narrative persuasion.

Twenty-five minutes of talk, twenty of Q&A. Here's the thesis I'm going to defend. Everybody already has a complaint about intuition pumps — that they're intuition-mongering, that they're just arguments wearing a costume, that intuitions are junk data because they shift with demographics and armchair training. I think that's the wrong complaint. My claim is that a thought experiment ships with an invisible harness: a bundle of unstated constraints that a trained audience supplies automatically. Only the stated facts vary. The technology works exactly as described, never mind the engineering. We're asking for a verdict about permissibility, not about what you'd personally feel like doing. The subject is an ordinary adult with no special history. Nobody has a third option. When a case gets retold for a general readership, harness members get shed one at a time, and they get shed silently, because a sentence stating a ceteris paribus condition reads like throat-clearing and it's the first thing an editor cuts. So the reader runs the pump raw, lands somewhere, and the writer reports that landing as though it were the expert verdict. It isn't. It's a different question in the same costume. And it compounds, because each generation of writers retells the retelling instead of the original — I want to call that harness debt, and its seperate thing from ordinary dumbing-down, which at least keeps the structure intact.

Two things I think keep this from being a vibe. It makes predictions: for any given pump you should be able to say in advance where lay verdicts will diverge from expert consensus, by identifying which constraint the retelling dropped. And there's a diagnostic I've been calling the swap test — take a retold version, vary one detail the original argument treats as irrelevant, and see whether the reported intuition flips. If it flips, the harness is gone and what you're measuring isn't the thought experiment anymore. I have real material for all of this: the self-driving-car trolley coverage on my own beat, a retold teleporter case I ran last spring where the comment section answered a question I hadnt asked, and a color-blind-scientist-sees-red-for-the-first-time sidebar that got quoted back at me for a year in a form I never actually wrote.

What I need from you is the prep sheet, not a defense of the view. Give me the objections this particular room will actually raise, in the words those people would use — the philosopher who thinks I've dressed up framing effects in nicer clothes, the comms psychologist who thinks I've dressed up framing effects from the opposite direction, the veteran editor who thinks the whole thing reduces to write clearer, the grad student who'll say the harness is unfalsifiable because I can always insist some constraint got dropped. Order them by how much damage they'd do. For each one, a reply I can actually deliver in under two minutes, and an honest mark of where that reply is soft. Tell me what to concede in the first five minutes so it can't be taken from me later, which single objection has to be answered for the talk to survive, and which of my three examples is the one I should not use on stage and why. Then give me a straight answer — not a dodge — to the question I'm praying nobody asks: isn't the harness theory itself an intuition pump with no harness, since every counterexample can be re-described as a missing constraint?

No bibliography, no reading list, I know roughly where to look and I don't have time for it anyway. And don't soften the bad news. I'd much rather find the hole in this argument in an empty room than in front of eighty people, half of whom write for a living.
