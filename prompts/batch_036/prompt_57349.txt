I keep running into a dialectical pattern in debates about the two familiar cosmological arguments — the one that reasons from the universe's having a beginning, and the one that reasons from the contingency of things — and I don't know what to call it. Granted on their own, each falls short of theism: the beginning argument is said to yield at most an uncaused cause, with nothing in it ruling out an impersonal or mechanical one, and the contingency argument at most something whose existence is necessary, which Spinoza's substance or the universe itself might equally satisfy. But when the first is pressed on why the cause must be personal, the reply is that the second has already secured a necessary being and the two arguments concern the same thing, so personality comes from over there; when the second is pressed on why the necessary being must be a free creator rather than a blind ground of being, the reply is that the first showed a cause that had to choose to produce an effect in time, so will comes from over there. Each argument's shortfall is discharged by an appeal to the other, and the pair is then offered as one combined case for a personal necessary creator.

Is there an established term for this? I'm not after the general complaint that such arguments stop short of God — I take it that objection has a name of its own — but the specific move of pairing two arguments so that each is said to supply what the other lacks. It doesn't look like two independent lines of evidence converging, since neither establishes that what the other delivers is the same thing, and if that identity is simply assumed, the gaps seem relocated rather than filled. Nor is it a retreat to a weaker claim once pressure arrives. Which established concepts come nearest, how does what I've described differ from each, and which fits best — or, if none does, how would you name and define it, including where it shades into a legitimate way of combining arguments?
