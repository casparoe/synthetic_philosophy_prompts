**Question 3 (10 marks, aim for about 400 words).** State van Fraassen's Principle of Reflection as precisely as you can. Explain what it does and does not have in common with Lewis's Principal Principle. Finally: Maya has volunteered for a study in which she will be given a drug tomorrow morning that reliably produces in her the unshakeable conviction that she is Napoleon. Does Reflection require Maya, today, to be confident that she is Napoleon? Use the case to say what limits, if any, Reflection has.

**Student's answer.**

Reflection says your present opinion about your future opinion should agree with it. Precisely: your credence now in A, conditional on your credence in A at the later time being p, should itself be p. The slogan is that you shouldn't expect to change your mind. If you knew you'd believe A to degree .9 tomorrow, you should believe A to degree .9 today, and any mismatch is a kind of incoherence — the diachronic analogue of holding contradictory beliefs at once. Van Fraassen backs it with a dynamic Dutch book: whoever violates Reflection will now accept a package of bets that guarantees them a loss, whatever evidence turns up. It comes out of his work on belief and the will, and its point is to cover cases where you deliberately arrange your own future opinions.

Lewis's Principal Principle turns out to be a special case of Reflection. Both are deference principles. Lewis says your credence in A should match what you take the chance of A to be; van Fraassen says your credence in A should match what you take your later credence in A to be. So Lewis has just substituted chance for the future self, and since a chance can't be mistaken about the thing it's the chance of, the Principal Principle is the infallible version of the rule and Reflection the fallible one. Both are constraints on how credences develop across time, which is why both of them can be attacked with Dutch books that span two time points.

There's also a corollary worth stating: your credence now in A should equal your expected later credence in A. But that follows from conditionalization alone, together with the assumption that you will conditionalize on your total later evidence, so it's a theorem rather than an extra rule. Which means Reflection isn't some further requirement sitting on top of Bayesianism. You can't violate it without also failing to conditionalize.

On Maya: Reflection requires her to be confident now that she will be confident tomorrow that she is Napoleon. It does not require her to be confident now that she is Napoleon, because Reflection is only ever about beliefs about beliefs. That dissolves the puzzle and shows the principle is much weaker than its critics pretend. The case does expose a limit on the rationale, though. The reason to defer to your future self is that they will be better informed than you are, and Maya's future self will be worse off rather than better, so her situation simply falls outside the principle's intended range. That's a little unsatisfying, since nobody has ever said precisely what "better informed" is supposed to mean, and the restriction looks like it's being tailored to whatever case threatens the principle.
