I spent 35 years as a limnologist (lakes, mostly phosphorus budgets) and since retiring I've fallen down a philosophy-of-science hole — Popper first, then the people who think Popper was too tidy, the Duhem-Quine business and Lakatos on ad hoc rescues. It turns out that's directly relevant to what I do now, which is volunteer on a county microgrant panel: six of us, only two with any lab background, handing out $500–$3,000 to local projects. A couple of times a year we get an application that is not testable in any sense I recognise, and right now we just argue for forty minutes and vote.

Could you write us a procedure we can actually run in about fifteen minutes per application — the questions in order, what counts as a passing answer to each, and where it stops at "no"? Two things it has to handle honestly rather than wave at. First, the hard middle: a claim that's testable in principle but where every failed test can be blamed on the apparatus or the operator, versus an ordinary measurement where the background assumptions are shared and someone else can check them — I want a way to tell those apart that doesn't just amount to "we know a crank when we see one." Second, how to spot the difference between a legitimate fix to a method and a patch whose only job is to save the claim, ideally with something about whether the fix buys you any new predictions or only excuses the old one. My own rough idea, which you can sharpen, keep or throw out, is what I've taken to calling the "who else would notice" test: write down the specific result that would make the applicant say they were wrong, then ask whether anybody besides the applicant could have anticipated that result or verify it afterwards.

Then run it on two applications. (a) A group wants money to map groundwater for a community garden with dowsing rods; they've designed their own blinded trial, but the protocol says the rods only respond if none of the testers present is secretly sceptical, so a failed trial always has a ready explanation. (b) A high-school club wants to count whooping swans on the reservoir every March for three years as evidence the lake "is getting healthier" — I like these kids and I've spent my life on that lake, so I'd really rather not have the procedure vote them down, but I can't see what result would count against their hypothesis.

Mark which steps are actually judgement calls dressed up as procedure, and tell me what the thing can't decide, since the vote is by majority and the two of us with science backgrounds shouldn't be able to steamroll the rest. No jargon we'd have to explain to the others — or if you need a term, define it in one line.
