I supervise a dissertation on the metaphysics of individuation, and my student's external examiner is a colleague whose verdicts on a sequence of thought experiments I recorded when he visited our departmental seminar last term. Her central case is one he was never asked about, and I should much rather know what he will say before the viva than during it.

Every case below concerns a universe containing nothing but what is mentioned, unchanging from eternity to eternity.

1. Two iron spheres exactly alike in every intrinsic respect, a mile apart, at rest relative to one another, in an otherwise empty space that is uniform at every place and in every direction. — Not a possible world. In any world like that, he said, there is at most one sphere. He took this to be the correct response to Black, whose symmetrical universe he regards as a bad description rather than a good counterexample.
2. The same, except that one sphere bears a shallow scratch the other lacks. — Possible; no difficulty whatever.
3. The same as 1, except that the universe also contains a single free electron, nearer to one sphere than to the other. — Possible; and he observed that the electron is doing all the work.
4. The same as 3, except that the electron sits exactly midway between them. — Not possible; the electron might as well not exist.
5. Three exactly similar spheres at the vertices of an equilateral triangle, nothing else. — Not possible; asked whether a hundred such spheres would fare better, he said that multiplying them changes nothing.
6. Two exactly similar spheres a mile apart, nothing else, except that the space has a centre — one place unlike every other — and one of the spheres sits at it. — Possible: they are then as different as can be. But he added that whether a description of that sort picks out a genuine possibility is a further question, which he was not prepared to settle on the spot.

Two proposals from the floor also deserve recording. When it was suggested that the spheres in 1 are distinguished after all by the relation of being a mile from one another, he said that both spheres bear that relation and bear it in the same way, so it does no individuating work. When it was suggested that we simply stipulate that one is Alpha and the other Omega, he said that we had named them rather than distinguished them.

My student's case is this: a universe containing two exactly similar iron spheres a mile apart and nothing else material, in a space that is closed, finite and lopsided — its curvature varies from place to place, and there is exactly one place at which it is greatest. One sphere occupies that place; the other does not.

I should be grateful for four things. First, state as precisely as you can the principles that would generate all six verdicts together with his two replies from the floor; I expect at least three candidates that are not merely notational variants of one another, and I should like to be told if any of them in fact coincide. Second, say what each predicts for my student's universe, and whether any is ruled out by verdict 6 or merely strained by it. Third, give me your best guess at his actual verdict, with the reasons for preferring the principle you attribute to him over the others; identify which of the six is the closest analogue to her case, and explain why that analogy may mislead. Fourth, describe one further case — in enough detail that I could put it to him verbatim — that would discriminate between the two leading candidates. And if you think her case, as I have stated it, invites a merely verbal answer, tell me how she ought to restate it so that his reply will reveal his principle rather than his scruples about the description.
