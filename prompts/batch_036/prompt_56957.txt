You're a social choice theorist who's also sat on real governance committees, and I want the version of this that's more specific than the Gibbard–Satterthwaite spiel that shows up in every voting-reform thread I've ever read. Think it through carefully: before you recommend anything, list every principle that's consistent with our track record, then run each one against the new case.

I'm on my org's process committee (~900 members). Our precedents so far:

- Plurality, two nominees: fine.
- Plurality, five nominees: not fine — we minuted the worry that somebody most members rank last could take it.
- IRV over those same five: fine, no reason minuted.
- A deadlocked subcommittee proposed drawing one member's ballot at random and letting it decide the whole thing: not fine. Someone called it a dictatorship wearing a lottery costume.
- A show-of-hands rule that's trivially gameable: fine for picking meeting times, not fine for the annual budget.
- A bylaw tweak that made two of five nominees constitutionally unwinnable, though the rule was otherwise anonymous and non-dictatorial: not fine.

New proposal for the chair election, five nominees: every member places the nominees on a single left–right line, and the nominee sitting at the median of the placed positions wins. It's provably manipulation-proof at the ballot stage (reporting your true spot is dominant), non-dictatorial, and every nominee stays winnable. The catch: the committee has to pick the axis first, in practice one person picks it, and with a badly chosen axis you can elect a nominee that a majority ranks below all four others.

So — what should we decide if we want to be consistant with ourselves? Which precedent is the closest analog, what principle does each option silently commit us to, and what's the single fact about our past rulings that would best settle which principle we've actually been running all along?
