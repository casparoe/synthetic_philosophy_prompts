I've been shortlisted for a six-month postdoc in a formal epistemology group and they want about 1500 words on what I'd actually do with the time. Deadline is friday. What I have so far is a hunch, not a plan — can you turn it into the proposal?

The hunch. Most of the energy in the literature on imprecise credence (sets of priors, credal sets, take your pick) goes into two fights: whether the imprecise agent can make coherent sequential decisions — the Elga-style objection that she'll turn down, one at a time, a package of bets she'd happily buy as a bundle — and whether her credal set misbehaves on learning, i.e. dilation and belief inertia. What I dont see anyone asking is the collapse question. Is the imprecise model adding expressive power at all, or is it a notational variant of a sharp model that just spends its precision somewhere else? Take any credal-set agent. Can you build a perfectly sharp Bayesian who is uncertain about extra stuff — which prior in the set is the true one, what evidence she'll get later, which updating rule she'll end up using, her own future credences — such that she matches the imprecise agent on everything thats supposed to matter: which bets she accepts at which prices, how her beliefs move on the same evidence, whether she dilates? If yes in general, the imprecise Bayesian owes us a reason to prefer the clunkier notation. If no, then the counterexample is precisely the phenomenon doing the real work, and we finally have something determinate to mean by "unspecific evidence calls for unspecific credence."

So the project: characterize which behaviors of a credal-set agent are exactly reproducible by a sharp higher-order Bayesian and which aren't, and pin down what "reproducible" is supposed to mean.

What I need in the document: a statement of the question precise enough that it has a determinate answer, which means making the reproduction criterion explicit — I suspect that's where the interesting choice is, since matching unconditional credences is too weak and matching every conditional is probably too strong. Then why neither side of the existing debate settles this: they argue about rationality and I'm asking about expressivity, and the proposal needs to say why that's a genuinely different question rather than a dodge. Then the method, month by month across the six months, beginning with fixing the target formalism and the class of higher-order models I'm allowed to use as rivals. Then what a result looks like — I'd be satisfied with a representation theorem in one direction plus one clean counterexample in the other — and what would count as the project failing. Then an honest scope check: is this doable in six months by one person who has to build the framework from scratch, and what do I cut if it isn't.

Two things I want handled head-on. First, there's a theorem I half-remember to the effect that you can't avoid dilation with respect to every possible piece of evidence unless your prior is non-measurable. If that's real it matters a lot here, since it would mean the imprecise side is already being pushed into exotic mathematics for reasons independent of my question — work it in if you can, and if I'm mangling it say so plainly rather than papering over it. Second, tell me which framings I should refuse: I can already hear one person saying just adopt a decision rule like maximin or alpha-maximin and stop asking, and another saying this is a rerun of the argument over whether higher-order evidence does anything at all. Explain why neither one dissolves the collapse question.
