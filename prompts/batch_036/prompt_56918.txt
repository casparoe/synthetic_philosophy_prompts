I'm the person who ended up with the made-up title of "narrative standards reviewer" at a mid-size game studio — basically I read every mission script, trailer line, and achievement description for our war games and flag stuff before it goes up to the creative director, who has final say. We're about a year out from shipping a realistic-ish contemporary-war single-player campaign, and I get maybe six to eight mission beats a month across my desk plus marketing copy, with roughly 45 minutes per beat. The people writing these are good writers with zero philosophy background, and they hate being told "it's complicated," so whatever I hand them has to be runnable: a list of questions in a fixed order, what counts as a passing answer to each, the points where the thing just gets a no, and the points where it has to kick upstairs to the creative director because it's a values call and not something a checklist can settle.

The reason I need this is that the studio has an official house principle we've been operating under, which I'll call the Symmetry Default: unless a beat explicitly earns an exception, combatants on both sides get presented as equally entitled to be fighting and equally worth mourning, and the game never invites the player to condemn an individual enemy soldier on account of whose war he's serving in. It was a deliberate choice. We got hammered a few years back by an academic who said our "everyone's just a soldier doing his job" framing effectively laundered an invasion, and then last year by a veterans group who said the mission that lingered on one enemy conscript's life treated our own guys as objects with no interiority, and I still cant articulate why both complaints are aimed at the same underlying argument. The philosophy underneath it, as best I understand: there's the older view that soldiers on either side of a war stand in the same moral position, that a man fighting for an unjust cause isn't a criminal because he's acting as the instrument of a state inside a set of conventions everyone benefits from, and that this equality is what makes the rules of war workable at all — you can't get an army to accept limits on how it fights while simultaneously telling its soldiers they're murderers for showing up. And then there's the revisionist line that liability to be killed tracks whether the threat you pose is itself justified, so a soldier fighting a war of aggression doesn't really have a moral right to kill the people defending themselves, and the symmetry is a legal and practical fiction rather than the truth, its a thing we agree to pretend, not a thing that's so. I don't need the procedure to referee that argument or point me at anybody to read. I need it to be honest about which side of the argument our Symmetry Default is quietly smuggling in, and to tell a writer when a beat can keep leaning on the default and when the default is going to produce something false or ugly.

So: write me the procedure. Questions in a fixed order, each with a short note on what a good answer looks like and what a non-answer looks like, the places where it stops at cut-or-rewrite, and the places where it hands the decision to a human because it's judgment wearing a checklist costume — mark those explicitly rather than burying them. Then run it on three things that are actually sitting on my desk right now. One: a mission where the player's side is clearly the invader and the setpiece is a night raid on a checkpoint held by very young conscripts, shown joking around and reading letters home, with no dialogue indicating they know their country started it. Two: a mission where the enemy commander the player finally kills has been established across four hours of play as personally responsible for massacres, including against his own people. Three: a trailer line, something like "no heroes, no villains, only survivors," laid over a montage that cuts between both armies. At least one of those should come out in a way I'm not going to like — I'd rather the procedure bite on our signature beat than flatter us. And finish with what the procedure flat out can't do: the cases where two honest writers will run it and land in different places, the beats it will misjudge, and the spots where it's really just encoding my taste and calling it method.
