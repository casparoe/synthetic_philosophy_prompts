I'm putting together a one-pager for a reading group that's half philosophy of science, half economic history, and we've now spent three weeks going round in circles on the 'why Britain and not China?' question. I want to kill the circling. Build me a decision tree that sorts people by what they actually mean when they ask it, and — where it can — tells them whether what they mean is a well posed question at all.

The crucial bit: every branch has to turn on the *form* of the question, not on which causal story you fancy. I don't want a node going 'do you think coal and colonies mattered?' — that's an answer, not a question. Nodes should be things a reader can settle just by interrogating their own interrogative: what's my contrast class? is my explanandum an event, a level, or a date? is the foil one place or a set of places? are the two sides even the same kind of unit? and so on.

Fix the root for me: 'why Britain and not China' splits into at least three readings — a location reading, a timing reading, and a 'why did sustained growth get going anywhere' reading — and I want those as the first branches with everything else hanging off them. If you think there's a fourth (I half suspect there's one lurking about *the form the growth took* rather than where or when), add it, but justify it in a line.

At each leaf I want four things:

1. a short descriptive name for the position — your own coinages, don't copy mine — in the style of 'well posed single-foil difference-maker question', 'mismatched units diagnosis', 'explanandum drift', 'necessary-but-not-sufficient', 'foil set too big for any single contrast to be privileged', 'there is no divergence in the data'.
2. one sentence on the most awkward thing the position is now committed to.
3. a foil-sensitivity test, which is my own term: re-ask the question three times, in this order — Britain rather than the Yangzi Delta, Britain rather than China as a whole, Britain rather than anywhere else in Eurasia — and say for each leaf whether it survives all three, survives one or two, or dies on the spot. Be strict here; this is the actual point of the exercise, and I'd rather have a leaf marked 'dies immediately' than a diplomatic shrug.
4. whether the leaf is occupied. Is there a live view that lands there, or is it a combination that's logically available and nobody defends? If it's empty, say why: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Also:

- somewhere around six to nine nodes and ten to fourteen leaves, and keep it printable on one side of A4.
- flag every pair of leaves your tree can't actually separate. I strongly suspect two of the ill-posedness diagnoses will collapse into each other and I'd much rather you said so than papered over it.
- give me a branch on whether the relevant counterfactual is even truth-apt — the 'would China have industrialised by 1850 if Britain hadn't' move, where the closest world is so far from ours that I'm not sure there's a fact of the matter. Let the reader say yes, no, or 'only for a very short antecedent', and make sure those three answers go somewhere different.
- one branch on whether a single-foil question is ever answerable when the honest causal story gives you a bundle of jointly necessary conditions, none of which seperates the case on its own.
- finish with three little vignettes of people asking the question out loud in a seminar, each landing on a different leaf, so readers can watch the tree work.

No reading list, no citations, no bibliography, please. If you want to hang a scholar's name on a leaf, only do it when you're confident you've recognised the view correctly — otherwise just write 'unnamed'. A wrong attribution is worse to me than none.
