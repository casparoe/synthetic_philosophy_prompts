Co-teaching a 75-minute session on the Lucas–Penrose arguement with a joint CS/philosophy reading group. The catch is that everyone in the room has already done a Gödel course — they can run the diagonal lemma, most have sketched the second theorem on the board before. So I don't want to re-teach incompleteness, and I especially don't want the "here's a Turing machine, here's a sentence it can't prove" retelling they'll have absorbed from a hundred YouTube videos. What I need is a plan for teaching the philosophical argument, not the argument itself.

Some things I want the plan to settle for me:

- The order. I keep flip-flopping on whether the second theorem has to be in place before Lucas's move even makes sense, or whether it only enters with the replies. Give me the dependency structure, claim by claim, and say which theorem is load-bearing at which point — that's the thing I can't get straight in my own head.

- The one example to hang everything on. I was going to build on "an AI that outputs all and only the truths of arithmetic," but I worry that smuggles soundness in before anyone notices there was a premise to notice. Tell me whether to keep it, patch it, or swap it, and what the carrying example has to have and what it must not have.

- The idealization ladder. My reading is that the argument needs the human to be consistent, then to know she's consistent, then sound, then to know she's sound — and that basically every objection is an attack on a specific rung. Lay the rungs out in the order you'd introduce them and map the objections onto them, including the "we don't know our own program" one and the point that an unsound machine could happily assert the Gödel sentence too, which is the move my students never see coming.

- The misconceptions and exactly where in the sequence to head each off. I'm braced for "Gödel proves AI is impossible" and "the sentence means 'I'm unprovable' so the human is just doing more arithmetic" — tell me the ones I'm not braced for.

- What I should openly refuse to cover. I assume Penrose's microtubule stuff gets cut, but tell me what else, and what sentence I should say out loud when I cut it so nobody thinks I'm dodging.

- One closing question that separates a student who actually got the argument from one who left with the vague feeling that machines are limited.

No reading list please, I've got the texts already.
