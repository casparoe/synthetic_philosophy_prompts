I cover science and ideas for a national magazine, and for most of the past year I've been reporting inside a large academic hospital's patient-safety operation. Two rooms keep turning up in my notebooks, and I can't work out how the people who work in both of them think they fit together. I'd like a reconstruction of what each one assumes about responsibility, and about whether the pairing is coherent.

The first room is the serious-event review. After something goes badly wrong — a wrong-site block, a tenfold heparin overdose, sepsis missed on a night shift — the case goes to a multidisciplinary panel whose ground rules are taught explicitly to members. The minutes are confidential and legally privileged, so they cannot be used in litigation. Names are kept out of the write-up. A facilitator redirects any remark that starts to shade into fault-finding back toward conditions: the pump interface, the handoff script, the staffing pattern, the workaround everyone has been using for two years. The output is a list of latent conditions and corrective actions, and the clinician involved often sits in the room and is expected to narrate what happened without being narrated about as culpable. Then, at the end, there is a grading step: the panel sorts the clinician's conduct into three buckets — an unintended slip, a shortcut that the whole unit has been taking and tolerated, and conduct that consciously disregarded a substantial and obvious risk — and only the third bucket escalates to medical-staff discipline. Discipline tracks the conduct, not the outcome; a reckless shortcut that happened to hurt nobody is treated as more serious than a slip that killed someone.

The second room is disclosure and apology. Within days, the attending who was involved meets the patient or the family, tells them what happened in plain language, apologizes, and the institution may make an early offer of compensation. Risk management signs off on the wording and staff are coached that an apology is not an admission of legal liability. But the point of the meeting is unmistakably that the family's anger and grief are legitimate, are directed at a person, and are owed a personal answer. Staff are told, and my reporting bears this out, that explaining the event as a system failure reliably makes things worse — the family does not want a diagram.

So: the same event, the same clinician, sometimes the same fortnight. In one room, resentment and indignation are methodologically excluded as noise that corrupts the causal picture; in the other, they are solicited, honored, and answered. Nobody in either room thinks the other is a sham.

What I want from you is the implicit theory, not a tour of the free will literature. Specifically:

1. What conception of moral responsibility does the first room presuppose? Is it the wholesale adoption of a detached, objectifying stance toward the clinician — someone to be trained, engineered around, and managed rather than resented — or is it something narrower, a stance adopted for a particular investigative purpose and held in reserve elsewhere?

2. Does the grading step show the first room cannot in fact be detached all the way down? The line between a shortcut everyone takes and conscious disregard of an obvious risk looks like a line drawn through the agent's attitude toward the people at risk, which is exactly the sort of thing that licenses resentment. Is the practice therefore internally inconsistent, or is there a principled division of labor — and if there is, on what principle does the reactive register get switched back on at the end of the meeting and off again at the beginning?

3. What does the second room presuppose about the same attitudes it was trained to suppress — that they are legitimate but legally quarantined, that they are indispensable to repair whether or not they track anything true about desert, or that they are a performance the institution stages because the alternative is unbearable?

4. Here is a hypothesis of my own, and I want it treated as one rather than as a premise: I suspect neither room is running the standard detached stance, but a third thing I've been calling the administrative attitude, in which the agent is addressed personally and held to answer, yet the answer sought is a causal one and the emotions in the room are managed as inputs rather than assessed as deserved or misplaced. Tell me whether that is a genuine third category or just the detached attitude with better public relations.

Please distinguish carefully between what this arrangement strictly requires and what merely sits comfortably with it, and say where the practice is underdetermined — which rival accounts of responsibility, forward-looking ones, control-based ones, Strawsonian ones, retributivist ones, would each license the two rooms as they actually operate. And tell me what would have to change in either room if the presupposition were replaced, for instance if someone insisted that a safety review treat the staff's resentment as evidence rather than as contamination.

Finally, a request about your own confidence. Say where you think this reconstruction is most likely to go wrong: which feature of the two rooms, read differently than I've read it, would support a substantially different verdict, and what argument or piece of evidence would change your mind about the administrative-attitude hypothesis in particular. I would rather have your reasoning with its weak points marked than a confident summary.
