I was a research astronomer for four decades, mostly concerned with the statistics of faint objects, and I retired five years ago into philosophy, where I have taken up the methodological literature on how archaeologists infer belief and institutional structure from things. One strand of that literature — the claim that the material record is a residue rather than a sample — struck me as a selection-effect argument of a kind I have made myself, so I attempted to write it out rigorously. The result troubles me, because it appears to prove considerably more than I am prepared to concede.

The argument:

(1) A claim about past belief, and equally a claim about a past institution, is an inference from surviving material traces to states of affairs that produced those traces and were not themselves preserved.
(2) Such an inference is licensed only if the mapping from what occurred to what survived — call it the selection function — is known, at least in broad outline.
(3) The selection function can be known only from the traces that survived, there being by hypothesis no record of what perished.
(4) Therefore any estimate of the selection function is itself an inference from a biased sample to the population that produced it.
(5) That inference is circular in the damaging sense: whatever error it contains leaves no mark on the sample, since a given assemblage is compatible with indefinitely many producing populations, each governed by a different selection function.
(6) Therefore the selection function cannot be known.
(7) Therefore, by (2), no inference from the material record to past belief or institution is licensed.
(8) Nothing in (3) through (6) appealed to anything special about belief or about institutions; the argument turned only on perishability and the absence of an independent record. It therefore applies to any unobservable feature of the past.
(9) Therefore the material record supports no conclusions about the unobservable past, and an archaeologist's reconstruction differs from a novelist's only in the confidence with which it is asserted.

I cannot accept (9), and not merely from taste. It would condemn inference practices I have used and trust. Deriving a luminosity function from a flux-limited survey is likewise an inference from a sample systematically biased toward bright objects; I regard such inferences as warranted, and they are checked against one another across surveys at different depths, where they have held up. So one of (1) through (8) must go, or must be weakened to the point where (9) ceases to follow. I should rather abandon any single step than keep the conclusion.

Which step do you reject, and why did it look innocent to me when I wrote it? Please distinguish steps that are simply false from steps that are true but too weak to carry the inference, and say what each rejection costs: I want to know how much of the argument survives in its strongest defensible form, and whether the residue is enough to worry an archaeologist. Finally, tell me whether my astronomical case is genuinely analogous or whether the analogy conceals a disanalogy that would leave (9) standing after all.

Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning. No preamble, and no restatement of the question.
