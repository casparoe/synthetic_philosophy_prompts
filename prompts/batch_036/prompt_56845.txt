I run a first-year seminar called Minds and Machines — 18 students, one 110-minute evening session with a break in the middle, six groups of three. Maybe a third of them have poked at Hofstadter's GEB and think they already know the MU puzzle; the rest have never seen it. I want them to run into the jumping-out-of-the-system idea on their own, and I want the GEB crowd to be tripped up by what they think they know.

Here's the shape I have in mind, tell me if its better done some other way. Round one is the ordinary MIU system: start from MI, and at each step you may (a) add a U to the end of any string that ends in I, (b) double everything after the M, (c) replace any III with a U, or (d) delete any UU. Groups race to derive MU, and separately have to say what they'd have to do to show it can't be done at all. Then I reveal the usual move — count the I's, notice the count never becomes a multiple of three. Round two is the same system plus one extra rule: any U may be replaced by II. Same target.

Before anything else, check my hunch about round two, becuase the whole session collapses if I'm wrong: I'm fairly sure that extra rule quietly wrecks the counting argument and MU does become derivable, but I never actually sat down and worked it out. Give me the shortest derivation you can find for my answer key — or, if you think it's still blocked, tell me what blocks it so I can pick a different extra rule.

Then design the thing itself: materials, and a structure that stops groups just shouting strings at each other; a requirement that each group writes down a prediction before round two begins (derivable or not, plus one sentence of reasoning) so I can hold their own confidence up against them in the debrief; a timeline that genuinely fits 110 minutes; and a debrief that walks from "we found an invariant" to what jumping out of a system actually amounts to — including that the outside view you land on is tied to the particular system you jumped out of, which is exactly why knowing the answer to round one makes you worse at round two. I want to be able to say at the end why the ambush on the GEB readers is the point and not just a gotcha.

Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time something like this is run and how you'd handle it, and be blunt about which part of the story the activity can't stage. I don't want them leaving thinking the MU puzzle is a Godel sentence, or that a formal system can hop outside itself, and I do expect one kid to ask whether any of this shows minds aren't machines — give me two or three sentences I could actually say back. No lecture up front, and nobody should hear the words invariant, JOOTSing, or Godel until the debrief.
