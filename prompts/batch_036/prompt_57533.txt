For eleven years I have worked at a small nonprofit that publishes an annual assessment of the health of democratic competition in roughly forty countries. Our lead coder — I will call her M., because the entire report rests on her judgment and she is retiring in the spring — assigns every country one of four labels each year: stable, illiberal drift, backsliding, or breakdown. She has never written down the rule she is applying. When I asked her for it last year, she said she could defend any individual call after the fact but could not have told us in advance what the next one would be, which is exactly the answer that makes me want the rule.

Here are eight of her calls. I have described them by their features rather than by country name, partly because names invite people to argue about the politics instead of the coding rule, and partly because several are composites. Please work from my descriptions and not from whatever you may suspect about which countries they resemble.

1. A governing party holding a bare majority rewrote the electoral law eighteen months before an election: new district boundaries that converted a four-point popular-vote margin into a twenty-seat supermajority, the chair of the electoral commission replaced by a former campaign manager, and four hundred regional newspapers folded into a foundation whose trustees were government appointees. Label: backsliding. Her note: "they bought the scoreboard."

2. A government lowered the mandatory retirement age for judges, clearing about forty percent of the bench within a year and filling every vacancy itself; placed public broadcasting under a government-appointed board; and required NGOs receiving foreign money to register as foreign agents. Label: backsliding. Her note: "the next government cannot undo this."

3. A president brought corruption charges against two opposition leaders under a statute that had been on the books for four decades and had also been used against figures in his own predecessor's government. Ordinary courts, ordinary procedure; one of the two was acquitted. The president then lost the next election by six points and left office on schedule. Label: stable. Her note: "hardball, not backsliding — the machinery was intact, and it produced a turnover."

4. A legislature, citing a border emergency, extended its own term by one year and postponed the scheduled election. When the emergency lapsed, the election was held and the governing party lost it. Label: backsliding, the most severe call in that year's report. Her note: "you do not get to decide when the voters get to decide."

5. A government criminalized a common protest tactic, raised the maximum fine for criminal defamation under a statute already used against journalists for decades, and deported a foreign correspondent. Broadcast licenses and the opposition press were untouched; the main opposition party won forty-four percent in the next election and formed a coalition government. Label: illiberal drift, explicitly not backsliding. Her note: "bad things done by a government that could still lose."

6. A high court struck down the government's signature policy. The government announced it would not implement the ruling, and the legislature then passed a statute removing that court's jurisdiction over the policy area. Label: backsliding. Her note: "when the players overrule the referees, the game is over."

7. A president governs largely by decree on economic policy, dismisses ministers at will, and uses a weekly broadcast to attack journalists by name. No changes to the courts, electoral administration, media licensing, or the legislature. He lost a midterm and accepted the result. Label: stable. Her note: "noise without purchase."

8. In a large federation, one state government replaced nonpartisan county election administrators with party appointees and refused to certify results in two counties. The federal government did nothing, and the national report assigned the country no downgrade. M. coded the state as backsliding on her own initiative and added a footnote complaining that national-level indices are structurally blind to subnational capture.

As far as I can tell, at least three rules fit all eight calls without strain: an institutional-target rule, on which backsliding obtains whenever the incumbent alters the structure, personnel, jurisdiction, or funding of an institution that administers or adjudicates competition — courts, electoral administration, media ownership and licensing; a competitive-chances rule, on which it obtains whenever the incumbent's actions materially reduce the opposition's realistic chance of winning the next election, by whatever means; and a turnover rule, on which it obtains whenever losing office, or acting on having lost, has been made less likely or less consequential. A fourth possibility is a rule keyed not to effect but to creation: whether the incumbent has built a new discretionary tool that a future incumbent could turn to any purpose, as opposed to using an old tool hard. Her notes gesture at these but each can be read at least two ways, and she has never seen a case of the kind now on my desk.

The file: a governing coalition, elected fourteen months ago with forty-one percent of the vote and a three-seat majority, has in that time done the following. It split the supreme court into an ordinary chamber and a new constitutional chamber, moved constitutional review to the new chamber, and filled its twelve seats by two-thirds legislative vote — a threshold that required the votes of the largest opposition party, which supplied them after public negotiations; no sitting judge was removed and none took a pay cut. It transferred the drawing of constituency boundaries from the interior ministry to a permanent commission of five retired judges drawn by lot from a list supplied by the bar association, serving single non-renewable nine-year terms; the commission's first map, published last month, cost the coalition three of its safest seats. By ministerial decree, under a statute that had previously gone unused, it redirected essentially all government advertising to outlets favorable to the government; state advertising had accounted for about a third of the revenue of the country's independent newspapers, and no licensing, ownership, or tax rule changed. It has touched nothing in the electoral commission, the voting rules, or the media licensing regime. In regional elections last month the coalition lost four of six regions and conceded on the night. The next national election is on schedule in three years and nine months.

What I want from you:

- Which rule is M. actually running? State your best single hypothesis, and for each of the other candidates say precisely which of the eight calls it would have to get wrong, or what auxiliary assumption it needs, to survive the record.
- Predict her label for the file — one of the four — with a rough probability, and say which of the eight calls is the closest analog and which is the closest analog pointing the other way.
- Recommend what our committee should publish if we want to be defensible against the charge that we are simply coding our own partisan priors. Drift is available as a label and I expect it will tempt you; tell me whether selecting it here is a substantive claim about the case or a way of declining to answer.
- Name the further case — described, like mine, by its features — that would most sharply separate the surviving rules, and say what each predicts about it.
- Finally: if the rules genuinely diverge on this file, tell me whether "backsliding" is doing more work than a description of what the government did plus an expression of disapproval, and commit to an answer rather than noting that reasonable people differ.

Two housekeeping requests. I am not asking for references, a reading list, or a survey of what the democracy-measurement literature says; I want your reasoning and your prediction, and I would rather you be checkably wrong than hedged. And please commit: give me one rule, one label, one recommendation, and your reasons, instead of laying out the options and leaving the choice to me. Write the answer in American English.
