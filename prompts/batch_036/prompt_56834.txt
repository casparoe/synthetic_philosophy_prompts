I am in the final term of a master's programme in philosophy, and the last five weeks of my philosophy of biology seminar have been devoted to biological information: whether the genome carries information in more than a metaphorical sense, and what would have to be true for the genetic code to be a code rather than a loose analogy. The seminar has no shared map of the terrain. Papers are assigned, positions are named in passing, and by week five most of us can no longer say which commitments actually separate which views. I have volunteered to circulate a decision tree to our ten-person reading group before the revision session, and I would like you to write it.

The instrument has to sort people by what they say about cases, not by asking which camp they belong to. Every branch node should therefore be phrased as a question about a concrete phenomenon that a student could answer from having read the biology, without recognising which position that answer leads to. Use two, or at most three, branches per node, and label each with the claim that separates it from its siblings. Please draw the nodes from the following fixed points, and from nothing else, so that the tree stays anchored in material the group has actually discussed: variant codes in ciliates and in vertebrate mitochondria, where the same triplet specifies a different amino acid or a different stop signal; the recoding of a stop codon to insert selenocysteine when a particular cis-acting RNA element is present; synonymous substitutions that nevertheless alter translational efficiency, co-translational folding, or splicing; post-transcriptional editing of messenger RNA, so that what the ribosome reads is not what the DNA encodes; programmed ribosomal frameshifting and overlapping reading frames, where one stretch of nucleotides supports two distinct products; cell-free translation, in which a synthetic message plus purified ribosomes, transfer RNAs and amino acids yields the expected polypeptide with no intact cell and no organism anywhere in the picture; horizontal transfer of a gene that comes to do work in a new host; maternal cytoplasmic determinants, and the fact that a genome on its own does not fix a body plan; sequences identical in DNA but differently expressed because of methylation; and the degeneracy of the code, including the fact that the pattern of redundancy is itself non-random, with chemically related amino acids tending to have related codons.

Give me between eight and twelve leaves. Each should carry a short label — the name of the view if it has one, "unnamed" if it does not — a one-sentence statement of its core claim, the single item from my list that it handles worst, and one awkward commitment it is stuck with. Five of the leaves must be mutually distinguishable: the view that the code is literal because the codon assignments are arbitrary in something like the way a convention is arbitrary and are read by dedicated molecular machinery; the deflationary view that informational talk is eliminable in favour of ordinary physical causation, with DNA a template and a chemical cause but nothing more; the parity view on which the genome is one inherited resource among many and nothing about its causal role singles it out; the view that semantic content in the genome is genuine but grounded in the selection history of the systems that use it rather than in the arbitrariness of the mapping; and the view on which genes are not carriers of content at all but the causes that actually make the difference to the phenotype in the contexts we investigate. If you think any two of these five collapse into one another, say so rather than forcing the tree to keep them apart.

Two further requirements. First, tell me which pairs of leaves the tree cannot separate — where two views give the same answer at every node — and what further question, if any, would drive them apart. Second, note which combinations of answers the space permits but nobody occupies, and for each say whether the cell is empty because the position is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has thought of it. I have two candidates in mind and want them checked rather than assumed: a view on which the code is real and arbitrary but semantically exhausted by translation itself, so that nothing downstream of the polypeptide inherits any content; and a view on which information is real but borne by the developing system as a whole, with DNA contributing no more of it than any other contributor does.

Finally, a check on myself. My seminar convenor holds a view I will describe without naming: the codon assignments are conventional in the relevant sense and are read by machinery, so the coding relation is literal, but it holds strictly between nucleic acid and protein and licenses no claim about what the genome says about the organism. Please locate that view on the tree and tell me whether it survives the cell-free translation case or has to weaken under it. Also tell me whether the order in which the nodes come is biased — whether entering through arbitrariness, rather than through whether DNA is privileged among the causal factors that vary, makes one leaf reachable earlier and more comfortably than it deserves — and if it is, propose an alternative ordering and say which leaves change position under it.

Format: an indented outline is fine, ideally one page of text and no more than two. No bibliography, no citations, no page numbers, and no quotations from the assigned readings. Close with three sentences on how the reading group should run the instrument in a session, including the point at which participants should be warned that a given branch reflects a contested interpretive choice rather than a settled matter of fact.
