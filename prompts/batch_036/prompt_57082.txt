---

Scanlon holds that wrongness is a matter of unjustifiability to others rather than of the badness of outcomes: an act is wrong just when it is ruled out by principles that nobody could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced general agreement. The test is not a prediction of what people would in fact accept, but a way of identifying the principles that could be justified to each person separately. That is what makes the theory non-consequentialist, and it is also where the individualist restriction enters.

Because justification is owed to each person individually, the reasons that bear on whether a principle can be reasonably rejected are the personal reasons of the individuals it affects — their complaints under that principle. The comparison is therefore conducted one person at a time: we ask who has the strongest complaint against the principle in question and who has the strongest complaint against each relevant alternative, and the principle is rejected if some alternative leaves that person with a materially weaker complaint. What the restriction excludes is the utilitarian move of pooling. I cannot justify imposing a burden on you by pointing out that the burdens it removes from a thousand others are greater in total. Rawls put the underlying idea when he said that utilitarianism fails to take seriously the distinction between persons, and Nozick pressed the same point: aggregation treats a plurality of lives as though it were a single life writ large, and thereby licenses using some people as mere means for the benefit of others. The restriction is, in that light, prioritarian in spirit — it assigns the greatest weight to the complaint of whoever stands to lose most, just as Parfit's prioritarianism (the 1991 Lindley Lecture) assigns extra weight to benefits for the worse off.

The difficulty is the numbers case. Five people will die unless we divert; one will die if we do. Intuitively we must save the five, yet the individualist restriction seems to bar us from noticing that there are five of them, since each of the five has a complaint identical in strength to the one's, so the strongest complaints tie. Taurek's response is to abandon the intuition: with no aggregation the claims cancel and we may toss a coin. Scanlon refuses this. He borrows an argument from Kamm: the complaint of the one can be matched against the complaint of one of the five, since the two are identical; but four people are left with complaints that go entirely unmatched, and a principle requiring us to disregard those four could reasonably be rejected by each of them. So we save the five without ever adding anything up.

I do not think this succeeds, and the reason is instructive. Once complaints are matched off and remainders are declared in this way, the individualist restriction has no work left to do. Matching-plus-remainder is simply summing, described in individualist vocabulary: where complaints are equal in size, declaring four of them unmatched just is counting four. So in every case where complaints are commensurable, contractualism reaches the same verdicts as consequentialism. And in the case that is supposed to be its showcase — one death against any number of headaches — Scanlon's answer rests on the bare stipulation that a headache is not the kind of complaint that can be weighed against death at all. That begs the question, since whether small harms may be traded against large ones is precisely what is at issue, and settling it by declaring the two incommensurable builds the desired conclusion into the criterion of reasonable rejection. Moreover, if reasonable rejection is fixed by what fully informed rational agents would reject when they do not know whose complaint will be theirs, a headache is exactly the sort of reason such an agent would insist be taken into account. The restriction therefore survives only where it is question-begging and collapses wherever it is informative.
