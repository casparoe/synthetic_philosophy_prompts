I am six years into a novel that lives for a hundred and fifty pages inside a low-temperature laboratory — a group that makes and characterizes semiconductor heterostructures, so the cast is half experimentalists and half people who keep the dilution refrigerators running. My technical reader is a retired experimentalist I will call R., who has been indispensable and, I have come to suspect, philosophically overconfident. His tic in the margins is a single sentence, which he says he picked up as a graduate student in the early eighties and which he attributes to Ian Hacking's *Representing and Intervening* (1983): "if you can spray them, then they are real." He deploys it as a conversation-ender, the way other men deploy a shrug. It has got me into a bind, because I have written a scene — Chapter 22, the night shift, the postdoc Neve and R. alone with a sample mounted in a cryostat — in which the sentence has to do an enormous amount of work, and I cannot tell whether it can bear that weight or whether I am letting a character win an argument he has not earned.

In the scene Neve raises two things. First: are electron holes real? She points out that a hole is an absence in a nearly full valence band, a piece of collective bookkeeping that behaves as though it were a positively charged particle, and yet she drifts holes, measures their mobility, and builds devices that would not function if they were not there — so by R.'s own criterion she seems licensed to believe in something she thinks is a convenient fiction about the behavior of a great many real electrons. Second: does the criterion really immunize him against the pessimistic induction? R.'s answer is that theories come and go while the things you can act on stay put, but Neve asks whether that history is any kinder at the level of entities than at the level of laws, and whether the referent of "electron" is even the same thing across the theories that have described it. R. waves both off with the sentence. I should add that he told me the line came from watching an experiment in which charged particles were sprayed onto a superconducting metal sphere; I gather that sphere was part of a search for fractional charge, and if that is right I would like to know whether the slogan's own origin story embarrasses it.

What I need from you is a disambiguation, not an essay on entity realism. Lay out as a numbered menu the distinct precise theses that one sentence could be expressing — for instance, a criterion of reality for unobservables grounded in our ability to intervene on them; a merely epistemic claim that successful manipulation licenses belief without telling us what existence consists in; a semantic claim that a theoretical term earns its reference by being used as a tool; the selective realist's division of labor between entities and theories, together with whatever motivates that division; and anything else genuinely distinct that the wording can carry. For each reading, give the one-sentence statement, a verdict label (platitude, substantive and defensible, vulnerable, or false), and the strongest pressure on it — I have in mind the worry that manipulation can succeed for reasons having nothing to do with the entity posited, the worry that the criterion is silent about the entities of astronomy and paleoscience that can only be observed and never handled, the quiet work being done by any requirement that the effects be investigable independently, and the difficulty of derivative or collective posits such as holes, phonons, or centers of mass. Then tell me, reading by reading, what Neve could say back that does not caricature him, and identify precisely which reading R. would have to be relying on to dismiss the hole question and which, if any, to dismiss the question about entities that have since been discarded. Say whether part of their quarrel is merely verbal, and say what a person who repeats the sentence at parties most likely means by it. Close with a single line of the form \answer{...} containing the label of the reading R. must be relying on to dismiss the hole question — or "none", if no reading earns him that dismissal.
