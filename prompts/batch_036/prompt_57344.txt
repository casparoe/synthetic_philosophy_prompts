Thirty years running an analytical chemistry laboratory left me with a habit of asking what a claim actually asserts, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy properly. Our local reading group recently took up classical Chinese thought, and one member — anxious to show that Western ways of sorting things are parochial — produced the thesis of Gongsun Long's White Horse Dialogue, "a white horse is not a horse," along with its supporting argument: if you ask for a horse, a bay or a black one will serve, whereas if you ask for a white horse it will not, so what is sought differs, and the compound name is therefore not the simple one. He also cited the other line of the dialogue, that "horse" designates shape while "white" designates colour and what designates colour is not what designates shape. From all this he concluded that kind terms are conventions we lay over the world with nothing in the animals answering to them, and that anyone who thinks "white horse" and "horse" pick out overlapping parts of reality has simply failed to notice how language works.

I should like the sentence itself taken apart. What distinct claims could it be making, given that the Chinese negation here can carry either non-identity or exclusion from a class, and given further slides among a name, what a name connotes, and what a name applies to? Arrange the readings from the trivially true to the plainly false, and say which of them each of the two arguments above actually establishes. Then commit yourself rather than leaving me a menu: on which reading is the dialogue best taken as serious philosophy rather than a debating trick, and does any reading license the conventionalist conclusion he drew?
