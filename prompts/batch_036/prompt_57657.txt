I direct a graduate seminar this term on the epistemic standing of moral intuitions — in particular, on how one is supposed to tell a case in which a person's intuitive response tracks some genuine normative feature of a situation from a case in which it is an artifact of framing, affect, or upbringing. Last week we were working through the familiar pattern in which people's verdicts about structurally similar cases reverse when the cases are described as acts rather than omissions, or presented in a different order. A colleague from another department who was sitting in remarked that he found the whole line of inquiry misguided, because in the end there is no accounting for tastes: the intuitions under discussion are preferences, and preferences are not the sort of thing about which a person can be mistaken or from which a person can be alienated. Two of my students took this to be decisive. I do not think it is, but I could not say on the spot why, and I want the seminar to have something better than my inarticulate unease.

Could you prepare an inventory of the distinct, precisely statable claims that this proverb might be used to assert, arranged from the merely platitudinous to the genuinely ambitious? For each candidate reading I would like a one-sentence formulation; a verdict as to whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, contentious, false, or unfalsifiable; and a note on whether it remains available to someone who accepts the order- and framing-sensitivity of intuitive response. Please include the possibility that in ordinary use the proverb is not an assertion at all but a conversational closer — a way of calling a halt rather than stating a thesis — and, if you think that is right, say what happens to it when it is pressed into service as a premise in an argument.

Then, separately and more briefly: which reading would my colleague have to be relying on in order to establish that our inquiry was misguided, and does that reading actually deliver the conclusion? I am especially interested in whether the remark does covert classificatory work — whether calling these responses "tastes" already settles what the seminar was trying to determine, namely whether they belong to a class of attitudes that admit of being right or wrong. And if there is a restricted version of the proverb that is both true and compatible with the inquiry's continuing, state it and state the restriction. The result should read as a list I can hand out at the start of next week's meeting, not as an essay.
