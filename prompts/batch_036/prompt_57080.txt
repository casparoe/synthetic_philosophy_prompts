So I volunteer at a public library series where I do one 70-minute session a month for whoever turns up — no syllabus, no assigned reading, mostly the same fifteen regulars who've been coming for two years. Next month is pragmatism about truth, and I want to do Peirce and James as two people who actually disagree, not as one school with two spokespeople. My problem is the only version of pragmatism in the room will be the self-help one: the guy who runs a landscaping business will say within five minutes that truth is whatever works for you and that's the end of it, and a retired engineer goes the other way and thinks any view that so much as mentions usefulness has already surrendered to nonsense. So give me a plan for how I explain it, not an explanation of it. Order the pieces and tell me what each depends on — I think they need something about belief being a rule for acting before anything about truth makes sense, but I can't work out whether peirce's maxim should come before or after James's talk of cash value, and whether the opinion-fated-to-be-agreed-to-at-the-end-of-inquiry idea is load bearing or just a distraction for this audience.

Then pick the one example that carries the whole thing, and say what it has to have and what it must not have. Not the squirrel going round the tree — I know that's the famous one (it opens James's first pragmatism lecture if I remember right) but it reads like a purely verbal dispute about which side, and I'm scared it teaches them that pragmatism dissolves questions rather than that it has an account of truth. I'd rather something mundane where "works" and "is true" can genuinely pull apart in a way they can watch happening. Two of the regulars are nurses, so a case where usefulness and truth come apart under pressure would be ideal, partly because I don't want James to come out trivially right.

Then the misconception map: what they walk in with, what they'll invent on the spot once I start, and the exact point in the sequence where each one gets headed off before it sets. I'm bracing for relativism, for "so lying is fine if it helps somebody", for "James is just an optimist about belief", and for "Peirce means science will eventually prove everything and that's what truth is". The thing I actually want them able to do by the end is say whether Peirce's complaint — that James took a rule about what our concepts mean and quietly turned it into a claim about what truth is — has any bite, or whether the two were closer than Peirce was willing to admit. Give me one question for the last five minutes that would show me whether that landed. And tell me what to cut and to say out loud that I'm cutting it: Dewey, Peirce's categories and the sign stuff, radical empiricism, whatever else won't survive the room. No reading list though — nothing assigned, nothing suggested — I just want the seventy minutes to work.
