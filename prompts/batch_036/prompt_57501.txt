Thirty-four years doing scattering experiments at a synchrotron, the last five of them chairing the commitee that hands out beam time, and now I'm two years retired and reading political philosophy at the kitchen table with a coffee and a pencil. Sen's question about equality of what has got under my skin, mostly because I spent a decade answering a version of it in practice without realising I was doing philosophy. I've also been through Dworkin on resources and I honestly can't tell whether the thing we were distributing was a resource or a capability or neither.

So here is what I actually did, case by case, and how it looks to me now.

Two proposals, same science, same score from the reviewers. One from a flagship lab with a sample robot, a staff scientist and a travel budget; one from a state college where the PI drives five hours each way and mounts her own samples by hand. I gave the second one more shifts. Still think that was right.

A proposal with badly designed controls that was going to waste the shift no matter how much time we gave it. I cut it. Cutting it made that group worse off and widened exactly the gap I'd just tried to narrow in the previous case, and I'd do it again tomorrow.

A famous group that had burned 60 shifts over three years asked for 20 more. The science was excellent and would certainly have produced papers. I ranked it below a decent but unspectacular first-time user.

A user who couldn't work the overnight shifts alone at the end station — she's a wheelchair user — so we put her in the day blocks and paid for an assistant. She cost the facility more beam time than anybody else that cycle. Obviously correct, no hesitation then or now.

A group got 16 shifts, their postdoc quit in month two, they used 4. I docked them the following year. Notice that this is punishing people for bad luck, and bad luck is precisely what I compensated in the state college case.

A year when the machine underperformed and all we could offer everybody was half of what they'd asked for. Perfectly equal, everyone equally badly served. That year was not better than the year before it, and I don't think the extra equality made it better in any way at all.

Industrial users could buy shifts outright and the money went back into the academic pool. I voted for that every single time, which means I was happy to let money buy more of the thing I'd just spent six cases insisting shouldn't go to whoever could pay for it.

My own attempt at a principle is this: equalise effective access, not equal hours — give people whatever it takes to convert a shift into science, with a floor below which nobody falls and a cap on how much one group can hold at once. It handles the wheelchair case, the state college, and the 60-shift group nicely. Its seems to blow up on the wasted shifts and the bad controls, because on a conversion story the right response to someone who can't convert is to help them convert, not to cut them off, and I definitely didn't want to fund their training out of my beam time.

What I want from you: the principle, or the small set of competing ones, that actually delivers these verdicts. State it precisely, clause by clause, and for each clause tell me which case above it's there to handle and what it costs you elsewhere. Where yours and my effective-access version come apart, say so plainly rather than reconciling them politely. Then run it on two situations I never had to decide. First, a proposal for a fully autonomous experiment that drives itself and needs no human on site at all — the per-person metric seems to lose its grip there and I want to know what happens to the cap and the floor. Second, a proposal from a grad student whose PI died halfway through, to finish work that only the dead PI really understood. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts can't survive: I'd rather lose one cleanly than keep all seven with a fudge factor bolted on the side. No reading list, please — I've got one book and a library card that lapsed.
