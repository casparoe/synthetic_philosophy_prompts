I have been keeping a list of the cases in which I am willing to say, without qualification, that a system believes or wants something, and the cases in which the same words come out of me as mere shorthand. I know the general terrain — Dennett's three stances, real patterns, the standing worry that instrumental success licenses only instrumentalism — but I am not interested in what the canon concludes about this. I want to know what my own verdicts commit me to, and whether they can be captured by anything worth calling a principle.

The verdicts, as honestly as I can report them:

1. A chess engine playing at roughly my strength. When I say it believed I would trade queens and wanted the centre, I mean it. This is not a façon de parler.
2. The same engine, explained to me in full by someone who understands it completely — evaluation function, search, pruning. My attributions survive the explanation intact; I find them easier to defend afterwards, not harder.
3. A robot vacuum. I say it thinks it is stuck, and I mean nothing by it. Strikingly, my willingness to say it collapses altogether when I learn that the bumper is a mechanical contact switch with no internal state worth the name. Its behaviour towards me is unchanged by that discovery. My verdict is not.
4. An undocumented scheduling system at my workplace, forty-odd years old, written by people long dead or untraceable, which nobody can explain any more. The only way anyone predicts what it will do is to treat it as a stubborn, suspicious clerk who resents being asked to do two things at once. I think these attributions are literally true, and that surprises me.
5. A hurricane. Forecasters will say a storm is holding itself together, is finding warm water, is being steered by the flow aloft. The compression here is genuine: I anticipate the storm's behaviour from such talk far better than from any fluid dynamics I could follow. I nevertheless refuse to grant it beliefs of any kind.
6. An ant colony that compares candidate nest sites before relocating. Shorthand, and I am unmoved by the fact that the comparison is demonstrably information-sensitive and demonstrably improves the colony's prospects.
7. My grandmother, late in dementia. Her attributions are unstable, contradictory hour to hour, and often flatly false about the room she is sitting in. She wants to go home; she believes her mother is alive. I will not call this shorthand and I would argue with anyone who did.
8. A large language model. I say it thinks I am testing it. I notice that I withdraw the attribution the moment I remember the weights, and restore it the moment it surprises me.

The principle I keep reaching for has three clauses, and I am not confident in any of them. Something is a genuine believer when (i) the intentional stance buys it predictions that neither the physical nor the design stance can match at comparable cost; (ii) those attributions survive learning exactly how the thing is built, and survive local damage to it, so that they are not artefacts of one implementation; and (iii) the thing is in principle vulnerable to reasons — it can be misinformed, corrected, or persuaded, and not merely altered.

I can see that (iii) is doing more work than I want it to. It is what makes 5 and 6 come out the way they do, and it also excludes 1, 2, and 4, whose verdicts I am least willing to surrender. I suspect it smuggles in a demand that the thing be rather like me, but I cannot tell whether that suspicion is sound or is only squeamishness about my own chauvinism. Cases 3 and 7 pull against each other in a way I cannot resolve: 3 shows my verdicts tracking implementation details that (i) says should be irrelevant, and 7 shows me holding fast precisely where (ii) fails. There is also a measurement problem I have not solved. Clause (i) compares stances by what they cost, and I have never once carried out a physical-stance prediction, so every comparison I make is against an idealisation I am guessing at. Tell me whether that is fatal to the principle or merely to my confidence in it.

What I want from you: state the principle, or the short set of candidate principles, that best accounts for this pattern, precisely enough to be applied to a case nobody has described. For each clause, identify which of my cases motivates it and what it is there to exclude. Then apply it to cases I have not listed — a satellite's attitude-control system, a central bank's stated expectations about inflation, a cuttlefish matching the seabed, a neural network whose weights and training data I can inspect exhaustively, a person acting on a post-hypnotic suggestion, a dead relative's estate as described in their will — and tell me what it says about each. Finally, be explicit about the cost: which of my verdicts your principle cannot save, which one you would overturn first, and why you think I am wrong rather than merely untidy. If the only principle that saves all eight does so by becoming as baroque as the list itself, say so and defend the simpler one instead.

Please commit to an answer. I want one recommended principle, or a ranked shortlist with your confidence in each stated, rather than a balanced survey of what could be said. And if you think the request rests on a confusion — if my verdicts are not tracking a single property at all, so that the honest response is to dissolve the question rather than answer it, or if you think clause (iii) is not merely mistaken but unintelligible — say that plainly at the outset and argue for it, instead of producing a principle to order because I asked for one. Please write in British English.
