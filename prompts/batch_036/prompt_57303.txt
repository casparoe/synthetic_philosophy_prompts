I am drafting a chapter on critiques of monetary gain, and I want to test a comparison that is almost always made lazily — the claim that Marx's formula for capital is simply Aristotle's objection to usury generalized. I would like you to determine whether that claim survives contact with the details.

The two items to compare are Aristotle's division of wealth-getting in *Politics* I.8–10 and Marx's distinction, in *Capital*, Volume I, chapter 4, between the two circuits of circulation. Set each out in its own terms before comparing them: what each takes money to be, what each treats as the limit on legitimate acquisition, and where each locates the wrong. For Aristotle, the wealth-getting belonging to household management is natural and bounded, since "the household branch of wealth-getting has a limit," whereas the wealth-getting of trade produces goods "not in every way but only by the method of exchanging goods" and yields riches that are "truly unlimited"; his diagnosis of why people pursue them without limit is that "their interests are set upon life but not upon the good life." Usury is the extreme case, hated "because its gain comes from money itself and not from that for the sake of which money was invented," and therefore "of all forms the most contrary to nature." For Marx the issue is not that anyone's ends are disordered but that the form of the circuit is inverted: in C–M–C one is "selling in order to buy," in M–C–M one is "buying in order to sell," and in the latter the money is "not spent, it is merely advanced," so that the circuit would be "absurd and without meaning" if the sum recovered did not exceed the sum laid out. (These quotations come from standard English renderings; work from whatever translation you know and do not worry about matching my wording.)

Then I want three specific pieces of work.

**1. An apparatus.** For any critique of monetary gain, call the *site* the location in the sequence of transactions where the wrong arises, and the *scope* the class of transactions the critique condemns. Place both thinkers on these two axes. Then tell me whether this apparatus is neutral between them or already biased toward one — my suspicion is that it flatters Marx, whose critique is explicitly about form and location, and distorts Aristotle, whose is about ends and character. If you agree, say what a fairer apparatus would look like; if you disagree, show that Aristotle's account really does have a site.

**2. A ranking inversion.** The two theories appear to order the same actors in opposite sequence. For Aristotle the usurer is worst of all, the retail merchant is "justly discredited," and the farmer or householder who acquires for use is unobjectionable. For Marx the industrial capitalist who buys labor-power and produces commodities is the paradigm case of the M–C–M circuit, while the merchant and the money-lender are derivative forms, living off surplus value generated elsewhere. Explain why the inversion happens and what it costs each view. In particular: does Marx have any analogue of a limit set by need, and does Aristotle have any analogue of production as the place where value originates?

**3. A test case.** Take the 2007–2008 financial crisis, and specifically the originate-to-distribute mortgage pipeline. The facts I will rely on, which you should correct if you think any is wrong: US lenders made mortgage loans not to hold them but to sell them; loans were pooled into mortgage-backed securities and tranched into senior and subordinate claims; rating agencies paid by the issuers assigned high grades to those tranches; banks and non-bank institutions financed long-dated securities with short-term borrowing in the repo and commercial-paper markets; credit default swaps sold protection against default without requiring the buyer to own the underlying debt; underwriting standards eroded toward low- and no-documentation loans with initial rates scheduled to reset. When prices stopped rising and defaults mounted, the securities became impossible to price, short-term funding evaporated, several large institutions failed or were rescued in September 2008, and credit markets seized.

Map each lens onto this sequence. Which features can Aristotle's distinction actually name? Can it tell the securitization desk apart from a grain merchant who buys in autumn to sell in spring? Does his framework have the resources to handle a wrong that appears to be nobody's intention, since the originator, the rater, and the repo desk each acted within what looked like ordinary business? And can he represent a claim on the future at all, or do his categories — stocks, household use, the good life — simply lack a slot for a derivative? Then ask the same of Marx: is M–C–M better equipped for the anonymity of the outcome, and what does his insistence that value originates in production make of a chain consisting entirely of claims on other people's future payments — parasitic on production yet to come, or something his account does not cover?

**Finally, a verdict, not a summary.** Which of the two lenses does more explanatory work on this episode — not which is more morally attractive, but which can say something about the shape of what happened: why the collapse arrived as a sudden stop in funding rather than a gradual decline, why it spread contagiously among institutions holding different assets, why the remedy took the form of restoring liquidity rather than redistributing anything. State whether the episode confirms one view, is an anomaly for it, or is merely something the view can redescribe after the fact, and say which of these you mean in each case. Also identify where the assimilation of Marx's formula to Aristotle's usury objection breaks down, and whether the resemblance between them is substantive or a matter of shared vocabulary.

No bibliography, no reference list, and no appeals to secondary literature — argue from the texts as you know them. Close by stating how confident you are in each of the main claims you have made: the mapping, the ranking inversion, and the verdict. Then single out the one point you are least sure of and explain why. Wherever your account depends on a contested reading — for instance, whether Aristotle's condemnation of trade is absolute or only relative to the ends of household management, or whether Marx's circuit is meant to describe individual transactions or the total social circuit — flag it as contested rather than presenting it as settled.
