I hold a position in the axiomatic theory of bargaining that I would like stress-tested, and I should say at the outset that I would rather be shown where it breaks than reassured that the debate is merely unsettled.

The position is this. Of the two classical solutions to the two-person bargaining problem, the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution is the correct account of a fair bargain, and its rivalry with the Nash solution is fundamentally a dispute about which features of the feasible set fairness may be permitted to track. Both accept Pareto optimality, symmetry, and invariance to positive affine transformations of each party's utility; Nash adds independence of irrelevant alternatives, while Kalai–Smorodinsky drops it in favour of a monotonicity condition on the feasible set. Because the Kalai–Smorodinsky allocation is the point where the ray from the disagreement point through the ideal point meets the Pareto frontier, my view is that fairness may look only at the two extremes of a bargain and must be blind to everything in between, whereas the Nash solution reads off the whole shape of the set while remaining blind to options nobody actually takes. I also hold that the intuition behind monotonicity — that a party who forgoes something genuinely available to them alone is owed a compensating share of what remains — is the right underlying moral story, not a rationalization of a geometric construction.

Here is the case I find hardest, and I want you to tell me whether it is fatal or merely awkward. Two research groups divide time on a shared instrument. One group points out that a certain operating mode is physically possible, though it lies outside the facility's certification and will never be requested by anyone. The mode raises that group's attainable maximum, and therefore its ideal point, and therefore its Kalai–Smorodinsky share of hours — while the hours actually on offer are unchanged. It seems a party can now enlarge its fair share merely by describing a possibility, and the very pathology independence of irrelevant alternatives was meant to prevent has been relocated to the boundary of the set rather than eliminated. My natural reply is that only genuinely available options belong in the feasible set, and that the certified mode was never in it. I want you to press on whether that reply is principled or is a qualification that insulates the view at the cost of its content, and whether insulation of that kind is a defect for a normative axiomatic claim in the way it would be for an empirical one.

Please address, and rank from fatal to merely awkward: the phantom-option problem above; what follows if the feasible set is not convex, or is open, or lacks a well-defined ideal point; the status of the disagreement point, which both solutions inherit but neither justifies, and which may simply encode an unjust status quo; whether the absence of any natural extension beyond two parties is a tolerable restriction or a sign the axioms are doing less work than I assume; and whether the availability or unavailability of a non-cooperative foundation for one solution but not the other should count as evidence about either. Say also what my position predicts that the Nash position does not, in a way that could be observed: I am designing a session in which subjects divide a pie, and in a second treatment a third option is added that raises one subject's nominal maximum and is never selected. Tell me what outcome there would decide anything, what outcome would not, and why.

For each defeater, distinguish what a committed Kalai–Smorodinsky partisan would themselves concede as counting against them from what they would dismiss as resting on a misunderstanding of the axioms, and say where you think the dismissal is dialectically legitimate rather than merely available. Do not supply references; I will handle those. Finally, indicate how confident you are in your overall verdict and in each of the ranked items, and single out the one point in your answer where you are least sure you have the technical or normative facts right. If any of my own framing above is mistaken — including my gloss on what the solution depends on — correct it before proceeding.
