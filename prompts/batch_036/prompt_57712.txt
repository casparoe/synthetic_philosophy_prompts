Just came out of a three-hour argument with a friend about advance directives and my head's still going, so I figured I'd dump the thing on you while it's fresh. She's a hospice nurse and she is *not* on board with what I'm writing. Below are my notes for an essay — not a draft, deliberately, because I want to know if the skeleton holds before I spend a week on prose. Comments on the skeleton, please, not on the writing.

**The notes.**

1. Setup. The standard picture is that a directive binds the later self because the earlier self owns it — same person, so their settled choices carry over even into dementia. My friend's line, which I think is right about the metaphysics and wrong about the conclusion, is that by the time the directive actually bites, the author is gone in every sense that matters: no carry-over of memory or intention, nothing that can be frustrated or fulfilled. So it looks like a dead hand.

2. My move. Authority here doesn't come from identity at all. It comes from authorship — specifically from being the last self in the sequence who was in a position to author anything. Call it the last-author principle: when the person in front of you has lost the capacity to organise a life as a whole (not to enjoy moments in it — to organise them), the default isn't that nobody's view counts, it's that the last competent author's view counts, because there is no one left to displace it. Authority, on this view, isn't transmitted down the chain of selves; it's what remains standing when the chain stops producing authors.

3. What it's supposed to explain. Why we feel so differently about a directive from a sharp 25-year-old trying to bind a still-sharp 80-year-old — there we resist, and on my account that's because the later self is an author too, so the earlier one is a competitor rather than a placeholder. And why refusals feel more compelling than demands. And why the argument people have about whether the demented patient is still the same person feels like it's going round in circles: on my view the same-person question was never doing the work.

4. The bit I'm least happy with, and where she really pressed. If the force of the principle comes from the later self's incapacity, then we're honouring the document not out of respect for a patient but out of the absence of one — authority from a vacuum. That makes the whole business about our own inability to cope with having no one to ask. My answer was the trustee analogy: when a beneficiary can no longer be consulted, the settlor's instructions don't weaken, they take up the slack. But I suspect the analogy is quietly supplying what I need, since a settlor and a beneficiary are usually the same person and mine by construction are not.

5. Related worry: is authorship just Dworkin's distinction between critical and experiential interests back in a trenchcoat? I organise a life as a whole sounds suspiciously like a critical interest described in the first person.

**What I want from you.** Take the last-author principle and tell me whether it does any real work or whether it's just a relabelling of the deadlock between the two sides — if it's the latter, say so without softening it. Then have a go at section 4 specifically: can the vacuum objection be met, and if the trustee analogy is smuggling, what would a non-smuggling version look like? And on section 5, tell me honestly whether the collapse into critical interests is fatal or just an embarrassment I can live with.

Don't hedge and don't be diplomatic about it. I'd rather you told me the framing is confused — e.g. that authorship and identity aren't actually separable the way I'm assuming, or that a principle whose trigger is the disappearance of the subject can't be a principle about that subject's authority at all — than have you polish something that doesn't stand up. If you think the whole essay can't be written as posed, tell me that instead of answering around it. And give me a rough sense of how confident you are in each verdict, not just the verdicts.

British English, please.
