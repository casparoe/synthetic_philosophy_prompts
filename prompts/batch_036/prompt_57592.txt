I would like help working out what principle, if any, lies behind my settled judgments about killing insects in the course of ordinary pest control. I am not asking about the ecology or the entomology, which I take as read, and I am not looking for a survey of the literature on insect sentience or wild animal suffering; I have read enough of it to know which moves are available. I want to know what my own verdicts commit me to, and whether they can all be kept. Here they are, in roughly the order they occur to me.

A house spider that has lived in the corner of my bathroom for some months: I trap it under a cup, slide a card beneath, and put it outside. That is permissible, and I think the method matters — squashing it would be worse — even though I fully expect it to be dead within a few days of release, from exposure or a bird.

Ants entering my kitchen: a bait station carrying a slow-acting poison, which the workers carry back and which destroys the colony, is permissible. So is a contact spray at the threshold. But I feel markedly cleaner about the bait, which kills several thousand, than about the spray, which kills perhaps two hundred.

Aphids on my roses: releasing ladybirds is permissible, and I regard it as better than spraying, even though the ladybirds will kill far more aphids and kill them slowly.

A municipal fogging programme that drifts insecticide across a whole neighbourhood to reduce West Nile risk: not permissible, notwithstanding the real public-health interest. A programme that instead treats standing water with a larvicide specific to mosquito larvae: permissible.

Termites working through the structural timbers of my house: whole-structure fumigation is permissible. The identical treatment applied to a decorative garden fence I would refuse.

A wasp nest in the eaves: I would have it destroyed at night, when every forager is inside, rather than by day. I think getting all of them at once is the more humane option, although the daytime method kills fewer insects on the spot and leaves the foragers alive — homeless, but alive.

Killing a bee as it works a flower feels worse to me than killing the same bee sitting motionless on a wall. I am not confident that a bee has anything I would recognise as a project, and I do not know what that difference in feeling is tracking.

Releasing large numbers of sterilised male mosquitoes, so that the eggs laid after mating never hatch: clearly better than spraying, even though across a few seasons it removes at least as many mosquitoes from the world as an insecticide campaign would. It prevents lives rather than ending them, and I cannot say why that should matter to the mosquitoes that never exist.

Eradicating the mosquitoes that transmit malaria, worldwide and permanently: a good thing. This sits badly with my general view that the extinction of a species is a grave harm, and worse still with the fact that eradication would end with the last generation being killed rather than merely not being born.

An island programme that kills millions of invasive ants in order to save a handful of endemic invertebrates: permissible, when the invader is abundant and the natives are rare. But I am not willing to trade one invasive ant against one endemic ant at parity. Abundance and rarity carry weight in my thinking that I cannot cash out in terms of anyone's welfare.

Driving: a long motorway journey kills thousands of insects on the windscreen and grille, and I regard this as entirely permissible and in no need of justification. Deliberately killing the same number by spraying a roadside verge would need one.

Leaving the garden untidy so that insects can overwinter in it, rather than tilling: better, though I would not call it a duty.

What I want from you is, first, the reconstruction. Give me the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that best captures this pattern, and state each clause precisely, tying every clause back to the particular verdict that motivates it, so that I can see which of my judgments is doing which piece of work. Where two clauses are in competition, tell me which is load-bearing and which is decoration. I should say that I am not attached to all of these verdicts; several may be nothing better than squeamishness, and I would rather be told which than have them preserved at the cost of a baroque theory.

Second, test the reconstruction on cases I have not listed, and give me its verdict on each: an ultraviolet light trap left running in the garden all summer, which kills whatever comes to it without any decision on my part on any particular night; buying vegetables grown under a conventional insecticide regime, where the deaths were arranged by a farmer before I ever reached the shelf and my own contribution to the price signal is negligible; a tenant with a severe spider phobia whose landlord declines to treat, and whether her fear changes the necessity calculus at all; draining a marsh for flood defence, which removes an insect population entirely while killing almost no individual insect directly; a gene drive that biases the sex ratio of a pest species so that it produces almost only males and fades out over a few generations; and taking an antiparasitic drug, where I am simultaneously the crop and the farm. I would also like one case of your own choosing in which the reconstruction, followed honestly, delivers a verdict I will find repellent. I would much rather see the reductio than a flattering systematisation.

Third, tell me plainly which of my verdicts has to go if the principle is to be simple, and what it costs me to keep it instead. I half-suspect that my view is not about insects at all but about me — that what I am tracking is not the badness of an insect's death but the quality of my own reasons for causing it, so that the wrong-making feature sits in my deliberation rather than in the insect's loss. If that suspicion is right, say so, and tell me what kind of view it makes mine and whether such a view can avoid collapsing into a form of self-regard that has nothing to do with the insects. If it is a mistake, point to the clause in the reconstruction that shows it to be one. You may use the standard apparatus where a clause genuinely needs it — the person-affect restriction, the deprivation account of the badness of death, the replaceability objection and its replies, expected-value reasoning under uncertainty about sentience — but please do not recite it for its own sake, and do not cite sources I would have to look up.

Aim for something in the region of fifteen hundred words. On presentation, one strict request: I want the whole of it as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter. No headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no tables, no bold or italicised emphasis. State each clause of the principle as a full sentence in the running text, and identify the cases by their content rather than by labels or abbreviations. I read this on a phone, in the gaps between other things, and I read it as prose or not at all.
