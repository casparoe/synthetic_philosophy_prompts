I'm a second-year moral philosophy PhD student working on the ethics of blame, and I've run into a configuration I can't place.

Someone blames another for a familiar fault — padding an expense report, massaging data — and has never done it, isn't complicit in the doing of it, hasn't benefited from it, and isn't about to do it. So the standard objections don't get a grip: sermon and conduct don't conflict. But the blamer is openly disposed to do exactly the same thing were the odds of getting away with it any better. A professor denouncing data manipulation who says cheerfully that the only reason he never indulged is that his subfield replicates aggressively; a manager who fires someone for expense fraud and tells the team, in the same meeting, that a near-miss early in her career rather than any standing commitment is what keeps her honest.

Is there an established concept for a standing deficit grounded purely in the blamer's avowed disposition rather than in a deed, an omission, or an inconsistency? Which existing categories come nearest — dispositional accounts of hypocrisy, complicity, the moral-luck objection to blame, bad faith, smugness — and how does this differ from each? And is the intuition I have here tracking a genuine loss of standing, or only an unattractive style of blaming that leaves the address intact? If nothing covers it, I'd rather you said so and proposed a term with boundary conditions than stretched an existing one: I particularly want to know whether disclosure does any work, whether an avowed disposition differs from a merely present one, and whether a blamer who fights the disposition is positioned differently from one who doesn't. I'm not after references — the carving is what I need.
