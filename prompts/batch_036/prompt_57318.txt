I sat in on a decision-theory reading group a few weeks ago and two of the regulars — Hanna and Rahul — got into a long argument about the Allais problem that the rest of us could not settle. I have no view about who won and I am reporting the exchange as neutrally as I can.

Both were working from the same setup, which they agreed on:

- Problem 1: Option A pays $1,000,000 with certainty. Option B pays $5,000,000 with probability 0.10, $1,000,000 with probability 0.89, and nothing with probability 0.01.
- Problem 2: Option C pays $1,000,000 with probability 0.11 and nothing otherwise. Option D pays $5,000,000 with probability 0.10 and nothing otherwise.

Hanna holds that preferring A to B while preferring D to C is irrational; Rahul holds that it is perfectly rational and that the axiom it offends is the culprit. Their exchange ran as follows, compressed but in order.

1. Hanna began by saying that such a person has not expressed a taste but made a mistake. Remove the component the two problems share — a million in the first, nothing in the second, both at 89 percent — and both problems reduce to the very same choice: an 11 percent chance at a million against a 10 percent chance at five million with a 1 percent chance of nothing. Preferring A and D is therefore preferring one option in the first problem and its exact reverse in the second, and no coherent weighting of outcomes produces it.

2. Rahul said that removing the shared component was the whole question at issue. In the first problem the common 89 percent means that in most branches the agent is already holding a million, so what is really being decided is whether to expose a secured position to a one percent chance of losing it outright; in the second problem nothing is secured and there is nothing to expose. A principle that renders the two situations identical, he said, has assumed away the only difference that could matter to someone making plans, and the value of a prospect taken as a whole need not be a probability-weighted sum of the values of its branches.

3. Hanna then offered what she called a pump. Suppose you hold A. She will give you one cent to swap it for a two-stage ticket: the first stage pays a million with probability 0.89, and if that fails it hands you, at the second stage, the choice between a million outright and a ten-in-eleven chance at five million. At that stage you take the gamble — it is just problem 2 with the common part already resolved, and you told everyone you prefer D to C — so what you end up holding is B. You have paid her a cent to trade something you rank above B for B itself, and she can offer you the trade again tomorrow.

4. Rahul replied that the pump works only on an agent too shortsighted to see it coming. If he knows he will take the gamble at the second stage, he refuses the cent at the first. The loss in Hanna's story arises from a defect in how the agent handles sequential choices, not from the static preference pattern, and an agent who plans by working backward from the choices he knows he will make is not exploitable at all.

5. Hanna said this concedes what matters. Either Rahul deviates at the second stage from the ranking he endorsed at the first, in which case that ranking is, as she put it, decorative and has no authority over his own conduct; or he binds himself at the second stage to take the million, against his preference at that moment, purely to keep a consistency rule satisfied. The second horn is worse: it means he will knowingly forgo what he prefers in order to protect a description of himself as consistent, and a principle with that consequence cannot be what rationality consists in.

6. Rahul said he accepts binding himself and sees nothing embarrassing in it, since people routinely sign away their later options for good reasons — and then turned the tables. Hanna's axiom, he said, cannot survive a change of scale. Shrink every prize by a factor of a thousand, so that A offers a dollar for certain and D a ten percent chance at five dollars, and the pattern disappears: people take the gamble. Inflate the prizes so that the bad branch of B costs a house and the good branch is generational wealth, and the pattern sharpens. Substitute doses of a scarce antivenom for money and it sharpens again. A genuinely rational preference should not switch on and off with the size of the stakes; what the pattern tracks is whether the bad branch destroys something the agent has already built, and that is a fact about the agent's situation, not a weight anyone has miscalculated.

7. Hanna asked for a line. If the value of certainty is not a fixed weight but something that comes and goes with the stakes, Rahul owes an account of when it is present and how large it is; without one he can rationalize any pattern of choices whatsoever by positing a suitable bonus for whatever happens to feel safe, and the position stops being a theory of rational choice and becomes a redescription of his own preferences. She added that the difficulty is worse than vagueness: there is no principled difference between a one-in-a-thousand chance of losing the million and a one-in-a-hundred chance, so if Rahul is right his preference should slide smoothly as the chance of the bad branch rises from zero toward one percent — and a smooth slide is exactly what her theory predicts and exactly what the jump at the word "certain" contradicts.

8. Rahul said the demand for a fitted parameter was no objection, since Hanna's theory is fitted too: nothing in expected utility determines the shape of the utility function, and that shape is set by whatever the data require. The difference, he said, is that his extra term answers to something an agent can point to — the difference between a position already secured and one merely likely — whereas her linearity in probabilities answers to nothing but the convenience of the mathematics. He closed by saying that the axioms were built for decisions small and fully surveyable enough that every consequence can be held in view at once, and that Hanna was applying them to a decision in which one branch ends the agent's ability to carry out any plan at all.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move rather than in the aggregate. For each of the eight moves, say whether it is clearly right, partly right, or clearly wrong, justify the rating briefly, and identify which particular claim inside the move is doing the work and which fails. Where a move rests on an empirical premise — moves 6 and 8 most obviously — say whether the argument survives if that premise turns out to be false. Then tell me what each of them gets right that the other misses, whether the fork Hanna poses in move 5 is exhaustive, and what neither of them raised. I am not after a general survey of the paradox or a catalogue of the theories designed to accommodate it; I want the argument above assessed as it actually stands.
