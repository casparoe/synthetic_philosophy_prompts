Just spent three hours on the phone with a friend about a biopic and I still can't get out of her argument, which is maybe the worst sign, since I'm pretty sure the conclusion is nuts. Context: we watched a movie about a real civil rights lawyer who died in the 90s, and it invents a lot -- a composite of two of her actual colleagues rolled into one character, and a whole confrontation with her father that nobody who knew her ever mentioned. My friend says that's not a gray area, it's lying, and here's the chain she walked me through:

(1) A film that shows an identifiable real person speaking lines is asserting that that person said those lines. Not suggesting, not inviting us to imagine -- asserting, because the film is presented as being about the actual world and the audience takes it that way.

(2) So a film that gives a real person lines they never spoke asserts something false.

(3) Asserting something false to an audience that trusts you abuses that trust: you get them to form a belief you have no basis for offering, and it doesn't matter whether you knew it was false or just never checked.

(4) When the person depicted is dead, the abuse can't be repaired -- they can't correct the record, can't object, can't retroactively consent -- so the wrong is worse than the same fabrication about a living person, not milder.

(5) A disclaimer card at the front doesn't rescue anything. Either the card is an assertion, in which case for a film this loose with the record the card itself asserts something false and you've stacked a second lie on top of the first, or the card isn't an assertion, in which case by (1)'s own logic neither is the dialogue and the film has told us nothing at all, true or false, so the whole idea of learning anything from it collapses.

(6) The same reasoning runs straight into narrative history, which also fills in rooms, weather, hesitations, and motives that no document contains.

(7) So either biopics and readable history both systematically wrong the dead and their audience, or something in (1)-(3) is false. She takes the first horn: stick to documentaries and archives, or at least to scenes you can source line by line.

Here's where I got stuck and she just repeated herself. A subtitled documentary shows a real person speaking English words she never uttered. Same with a translated interview, same with a historian putting 18th-century speech into modern English. By (1) and (2) all of those assert falsehoods about what was said, and yet nobody thinks a subtitled documentary is lying to you. So either (1) is really about content rather than wording, and then I can't see what's doing the work in (2) anymore, because the composite colleague and maybe even the invented confrontation are arguably content-preserving compressions of things that did happen -- or (1) is about wording and the argument condemns translation, which she won't swallow either. She never picked.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? A few specific things I want priced:

- If the fix is that films with real subjects don't assert at all -- some pretense or make-believe reading of the whole thing, Walton-style -- how do you stop the documentary's assertions from being mere pretense too, and what happens to the second horn of (5)?

- If the fix is that they do assert but only something hedged or approximate, tell me exactly what the hedged content is, and whether it's a claim about what was said or about what was probably the case. Because that choice seems to decide whether the composite character and the invented confrontation land on the same side of the line, and her argument treats them as one and the same.

- If (4) is the premise to drop, what's left of the idea that we owe the dead anything? Honestly I'd rather lose (1) than (4), so tell me what I'm paying for that preference.

- And whatever you do with (6): if narrative history is off the hook but the biopic isn't, what's the actual difference, given that both fill gaps from thin evidence and both get believed by people who will never check?

Don't bother with citations or a reading list, I'll chase those down myself. Just tell me where it breaks, why the broken step looked so fine going in, and what each repair costs.
