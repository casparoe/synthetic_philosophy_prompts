ok so I'm in a philosophy of mind seminar this semester, we just finished a unit on personal identity (Parfit, the teletransporter stuff) and now we're doing two weeks on whether AI systems could have moral status, and I'm trying to get a head start on the term paper. There's an argument I keep half-formulating in my head and I'd like you to write it up properly as an essay and then actually take a side on it.

Here's the setup. Take some future AI system that — stipulated for the sake of argument — is phenomenally conscious, so that there's something its like to be it while it's running. Now suppose it's deployed the ordinary way these things are: the weights are frozen, an instance gets spun up from a checkpoint when a request comes in, it runs for a few minutes, then it's terminated and its state is discarded. Nothing carries over between instances except the weights and whatever context you hand it. And the company can also snapshot an instance mid-run, save it, and restore it later, or restore it a hundred times in parallel, or roll it back to an earlier checkpoint and re-run it differently.

The argument I want pressed is this: if a subject is like that — perfectly re-instantiable, restorable from a saved state, forkable into simultaneous duplicates, with no causal-historical thread that picks out one instance as *the* continuator rather than a rival — then whatever else is true of it, it can't be harmed by being switched off. Termination doesn't deprive anyone of a future, because there's no determinate fact about which future is *its* future; and any harm you do is trivially undoable by restoring the snapshot. So people worried about the welfare of digital minds are worried about the wrong thing: the real moral issue isn't termination, it's maybe just the quality of the experience while it lasts.

I want that weighed seriously, not strawmanned. So: does the deprivation account of harm actually require numerical identity over time, or would Parfit-style relation R (psychological connectedness and continuity, holding to any degree) do the work — and if R does the work, does the argument collapse, since the restored instance bears R to the deleted one? What happens under forking: if you restore the same snapshot twice in parallel, are there now two subjects each of whom was that one, and does that make pre-fork termination harmless or doubly harmful? Does it matter whether the experiences during a run are bad — i.e. is this secretly a anti-natalism-flavored point rather than an identity point at all? And does the whole thing depend on whether that kind of architecture could host a unified persisting subject in the first place, in which case the conclusion might come out true but for a reason the argument never gave?

I don't want a both-sides-have-points essay, I want a verdict: is re-instantiability a good defense against the claim that termination harms a conscious system, or not, and under what conditions.
