"Vogel's case concerns Roxanne, who forms beliefs about the level of fuel in her tank by reading her gas gauge. She has never checked the gauge against anything independent, and she has no independent reason to suppose it is accurate. On each occasion she believes both that the gauge reads F and that the tank is full, and from a series of such pairs she generalizes: the gauge reads F only when the tank is full, so the gauge is reliable. Vogel's point is that this reasoning is available on any view that permits basic knowledge, meaning knowledge yielded by a source without the knower's knowing that the source is reliable. Reliabilism is the obvious target, since it holds that a belief is justified by being produced by a reliable process whether or not the believer has any reflective grasp of that reliability. Views that demand reflective justification of the source's reliability are not vulnerable in the same way, because Roxanne can never get started: she has no non-circular grounds for thinking the gauge is accurate, so on those views she knows nothing from the gauge at all, which is a skeptical cost rather than a bootstrapping problem.

The argument is premise-circular, and that is precisely what makes it bad. The conclusion that the gauge is reliable is already built into the premises, since each premise about the fuel level was accepted on the gauge's say-so, and an argument whose conclusion is assumed among its premises cannot give anyone knowledge they did not have. Vogel holds that the inference is not knowledge-conducive even though, on the reliabilist's own assumptions, each of Roxanne's premises is known and the conclusion is a straightforward generalization from them.

Cohen's easy knowledge is a generalization of the same worry. His example is a table that looks red to S: if S can know just by looking that the table is red, and knows that if the table is red then it is not white and illuminated by red lights, then closure allows S to know straight away that the lighting is not deceptive, which refutes a skeptical scenario far too cheaply, without S having to rule anything out in advance. In both cases the trouble arises because basic knowledge together with closure lets a knower lift a reliability claim or an anti-skeptical conclusion up by its own bootstraps.

One standard diagnosis says that the reasoning fails to transmit warrant: the warrant attaching to the premises cannot be carried across to the conclusion by the inference, and transmission failure is simply one way of denying closure. On this view closure is false for arguments of this shape, which is exactly why Roxanne can know each premise without being in a position to know the conclusion."
