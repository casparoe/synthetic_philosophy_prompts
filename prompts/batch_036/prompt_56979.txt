I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I've spent the better part of a year on a piece about wasteful display — why organisms and institutions so reliably burn resources they could have kept, and what an observer is supposed to learn from the burning. My working sections so far have been the biology: Zahavi's insistence that a signal has to hurt in order to be believed, Grafen's formal version of that intuition, Spence's job-market model, and the anthropologists who carried the framework into human gift economies, including the competitive feasts of the Pacific Northwest coast where a host's standing rose in proportion to what he could afford to give away or destroy. The historical section is where I've stalled, and I want to hand you the part I can't do myself: work through the court of Louis XIV at Versailles as a costly-signaling system, in the technical sense, rather than as another set piece about Baroque extravagance. I'm not looking for atmosphere or for a survey of the reign. I'm looking for the mapping.

Here are the facts I'll assume, and you should tell me if any of them are load-bearing in a way I've misdescribed. Louis came to the throne as a child in 1643, lived through the Fronde, the noble and parlementaire revolts of 1648 to 1653 during which the royal family was driven from Paris, and never appears to have forgotten it. He ruled personally from 1661, moved the court permanently to Versailles in 1682, and by then the château and its gardens had consumed decades of labor and treasure, with the Hall of Mirrors finished around 1684. Court life was organized into graded rituals — the morning and evening levées, above all — in which proximity to the king's body was itself the scarce good: who was admitted at which stage, who was permitted to hand him his shirt, who could claim to have been seen by him. Attendance required enormous outlays on dress, lodging, gambling, and retainers, and the traditional gloss, which I treat as an interpretation rather than a documented intention, is that this was a mechanism for converting a warrior aristocracy into a class of courtiers too busy and too encumbered to conspire. Meanwhile Colbert died in 1683, the Edict of Nantes was revoked in 1685, hundreds of thousands of Huguenots left for the Dutch Republic, England, Prussia and elsewhere, and France fought two long, ruinous wars between 1688 and 1714, with the king dying in 1715 to a treasury in disarray.

With that in hand, I want four things. First, the disciplined mapping: who are the signalers and who is the audience, since it matters enormously that the primary audience is domestic rivals rather than foreign monarchs; what exactly is the signal, given that the expenditure is largely sunk into stone, cloth and table rather than gifted to the audience the way it is in the potlatch cases; what is the currency in which costs and benefits are denominated, and is there any honest way to translate political survival and marriage-market advantage into the fitness terms the handicap principle is built on; and what makes the signal credible rather than cheap talk — specifically, whether what operated at Versailles was a condition-dependent handicap, an index that couldn't be faked because presence and money were physically and legally required, a commitment device, or a positional arms race of the expenditure-cascade sort. Second, I want you to take seriously the two features that seem to me to break the standard model. One is that the costs were substantially financed out of tax revenue extracted from people who were not in the audience at all, which ought to weaken the inference from cost to quality — if a duke's display is paid for by peasants rather than out of his own condition, what does the display tell anyone about him? The other is the inversion of who captures the surplus: in Zahavi's and Spence's settings the signaler pays and the receiver reads, but here the receiver appears to have designed the signaling environment, set the terms of the contest, and harvested both the rents and the political neutralization of his competitors, which looks less like signaling than like a screening or mechanism-design problem run by the party with the power to define what counts as a credible signal. Third, tell me what the episode shows about the theory in the other direction — is Versailles a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the framework was never built to handle, and if both wasteful display by a strong monarch and the fiscal collapse that display helped produce can be narrated as consistent with the handicap principle, what observation would have counted against it? Finally, be explicit about where the framework goes silent: the venal offices and borrowing that actually funded the court, the international signaling game with other monarchs, the women of the court whose positions were acquired and defended through the same rituals, and the possibility that a better account is institutional or sociological rather than signaling-theoretic — Elias's reading of the court as a figuration that disciplined its members through mutual observation, say, or a straightforward story about war finance and elite bargaining. I don't want a bibliography or any reading recommendations; I want the argument. And if you think my framing is itself tendentious — that I've built the puzzle so that costly signaling has to fail — say so first and then give me the version of the analysis you'd defend instead.
