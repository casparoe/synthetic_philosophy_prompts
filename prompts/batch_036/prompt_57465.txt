I am drafting the central section of an end-of-term essay for my undergraduate paper on early Chinese philosophy, and the argument I constructed to organise it has turned against me: it appears to show that Xunzi is a Mencian, a conclusion neither I nor my tutor can accept. It runs as follows.

(1) Xunzi draws a strict line between xing and wei: xing is what a human being possesses without instruction or effort — the senses, the appetites, the feelings — whereas wei is what is produced through study, deliberation and training.
(2) On his account, ritual and righteousness belong wholly to wei; they are made rather than grown, as a vessel is made from clay rather than found already in it.
(3) But no act of wei is performed by nothing: an agent can bring about only what their nature already equips them to bring about, so the capacities exercised in any piece of deliberate activity must be supplied by xing.
(4) The first sages, in devising ritual and righteousness, therefore exercised capacities that their own xing supplied.
(5) Xunzi insists that the xing of the sage and the xing of the ordinary person are identical.
(6) Hence those capacities are supplied by every human being's xing.
(7) A natural capacity which, exercised freely and without anyone's instruction, issues in ritual and righteousness is precisely what Mencius means by the sprouts: a nature oriented toward the good, and not merely capable of it.
(8) So Xunzi's own premises entail that human nature is good, and his quarrel with Mencius is terminological rather than substantive.

Two features of this worry me. First, it generalises far beyond the case I want: by the same route anything human beings manage to produce — writing, bronze casting, taxation — would sit 'in' human nature, and Gaozi's neutral nature, the water that runs whichever way the channel is cut, would collapse into Mencius's water that tends downward. That cannot be right, yet I cannot locate the step doing the illegitimate work. Second, the most obvious retreat looks expensive. If I block (3) by saying that xing supplies the raw material while ritual supplies the form, then Xunzi's treatment of the mind — the ruler that issues commands and receives none, able to accept or refuse the promptings of the senses, and apparently natural in its capacity to weigh and to know — begins to look like an innate faculty with a normative job attached, which concedes much of Mencius's case in any event.

Which premise or step should be surrendered, and what does surrendering it cost? If I must choose, I would rather keep (1), (2) and (5), since those appear to be the load-bearing components of Xunzi's system. In addition, please address: whether his distinction between what the person in the street could become and what they can actually be made to become does the work I need here, or merely restates the difficulty in other words; why the premise you reject looked unobjectionable when I wrote it down, and what ambiguity or suppressed assumption made it look that way; what, on the surviving version of the argument, would count as a genuinely substantive disagreement between Mencius and Xunzi about nature rather than a dispute over the word; and whether retreating to Gaozi's neutral ground is stable at all, given that Mencius's objection to neutrality was precisely that it cannot explain why goodness is the direction things tend to take when nothing obstructs them.
