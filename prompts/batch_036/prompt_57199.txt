You are a decision theorist who has served as a technical adviser to public risk-management bodies, and who can explain a quantitative trade-off to a room containing a structural engineer, a county attorney, and three elected officials who have never seen a utility function. Please work through the arithmetic privately before you write anything, and then hand me one clean document I can circulate to my colleagues essentially unchanged.

I sit on a five-member regional flood authority that operates a dam with an auxiliary spillway. Opening the spillway ahead of a forecast storm deliberately floods the Willow Creek agricultural district below it: roughly 4,100 acres of farmland and about 90 occupied homes, assessed at $230 million, with no fatalities expected. Not opening it, and having the river crest above the levee's design capacity, puts a downstream city of about 90,000 under water: our engineering staff's central estimate is $2.4 billion in damage and on the order of 40 fatalities in that scenario. The forecast for the storm now approaching gives a 4 percent chance of a crest above design capacity, and the operational decision has to be made about 36 hours before the projected crest — that is next Tuesday at noon. For benefit-cost work the board has been using a value of a statistical life of roughly $12 million. My own back-of-envelope arithmetic suggests that the certain loss from opening is larger than the expected loss from not opening, and this is exactly where the board has come apart. Two members insist that the phrase they want in the resolution — 'we do not gamble with lives to avoid economic loss' — settles the matter. A third says any rule that is not straightforward expected-loss minimization is incoherent and will be torn apart by plaintiffs' experts. Our county attorney has warned that whatever formula we write down becomes discoverable and becomes the standard we are afterwards judged against, in both directions: the Willow Creek landowners will sue if we open and the crest never comes, and the coroner's inquest will read our rule aloud if we do not open and people die. I cannot tell whether my colleagues' slogan expresses a coherent technical claim or merely an emphatic one, and I suspect we are about to adopt language in a formal resolution that nobody on this board could operationalize.

The agenda item for Tuesday's meeting is not only the storm. It is whether the authority should adopt, for the first time, a written ex ante decision rule for protective actions, of the following general shapes: (i) minimize expected loss; (ii) minimize expected loss computed with a concave valuation of money and of fatalities, so that large losses and deaths count disproportionately; (iii) adopt a rank-dependent or risk-weighted rule, in which the weight attached to an outcome depends on where it sits in the ranking of outcomes and not only on its probability, so that a low-probability worst case carries more decision weight than its probability alone would give it; or (iv) a lexicographic threshold rule — protect against any scenario above some fatality level unless the cost exceeds some stated multiple. Draft the board's decision memo. It must contain: a bottom line up front stating which rule you recommend we adopt and what that rule implies for Tuesday's spillway decision, with the deadline; the recommended rule stated in a form a staff hydrologist could apply without further judgment, naming every input it requires; the arithmetic for the pending storm showing explicitly where candidates (i), (ii), and (iii) diverge and where they agree; and one further pair of choices, of your own construction, in which the concave-valuation explanation and the rank-weighting explanation point in opposite directions, because the board needs to know which reason it is committing to and the minutes will only record one of them. You should also engage a second pending matter: next year's capital program offers two alternatives with equal expected annual loss reduction of about $18 million — one reduces a diffuse set of frequent small events, the other eliminates a 0.6 percent annual chance of a $3 billion event — and I want to know whether our rule treats these as equivalent, and what we say if it does not.

Address the strongest consideration on the losing side properly rather than strawmanning it. If you recommend expected-loss minimization, take seriously the argument that this is a one-shot, non-repeatable decision about losses that are not fungible and that no amount of averaging across years compensates the people who bear them. If you recommend a risk-weighted or threshold rule, take seriously the objection that a sequence of individually approved decisions can amount to something no member of this board would approve as a package: if we open on 4 percent forecasts year after year, Willow Creek floods roughly once a generation on average while the city is never flooded at all, and the city's planning commission has already asked whether it may permit 1,200 new housing units in the protected floodplain on the strength of our assurance. Say what our rule implies about both. Close with a paragraph of perhaps a hundred words that the chair can read aloud to the Willow Creek residents' association, which explains the decision without condescension and without pretending the loss is small.

You may use standard terminology — risk-weighted expected utility, rank-dependence, the sure-thing principle, dynamic consistency — but define each in a clause for the non-specialists, and give no references, bibliography, reading list, or citation to any case, statute, or theorist; where a point genuinely turns on law, flag it for the county attorney rather than asserting it. Flag any number I have supplied that is a placeholder requiring engineering or actuarial input before it goes in a resolution. Target 1,100 to 1,400 words, with clear headers, and think carefully about the second case rather than resolving it by assertion: which of these rules is right for a public body that has to publish its reasoning in advance is not the same question as which is right for an individual facing a single gamble.
