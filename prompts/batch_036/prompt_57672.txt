I write a long-form column on science and ideas for a weekly current-affairs magazine, and I am a fortnight from filing a piece on what it now takes for a theory in fundamental physics to count as confirmed. To get the strongest version of one side I interviewed a theoretical physicist who has worked on quantum gravity for thirty years; afterwards he sent me his case tidied into numbered steps, saying he would be happy to be quoted on the conclusion if not on the reasoning.

His steps:

(1) What evidence does is narrow the range of theories compatible with it; a theory's support is a matter of how well it is discriminated from the alternatives that were genuinely on offer.

(2) We sometimes acquire reason to think the space of serious alternatives has been canvassed exhaustively without touching the data at all: when a large, well-resourced community has hunted for a rival for decades and found none; when a single framework keeps surfacing, unbidden, in domains it was not built for; and when past episodes in which no rival turned up eventually ended in empirical vindication.

(3) In such a case raising one's confidence is rational, and what one has is confirmation in the full sense rather than a promissory note redeemable later against experiment.

(4) String theory meets all three conditions: decades of intense work, no rival quantum theory of gravity of comparable scope, and payoffs nobody set out to get, among them a microscopic counting of the states of certain highly symmetric black holes that lands exactly on the entropy their horizon area implies, together with a duality that converts otherwise intractable strongly coupled problems into soluble weakly coupled ones.

(5) So string theory is confirmed, and the charge that it predicts nothing at energies we can reach is beside the point.

I then wrote the following myself, as a test, and he has not yet answered it:

(6) Apply (1) to (3) to the most promiscuous multiverse hypothesis on offer, the one on which every self-consistent assignment of physical parameters is realised somewhere. Its case is stronger than string theory's. No conceivable observation could conflict with it, so any search for a rival is guaranteed to come up empty, and the emptiness is therefore as informative as emptiness can be.

(7) Nothing is more thoroughly discriminated from its alternatives than a theory that has none.

(8) So (1) to (3) entail that the emptiest cosmology available is the best-confirmed theory we have.

(9) That is intolerable; hence (1) to (3) are false, and confirmation must after all be empirical.

My editor wants the piece to turn on (9), on the grounds that a reductio is a reductio. I am not convinced, and I cannot tell whether my reluctance is philosophical judgement or attachment to an argument I have grown fond of.

Take the nine steps as a single chain and tell me where it breaks. I want to know which step you would reject and why it managed to look unobjectionable on a first reading; whether my extension at (6) to (8) is a fair application of the physicist's principle or a distortion of it; and what each available exit costs. Please be specific about that last part. I cover cosmology, climate modelling and machine-learning research, and if accepting (9) means I can no longer call anything confirmed until it has been tested, I want to know what that does to the ordinary work of reporting on models of the early universe, of the ice sheets in 2100, and of systems whose designers cannot say what they are optimising for. I suspect that is a price I cannot pay and still keep the column.

Two claims I would rather not be talked out of, though I may have to be: that researchers can rationally trust a theory well beyond the reach of experiment, and that a theory compatible with absolutely everything is not thereby well supported. I have no attachment whatever to (5).

Finally, please state how confident you are in the diagnosis you give me, and single out the point in it about which you are least sure. It will go past a fact-checker who read philosophy as an undergraduate, and I would much rather flag the soft ground myself than have her find it.
