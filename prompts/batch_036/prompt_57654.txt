My dad was a cabinetmaker in upstate New York, mostly self-taught, and for about thirty years he made copies of a famous Danish lounge chair from the fifties — you know the type, wooden frame with curved laminated arms, the ones that go for five figures at auction when they're original. He never sold a single one. He gave them to relatives, made a few for his bowling buddies, and kept two in the den where we watched football. There was a photo of the chair in a library book he'd traced the proportions from, and he'd hand-cut all the joinery, steam-bend the arms himself, and stamp the underside with his own shop mark. He died last spring and my sister and I split the four that were in the house. I got the two den chairs, which is the one I actually sit in every morning with my coffee.

Here's the thing. A dealer two towns over heard about them and drove out to look. He was honest, said he'd list them exactly as what they are, a mid-century copy in the manner of the original, and offered about eleven hundred for the pair. He also said, almost apologetically, that if they were the real thing he'd be writing a check with another zero on it. My sister thinks we should take the money and split it. But since that visit I can't look at the chairs the same way, and I hate that about myself, because nothing about them changed. My friend Marcus says I'm being an idiot, and his argument is basically: if you swapped your dad's chairs into a museum next to a real one and nobody on earth could tell the difference, nothing would have changed, so whatever you think you're missing isn't in the chair, it's in your head — and besides, your dad's joinery is better than the licensed reissues they sell today, so you should be proud. That sounds right when he says it, but it doesn't make me feel any better and I can't figure out why not.

One more detail that keeps nagging at me. Dad got the back angle wrong. He was working off a photo and his seat-back sits maybe three or four degrees more upright than the real one, so his chairs aren't quite the same shape, and once you know it you can see it. Part of me thinks that makes them worse copies; another part thinks it makes them something else entirely, a chair he designed by accident. So — is Marcus actually right, or is he missing something? Is my deflation at the eleven-hundred-dollar offer irrational, and if not, what exactly am I responding to? And practically, should I sell, keep both, or is there a third option I'm not seeing? Please answer in American English.
