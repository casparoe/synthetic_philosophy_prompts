New job and I'm already in over my head a bit. I'm an analyst at a regional hospital group, and my whole first quarter went into a memo on whether we should replace our appointment-scheduling system — 25 years old, held together with overnight batch scripts, hated by everyone, touched by no one. At the review meeting our CIO waved the recommendation off with "history matters." Not as a throwaway. He said it like it was a theorem, and the room nodded.

What's been bugging me is that the exact same phrase did three different jobs inside ninety minutes. Him: our workflows, vendor contracts and everyones habits were built around the incumbent, so a migration fails no matter how good the replacement looks on paper. A physician lead, five minutes later: the only reason we're on this thing is that a vendor happened to be demoing it in 1999 and the guy who picked it left a year after, so the fact that it's incumbent tells you nothing about whether it's any good. Then someone from finance used it to argue something closer to the opposite of both: whatever we choose next will still be here in twenty years, so we should absolutely spend another year evaluating.

So: I want the readings pulled apart. What are the distinct claims that one sentence could be making, from the ones that are trivially true of anything with a state to the ones that are contentious or maybe just false? I can feel that some of them are about the endpoint not being pinned down by present conditions alone, some are about there being several possible stable endpoints with which one you get decided by early noise, some are about backing up not undoing what going forward did, and at least one is about the endpoint being worse than something that was available and still sticking around. Which is which, which actually follow from which, and which ones just get run together? Specifically: does the physician's "it was an accident" point license "so it's probably not the best one," or is that a separate and much bolder claim? And is the CIO's version — locked in, migration doomed — even the same thesis, or is he just borrowing its authority?

One friend says this is all just the keyboard-layout story ported to hospitals, but I don't buy that either, since that story is about a market holding onto something inefficient and I'm not sure anyone in my building is claiming inefficiency.

Give me a list I can basically drop into the follow-up memo: each reading stated plainly, a verdict on how strong it is, and what it would and wouldn't let us conclude about the migration. Also, if there's a cheap test — something in our own rollout history or data that would tell the readings apart — say so. And when people in operational roles repeat the slogan, which reading do you think they usually mean? No references or reading lists, please, I just need the distinctions.
