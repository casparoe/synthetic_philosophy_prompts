I run an informal reading group in moral psychology, and this term we took up the question whether self-deception in the strict sense — as opposed to mere motivated error — is even possible. One of the members came in with the following argument, which he says he assembled out of pieces everyone in the room already accepted, and I have spent two evenings failing to find the exit. It seems airtight through step (7) and then obviously wrong somewhere around (9), and I cannot see which step licenses the slide.

(1) To deceive someone is to intentionally produce in that person a belief one takes to be false; without the intention it is merely misleading, and without the falsity-assessment it is merely being wrong together.
(2) So if S deceives herself into believing that not-p, S intentionally brings it about that she believes not-p while believing that p.
(3) An agent can intentionally bring about a state only if there is some means at her disposal for bringing it about.
(4) The only means S has over her own believing is the management of her cognitive economy: which evidence she attends to, which people she sees, which questions she permits herself to raise, which considerations she rehearses.
(5) Any belief S acquires by such management is a belief whose provenance she knows: she knows she arranged to have it, and she knows that what moved her to arrange it was wanting it rather than finding it true.
(6) A belief that one knows one engineered, for a reason unconnected with its truth, cannot do the work of a belief. It cannot present the world to one as being a certain way, because the presentation one did not engineer is standing right beside it, and one knows which is which.
(7) Therefore S cannot succeed at strict self-deception: she either fails to acquire the belief, or she acquires something that is not a belief — a hope, a pretense, an avowal she cannot help making.
(8) Nor is any softer version available. The indirect routes we usually point to — the unread newspaper, the drink before the difficult conversation, the friend who always agrees — are just (4) spread out over weeks, and (5) and (6) apply to them with exactly the same force, since the delay does nothing to erase S's knowledge of what she is doing.
(9) But (8) cannot stand, and not only because we all recognize such people as self-deceived — indeed the reproach takes for granted that they could have arranged things differently. The real trouble is that (5) and (6) also condemn the mirror-image case. Suppose S suspects that p and resolves to fix that: she forces herself to read the arguments she has been avoiding, sits in on a course, asks the colleague she trusts to correct her in public, and keeps at it until she believes that p. Every one of those steps is engineered by S, deliberately, with full knowledge of the provenance, and the resulting belief is exactly what the engineering was for. If (6) is right, that belief is no belief either, and no one can ever improve her own mind by deliberate means.
(10) So the argument appears to show either that strict self-deception is impossible or that deliberate belief-management of any kind is impossible, and I do not believe the second and I am not prepared to assert the first.

What I want is a diagnosis rather than a survey, and for the same reason I would rather not receive a bibliography or a list of who has said what: I want the machinery of this argument taken apart. Which step do you reject, and why did that step look acceptable when it was first put on the table? Is this the sort of argument that contains a genuine flaw — an equivocation, a slide in what the agent is said to know, a principle stated more strongly than the cases support — or is it valid and merely forces a costly choice among plausible premises? Please say which of those two it is, since the group has split on exactly that. Then tell me whether (6) is doing its work through the provenance of the belief or through something else, and whether it makes any difference to (7) if the case in (9) is stipulated to be identical to a self-deception case in every respect except the agent's reason for arranging it. Finally, price the exits, one at a time: what is lost if I give up the intention requirement in (1) and (2) — does self-deception then collapse into ordinary motivated bias, and is anything left of it as a distinct thing to accuse someone of? What am I committed to if I deny (3) or (4) — does that amount to supposing one can believe at will, and how should I answer the objection that belief aims at truth and so cannot be acquired for reasons of convenience? What does a Davidson-style partitioning of the mind cost me at (2), and is a belief in a sealed-off compartment still a belief at all? And what should I say if I simply accept (7) and insist that the people in (8) are not self-deceived but merely biased — what distinguishes bias from self-deception on that view, and is the distinction one I can actually draw? For what it is worth, if I am forced to choose I would rather keep (9) than (1), and I am not attached to (4) at all.
