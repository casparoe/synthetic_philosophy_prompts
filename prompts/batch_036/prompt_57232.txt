I'm in the middle of a dissertation chapter on Hofstadter's case against machine translation — the idea that a statistical system is doing elaborate surface-string matching and never actually models the situation the words describe. He makes most of his case with Marot's little poem to a sick young lady, but I want a prose example, because the poetry one lets the other side say the complaint is really about form and beauty rather than meaning, and that's a fight I don't want to have.

So here's the ask. Give me one worked example of a failure mode I've been calling cue-dependence: a translation counts as cue-dependent when the system gets it right only because the source string, or something nearly identical to it, is very predictable from the training data, so a pertubation of the source that leaves the described situation exactly the same but breaks the surface cue makes the output go wrong. I want the whole package — the original sentence, the fluent-but-wrong output a big neural system would plausibly produce, the minimal meaning-preserving edit that exposes it, and a couple of sentences on why that wrong output shows the system had no representation of who did what to whom (scope of a negation, which noun the modifier attaches to, whatever you pick).

Please don't use the old vodka-and-rotten-meat back-translation story, its apocryphal and everybody reaches for it. And steer clear of puns and idioms — the failure should be visible in completely literal prose. If you think my notion doesn't actually carve out anything interesting, that the example just shows a coverage gap rather than a gap in kind, say that and give me the example anyway.
