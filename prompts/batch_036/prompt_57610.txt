I have been assembling a position on the epistemics of mass politics for a reading group I attend, and before I present it I would like a genuine consistency audit rather than encouragement. I have spent some years reading rationalist blogs and forums, so I am used to having views stress-tested; what I want here is that treatment applied to a package I suspect is more strained than I have admitted to myself.

Here is the package. Assume I know the ancient cases well; please do not spend any of the answer retelling them.

(1) I accept Ober's account of classical Athens: that Athens outperformed its rivals for a couple of centuries because its institutions were unusually good at gathering and deploying dispersed knowledge — open deliberation in the assembly, sortition, payment for public service, decrees inscribed and publicly posted, and a legal culture that let any citizen challenge a decree as unlawful. Crucially, Athenian decision-makers were priced: a general who failed could be tried for his life, and after Arginusae several were. For me this is positive evidence that democratic institutions can be knowledge-aggregating.

(2) I also accept Caplan's rational irrationality: the median voter is not merely uninformed but systematically biased, and because a single vote is instrumentally worthless there is no price attached to holding comfortable false political beliefs. That is why modern mass democracy chooses bad policies. It is a bias story, not an ignorance story, and I care about that distinction.

(3) I take the Condorcet jury theorem seriously but restrictively. Aggregation delivers only if individual competence is better than chance and errors are independent. In modern politics independence fails comprehensively — shared media diets, party cue-taking, information cascades — so I think invoking Condorcet on behalf of actual electorates is usually a cheat.

(4) The Mytilenean affair is, for me, the single most important datum in the ancient record. The assembly decreed the execution of the adult males of Mytilene, reconvened the following day, was persuaded to rescind, and got a second trireme to Lesbos before the massacre. That is error detection and repair inside roughly twenty-four hours, performed by the very body that committed the error, with no external check imposed on it. Insulated expert rule has no equivalent second vote.

(5) But the Sicilian expedition points the other way. Twelve years later the same institution committed the city to a war against an island whose size and population, as Thucydides remarks, most of the voters did not know; the general who had argued against going ended up arguing for a considerably larger force; and the whole enterprise was annihilated. So the mechanism I praise in (4) plainly does not generalize.

(6) I do not think the trial of Socrates is evidence about the epistemic performance of democracy at all. It was a lawful trial before a lotteried jury, the defendant had every procedural option Athenian practice afforded him, and the charges were impiety and corrupting the young. The political reading — that the real targets were Critias and Alcibiades, and that a religious charge was used because the amnesty following the fall of the Thirty blocked direct prosecution of the oligarchic period — strikes me as speculative reconstruction dressed as history. And in any case one bad verdict in one case tells you nothing about a decision procedure's error rate.

(7) I nevertheless support sortition as a modern reform: some portion of a legislative chamber, or a standing citizen assembly with real agenda power, selected by lot.

(8) I also think the forecasting-tournament literature shows what aggregation looks like when it works — scored, repeated, calibrated, with reputational skin in the game. My general principle, which I take from (2), (3), and (8) together, is that a collective judgment procedure deserves trust only to the extent that its participants pay for being wrong.

I expect the headline problem to be (4) and (5), which look like a flat contradiction, and I have been treating them as the weak joint I need to defend. I suspect I have misidentified where the weak joint actually is. So:

- Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and for each state precisely what would have to be given up and what giving it up costs me.
- Tell me which pairs only look inconsistent and dissolve under inspection, and explain why they dissolve.
- Point out anywhere I am holding the same body of evidence to different standards depending on which of my conclusions it happens to support.
- Identify the single revision that would restore the most coherence, and say whether it leaves anything I actually care about intact.

I would rather be told the package cannot be held together than reassured that all of these commitments are "in tension but manageable." If the correct diagnosis is that one of these is doing all the load-bearing work and is itself unjustified, say which one and show me the argument that depends on it.
