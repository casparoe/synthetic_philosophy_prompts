So I'm a data analyst who got hooked on Turchin's stuff over the past year — the secular cycles idea, structural-demographic theory, the argument that agrarian states run on roughly 250-year cycles where population growth pushes down real wages, the state goes broke, and then you get what he calls elite overproduction: way more elite aspirants than elite positions, so the elites start fighting each other and instability spikes. I take his leading indicators seriously enough (real wages, urbanisation, elite numbers, state finances) even though I know the critics say he's just fitting curves to the same history he claims to be predicting.

Then somebody pointed me at Henry Thomas Buckle as the Victorian ancestor of all this, and I've been reading the first volume of his History of Civilization in England in the Project Gutenberg text (ebook 44493). I'm stuck in the big opening chapters where he lays out his method, and I'd rather actually understand him than skim past.

The bit I'm stuck on runs roughly like this — a paraphrase, not a transcription, I'm not going to pretend I can type out his sentences exactly. He says that if you look at any single man's actions you can't predict them, they look irregular and free, but look at large numbers of men acting together and the results are strikingly uniform: the annual totals of things like marriages and crimes in a big society come back nearly the same year after year, and he takes that uniformity to beat anything you'd find in the physical world. From there he concludes that history must be governed by fixed laws, that the free will doctrine has been a disaster for the study of history because it made historians explain events by individual volitions instead of general causes, and that the way forward is to treat human affairs statistically. Then he does something I dont follow at all: he sorts the causes of history into moral ones (motives, feelings, character) and intellectual ones (knowledge, discovery, skill), says the moral ones are stationary — the same in every age and every country, so they never vary — while the intellectual ones are progressive, and therefore civilization is driven by intellect and the moral causes basically aren't causes of change at all. (There's a third category he brings in later about nature and climate, which I'm setting aside.) And somewhere in the middle of all this he concedes we can't actually predict anything yet, because we don't have the data or the laws worked out.

Can you help me with it?

1. What is the actual inference from "aggregate totals are regular" to "history is law-governed"? Is he claiming individual acts aren't free, or only that individual freedom is noise that cancels out and doesn't matter for the science? He seems to slide between those two and I can't tell which one his conclusion needs.

2. The moral/intellectual split. Is his principle something like "a cause that never varies can't explain why things vary"? If that's it, is it legitimate, and what would have to be true for it to work — do the moral causes have to be constant across all societies, or only across time within one society? Would a single counterexample, one culture with genuinely different motives, break it, or does he have an escape hatch?

3. How does his determinism-without-prediction compare with what Turchin does? Turchin was willing to say instability would peak somewhere around 2020. Buckle says the laws exist but we can't use them yet. Which posture is more defensible, and is Turchin's move — we predict aggregate dynamics, not individual events — really the same move Buckle makes, or a different one that only looks similar?

4. Buckle's explanans is knowledge, Turchin's is demography and elite competition. Is there any honest sense in which Buckle is a proto-cliodynamician, or is he doing something categorically different — a directional theory of progress rather than a cyclical one, with no mechanism you could operationalise? Don't force the analogy, I'd much rather hear where it breaks.

No bibliography or reading list please, I just want the argument untangled.
