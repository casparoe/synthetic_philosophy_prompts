You are a philosopher of mind with substantial experience as an evaluator of machine learning systems — someone who can read a technical measurement protocol and say precisely what theory of cognition it silently commits its authors to. Please treat this as an exercise in conceptual reconstruction rather than as a request for advice or for a verdict on whether models "really" understand anything.

I work on the evaluation side of a lab that builds large language models, and over the past year I have become uneasy about our contamination policy, which I will describe as concretely as I can. Before each release, we screen the model for verbatim familiarity with the items of widely used public benchmarks: we look for long exact overlaps between model outputs and benchmark text, and in some cases we check whether the model can reproduce unique random identifier strings that a benchmark's authors deliberately embedded in their released test data. If a model trips a threshold, we void the score for that entire benchmark — not merely for the individual items it appears to have seen, but for the instrument as a whole, on the reasoning that the benchmark has stopped measuring anything. Alongside this, we commission private problem sets that are never published: contractors write them, they are rewritten each cycle with new names, new numbers, and new surface phrasing but deliberately the same underlying structure, and access is restricted to a handful of people. These private sets, plus "live" evaluations built from problems that only came into existence after the training cutoff — this month's examinations, puzzles released last week — are treated internally as our strongest evidence about what the model can do. We also, occasionally, seed plausible but fabricated claims into places likely to be scraped, and treat a model's confident repetition of such a claim as strong evidence that it ingested that material, while treating its silence as only weak evidence that it did not.

Here is what puzzles me. We still publish and celebrate headline scores on older public benchmarks whose items, solutions, and walkthroughs were debated at length across the open web long before our training cutoff, and we describe year-over-year gains there as evidence of improved reasoning. And when a model solves a fresh private item that is structurally identical to thousands of near-duplicates in its training data, we call that generalization rather than retrieval. So the policy appears to hold that the provenance of an answer can disqualify it as evidence, and simultaneously that provenance is irrelevant whenever the surface form has been altered. If reproducing a memorized answer tells us nothing about whether the model grasps the problem, why does reproducing a memorized procedure — one instantiated in a vast family of superficially different training items — tell us a great deal? Is the distinction we are drawing really about memorization at all, or about something else: a performance-versus-competence distinction, a requirement of counterfactual robustness, a requirement that the right answer be produced by the right kind of internal process, a worry about the measuring instrument rather than about the mind being measured? I would like these possibilities sorted out rather than listed.

Please think carefully and, before you commit to an interpretation, spend a short opening section brainstorming the candidate presuppositions the policy might rest on, including at least one you end up rejecting and explaining why. Then reconstruct the implicit theory of understanding and of evidential support that makes this bundle of rules coherent: what must be true of understanding for verbatim reproduction to be evidentially worthless while structurally parallel novel performance is evidentially gold? Say explicitly which rival views the policy rules out or ignores — a purely dispositional or functionalist account on which understanding just is a profile of input-output dispositions, so that where the answer came from cannot matter; the stochastic-parrots line of objection, on which success on a freshly written item is no better evidence of comprehension than success on a memorized one, since both are recombinations of training patterns; and any others you think are being quietly excluded. Assess whether the parts of the policy presuppose consistent things with one another, paying particular attention to the all-or-nothing voiding of a whole benchmark, to the asymmetry between how we weight a repeated fabricated claim and how we weight silence about one, and to the tension between our treatment of old public benchmarks and our treatment of contamination. Distinguish throughout what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and flag the places where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that two or three incompatible theories would license exactly the same rules. Finally, tell me what would have to change in the policy if we adopted the strongest rival account instead — would contamination screening become pointless, or would it survive as a measurement-validity concern rather than a concern about minds? Do not give me citations, a reading list, or named sources; argue from the concepts. Roughly nine hundred to twelve hundred words, and if you need to assume something about how evaluation is actually done that I have not stated, say so explicitly when you rely on it.
