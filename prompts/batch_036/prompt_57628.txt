I run the volunteer training program for a network of about thirty community gardens, and the block I get asked to teach every spring is integrated pest management — thresholds, scouting, the prevention-first hierarchy with chemicals as the absolute last resort. This year I want to bolt on a forty-minute evening session that asks the question IPM politely declines to ask, which is whether killing the things eating the kale is itself a moral problem or just a management one. What I need from you isn't an essay, it's a sorting quiz: nine questions, each either a concrete decision a gardener actually faces or a single sentence they can agree or disagree with, three options per question, and a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto named positions. The whole point is to sort people by what they say about cases rather than by what they call themselves, so no option may contain words like utilitarian, deontologist, sentient, intrinsic value, or speciesism — those show up only in the key. And nothing should be answerable by knowing what a good environmentalist is supposed to say; I want at least two questions where the "green" answer and the "kind" answer point in opposite directions.

That last part is the reason I'm asking instead of writing it myself. I've become fairly convinced that the organic/synthetic axis and the insect-welfare axis come apart almost completely and that none of my gardeners have ever thought about it: releasing ladybugs or parasitoid wasps means the aphid gets eaten from the inside over days, Bt works slowly and from within the gut, spinosad and pyrethrins knock down fast, and a systemic neonicotinoid poisons slowly, for a long time, and everything that touches the plant. Someone can be scrupulously organic and be choosing the most drawn-out deaths on the shelf. So I want at least one leaf in the key that is basically "manner of death matters a great deal, number of deaths doesn't," which I think is a live position that nobody in my network would recognize themselves in. Other leaves I want represented, though you should tell me if any of them is a strawman nobody actually defends: insects have no morally relevant experience so the question never arises; we don't know whether they do, and the right move is to weight that uncertainty by some small-but-nonzero probability; we don't know, and the right move is to refuse to speculate and fall back on ecological role; individual insects matter but are so numerous and so quickly replaced that deaths are offset by births; individual insects matter and their sheer numbers swamp every other consideration; the population or ecosystem is the unit of concern so killing to restore habitat is fine and spraying for cosmetic reasons isn't; killing is flatly ruled out and the only remaining tools are exclusion, tolerance, and letting crops fail; and a relational one I keep half-formulating, something like "they're pests only relative to our property claims, and we built the monoculture that made them abundant, so what's owed is hospitality rather than extermination."

Two more things before the format. I want the logical space visible, not just a roster of names: after the key, tell me which combinations of answers correspond to views nobody holds, and for each one say whether that's because the combination is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody has gotten around to it yet. And flag every pair of leaves these nine questions can't actually separate — I'd much rather read "these two come out identical on this instrument" than have you fudge a question to pretend otherwise. If you think the whole enterprise is broken, say that in one sentence before question one and that's the only preamble I want; I have a nagging worry that a quiz answerable by people with no philosophy background collapses into "do you like bugs or not," and I don't want to be told I'm wrong about that just to be polite. Otherwise: start with question one, no preface, no summary of what I asked for, quiz then key then the diagnosis of the empty cells and the inseperable pairs, and finish with two sentences naming which single question does the most sorting work and why.
