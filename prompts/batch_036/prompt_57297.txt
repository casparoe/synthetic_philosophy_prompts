I chair my hospital's committee on novel and non-protocol interventions: roughly once a month we receive a request to permit something whose evidence base is thin — an unapproved device for a single patient, a prediction algorithm validated only retrospectively, a protocol adapted from an analogous condition. Our members are clinicians and administrators with no decision theory, we have forty minutes per case, and the chief medical officer signs off on our recommendation. I want a decision procedure the committee can actually run, not an essay on ambiguity aversion.

The procedure should preserve what I take to be the substance of the Ellsberg material rather than its slogan. Reluctance to act when probabilities are imprecise is not one thing, and it matters whether we have no information at all, information from a source we cannot evaluate, or conflicting information from sources we can evaluate separately. Aversion to an unfamiliar source often shrinks once that source is judged on its own instead of against a familiar benchmark; a committee that always sees the novel request next to standard care will be systematically harsher than one that sometimes does not. And the formal responses are different procedures with different consequences, not interchangeable glosses: adopting a set of admissible probability judgments rather than a single one; choosing what performs best under the least favorable member of that set; or declining to rank at all and defaulting to the status quo until some option dominates. Tell me which of these you are building into our workflow, and what following each of the others would have required instead. The procedure must also include a step that decides whether further evidence could reduce the ambiguity or would only relocate it, and it should treat squarely the possibility that commissioning another study is itself a way of avoiding the decision.

Then run it on three cases: (i) single-patient use of an unapproved implantable device for a condition with no remaining alternative, where the mechanism is plausible and three foreign case reports exist; (ii) hospital-wide deployment of a sepsis early-warning model with strong retrospective performance on our own data, no prospective validation, and evidence that it underperforms in a population we serve heavily; (iii) continuation of a two-year pilot whose outcome data are mildly positive but statistically weak, and which clinicians now depend on. I expect (ii) to be permitted and (i) denied; if your procedure says otherwise, identify the conflation my expectation rests on.

Please use these sections, in this order: "What the theory licenses" (about 250 words); "The procedure" (numbered questions in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each and the stopping rules for deny, permit, and permit-with-conditions; about 700 words); "Where judgment cannot be eliminated" (about 300 words); "Worked cases" (about 600 words); "How this procedure will fail" (about 250 words); and "What would change my mind" (about 200 words). In that final section, be candid about which consideration would move you to a different verdict on how much weight imprecise evidence should carry — a successful Bayesian rejoinder, a reason to think the laboratory findings do not generalize to committees deciding about identifiable patients, or a defect in how I have separated the three kinds of epistemic predicament — and say where your own reasoning is most likely to break.
