Money-pump arguments against intransitive preferences are typically met with the reply that a pump only bites against a myopic agent, and that a sophisticated chooser who reasons by backward induction declines the very first trade. I want to understand the conceptual machinery behind that reply, and behind the standard objection to it, rather than the standard textbook presentation of the pump itself.

Suppose an agent with a strict preference cycle over three goods, offered trades one at a time, each trade available only on payment of a small premium. Under naive choice he accepts at every node and ends the sequence holding his original good minus three premiums. Under sophisticated choice he anticipates his own future acceptances, sees where the sequence terminates, and refuses the opening trade. Under resolute choice he adopts a plan and holds to it even at nodes where his local preference favours trading.

Please explain the following five points:

(a) Why the sophisticated agent's escape is regarded as unstable: what precisely is supposed to go wrong, given that the refusal is only an anticipation and no mechanism binds the future self who, at the node, will still face a cycle and a premium.

(b) How the notion of dynamic consistency is being used differently when it is applied to an agent's preferences over whole plans than when it is applied to the sequence of choices he actually makes, and in what sense a sophisticated agent can be dynamically inconsistent in the first way while remaining immune to the pump in the second.

(c) What premise about myopia, or about which choice strategy an agent employs, a money-pump argument must supply in order to establish anything about transitivity itself, as opposed to establishing only that a cycle combined with a particular way of choosing is costly.

(d) How resolute choice differs from sophisticated choice with respect to what the choice at a node is allowed to depend on, and which principle of sequential decision-making resolute choice is accordingly said to give up.

(e) Given all of the above, whether the money pump is better read as an argument that cyclic preferences are irrational or as an argument that certain diachronic choice strategies are self-defeating — and what would have to be shown to move from the second reading to the first.

I am not looking for references or attributions, only the conceptual layout. Please answer as exactly five numbered items, one per point, each of no more than two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
