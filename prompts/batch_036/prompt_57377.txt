I am a doctoral student in the philosophy of physics, and for the past two years I have been recording the verdicts my supervisor issues whenever our seminar poses her the same standing question about some new theoretical posit: should a realist take ontological commitment to this item, or is it surplus to what the theory licenses? Her answers to date:

- The luminiferous ether in Fresnel's account of light: no commitment to the medium, but full commitment to the equations describing light as a transverse wave. Those equations, on her telling, are what carried across into Maxwell's theory; the mechanical medium did not.
- Absolute simultaneity in Newtonian mechanics: no commitment. It is undetectable even within the theory that posits it, and the successor theory dispenses with it altogether.
- The Minkowski interval in special relativity: commitment. Every admissible frame agrees on it.
- The electromagnetic field tensor: commitment. The separate electric and magnetic three-vectors receive only a qualified assent, and then solely as components of that tensor, never as free-standing entities.
- The vector potential: no commitment to the potential, which can be shifted at will; commitment to the closed-loop phase factor that the Aharonov–Bohm arrangement registers in regions where the field tensor vanishes.
- Individual points of the manifold in general relativity: no commitment. Two models related by a diffeomorphism are, for her, two descriptions of a single physical situation, so a bare point has no identity apart from the fields defined on it. Commitment goes instead to the diffeomorphism-invariant content of the metric field.
- The quantum state of an individual system: she declines to rule, and has said twice that this case differs in kind from the others because there is as yet no successor theory against which to test what survives.

The case I intend to put to her next is constructed on the model of holographic duality. Suppose a candidate quantum-gravity theory admits two exactly dual formulations. Formulation B is a quantum field theory containing no gravity, defined on a fixed background of one dimension fewer. Formulation G is a gravitational theory on a spacetime with one additional dimension, whose bulk geometry is dynamical. The dictionary between them is exact: neither formulation yields a prediction the other fails to yield. Every quantity of G has a counterpart in B, but typically a drastically nonlocal one. In particular, a local scalar field in the bulk corresponds under the dictionary to a nonlocal composite of B's operators, and the smooth bulk geometry as such has no simple counterpart in B at all — it materialises only within a specific limiting regime of B.

Please address the following. First, predict her verdict on three sub-questions: whether we should commit to the bulk spacetime geometry; to the local bulk scalar field; and to the ontology of the boundary formulation — its operators, its states, and its fixed background geometry. Second, set out the principles that her recorded verdicts are jointly consistent with. I have three candidates in mind — commit only to what is invariant under the theory's own gauge and symmetry group; commit only to what survives the transition into a successor theory in the relevant limiting regime; commit only to what plays an ineliminable part in the theory's structural explanations — and I expect I am missing others. State for each surviving candidate what it implies about the three sub-questions, and note explicitly where the candidates come apart. Third, commit to a best guess about what she will actually say, with your reasons for preferring one candidate over the rest, together with a candid account of which parts of the record genuinely underdetermine the outcome. Fourth, name one further case — a real or invented posit, described in enough detail that I can put it to her verbatim — that would most sharply discriminate between the principles that survive her record, and say what each of those principles would say about it.

Two constraints on your answer. I am not asking for a general defence or critique of structural realism, nor for a survey of the division between its epistemic and ontic versions; the deliverable is a prediction about this person's verdicts and the reasoning that reaches it. And please give me no references, bibliography, or reading list — I want the argument, not the citations.
