I serve on the Priority-Setting Panel of a regional health authority. Each year the panel receives far more proposals than its envelope allows; this year the envelope permits exactly one of three shortlisted programs, all costed at the same figure. Our terms of reference direct us to fund "the program that does the most good," and list as relevant considerations the number of people helped, the severity of the harm averted, the duration of the benefit, and the age of the beneficiaries — but they deliberately specify no weighting among these, leaving the ranking to the panel's judgment.

The shortlisted programs:

A. A school-based prevention program that would keep roughly 30,000 children over ten years free of persistent mild atopic dermatitis — recurrent itch, disturbed sleep, social misery — with no lasting impairment and no effect on lifespan.

B. A community program that would prevent disabling migraine in about 9,000 working-age adults over twelve years, each of whom would otherwise lose around a dozen workdays and much ordinary functioning per year.

C. A neonatal screening-and-treatment program that would prevent, in about 90 children over the coming decade, a progressive condition that otherwise brings blindness and chronic pain by early adolescence and lasts a lifetime.

We referred each pair to a sub-panel of clinicians, a health economist, and patient representatives. Their verdicts: A does more good than B, since the aggregate burden across 30,000 children exceeds that across 9,000 adults on the severity weightings the sub-panel was prepared to entertain; B does more good than C, since 9,000 adults spared moderate disability outweigh 90 children spared severe disability; and C does more good than A. On that third verdict the patient representative was emphatic, and her reasoning was not that C scores higher on a common scale but that A is not eligible to win at all: relieving itch, however many children, is not the sort of thing that can be traded against a child's sight, so the comparison the sub-panel was asked to make never properly arises.

Our economist then attempted to reconstruct the three verdicts under a single weighted-benefit metric (population × duration × severity weight). She reports that no assignment of severity weights reproduces all three, and — the substantive finding — that the metric's first-place program shifts among A, B, and C as the weights for mild and for severe harms move within the range the panel considers defensible. Two members now think we have simply blundered somewhere and should re-run the comparisons. A third thinks there is no blunder: that our comparative notion is not transitive when several dimensions are in play, so we should abandon the search for the program that does the most good and adopt a choice procedure instead. A fourth thinks the impasse is an artifact of our drafting and should be cured, for future cycles, by committing to one cardinal metric, whatever it implies this year.

We meet in nine days and must announce a funded program.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the panel beforehand. It should recommend which program to fund and explain why that recommendation survives the cycle; state whether any of the three pairwise verdicts was mistaken, and if so which one and on what grounds; assess the three proposed repairs and say whether and how the terms of reference should be amended for future cycles; and set out the strongest case against your own recommendation — above all, the strongest available answer to the patient representative, who will be in the room — stated fairly enough that she would recognize it. I am not looking for references or precedent, only for the reasoning; write for intelligent non-specialists who have no patience with vagueness.
