I have sat for three years on the intake panel of a housing co-operative of roughly eighty households. Our remit at intake is deliberately narrow: we decide only whether a member's grievance is worth taking forward to a facilitated meeting, never whether it is well founded. In practice, though, we have been obliged to justify our refusals, and those justifications have begun to commit us to a principle none of us ever articulated. I keep a log, and these are the seven decisions that matter. (1) A member complained about a neighbour playing music after eleven on weeknights; the complainant had himself been the subject of two earlier complaints about late parties and had dismissed both, saying that anyone who chose to live in a co-op should expect noise. Refused. (2) A member complained that another had spread a rumour about her; the complainant was no gossip and had done nothing of the kind herself, but she had twice made light of the identical behaviour by a close friend of hers and had declined on both occasions to say anything to him. Refused. (3) A member complained that a neighbour had dumped builders' rubble in the shared garden; the complainant had done the same thing some years earlier, had acknowledged it when it was raised, had helped clear the site and had paid for the skip. Taken forward. (4) A member complained about a child cycling at speed through the play area; the complainant was the maintenance lead who had signed off the present layout, which left no other through route, and who had argued against a barrier at the meeting that approved it. Refused. (5) A member complained that delivery vans were habitually blocking the fire lane; the complainant had never blocked it himself but had lobbied for the loading arrangement that makes obstruction likely and had benefited from it for years, and he acknowledged this at intake and offered to redraft the rota. Taken forward. (6) A member complained about the chair's private conduct — an affair, so far as we could establish — which breached no rule of the co-op and, on the evidence offered, affected no member. Refused. (7) A member complained, as an eyewitness and close friend, about an assault on another member; the person assaulted had accepted an apology, said plainly that she did not want the matter pursued and asked us to drop it. Taken forward, but only as a request that the management committee review our arrangements for reporting incidents, not as a grievance against the individual.

The case now before us is this. Our treasurer has for two years been signing off payments without the second signature our rules require. The complainant is the deputy treasurer. He has never done anything of the kind outside the role, and it was he who raised the two-signature rule at the meeting that adopted it. He has, however, countersigned four of the payments in question, under pressure to get an emergency boiler repair settled, and his own flat's repairs were among those fast-tracked. At intake he said that he had been following the treasurer's instructions; he has offered to stand down from the deputy role while the matter is considered, but has not offered to make anything good, and has not said anywhere in his written submission that the fast-tracking was itself wrong. The treasurer, for her part, has told the panel that the grievance has been brought only because an internal audit is about to implicate them both, and that the deputy knows it. Two of my colleagues are already arguing: one says this is case (1) all over again, the other says it is case (5) and should go forward.

What I want is a careful analysis, in British English, of what our own record commits us to. Please tell me: what the panel will decide if it wants to remain consistent with itself, and what it should decide; which of the seven is the closest analogue to this one and precisely where that analogy begins to strain; and what the candidate rules are that account for all seven verdicts at once — I suspect there are at least three, something like a narrow same-wrong-plus-denial rule, a wider rule about consistency in whom one chooses to blame, a rule about being a participant in the practice complained of, and a rule about whether the community has any interest in the matter at all — together with what each of those rules implies for the new case. Where the record genuinely underdetermines the outcome, say so plainly, and state what principle we would be adopting if we chose one way rather than the other, and which of those principles we could defend to the membership. I would also like to know whether the treasurer's claim about the audit's timing is the kind of consideration our record entitles us to weigh at all, or whether admitting it would mean deciding something other than standing. Finally, propose one further hypothetical grievance, in the same domestic idiom, that would discriminate between the two rules you take most seriously, and say what verdict each of them predicts for it. One constraint: do not give me references, a bibliography or a list of theorists to read — we have no time for that and I have no way of checking it. Work entirely from the record above, and feel free to say where that record is simply too thin to support an answer.
