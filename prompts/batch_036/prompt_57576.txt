I build retrieval and recommendation systems, not cognitive models, but I've been chewing on Hofstadter and Sander's thesis that analogy isn't one special-purpose ability among many but the core of all cognition: on their view, to possess a concept is to have a category whose boundaries stay fluid and shift with context, and every act of categorization is an implicit analogy to prior cases. Write a dialogue between a defender of that thesis and a defender of the rival picture, on which concepts have structure — features, relations, or a generative model — and generalizing to a new case is inference over that structure, so that "analogy" names a surface pattern rather than a mechanism.

To keep them arguing about the same thing, let them fight over a case I'll supply: a system I built that classifies a new input by retrieving its nearest neighbours in a learned embedding space and returning the majority label. No rule is stated anywhere, its boundaries drift smoothly when context is folded into the embedding, and it generalizes to novel cases better than the hand-built feature system it replaced. The structuralist says this is ordinary similarity-based inference and demonstrates nothing distinctively analogical; the analogy theorist says it is precisely their thesis mechanized, with no crisp categories anywhere in the pipeline.

Have each press where the other is genuinely exposed — what the retrieval system gets wrong that a structured model handles, and whether the analogy thesis can be stated so that some finding would refute it rather than be absorbed as one more analogy. Neither position should be a strawman or win by definitional fiat, and I'd like them to close by agreeing on an experiment that would actually separate the two, or failing to and saying why.
