So I'm putting together a talk for a little philosophy-and-fiction reading group and I've gotten myself into a hole. The setup: Tolstoy's War and Peace read against Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation — the one on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, with the monumental / antiquarian / critical triad and the whole business about the historical malady and what he calls plastic power.

The thesis I want to defend is that Platon Karataev is Nietzsche's unhistorical man in human form — no memory, no resentment, proverbs instead of a past, happy the way the beast is happy in the opening of the essay — and that Tolstoy endorses him, which makes the novel an argument against monumental history: Andrei worshipping Napoleon, getting shot at Austerlitz, looking up at the sky and finding the whole heroic-model project suddenly very small.

Here's the problem. If I remember the epilogue right, Pierre can't actually keep any of it. He thinks and talks about that month of captivity for the rest of his life, gets teary about Karataev's sayings in front of Marya and Natasha — and then goes back to being a Petersburg committee man with a portfolio of grievances. So does that kill my reading, or is it exactly Nietzsche's point, i.e. the unhistorical isn't a position you can adopt, it's a capacity you either have or dont, and Pierre is a monument-collector who briefly borrowed one? Related: isn't Natasha's dance at her uncle's house a better candidate for the unhistorical moment than Karataev at all, which would be awkward for me because it puts the thing in an aristocrat's body rather than the peasant's.

What I want from you: which scenes and episodes the three modes actually map onto (not just namedropped — where each one is doing work, including that oak tree Andrei keeps passing), where the lens distorts the novel and an honest critic has to say so (Tolstoy's Christianity and Karataev's fatalism look like things Nietzsche would despise, not celebrate), and what to make of the second epilogue, where the novelist turns into a philosophy-of-history lecturer for forty pages — is that critical history in Nietzsche's sense, or the historical malady swallowing the book whole? And finally: does the novel add anything to the essay that the essay couldn't say on its own, or am I just decorating Tolstoy with German vocabulary? I've been argueing about this with a friend for two weeks and would like something to bring to the group.
