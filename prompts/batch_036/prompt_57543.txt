Please take on the role of a professional philosopher whose research sits at the border of welfare economics and ethics, and who has helped broker adversarial collaborations between researchers who genuinely disagreed. Think the problem through carefully before drafting. In particular, begin with a short, unnumbered brainstorming section in which you sort the disputes you can identify into three bins: genuine substantive disagreements, disagreements that turn on a single term being used in two senses, and disagreements that are really about decision procedures rather than about facts. Only after that should you produce the document itself.

Here is the situation. I am a high school senior and the president of my school's Political Economy Society. For most of the year our membership has been split into two factions that argue past each other, and I have been asked by our faculty adviser to get them to produce something jointly signed before our spring meeting.

The first faction — three students who came to the club through Austrian economics and praxeology — holds that utility is strictly ordinal, that only an individual's own ranking of alternatives has content, and that a sentence comparing one person's gain to another person's gain is not merely difficult to verify but has no truth conditions to verify. They think the ban on interpersonal comparison associated with Lionel Robbins was right, is still right, and that anyone who ignores it is doing politics while calling it measurement. They are broadly suspicious of survey numbers and resist the idea that a self-report scale could be a measuring rod.

The second faction — four students in the effective-altruism orbit — allocated last year's club donation using estimated quality-adjusted life years per dollar. They hold that interpersonal comparisons of well-being are not only meaningful but unavoidable, and that the first faction's scruple functions in practice as a refusal to decide. Their standing argument is that ordinary technical practice already commits people to comparability: pooling different respondents' answers on a zero-to-ten life-satisfaction scale, converting everything into willingness-to-pay, and scaling the value of a statistical life with income all seem to require some assumption about how one person's gains line up with another's.

The practical stake is small but real. We have roughly six hundred dollars raised, three candidate recipients, and no agreed procedure for choosing among them.

I want you to test rather than accept my own diagnosis of where the argument goes wrong. I suspect the two factions slide among three claims that should be kept apart: first, that a comparison of two people's levels of well-being could not be true or false at all; second, that such a comparison could be true or false but lies beyond any evidence we could gather; third, that such comparisons are in principle evidentially accessible but are not licensed by the particular instruments we currently use. I also suspect they slide among comparisons of levels, of differences, and of ratios, which have different requirements — a comparison of differences may need only a shared unit, whereas pooling two people's satisfaction scores may need a shared zero and a shared way of using the scale.

Please draft the joint statement these two factions should have produced a year ago. I would like the following structure:

1. An agreed-claims section, numbered. These should be claims both factions could sign — about the formal structure of utility and what revealed-preference data can and cannot show, about which existing practices embed some comparative assumption, about what our decision procedure has to accomplish, and about what would count as a bad argument on either side. Do not let these be trivialities: each one should be something a member of one faction would previously have assumed the other faction denied.

2. A disagreements table with these columns: the disagreement; the first faction's statement of its own position, worded so its members would accept it as accurate; the second faction's statement of its own position, worded so its members would accept it as accurate; whether the disagreement survives once terminology is held fixed; and what specifically would have to be settled for it to dissolve.

3. Three to five cruxes, meaning sub-questions whose resolution would actually move at least one side. For each crux, give a concrete case or piece of evidence that both factions would agree bears on it — for instance, whether presenting different respondents with identical written descriptions of the same hypothetical people and then rescaling their answers can repair cross-person differences in how they use a satisfaction scale; or whether the marginal-utility assumptions built into income-scaled values of a statistical life amount to an interpersonal comparison or merely to a reporting convention — and record each side's prediction about that case in advance, together with what result each side now commits to treating as a reason to revise.

4. A section isolating whatever part of the dispute is merely verbal. Name the sentence or sentences that are being used in two senses, say what the two senses are, and rewrite the sentence so the ambiguity disappears and each faction can then say plainly whether it accepts or rejects the result.

5. A short list of claims both factions should stop making.

6. A closing paragraph giving a decision procedure for our six hundred dollars that both factions could live with even if the theoretical dispute is never resolved.

Two constraints. First, do not give me citations, a bibliography, a reading list, or named papers; I have no way to look anything up and I would much rather have the reasoning than a set of possibly invented references. Argue from the structure of the problem. Second, do not adjudicate. I am not asking who is right, and a verdict would leave the document unsigned by one side. Where you think one faction's position is weaker than its members admit, say so in a way that is neutral and that does not quietly settle the question.

Aim for roughly thirteen hundred to eighteen hundred words, and write it so that two seventeen-year-olds could read it aloud to each other without either feeling caricatured.
