I serve on a state sentencing commission redrafting its guidelines, and Friday's agenda carries a proposal to strike the culpability clause. Under current law every sentence must be scaled to the offender's degree of culpability, with mandatory floors for violent felonies. The proposal would substitute a public-safety standard: detention permitted only as the least restrictive means of preventing serious harm, its length set by a risk instrument and reviewed annually. In validation data, the instrument the draft would adopt flags as high-risk a group of whom roughly three in ten are charged with a violent offense within five years, against roughly one in twenty-five of those it does not flag.

Supporters argue that culpability is a metaphysical fiction doing covert retributive work, and that a system owning up to the fact that it incapacitates will constrain itself better, not worse. Opponents argue that the proposal severs sentence length from the offense committed: a murderer scored low-risk could be released while a burglar scored high-risk serves a decade, and in the ordinary case seven in ten of those detained would never have reoffended — precisely the excess that proportionality exists to prevent.

Draft the memo I will circulate to the other commissioners. Open with a recommendation for Friday — adopt, reject, or adopt with amendments; if amendments, give their operative language and name what the commission should study before the next cycle. Then give the reasons, engaging both the compatibilist case that desert tracks real capacities we have no reason to abandon and the skeptic's case that a quarantine model is more honest and better disciplined than the practice it replaces. Say which of the two survives contact with the numbers above, and let the losing side state its strongest objection to your recommendation.
