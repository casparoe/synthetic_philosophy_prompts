I am preparing a paper on Hume's essay Of the Standard of Taste and want a case-driven analysis rather than an exegesis. The case is the Han van Meegeren affair. Here are the salient facts, so that nothing has to be looked up: van Meegeren, an unsuccessful Dutch painter with a grievance against the critics, spent the 1930s producing canvases designed to pass as seventeenth-century Dutch work. In 1937 a religious picture of his was attributed to Vermeer by Abraham Bredius, former director of the Mauritshuis and the most respected authority on Dutch painting of his generation, who published an enthusiastic attribution that year; the canvas was then acquired by the Boijmans museum in Rotterdam at a price commensurate with a major Vermeer. Van Meegeren kept selling forgeries through the occupation, one of them to Hermann Göring. Arrested after the war for having sold Dutch cultural property to the enemy, and facing a possible death sentence, he defended himself by asserting that the picture Göring had bought was no Vermeer at all but his own work. He was convicted of fraud, sentenced to a year, and died weeks later. Once he had demonstrated his authorship, the critical consensus reversed with remarkable speed, and paintings praised for a decade as masterpieces were reclassified as competent pastiche. The canvases themselves did not change.

Map the 1937 verdict onto Hume's machinery with some precision: which of the qualifications he demands of the true judge — delicacy of sentiment, practice, comparison, good sense, freedom from prejudice — Bredius and his colleagues met, and which they failed; and whether the 1937 attribution counts, on Hume's own criteria, as the joint verdict of qualified judges and therefore as the standard of taste for that canvas. Then say what the episode does to the account. Three points in particular:

- Hume illustrates delicacy with the two wine tasters in Don Quixote who report iron and leather in the cask and are vindicated when a key on a leather thong is found at the bottom. That supplies an independent check on the refined palate. Is there any analogue of the key here — the confession, the pigment and craquelure analysis, the later connoisseurship — or does the case leave Hume without independent purchase, so that the true judge is identified only by the verdicts we already accept, and the verdicts only by the judges? How damaging is that circularity, and what resources does the essay have against it?

- The 1937 panel and the panel that undid its work were largely the same population: the same training, the same eyes, the same pictures on the wall. What changed was their information about the work's history, not their perceptual access to it. Does Hume's framework have a place for the thought that provenance and the achievement behind an object bear on its beauty, or can it register the reversal only by demoting the earlier judges to unqualified?

- Hume treats durability across ages and countries as evidence of genuine merit. Here durability was bought with a false attribution and then revoked. Does that vindicate the test — the picture did not survive, and so the test flagged it correctly — or does it show that the test tracks the survival of an attribution rather than any property of the object?

Finally, be candid about what the episode cannot settle. The postwar demotion was itself shaped by revulsion at the forger, by the embarrassment of the experts who had certified him, by the collapse of market values, and by the awkward fact that van Meegeren became a kind of national hero for having swindled Göring — each of which looks like precisely the sort of prejudice Hume wants the true judge cleared of. If both verdicts are contaminated, does the case simply fail to discriminate between realism and response-dependence about beauty? Where the theory is silent, say so rather than making it speak.
