So — I'm TA-ing an undergrad seminar this term on grand narratives of human history, the kind where you read the fat airport bestsellers about how foragers became farmers became accountants, and then you read the archaeologists and anthropologists who think all of that is a load of confident nonsense. Week five was the critics week and my tutorial group has basically split into two camps, one of which keeps saying "well but the axis of the continents" as though that settles absolutely everything. For the last session I want to hand out something funnier than another reading response and that makes the argument better than I can from the front of a room.

Here's what I'd like. You're a senior developmental editor at a big London trade house, twenty-odd years in the job, four number-one "history of everything" titles on your list and very strong views about pace. Write an internal editorial memo to the author of a forthcoming blockbuster — call him Professor Emrys Kettleborough, a man who holds a chair in something interdisciplinary and has never excavated anything — whose manuscript is provisionally titled *The Ladder: How We Got Here and Why We Couldn't Have Got Here Any Other Way*. Numbered notes on individual chapters, plus something on the subtitle, the cover map and the back-cover blurb. Somewhere around 750–850 words, and British English throughout, obviously. The notes should read like a real editor writes them: warm, brisk, faintly menacing about the market. And every single one of them should push the manuscript further up the ladder. Ask him to pin the whole thing on one crisp moment in the deep past when our species' brains switched on, and to give it a date. Insist that farming, cities, kings, writing, patriarchy, slavery and taxation are a package you buy together or not at all, and cut the passage where he has some foragers planting in the spring and walking away from the fields in the autumn, because it loses the shape. Ask him to cut the city that had no palace in it, or failing that to give it a pyramid, since readers need to know who was in charge. Praise the chapter where geography does all the work and suggest he rotate the map so the arrows point somewhere more purposeful. Tell him the collapse chapter is the best thing in the book, make the islanders' fate into a parable, and gently advise against the new footnote where he concedes that the islanders didn't all die, on the grounds that footnotes are where blockbusters go to lose their nerve. Tell him the peer reader flagged that chapter nine lets people make choices and that we'll need to fix that. Give him a target for how often the reader is folded into a "we", and ask for a subtitle with three nouns in it.

The test I want it to pass: by the end, someone should be able to hear exactly what's wrong with the genre — the teleology, the single subject doing everything, the flattening of every actual society into a rung — without the memo once admitting that anyone has ever objected to any of it. The editor is sincere. She likes the book. Don't let her wink. Before you draft, spend a short paragraph or two brainstorming out loud which habits of this tradition you're going to exaggerate and which you'd better leave alone because they're load-bearing, then give me the memo proper in a form I could photocopy. Two constraints: no real authors, no real book titles, and no quotes from actual texts — everything invented — and definately no bibliography or further reading tacked on the end. I want the jokes to come from getting the arguements right, not from name-dropping. If a specialist reads it they should feel slightly seen; if one of my second-years reads it they should just laugh.
