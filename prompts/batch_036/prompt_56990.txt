I've been going back and forth on those Libet wrist-flick experiments for a while now, and I finally sat down with a transcript of a podcast where the host summed them up like this:

"Libet put electrodes on people's scalps and had them flick their wrist whenever they felt like it, while watching a dot sweep around a clock face. What he found is that the brain starts getting ready to move about half a second before the person reports deciding to move — there's a slow ramp of activity called the readiness potential, and that ramp is the decision itself, happening unconsciously. So the feeling of choosing is a report your brain files after the fact. Libet tried to rescue free will by saying you can still veto the movement in the last hundred milliseconds or so, but that doesn't work either, because a veto has to be a brain event too, and it would have its own unconscious build-up."

Most of that sounds right to me and honestly I lean toward the conclusion anyway — I've never felt like my concious thoughts were running the show. But a few of the steps feel too fast. Can you go through it and sort the claims into what someone who works on this would sign off on, what's a defensible but contested reading, and what just isn't supported by the experiment? Two I especially want to know about: whether "the readiness potential is the decision" is being made to carry more weight than it can bear, and whether the host's knockdown of the veto idea is actually an argument or just an assertion. And please be explicit about what's settled here versus what people who know the litrature genuinely disagree about — I don't want to come away thinking there's a consensus when there isn't.
