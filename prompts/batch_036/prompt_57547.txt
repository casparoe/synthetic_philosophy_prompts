I write novels, and one of my vices is transcribing arguments I overhear and putting them in books with the serial numbers filed off. At a dinner last month two guests had clearly been having the same fight for years, and I reconstructed it on the train home, so some of the wording is mine and the order may be slightly off, but I'm confident about the moves. One is a philosophy teacher at a Jesuit high school (call him Ray), the other a Reformed pastor with a philosophy doctorate (call her Ines). Subject was Molinism — specifically middle knowledge.

1. Ray opens by saying Molinism is the only position that gets both things right at once. God's providence stays detailed enough to deserve the name, and God isn't the author of anybody's sin, because God's knowledge of what a free creature would do comes before any decision to create that creature rather than after it. It's knowledge of what's already the case, not knowledge caused by a decree.

2. Ines says that's exactly where it comes apart. Before God decides anything, what makes it true that this particular woman would freely refuse a bribe if she were offered one? Not God's will — that hasn't happened yet. Not the woman — she doesn't exist. Not the circumstances, because on real libertarian freedom the circumstances plus the whole past plus the laws all leave it genuinely open which way she goes. So there's nothing for the proposition to be made true by. She called this the grounding objection, or whatever the standard name is, and added that a truth with no truthmaker isn't the kind of thing you can know, even omnisciently.

3. Ray: not every truth needs a truthmaker. Some propositions are just true and that's the end of the story, and an omniscient being knows all truths, including those. Anyway the counterfactual isn't a coin flip — it tracks what sort of person she is. Her being the sort who'd refuse is a real fact about her, and that fact doesn't shove her around, it just describes her.

4. Ines: now you're on the horns of a dilemma. If "the sort of person she is" actually settles the answer, you've let determinism in the back door — her character is prior to and productive of her free choice, which is the very thing you said you were avoiding. If it doesn't settle the answer, you're back to a brute truth with nothing behind it. So middle knowledge either isn't middle, because it's really God reading off essences he didn't choose, or it isn't knowledge.

5. Ray: Molinism doesn't need all the counterfactuals to be true, it needs enough of them. Give God a decent sparse set — this man would repent if he heard this sermon, that one wouldn't — and he can steer history where he wants without forcing anybody, which is all providence ever claimed. And look at what the alternative costs: if there are no true counterfactuals of freedom, God creates blind and doesn't know how the world will turn out, which is open theism, and thats a far bigger concession than tolerating a few brute conditionals.

6. Ines: but that gives away the product you came here to sell. If the true set is sparse, then for most situations that actually matter there's no fact about what the creature would have done, so God can't guarantee his plan gets carried out and can't explain why this world contains the evil it does rather than less. And the survivors of the sparse set are the hardest ones to believe in, since a conditional that's true for no reason is exactly the kind you should distrust. You've kept the whole metaphysical bill and lost the payoff.

Somewhere in there Ray also reached for the idea that every creaturely essence is ruined in every world where it gets actualized — transworld depravity, I think he called it — as a way of explaining why God can't simply pick a world in which everyone freely does the right thing. Ines said that was a convience hypothesis: it makes God's creative choice look like a lottery he rigged after the fact and can't lose.

Nobody conceded anything and I've been sitting on it for a month. Can you go through it move by move and tell me who's actually right at each step? Rate them roughly — clearly right, mostly right, defensible but oversold, wrong — and say why, because I want to write this as a scene and I need to know whether somebody genuinely wins or whether they're trading punches past each other. Then tell me who comes out ahead overall, what the fight really turns on, and what both of them left out that a referee who knew the terrain would have thrown into the ring. Don't send me off to read anything; I want your judgment, not a bibliography.
