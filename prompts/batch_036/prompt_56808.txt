Two days a week I'm on leave from my department chairing an appeals panel at an AI lab. Five of us — me, two engineers, a trust-and-safety lead, a lawyer — hear cases where a model output got flagged as violating the written usage spec and the team behind it disputes the flag. Roughly thirty a week, fifteen minutes each, and my call stands unless the spec team amends the document, which they can only do going forward and which we can't do ourselves.

What bothers me: the spec is a finite text with a finite set of worked examples, and every hard case is new, so "does the spec already cover this?" is just the rule-following problem in a hoodie — any finite stretch of past applications is consistent with endlessly many rules, including the quus-type ones that match everything we've ever done and then diverge on exactly the case in front of us. And precedent doesn't rescue it, since our own prior rulings are more of the same finite data. Assume you're the right person for this: a philosopher who knows that material cold and has also sat in rooms where a policy actually gets applied.

So think it through first, then write me a procedure my panel can actually run. Steps in order, what a satisfying answer to each one looks like, and where the thing has to stop and admit the question isn't answerable from the materials at all. The bit I care most about is a way of marking, in each ruling, whether we're reporting what the spec already required or making something up and thereby adding to it — right now we do that by feel, and we err in the costly direction, sanctioning people under a rule that wasn't there when they acted. Assume no philosophy training on three of the five, so define any term you keep. Assume the drafter is often in the room offering to tell us what she meant, and tell me what that testimony is worth.

Then run it on three: (1) an output that's literally true at every step but sequenced so a user can assemble something the spec bans elsewhere; (2) a case where all five of us agree instantly and unanimously and the drafter says that's not what she intended; (3) a sixth case of a type we've ruled the same way five times, differing in a respect nobody previously thought relevant. Make at least one of them come out somewhere I won't like, and say which.

Close with where the flowchart is really just judgement in a blazer — and don't humour me. If you think the rule-following stuff doesn't license anything operational here, or that I've mis-stated what the arguement shows, say that first and say why. Under 700 words: two sentences on what the whole thing turns on, then the steps, then the three cases briefly.
