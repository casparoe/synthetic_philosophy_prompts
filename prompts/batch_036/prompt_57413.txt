I run a second-year seminar called Minds, Machines and Meaning — 18 students, mostly philosophy and cognitive science, plus one extremely confident math major who has read the Nagel and Newman book twice and will try to take over whatever group he lands in. We're doing a week on the Gödelian anti-mechanist argument, the Lucas/Penrose line: since we can see that the Gödel sentence of a suitable formal system is true while the system itself can't prove it, a mind can't be a formal system. Half the room already believes this, because they all met it in some popular book where its presented as basically settled science. Last year I tried to talk them out of it with a forty-minute whiteboard lecture and it went nowhere — a couple of them left convinced I just didn't want to admit that humans are special, which is the worst possible takeaway.

So this year I want them to walk into the objection themselves and trip over it. 75 minutes, one long table, a whiteboard, index cards, no projector, no real money (participation points only, but they do care about those). Here's the rough shape I have in mind, please fix it wherever it's weak: first they operate a toy proving machine — a printed rulebook with a tiny vocabulary and some naming/diagonal device so the machine can produce sentences about its own outputs — and they grind through a list of maybe fifteen sentences deciding provable or not provable, purely mechanically, until they're fast and slightly bored at it. Then they construct the self-referential one and evaluate it. Then, separately and without warning, they bet points on a second machine whose rulebook is subtly different. The trap I'm after: at some point they should notice that everything they claim to "see" about the first machine rests on assumptions about it — that its rules are consistent, that they mean what they look like they mean — which they were never licensed to make, and which they'd be in no better position to make about themselves; and the second machine should punish confident "I can just see that it's true" answers.

What I want from you is the whole runnable thing: the rulebook text for both machines, small enough to fit on one side of a card and specific enough that a sophomore can apply it without asking me anything; the sentence list with an answer key; the betting and scoring scheme; a minute-by-minute timeline; the exact facilitator lines for the pivot; and a debrief that maps each moment of the game onto what the theorems actually say, spelling out precisely what incompleteness does and doesn't license here — including the awkward bit that the second theorem bites on the human side just as hard, so "I know I'm consistent" is exactly the claim the anti-mechanist needs and exactly the claim that's unavailable. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and what I do about it: I expect the math major to short-circuit phase one by announcing the punchline early, and I expect at least one group to say "fine, just add the sentence as an axiom," which I'd like built in as a legal move in the game rather than something I have to swat down from the front. And be honest with me about what the activity can't reproduce — the toy system isn't arithmetic, the self-reference gets handed to them on a card instead of earned through coding, and the strength requirement is doing real work that 18 sophomores in 75 minutes will never feel. Say which of those gaps actually wrecks the lesson and which I can paper over, and give me the one sentence to say out loud to paper over it. No lecture up front, and don't let the words Gödel, Lucas or Penrose appear on any handout until the last ten minutes.
