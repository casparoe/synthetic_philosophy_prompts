Half my internet life has been spent on rationalist blogs and forums, so I've got a decent amateur grip on the standard furniture — steelmanning, the map/territory thing, how to spot a bad argument. My day job is the other half of my brain: fifteen years of backend work, mostly compilers and static analysis lately, so I spend my time thinking about what has to be true for a program to even be checkable. Scope, binding, well-formedness, what counts as a static error versus something that only blows up at runtime. Last month I got talked into a reading group on transcendental arguments — Kant's, plus the twentieth century attempts to keep the argumentative structure while throwing out the idealism — and I keep having the same reaction, which is that the whole shape of the thing looks to me like a claim about notation. "You couldn't deny X without already using X" reads exactly like "you can't write a well-formed term mentioning `foo` unless `foo` is bound in scope," or like the way a definite description keeps demanding its referent even when you negate the sentence. And I'm aware that my crowd has a reflexive dunk on this move when it turns up in apologetics (you can't argue against logic without using logic, etc), so I'm suspicious that I'm just pattern matching rather than actually seeing the structure.

So here's what I want. Take the position in its strongest form — the version thats supposed to answer a skeptic, not the watered-down version that just describes what our concepts already contain — and restate it in my vocabulary, step by step rather than as a summary. Be precise with the specific tools: well-formedness and scope; static versus dynamic failure; what it means for one system to be a conservative or merely definitional extension of another; the difference between a claim being expressible and a claim being true; presupposition as the thing that projects through negation and questioning. And keep the translation honest. If the only way to render a premise in type-checking talk is to change what the premise asserts, mark it as a failure rather than quietly rewriting it into something my framework would find unobjectionable. If you catch yourself thinking "well, in his terms this just says the argument is about his own language," treat that as a red flag to report, not as a finding to bank.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Three buckets: claims that come across clean; claims that come across but distorted, with an account of what the distortion costs; and claims that have no expression at all in my vocabulary. For that third bucket I specifically want you to go after whether the necessity in a transcendental argument is genuinely modal in a way that "this term wouldn't be well-formed" isn't; whether the conclusion is supposed to be about the world rather than about the scheme; the uniqueness step, since one guy in the group insists the argument can show that some categorial apparatus is required but never that this one is the only possible one; and whether such an argument can be an argument at all rather than a restatement of the grammar it's written in. Finish by telling me which side the residue favors — is my vocabulary missing something real, or does the position turn out to be less substantive than it sounded once the unsayable parts get subtracted? Pick a verdict and defend it, don't retreat into both-sides mush. And end with what would change your mind: which single argument or consideration would flip you to the other verdict, plus where you think your reasoning above is most likely to have gone wrong. My guess is the modality or the uniqueness bit, but tell me if you think my guess is off.
