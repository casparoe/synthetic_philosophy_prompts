I teach philosophy of biology, and I want to pin down what my own function attributions actually commit me to — not a survey of the two received accounts, which I know well, but the principle, if there is one, that generates my verdicts. I should say at the outset what I am not after: no references, no history of the debate, no catalogue of positions. I want you to treat the cases below as data and do the work of extraction, testing, and repair on them. Where you need a name for something, coin one and define it.

Here are my considered verdicts. Some are familiar; several I built for a seminar and I am not confident about any of them in advance.

1. A healthy human heart. Its function is to pump blood. Unproblematic, and I want the principle to be uncontroversial here.

2. A donor heart in a cooler, or the heart of an anencephalic newborn that never contracted once. I still say its function is to pump blood. It does nothing, will never do anything, and yet the attribution does not seem to lapse for me.

3. A heart with a large septal defect that pumps badly. It has the function of pumping blood and it is malfunctioning. Take this as a constraint rather than a datum: whatever principle survives must license the doing-versus-failing distinction, since that is most of what I want function talk for.

4. The human appendix. On one hypothesis it acts as a refuge for commensal bacteria and helps repopulate the colon after a bout of diarrhoeal illness. Grant the hypothesis for argument's sake, and grant that the tissue performs the role well. I remain unwilling to say that harbouring flora is the appendix's function; I want to call it a vestige that happens to be useful. But notice that in case 2 I was perfectly happy to attribute a function to tissue doing nothing whatever. I do not know how to hold both of those.

5. A bacterial population in which a plasmid-borne enzyme that degrades a novel antibiotic appeared yesterday and is now sweeping to fixation. I say the enzyme already has that function. Nothing about its ancestry seems to me to matter; what matters is that it is doing the work and that the work explains why it is spreading.

6. The crystallins of the vertebrate lens, several of which are homologous to enzymes or to small heat-shock proteins that do quite different work elsewhere in the body. I say that in the lens these proteins have a refractive function, and that where the same protein still does its old enzymatic job it keeps that function as well. One molecule, two functions, neither of them derivative of the other.

7. A stretch of the genome that is transcribed, whose transcript is bound by proteins and measurably alters local chromatin, and whose transcription is, so far as anyone can tell, noise. I deny it any function, even though its activity is a genuine causal difference within the system. This is the shape of the standing dispute over how much of a genome is functional, and I take my side in it seriously.

8. A moth lineage bred in the laboratory for fifty generations purely for the geometry of its hindwing eyespots, never exposed to a predator. The eyespots still startle birds when birds are presented with them. I say the startling is no longer what they are for.

9. A valve printed last week from a patient's own cells and seated in that patient's aorta, opening and closing competently. I say it has the function of regulating flow. If you tell me this is only because someone intended it, I want to reply that the intention looks beside the point: the valve would still have that function had it arisen through an accident of manufacture rather than a design brief.

Two pairs here are, I think, genuinely irreconcilable on the resources I have, and I want you to confront them head-on rather than explain them away. Cases 6 and 7 look structurally identical — a molecule making a current causal contribution it was not selected for — and I judge them oppositely. Cases 8 and 9 likewise: neither trait has a selectional ancestry for the effect in question, both produce the effect reliably now, and I judge them oppositely.

My own tentative principle, which I will call retentionism, a coinage of mine and not a view in the literature. A trait has F as a function if and only if (i) the trait has the capacity to F, and (ii) the trait's continued presence in the population of which it is part is explained, at least in part, by that capacity, where the explaining process need not be natural selection but may be any population-level filter that retains some variants and discards others — ongoing selection, artificial selection, cultural transmission, clonal expansion, market and clinical filtering. Clause (ii) is what does the work, and the deliberate generality about the filter is what I suspect is either its virtue or its fatal vagueness.

What I want from you:

(a) State the principle, or the small number of competing candidates, precisely, clause by clause, and for each clause tell me which of my verdicts it exists to save.

(b) For each candidate, say plainly which of my nine verdicts it gets wrong. I would rather have a two-clause principle that overturns case 4 or case 7 than an eight-clause principle that saves everything; tell me which verdict you think I must abandon, and argue for that choice.

(c) Decide whether retentionism is a genuine third account, an etiological account with the time window widened, or a causal-role account with a filter bolted on. If it is the second or third, say where the disguise fails.

(d) Deliver verdicts, on the principle you end up recommending, for three cases I have not listed: lactase persistence in a population with a long dairying history and in one without it; a gene-drive construct in mosquitoes whose spread is explained by the drive machinery rather than by any advantage to the bearer; and the dewclaw of a show-line dog, retained for fifty years by breed standard and now doing nothing at all.

(e) Finally, tell me whether the principle you land on licenses function explanations of the sort that actually appear in biology — explanations of why a trait is there by appeal to what it does — or whether it licenses only a taxonomy, a way of sorting traits into functional and non-functional piles with no explanatory force behind the sorting. That is the question I care about most, and it is the one I have the least confidence I can answer myself.
