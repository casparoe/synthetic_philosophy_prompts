I am a novelist, and in six days I give a paper at a law school jurisprudence workshop — forty minutes to speak, thirty allotted for questions, though it never divides that way — in one of those rooms that invites an outsider twice a year and is unfailingly courteous about it. The talk grows out of a book I have been working on for five years, set in an invented harbour republic whose founding charter ends, as real ones do, with the clause governing its own amendment. My Article 46 permits amendment by two-thirds of the Assembly with ratification by the guilds, and it states in terms that it governs every article of the charter "including this one." In the middle of the novel a frightened Assembly entrenches a list of provisions against alteration by any body whatsoever, and the entrenching article entrenchs itself but — by an omission I chose deliberately, and which I intend to own in the talk — says nothing at all about Article 46. So the Assembly repeals Article 46 lawfully and on its own terms, leaving a charter that contains an unamendable clause and no power to amend anything. A generation later the city urgently needs to change one of the entrenched articles, and, having no amendment power, passes an instrument it calls a Declaration, and five judges are asked whether it is law.

What I mean to argue is that there is nothing in that charter for the five judges to decide with: not a gap awaiting construction, not a conflict awaiting an order of priority, but a silence no rule speaks into, so that the question of the Declaration's validity has no legal answer until somebody obeys it and becomes settled the moment they do. I want to press the point further and say that this is true of real entrenchment too, and that the difference between my republic and theirs is only that ours has been tested. I also want to claim that narrative is the right instrument for this, because a novel can represent the interval — in my book, eleven months between the Declaration and the first case brought under it — during which the charter is silent and the city goes on being governed anyway, an interval that jurisprudential prose cannot hold, since it has no duration. The room will be about twenty people. Three or four self-described Hartians convene the series and will treat my position either as a restatement of theirs or as a failure to grasp theirs. There is a comparativist who works on unamendable provisions and eternity clauses and who, I am told, has actual data on courts striking down constitutional amendments. There is a formally minded junior scholar who will say the puzzle evaporates once you index rules to levels, and that a mid-century Scandinavian paper said so long before I began drafting. There are two practitioners and a judicial clerk who want to know what a judge writes on Monday morning. There is a legal historian who will observe that polities have done precisely this and survived, which sounds like support and is not. And there are two or three graduate students who will be kind to me and ask about my characters, which is the outcome I most want to avoid, because it lets everyone else off the hook.

Give me my question period before it happens. I want the eight or nine objections I will actually draw from this room — not the ones a philosophy-of-logic audience would raise, and not the ones reviewers of the manuscript have already given me — ordered by how much damage each would do if it landed badly, and phrased the way the person raising it would phrase it, with their vocabulary and their impatience intact. For each, tell me what I can say back while standing up, in about two minutes, and be candid about where that reply is soft, or buys its plausibility by giving away more than I notice at the time. Mark the ones I should absorb in the first five minutes of the talk so they cannot be sprung on me afterwards, and tell me which single reply has to land for the afternoon to have been worth the trip. Then tell me the question I am hoping nobody asks: I suspect it is the one about my own ending, since in the last chapter the Declaration is obeyed, and I therefore appear to settle in the plot exactly what the paper claims cannot be settled — but there may be something worse, and I would rather see it here than in the room. Finish with two sentences I can say, without flinching, when I am told that I have misunderstood a distinction nobody ever taught me. Please write all of this to me as a letter: continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold, no tables, and if you must rank the objections, do it inside your sentences. Send me no reading list either; I have a week, and I need things I can say aloud, not sources to chase. Naming a distinction properly is welcome — building a reply on a citation I would have to look up before I could use it is not.
