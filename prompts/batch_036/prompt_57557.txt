Some context. I was allowed to sit in on a graduate reading group last week. Two students — I will call them Nora and Tomás — got into an argument about whether the base/superstructure distinction is even coherent, and they asked the convener to referee. He declined, and I am now asking you. Neither of them is a strawman; both know the literature, and both make moves I could not answer on the spot. Here is the exchange, roughly in order:

(1) **Nora:** The doctrine says the economic base explains the legal and political superstructure. The Preface says the totality of the relations of production constitutes the economic structure of society, "the real foundation, on which arises a legal and political superstructure." But under capitalism the relations of production *are* relations of property, and property is a creature of law. So the foundation is built out of the very material the theory assigns to what it is supposed to explain. The distinction does not merely blur at interesting cases; it collapses at the core, and with it any claim that the base determines anything.

(2) **Tomás:** That is precisely the objection Cohen took most seriously, and it has an answer. Ownership need not be described in legal vocabulary at all. What matters for the base is effective control: who in fact decides how the means of production are used, and who in fact appropriates the surplus. Legal title is a shell that sometimes fits the underlying facts and sometimes does not. Redo the description of the base in that non-legal register and the collapse disappears.

(3) **Nora:** Then you have saved the distinction by abandoning Marx's own account. Two sentences later the Preface describes the productive forces coming into conflict with the existing relations of production "or – this merely expresses the same thing in legal terms – with the property relations within the framework of which they have operated hitherto." Marx thinks the legal formulation expresses the same thing. And your non-legal redescription has no determinate content anyway: whether the board or the shareholders control the firm, and whether a delivery courier counts as a worker or an independent contractor, is settled by corporate law and employment law. Strip the law away and there is no fact of the matter about who has effective control. You get the superstructure wearing economic clothing.

(4) **Tomás:** I will concede that in capitalism you cannot individuate the base without some reference to legal form — but the explanatory claim was never one of detailed one-way determination. It is the primacy thesis: superstructures are explained, functionally, by their tendency to stabilise the relations of production that they also constitute. The fragmentation of jurisdiction under feudalism and the generalisation of contract and property law under capitalism both fit that pattern, and that fit is the evidence. Note also that the Preface says the mode of production of material life "conditions" the general process of social, political and intellectual life — conditions, not mechanically causes.

(5) **Nora:** A functional explanation with no elaborated feedback mechanism is a just-so story: you can always discover a stabilising benefit after the fact, so the thesis explains every superstructure that survived and none that failed to. That is Elster's charge, and it is decisive. And your evidence is selected. English enclosure was enacted by statute — political power made agrarian property relations, not the reverse. And the twentieth century gives you revolutions in which a party seized the state and then constructed an industrial base from above, which is the primacy thesis exactly inverted.

(6) **Tomás:** Enclosure presupposes markets, purchasers, sellers and a class structure already in motion; the statutes ratify a transformation under way rather than initiating one. And the Soviet case does not invert the thesis but illustrates its other half: a party can build only what the existing level of productive development permits, and where it attempted to build beyond that level it failed. I would also say that Nora slides between "the base explains everything else" and "the base explains something in every society" — the second, much weaker claim is the one actually under dispute, and her examples do not touch it.
