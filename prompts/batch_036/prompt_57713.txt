You're a legal philosopher who's put in real time on transitional justice — imagine you're the person a truth commission rings up when it wants to know whether it's allowed to prosecute things that weren't crimes when they happened. Think hard about this rather than giving me the textbook tour; I want a principle I can actually apply, and I want you to be willing to tell me I'm inconsistent.

I keep having intuitions about retroactive punishment and they don't line up, and I can't tell whether that means I need a more complicated principle or that some of my verdicts are just wrong. Here they are:

1. The Nuremberg "crimes against peace" count — planning and waging aggressive war — against the men who did exactly that: I think the conviction was illegitimate, even though starting the war was the worst thing any of them ever did. Nobody had ever been prosecuted for aggression and no statute made it a crime.
2. But the atrocity counts in those same trials, the ones about what was actually done to people: legitimate, no statute needed.
3. Germany prosecuting the soldiers who shot people trying to get over the Berlin Wall, when East German law not only permitted it but treated it as a duty: legitimate. They were acting under the valid law of their own place and time and I still think convicting them is fine.
4. Prosecuting a judge who faithfully applied a valid statute of his own regime that criminalized something trivial — say a wartime decree against listening to foreign broadcasts: NOT legitimate. This one wrecks me, because it looks structurally identical to #3 (an official applying the law of the regime he serves) and I want the opposite verdict.
5. A conscript who fired at the wall under an order he'd have been shot for disobeying: not legitimate to convict him, though the officer who gave the order is fair game.
6. Retroactively extending a limitations period for a serious crime while the old one still has time left: fine. Retroactively reviving one that already ran out forty years ago: not fine — and I honestly can't say why the line sits there.
7. Retroactive tax legislation, and retroactive civil liability for old harms (asbestos, poisoned land): basically fine, small twinge only.
8. Applying a new, harsher sentencing range backwards to a crime committed before it existed: not fine.
9. Retroactively decriminalizing something, or amending a charge in the accused's favor: obviously fine, and I think any principle that can't explain why this direction is different is broken.
10. A country abolishes the marital rape exemption and then prosecutes a man for conduct from ten years earlier that was legal then but which he and everybody else knew was monstrous: genuinely don't know. I lean toward legitimate, which makes me nervous about #1 and #4.

My tentative principle is that retroactive punishment is legitimate only if (a) the defendant could reasonably have known the act was seriously wrong by a standard available to him at the time, independent of the state's say-so, and (b) the tribunal would be willing to apply the same retroactive standard to its own side's conduct. I suspect this is too crude — clause (b) doesn't look like it belongs in a theory of legality at all, it looks like a fairness-of-the-court requirement smuggling itself in.

Then commit to one. State it precisely, clause by clause, and for each clause tell me which numbered case motivates it and what the clause rules out. Point at #3 vs #4 specifically — if your principle can't separate them, say so plainly instead of fudging.
