**Draft: "Weighting, Not Content: What the WEIRD Data Actually License"**

The argument from moral disagreement is usually run as follows. Widely separated human populations converge on incompatible moral judgments about the same matters. If those judgments tracked mind-independent moral facts, we would expect convergence, at least among competent judges. Therefore the judgments do not track such facts.

My claim is that the empirical literature on cultural variation in moral psychology does not supply the second premise in the form the argument needs, and that the standard realist repair is worse off than the standard objection to it suggests. Both halves of this claim turn on a distinction that I think the debate has run together.

Call a *moral domain* a family of considerations that can generate normative force for a population: considerations about harm and care, about fairness and reciprocity, about loyalty and rank, about purity and pollution, about the proper exercise of authority. Variation between populations in their moral judgments can then be sorted in at least three ways.

First, *presence variation*: one population treats a consideration as morally force-bearing at all, another does not. The classic candidate is purity. Second, *weight variation*: both populations treat the consideration as force-bearing, but they assign it different priority relative to other considerations when the domains conflict. Third, what I will call *annexation*: a consideration that in one population is handled by a non-moral normative system — etiquette, prudence, ritual propriety, aesthetics of the person — is in another population annexed into the moral economy, so that its violation becomes a matter of blameworthy character rather than of bad manners or bad luck.

The distinction matters because the three kinds of variation have very different evidential bearing. Presence variation is what the anti-realist needs: it would show that the moral faculty, or the moral domain space, is not universal in structure. Weight variation and annexation do not show this. They show that a shared structure is parameterized differently by different histories. A scale whose pans are loaded differently in two places is not evidence that the two places lack scales.

My reading is that the record is overwhelmingly a record of weight, annexation, and scope variation, with very little clean presence variation. In the fifteen-society economic games project, the striking result was not that some populations were indifferent to the normative stakes of the games; it was that the predictions of narrow self-interest failed everywhere and failed differently in different places. Some populations made low offers and almost never punished low offers; some rejected offers above an even split because accepting a large gift imposed an unrepayable status debt; the most generous populations tended to be the most market-integrated or the most deeply embedded in large-scale cooperative institutions. Every one of those results describes a population responding to the same structural feature of the situation — that a division is being proposed and that others can see and sanction it — with a different calibration. Similarly, the cross-cultural work that is usually adduced as evidence of missing domains, on harm versus purity versus community, generally shows populations disagreeing about whether a harmless violation carries normative force, which is an annexation question, or about which of several acknowledged considerations wins, which is a weighting question. And the East–West cognition literature is mostly about how much of the perceptual and explanatory field a judgment takes in — again, a parameter, not an architecture.

So the anti-realist needs an extra premise: that disagreement persists among judges who are fully informed and identically weighted. That is exactly what idealization views — ideal observers, reflective equilibrium under improved conditions — claim to deliver.

Here is my central argument, and the part I most want stress-tested. The idealization reply fails, but not for the familiar reason that specifying ideal conditions begs the question by smuggling in substantive moral content. It fails because *idealization is weight-relative*. To specify a fully rational and fully informed judge, you must specify how that judge weighs inputs when the domains conflict, because full information does not by itself resolve conflicts between acknowledged considerations: information tells you more about the harm and more about the pollution, not which wins. So any specification of ideal conditions fixes a weighting function. But the weighting function is precisely the parameter that varies across populations, and the variation is not random — it is correlated with variables that no realist would call truth-conducive: subsistence style, degree of market integration, the historical erosion or preservation of intensive kinship, settlement density. Any weighting the realist builds into the idealizing conditions is therefore a weighting whose provenance is the same as the provenance of the weights it is meant to adjudicate. Call this the *weighting-fixity problem*: there is no culture-neutral way to fix the weights, and the weights must be fixed before idealization is defined.

If that is right, realism is not refuted, but it is relocated. In the last section I propose a substitute criterion, which I call the *counterweight test*: a domain-weighting is eligible to be treated as tracking something real only to the degree that it is stable under variation in ecological and historical variables that have no epistemic bearing on the question at hand. Where a weighting shifts systematically with market integration or with kinship structure, that is a defeater for the weighting, not for the domain it weights. Realists get objectivity at the level of domain presence and of the sensitivity functions that map situations to judgments; they lose determinacy at the level of weighting, which becomes genuinely underdetermined by the moral facts. The resulting view is objectivity with a hole in the middle, and I argue in the conclusion that this is a stable position rather than a waystation to anti-realism, because the anti-realist's own argument, once repaired with the premise it needs, establishes only that weightings are underdetermined.

**Comments I would like**

1. **Does the taxonomy hold?** Argue whether presence, weight, and annexation are three genuinely distinct categories or whether annexation collapses into weight variation — an annexed consideration is arguably just one with a very low weight, or a non-zero weight where others have zero. If it collapses, tell me whether the paper can survive on a two-way distinction, and what I would lose.

2. **Is the weighting-fixity argument valid?** Trace the inference step by step and identify the exact premise you think fails. I am especially worried that the move from "the weighting varies with non-truth-conducive factors" to "there is no culture-neutral way to fix the weights" is a non sequitur, since a realist could hold that the correlation with ecology is itself explained by the weights tracking different real features of different ecologies. Say whether that reply works, and if it does, whether I can block it without abandoning the argument.

3. **The self-undermining worry.** If my counterweight test disqualifies weightings whose provenance is historical and ecological, does it not equally disqualify my own weighting, including my high weight on the criterion of provenance-independence? This seems to me like a genealogical objection turned on myself, and I do not currently have an answer. Tell me whether section five can be revised to escape it, or whether the paper should concede asymmetry and explain why the concession is not fatal.

4. **Empirical accuracy.** Go through my characterization of the economic games work, of the harm–purity–community literature, and of the East–West cognition work, and flag every place where I have overstated, mischaracterized, or leaned on a result that is contested or has failed to replicate. Be specific about which sentence is in trouble. Also tell me whether my claim that clean presence variation is rare is defensible, or whether there is a well-attested case that would serve as a counterexample I would have to confront.

5. **Interest and economy.** Tell me whether the paper's contribution is the taxonomy, the weighting-fixity argument, or the counterweight test, and whether one of the three is dispensable. I suspect section four — the empirical survey — is longer than the argument requires and reads like a literature summary that a specialist audience already knows. Say whether to cut it to two sentences or whether it is doing load-bearing work I have not noticed. Do not rewrite the paper; tell me what to change and why.
