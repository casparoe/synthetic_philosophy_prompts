I've been running a small reading group on classical Chinese philosophy that meets Sunday afternoons in the back room of a used bookstore, and this month we're finishing a stretch of weeks on the Zhuangzi's treatment of death. The group has settled — a little too comfortably, in my view — into the standard line: Zhuangzi achieves equanimity because life and death are just phases of one continuing transformation, and grief is a failure to understand what has been appointed. One of our members is a palliative-care nurse who finds this consoling, another is a graduate student who finds it quietist, and I've volunteered to play the internal critic at our last two sessions. I want you to write the case I'll be presenting. I'm working from a printed rendering of the text that I've since lent out and cannot lay hands on again, so please treat my summaries below as the text itself; do not attempt to reconstruct or restore wording, do not put anything in quotation marks as if it were Zhuangzi's phrasing, and do not supply citations, chapter numbers, translator names, commentarial authorities, or a bibliography of any kind. Write in American English.

Here is the material I'll be drawing on, in my own paraphrase. First, the well-known scene after his wife's death: a visiting logician finds Zhuangzi sitting with his legs sprawled, drumming on a basin and singing. Zhuangzi concedes that when she first died he was as distressed as anyone, but then he reflected that before she was born there was not only no life but no form, and before there was form there was not only no form but no vital breath; out of the undifferentiated blur, change produced breath, breath changed into form, form changed into life, and now life has changed into death — a sequence no different from the running of spring, summer, autumn, and winter. To keep weeping and wailing would show that he has not understood what is appointed, so he stopped. Second, the roadside skull: Zhuangzi finds an empty skull, taps it with his whip, and asks it how it came to this — through some greed, some disgrace, some crime against the state, or simply through the years. That night the skull appears to him in a dream and explains that among the dead there is no ruler above and no subject below and none of the business of the seasons; one takes heaven and earth for one's own calendar, and the joy of a king enthroned facing south cannot surpass it. Zhuangzi asks whether he would like his body, kin, and old life back; the skull knits its brows and says it could not imagine trading the joy of a south-facing king for the toil of being human again. Third, Zhuangzi's own dying: his disciples begin planning a lavish burial, and he tells them he has heaven and earth for his inner and outer coffins, the sun and moon for paired jade discs, the stars for pearls, and the ten thousand things for grave goods — what burial fitting is missing? They say they fear the crows and kites will eat him; he answers that above ground he would be food for crows and kites and below ground for ants and mole crickets, and asks how partial it is to take from the one to give to the other. Fourth, and crucially for my purposes, the countervailing strand: walking in the mountains, Zhuangzi passes an enormous tree in full leaf that a woodcutter has stopped beside but declined to fell, on the grounds that its wood is good for nothing; Zhuangzi remarks to his disciples that this tree got to finish out its natural years by being useless. Leaving the mountains, he lodges with an old friend, who is delighted and orders a servant to kill a goose and cook it; the servant asks which of the two to kill, the one that can honk or the one that cannot, and the host chooses the one that cannot. The next day the disciple puts the puzzle to Zhuangzi — the tree survived its natural span by uselessness, the goose died by it, so where will the master stand? Zhuangzi laughs, says he will stand between the useful and the useless, immediately adds that standing between them is near the mark but not at it and so is not free of entanglement, and turns instead to riding along with the Way and its virtue, drifting where praise and blame cannot reach. Elsewhere in the same spirit there is a man so crooked and deformed that he is exempted from conscription and from public labor, who draws government rations and firewood along with the other exempt, who thereby nourishes his body and completes his natural years.

The position I want argued is this: that the "useless tree, useless goose" strand commits Zhuangzi to the view that a death arriving at the wrong time is a genuine bad for the one who dies, and that the equanimity of the drumming episode is therefore not the metaphysical insight it presents itself as, but a second-best consolation adopted only after the prudential project has failed — with the move to "between the useful and the useless" and then to riding on the Way functioning as a retreat that preserves rather than retracts that prudential commitment. Make this a real argument, not a survey: state the thesis, set out its premises in labeled steps, defend each step, and show where the textual material supplies it. The reasoning has to be built out of Zhuangzi's own vocabulary and episodes — transformation, the useful and the useless, completing one's natural years, what is appointed, the Way — so do not import any post-Zhuangzian machinery by name or by structure: no Epicurean symmetry between prenatal and postmortem nonexistence, no contemporary deprivation account of the badness of death, no reductionism about personal identity, no appeal to Stoic or Buddhist resources. Where you think my own framing does the argumentative work for you rather than the episodes doing it, say so explicitly. Then, in a clearly separated section, press the two strongest objections a defender of the standard equanimity reading would raise against you — for instance, that "natural years" is a descriptive, species-typical measure rather than an evaluative one, that the goose episode is aimed at strategy as such and so reinforces equanimity instead of threatening it, or that these are speeches placed in the mouths of characters and the text declines to assert doctrine in its own voice — and state precisely what your argument must concede in order to survive each, and whether that concession costs you the thesis. Close with a short assessment: which single episode is doing the most work in the case, what remains of the argument if that episode is struck, and any place where you suspect my paraphrase has distorted the original enough to matter. Aim for roughly 1,100 to 1,400 words, organized under a few section headings, in a formal register.
