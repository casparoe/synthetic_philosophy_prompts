I belong to a reading group that has been asked to lead next month's session on blame and its discontents, and one of our members has circulated, as the starting text, a long essay from a general-interest magazine. Its closing section undertakes to summarise Strawson's argument about freedom and resentment and then draws a conclusion about penal reform from it. I have read the section several times and I suspect it is doing something slippery, but I have never studied Strawson and could not defend that suspicion if challenged. The passage runs as follows:

> Strawson's achievement was to notice that the free-will debate had been asking the wrong question. Instead of enquiring whether determinism is compatible with responsibility, he asked what actually holds our practice of holding one another responsible in place. His answer was resentment, gratitude, indignation, forgiveness — the reactive attitudes. These are not doctrines we adopt but natural human responses, and because they are natural they are not hostage to metaphysics: no argument about the causal structure of the universe can show them to be mistaken, any more than it can show appetite to be mistaken. Three things follow. First, blame is a feeling rather than a judgement — a species of moral indigestion, in which our resentment dresses itself up as a claim about the world and mistakes its own projection for a property of the offender. Second, we could not opt out of these attitudes even if we wished to, so the question whether they are justified has no purchase and the truth or falsity of determinism is simply beside the point. Third, what Strawson called the objective attitude — the stance of the clinician or the social engineer, who regards the offender as a problem to be managed rather than a person to be blamed — is the direction a scientific culture must travel, and Strawson welcomed the journey as a gain in tolerance. He is thus a compatibilist in the strictest sense: he proved that responsibility and determinism cannot conflict, because responsibility is constituted by how we respond rather than by how the world is. The practical upshot is uncomfortable for reformers. Those who would abolish retributive punishment on determinist grounds cannot live their own theory; their programme is not merely false but self-defeating, and its advocates are in bad faith.

The essay illustrates the point with a magistrate who, having absorbed a great deal of neuroscience over a long career, reports that she can no longer resent her defendants — but finds that she can no longer feel gratitude, forgiveness or ordinary affection either, and describes the result as a liberation.

Could you audit this passage for me, paragraph by paragraph and claim by claim, sorting its contents into three piles: what Strawson would accept as a fair statement of his view; what is a legitimate simplification for a general audience, carrying no real distortion; and what he would reject, either because it contradicts him or because it attributes to him a conclusion his argument never reaches? I should like particular attention to the following points.

1. The passage seems to run together two distinct claims: that the framework of reactive attitudes cannot be shown to be irrational from some external standpoint, and that no general question about that framework can intelligibly be asked at all. Are these the same claim? Does Strawson endorse both? What does he think a general question about the framework could amount to if it is asked, and does determinism have any bearing on a question so construed?

2. The gloss on blame as a feeling rather than a judgement, and as resentment projecting itself onto the world, reads to me like a projectivist or error-theoretic story. Is that compatible with what Strawson says the reactive attitudes are responses to? And does anything in his account supports the further suggestion that the point of blaming is the discharge of a feeling?

3. How much of the third point survives contact with the essay? What does Strawson actually think about adopting the objective attitude across the board, about its emotional costs, and about its connection with tolerance and with the possible truth of determinism? Would he describe the magistrate's condition as a liberation, a loss, or neither?

4. Is it accurate to call Strawson a compatibilist in the strictest sense who proved that responsibility and determinism cannot conflict? If that overstates him, what is the modest thesis he is offering instead, and how does it differ in kind from the traditional compatibilist strategy?

5. Most importantly for our session: does the closing conclusion about penal reformers survive once the summary is corrected? If it does not, is there a weaker version of the self-defeat worry that a Strawsonian could endorse — something about what it takes to sustain a wholly objective stance in practice, perhaps — and how would I tell that weaker version apart from the one the essay actually asserts?

Please also flag anywhere the passage deploys a term of art in its everyday sense, or takes a standard objection to Strawson and presents it as though it were his own commitment. I am not after references, a reading list or the scholarly quarrels over him; what I need is your judgement on this text. End with a plain verdict I can put to the group: is the essay broadly sound in its summary and unsound only in its conclusion, is the summary itself already distorted, or is my suspicion unfounded?
