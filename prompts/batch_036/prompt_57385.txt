So I'm a third-year in philosophy of physics and last week at our foundations group I cooked up what I thought was a devastating little argument, and half the room bought it while the other half said I was cheating, and I genuinely can't tell which half is right. Setup, in the standard ontological-models / Harrigan-Spekkens idiom: a preparation P gets you a probability measure μ(λ|P) over an ontic state space Λ, a measurement M gets you response functions, and the model reproduces the theory when the observable statistics come out right. A theory is ψ-ontic if distinct pure states always get measures with disjoint support, ψ-epistemic if at least one pair overlaps.

The argument:

(1) Let Λ be a single point λ*. Every preparation then gets the same measure — the delta at λ* — so every pair of distinct pure states overlaps, indeed all of them are compatible with one and the same ontic state. As ψ-epistemic as it is possible to be.
(2) Alternatively let Λ be the set of pure states itself, with preparation P_ψ assigning the delta at ψ and the response functions given by the Born rule. Supports disjoint everywhere, so ψ-ontic.
(3) So both verdicts are available for quantum theory, and mutatis mutandis for any operational theory you care to write down: you just pick Λ and the measures.
(4) A property of a theory that you can make come out either true or false by a free choice of modelling vocabulary isn't a property of the theory.
(5) Therefore "is the wavefunction real?" is a verbal dispute, and PBR — supposedly a proof of ψ-ontology from preparation independence — is just a theorem about one bookkeeping convention, no more weighty than (1).

Where I keep getting pushed: someone says (1) doesn't reproduce the statistics unless I let the response functions see which preparation was used, and apparently that's a sin. But I dont see why. Sure, λ* does no work, but nobody promised Λ had to do any work — and everyone already concedes that a ψ-epistemic model has to buy its overlap with something, contextuality or giving up preparation independence or whatever, so the cheating charge starts to look like a seperate assumption being smuggled in after the fact. I'm most attached to (4), honestly — that's the step that feels like it says something — but I'm not married to it. Which step do you actually reject, why did it look fine to me, and what does rejecting it cost?
