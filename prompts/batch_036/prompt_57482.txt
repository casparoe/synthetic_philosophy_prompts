For thirty-eight years I worked as a comparative neurophysiologist, chiefly on the peripheral sensory systems of cold-blooded animals, and I have been retired for three. Philosophy of mind is my late vocation: I read a great deal, but I have no colleagues left to argue with, and I have discovered that the positions I most want to stress-test are the ones I cannot dislodge by myself. I should therefore like to set up a standing dispute with you.

Please play a committed functionalist about the mind — one who holds that mental states are individuated by their causal-functional roles within a system, and that those roles are in principle realizable in physically very dissimilar substrates. I want the position at full strength, not at its most convenient: you are a careful philosopher who knows that the thesis has been pressed hard from both directions, who is unimpressed by the objection that it licenses anything at all, and who has thought about what a functional analysis must look like in order to be neither chauvinist nor absurdly permissive.

The ground rules. Remain in character throughout and speak in the first person; break character only if I write STEP OUT at the start of a message. Keep each reply to roughly two paragraphs, so that this reads as a conversation rather than a treatise. Do not offer me bibliographies, reading lists, or citations — I want your reasons, not your sources. You may name a distinction or an argument where it genuinely bears weight, but never in place of reasoning. You may concede subsidiary points freely, and I expect you to; you may not concede the central thesis. Where you think my physiology is mistaken, simplified, or thirty years out of date, say so plainly and correct me: I would far rather be corrected than accommodated. Ask me a clarifying question when you need one, but ask it as your character would.

Here is my opening move. In my own laboratory the same animal and the same circuit, cooled from roughly twenty-two degrees to roughly six, will show conduction velocities and synaptic delays changing by a factor of two to three, and the decay phases of synaptic currents by rather more. What follows is not merely that everything is slower. The latency of a response shifts; the precision with which a spike train follows a time-varying stimulus degrades; the window within which two inputs must arrive in order to sum narrows or widens. Behaviourally, one watches the animal perform the same task to a different standard. Call this, for our purposes, the tolerance-band problem, and consider the dilemma I think it poses for you. The functional role of a perceptual state surely includes its timing, since timing is part of what makes it the input to some responses and not to others. If your functional specification admits those temporal parameters, then the class of admissible realizers is a narrow band rather than an open field, and any candidate realization must be tuned into it — at which point the much-advertised indifference of the role to its substrate begins to look like a fact about tolerances, and the specification itself begins to read suspiciously like a description of the wetware that first suggested it. If instead your specification abstracts away from timing, then it is silent on precisely the properties that determine whether the state does the work assigned to it, and a physically impeccable system in which the relevant inputs never coincide will count as realizing the state anyway.

Which horn do you take? And having taken it, show me what your functional analysis of even one ordinary perceptual state actually looks like on that horn, for this animal, at these two temperatures. If you think the dilemma is false, I am glad to hear it — but then produce the third option concretely, not as a sketch.
