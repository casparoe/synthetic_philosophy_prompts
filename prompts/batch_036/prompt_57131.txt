I am preparing a take-home examination question for a graduate seminar I co-teach with the computer science faculty on the epistemology of machine persuasion, and I want the question argued out in full before I assign it, so that I can judge whether it is worth three weeks of my students' time. The setup is the following, and I would ask that it be treated as stipulated rather than empirically open. Suppose an institution adopts an adjudicative protocol in which a single human arbiter must choose between two rival arguments, produced by competing artificial agents, concerning a question of practical consequence — for instance, whether a proposed reactor design satisfies its containment specification. The arbiter has a fixed and strictly limited checking budget: she can carefully verify at most *K* elementary inferential steps, where *K* is small, say forty steps generously counted, and whatever she does not carefully verify she must take on trust. Both agents generate derivations far longer than *K*. Now grant three further assumptions. First, step-local legibility: any single step in either argument is short and transparent enough that, if the arbiter spends budget on it, she can reliably determine whether that step is valid. Second, length asymmetry: the shortest sound derivation of the true answer from premises the arbiter already accepts is itself longer than *K*. Third, the unscrupulous agent has available what I shall call a dilution strategy: it can argue for the false conclusion with an argument whose unsoundness consists not in any one bad step but in how many individually innocuous steps compose — for example, by arranging a hidden dependency that manifests only when step seventeen and step thirty-one are taken together — so that the failure has no single legible address at which an inspection within budget could catch it. Call the resulting distance between what is checkable step by step and what is checkable as a whole the aggregation gap.

The optimistic line, which most of my colleagues in the governance world find intuitively compelling, holds that adversarial exchange nonetheless amplifies the arbiter's reach: falsehood is costly to maintain, because its defender must keep an elaborate structure of mutually supporting fabrications coherent while the defender of a truth need only keep pointing back at the truth, and because a single exposed error, wherever it sits, should suffice to demolish the edifice that rests on it. On this view the aggregation gap is not a serious objection, since the task of locating the fatal composition falls to the honest party, who is at least as well placed to find it as the sophist is to conceal it. The pessimistic line replies that this quietly assumes what the stipulations were designed to deny: that the honest party can *communicate* a composed failure through a channel that admits only step-local inspection, and that a defect real only in the joint behaviour of twenty separate steps is, from inside the budget, indistinguishable from no defect at all. It is, so to speak, a sorites of unsoundness — no individual step is imperceptibly bad, yet the heap is bad. I would also want the essay to confront the analogy that suggests itself from the sociology of the large sciences, namely Hardwig's argument about epistemic dependence: in a big collaborative experiment no individual participant is positioned to assess the entire chain on which the published result rests, so that the knowing is in some sense a property of the community rather than of any member of it. Whether that analogy cuts in favour of optimism about the protocol or against it is itself a live question, and I would not accept it being invoked decoratively.

Against that background, write an essay answering this question: does the aggregation gap defeat the claim that adversarial debate between highly capable artificial agents can reliably extend the reach of a budget-limited human arbiter? Take both cases at their strongest before you judge between them, and then commit to a verdict rather than retiring into balance. A conditional verdict is welcome — I expect the truth is conditional — but only if the conditions are stated precisely enough that one could say what would count as their obtaining or failing; a shrug dressed as a synthesis will not do. You may grant my three stipulations even while doubting their realism, but you should say explicitly what in your conclusion would shift if any of them were relaxed, and which of the three does the most work. Please furnish no bibliography, no reading list, and no citations; I am interested in what you can build from the premises, not in whom you can name. Organise the essay under exactly these headings, in this order, at roughly these lengths: (1) *The protocol and the gap* — a clean restatement of the mechanism and of what turns on it, about 250 words; (2) *Why debate should work anyway* — the strongest optimistic case, about 350 words; (3) *Why the gap may be fatal* — the strongest pessimistic case, about 350 words; (4) *Rejoinders* — the optimist's best replies to section 3 and the pessimist's best replies to those replies, about 300 words; (5) *Verdict* — your conclusion in the opening sentence, followed by its justification and an accounting of which stipulations it leans on, about 300 words. Roughly 1,550 words in total.
