Our company announced a new parental leave package at the all-hands on Thursday: six weeks paid and non-transferable for the person who gives birth, described in the deck as "recovery," plus a 20-week pot of "family leave" the parents can divide however they like, plus a flat $3,000 baby bonus. Afterward a bunch of us went to the pub and two colleagues ended up in a real argument about it, I got nominated as referee, and I completely flubbed it. Could you go through their exchange move by move and tell me who's right where?

Dana opened (1) by saying the family pot is a fiction, because at every company that's tried this the second parent takes a token two weeks and the mother takes the rest, so a policy that looks neutral on paper just hands the whole thing to mothers and lets the company shrug. Marcus (2) said the alternative is worse: a policy that hard-codes the mother as the long-leave parent is the actually sexist design, whereas the pot lets a couple where the father is the lower earner, or works remotely, or where the mother is mid-fellowship decide for themselves, and the six non-transferable weeks correctly separate the medical part from the parenting part, which is the one place unequal treatment is justified. Dana (3) said fine, then you agree the split should follow reasons, and after six weeks there are no medical reasons, so what's making it come out 18/2 in practice — nothing except the assumption that the mother's career is the flexible one. Marcus (4) said the 18/2 isn't caused by the leave design, it's caused by the existing wage gap and by whose job is easier to cover, and couples are just responding rationally to their own household budget; blaming the policy for that is blaming the thermometer. Dana (5) said it's a closed loop, because a big chunk of the wage gap is made of the career penalties that come from having taken the long leave, so the design helps produce the fact it then appeals to, and that's precisely why Norway and Sweden reserve a block of leave that the second parent simply forfeits if he doesn't use it — they tried transferable leave first and the fathers didn't take it. Marcus (6) said earmarking is worse than the disease: it takes weeks away from mothers who genuinely want them, does nothing for single parents, adoptive parents, or a couple using a surrogate or a donor where the birth-parent category doesn't map onto who's actually caring for the baby, and it turns a family's own judgement about its own life into a company mandate. Dana (7) then made the move I couldn't answer — she said Marcus's own economics commits him to her side, because if employers statistically discriminate against women of childbearing age on the expectation that they'll be gone for months, the only thing that removes the hiring penalty is making the expected absence identical for men and women, and you don't get identical absence out of free choice while the norms are what they are. Marcus (8) said he accepts that prediction but denies it's decisive, since a policy that improves the average by overriding particular families' judgements about their own lives does something illiberal even when the arithmetic works, and he'd rather attack the discrimination at the employer end than make a father's leave compulsory. Dana said the employer end is hopeless because the discrimination is about expected absence and expected absence is exactly what's observable, and then they both looked at me and I mumbled something about the baby bonus being regressive, which nobody had asked about.

So: rate each of the eight moves, roughly clearly-mistake to clearly-right, and say which ones land and why. Flag anywhere one of them is right for a bad reason or has quietly shifted what they're arguing about — I think Marcus changes the subject somewhere between 2 and 8 but I can't say where. Say what both of them are missing, including anything about the six-week recovery block or the lump sum that neither one touched. And finally: is there a version of this policy that Dana's and Marcus's own starting principles would both permit, or are they actually incompatible rather than just disagreeing about evidence? I'm asking because I'm on our parent employee group and we get to file a counterproposal, and I don't want to put forward something one of them could take apart in five minutes.
