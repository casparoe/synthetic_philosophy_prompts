I serve on the board of a small medical relief organization, and I need to circulate a written decision memo to my fellow trustees ahead of our meeting on the 14th.

Our charter contains a single sentence bearing on the question now before us: "The Organization shall not use force, nor place itself under the protection of those who do." The second clause has never been tested. For eleven years we have run a surgical and vaccination programme in a valley reachable by only one road, and nobody offered us protection. That changed six weeks ago. Two unescorted convoys were stopped and looted; in the second, a driver was killed. The armed group controlling the road has now offered to put its own fighters in our vehicles for the four-hour run, at no cost and with no conditions attached to whom we treat. Separately, the regional military command has offered an armed escort, but requires advance notification of routes and monthly figures on patients treated, by village. Our counsel advises that any arrangement with the group would expose the organization and its officers to prosecution in two of our three donor countries; please treat that advice as a settled constraint rather than as something to be argued with.

The board is evenly split. Several trustees hold that accepting the group's fighters would breach the charter and destroy the appearance of independence that has kept us operating at all, and that a refusal is not itself a decision to expose anyone to harm — it is only a refusal to do wrong with our own hands. Others note that we already accept police guards at our compound in the capital, paid for by a government that is a party to the fighting in the valley, and they cannot see what distinguishes those guards from an escort on the road.

Please draft the memo I will circulate. It should recommend one course of action for the coming dry-season programme — twelve convoy runs over the next four months — and state plainly how the charter's second clause ought to be read, since the reading largely determines the answer. It should give the trustees on the other side considerations they could actually respond to rather than dismiss, and it should say what developments mid-season would count as reasons to reverse the recommendation. Under 900 words.
