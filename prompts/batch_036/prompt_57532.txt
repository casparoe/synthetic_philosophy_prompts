I've been on the night shift at a river forecast center for about four months now. We put out flood guidance for three small basins and somebody downstream actually reads it, which I hadn't fully appreciated before I started. Last month I was the duty officer on a call I got wrong — not catastrophically, but a town got an advisory at 3am about moving livestock to higher ground and it turned out to be nothing, and the farmers association has been letting us hear about it ever since.

Since then a friend of mine from before this job has been running an argument at me. He reads alot, no physics training, but he's sharp. It's been weirdly comforting, which is the part that scares me, because the comfort is basically "your job is metaphysically confused so the bad call wasn't on you." Here it is, cleaned up from memory, roughly the way he puts it:

1. If the future is settled — genuinely fixed, not just "we don't know it yet" — then it's the kind of thing that could in principle be read off the present state plus the laws.
2. Reading it off requires knowing the present state.
3. Any measurement of the present state has finite precision. There's always residual error, however small.
4. In a chaotic system error grows: make the initial uncertainty arbitrarily small and it still amplifies until it's larger than the difference between two completely different outcomes. So (3) isn't fixable by better instruments, better computers, or being cleverer, becuase the error is in the input, not the arithmetic.
5. So for any chaotic system there's a horizon past which your prediction is worth no more than a coin flip.
6. So by (1) the future of a chaotic system isn't settled.
7. What I forecast is a chaotic system. So are markets, traffic, epidemics, and brains, since brains are made of matter.
8. So the future that actually matters to us isn't settled, and "determinism" is either false or doesn't mean anything.

I pushed back once and said we do keep measuring better, and he said (4) already covers that — arbitrarily small means arbitrarily small. I didn't have an answer. The other thing I tried was that plenty of what I do isn't hopeless: I can't tell you where a rain cell will be in nine days, but I can tell you the basin runs wetter than normal this season and be right. He said that's just describing the system more coarsely, which doesn't touch the argument at all. I'm not sure that's a real answer but I couldn't say why not.

If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (4) than (1). (4) is literally my job, I watch it happen on the screens every shift. But I don't want (8), and I can't see how to deny it without handing him (1). Which step breaks, and what does breaking it cost me? And is there a weaker version of his argument that survives the repair — I have a feeling there is, and I'd rather hear it from you now than rebuild my confidence on something that's about to collapse again. No reading list, I'm not going to read it, just tell me where the thing snaps.
