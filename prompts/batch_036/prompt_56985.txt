I work as a software engineer and have no formal training in philosophy; I read it on my own in the evenings. For the past two years I have been technical lead on a system that handles intake, medication reminders, and daily check-ins for a home-care provider — work that previously required a paid caregiver to be physically present in the client's house. While preparing to defend the product internally, I started reading in care ethics (Held, Tronto, some Noddings), and I have run into a recurrent argumentative move that I cannot find a name for and that I am not sure I have ever seen stated as such.

Here is the move, in two settings. First: an elderly parent needs care, and an adult child pays an agency to supply it. Everyone concedes that the paid carer performs every task at least as well — she is more reliable, better trained, never resentful, never distracted. Yet the discussion insists that something has been lost, and when pressed about what, it locates the loss not in any feature of the care delivered but in the motive of the person delivering it, or in the relation between the parties. Second, the same shape with my own product: no human is involved at all, and the residue is still posited, now located in the relation itself.

What strikes me is how the residue gets characterized: exclusively by subtraction. Everything that can be replicated is granted as replicated, and what remains is whatever is left over, with no independent test for its presence or absence. And the same authors who assert the residue in one argument seem to drop it in another when the conclusion requires it — paying someone to care is then said to discharge the obligation in full, with no further mention of motive. So the residue is weighty enough to decide cases and uncheckable enough to survive any case.

My questions: is there an established concept for this — a value residue defined only by elimination and located in motives or relations rather than in anything observable about the outcome? I can see neighbouring ideas (objections to commodification, claims that certain goods are incommensurable, the worry about doing the right thing for the wrong reason), but each appears to name something else. I would like the nearest established concepts laid out, how the pattern I have described differs from each, which fits best, and where its edges lie — what a case would look like that involves the same intuition but is not this pattern. If nothing fits, name and define it yourself and give me boundary cases. I also want your view on whether, charitably read, the move is a substantive claim about value that has merely been stated badly, or a shelter that any conclusion can retreat into. I am after the carving rather than the label, and I am not looking for a reading list or citations; I will track down the sources myself.
