For the past eighteen months I have worked as the recording analyst for the business conduct panel of an international engineering and infrastructure group, and one of my standing tasks is maintaining a register of its written determinations on gifts, hospitality, and community contributions. The panel rotates its membership, hears roughly a dozen matters a year, and reasons from a code whose two relevant provisions pull against each other: one bars an employee from accepting anything of more than nominal value from a party that has a matter pending before that employee or the employee's organizational unit, while the other exempts hospitality that is customary or ceremonial in character, modest in value, and disclosed within the reporting window. Nobody on the panel has ever said which provision controls when they conflict, and the general counsel's office has now asked me to prepare a memo ahead of next month's sitting. Here is the record I have coded, in the order the determinations were issued. A country manager accepted an engraved desk pen worth about sixty dollars from a supplier at the signing ceremony for a contract that had already been awarded, and logged it inside the five-day window; cleared. A project engineer accepted two nights of lodging paid for by a contractor whose bid on the package he was inspecting was then live, the nearest alternative accommodation being a four-hour drive; his request was approved in advance and the contractor lost; cleared, with the panel remarking that the alternative was to forgo the inspection altogether. A site supervisor accepted two hundred dollars in cash from a village chief at a road inauguration, where a refusal would have been received as a rejection of the community itself; he logged it the same day and the sum went into the site welfare fund; cleared. A procurement officer took a week of manufacturer-funded technical training abroad, two days of which were sightseeing, while his unit was evaluating that manufacturer's bid, though he personally cast no vote; his line manager approved it and it was logged; violation. The firm gave five thousand dollars to a village school at the sitting mayor's request while a municipal tender was live, the gift was announced in the local press, and no official received anything personally; violation. A regional director accepted a corporate-box seat valued at roughly four hundred dollars from a supplier that had recently been delisted and had nothing pending anywhere in the group; logged and approved; cleared. A field engineer accepted a branded jacket worth about thirty-five dollars from a subcontractor whose invoices he certified monthly, logged it promptly, and was still found in violation, the determination reasoning that in a certification relationship any transfer, however small, is capable of being read as a payment.

The matter now before the panel is this. A senior hydrologist on the firm's payroll is seconded three days a month to a national water authority's technical committee, where she is one of nine members voting on groundwater extraction quotas. Each autumn the regional growers' association presents every member of that committee, along with the parish priest and the head of the village school, with a case of wine and a handwoven table runner; the practice is locally documented from decades before the authority existed, and it is extended without reference to whether the recipient has any business before the association. The value is roughly one hundred eighty dollars, and she disclosed it within the window. The association has an application for an enlarged quota that the committee will hear in four months' time; she has not recused herself. Two members have asked whether recusal from that particular hearing would cure the problem, and a third has circulated a note arguing that the entire line of determinations quietly presupposes that a regulatory office can be staffed by someone with no social ties whatever to the people it regulates, a condition no society has ever actually satisfied.

What I need is a prediction of how the panel will rule, with some indication of how confident you would be and which way you expect the vote to split. Please reconstruct the candidate principles the record is consistent with, point to the determinations that are doing the discriminating work among them, and identify the closest analog in the record together with the precise point at which that analogy begins to strain. Then state what the panel would be committing itself to if it clears her and what different thing it would be committing itself to if it finds a violation, including whether recusal changes either answer or merely relocates the problem. Finally, describe one further hypothetical matter, itself consistent with both provisions of the code, whose determination would force a choice between the principles you judge to have survived the existing record.
