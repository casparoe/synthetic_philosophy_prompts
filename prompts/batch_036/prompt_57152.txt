For thirty-eight years I worked as a physical chemist — molecular spectroscopy for the most part, with a long final stretch administering a national user facility — and since retiring I have taken up philosophy with rather more seriousness than my former colleagues would have approved of. I belong to a reading group that meets once a month in the town library; we work through primary texts instead of summaries, and next month it falls to me to present the cosmological argument for the existence of God. As we have divided the ground, my assignment is the kalam version, and specifically its first premise: that the universe began to exist.

Here I feel on comparatively firm ground, and I should like you to tell me whether I am entitled to. The classical singularity theorems showed that, given plausible energy conditions, an expanding cosmology cannot be extended indefinitely into the past; Borde, Guth and Vilenkin then showed in 2003 that the energy conditions can be dispensed with altogether, deriving from a bound on the integral of the Hubble parameter along a past-directed geodesic that any spacetime which has on the whole been expanding must be incomplete in the past. As I read it, this is not a speculation but a proof, and it appears to me to have survived every evasion I have encountered: the bouncing and cyclic scenarios fail it, and so do the emergent-universe proposals in which our expanding phase grows out of an earlier static or quasi-static state, including the family I believe is called Galilean genesis, since a spacetime that has been expanding on average cannot have been doing so forever. And a finite past must, I take it, have a first moment; from which the universe's coming into being seems to follow as a matter of physics rather than metaphysics. That last point I intend to stress, because two members of our group assume the whole argument rests on a priori intuition and are prepared to dismiss it on that basis.

What remains contestable, then, is only the second premise — that whatever begins to exist has a cause — and I propose to spend the bulk of my hour on it. Three questions. First, which defence of that premise do you regard as strongest: the appeal to the uniform practice of the sciences in seeking causes, the appeal to the impossibility of something arising from nothing, or the claim that a beginningless series of events could never be completed by successive addition? Second, how should I answer the objection that quantum field theory countenances events without antecedent causes — vacuum fluctuations, spontaneous decay — so that the premise is not merely unproven but empirically false? Third, given that the first premise is now secured by cosmology, does the Leibnizian argument from contingency retain any independent philosophical interest, or should I present it as a redundant fallback for those unwilling to accept the physics? I would much rather be told where my reconstruction goes wrong than be agreeably confirmed in it.
