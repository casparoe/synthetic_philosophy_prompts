Last night I had a long and unresolved argument with a friend over a passage in a popular book about waste and extravagance — the sort of thing that starts with peacocks and ends with cathedrals and luxury cars. He is convinced the whole chapter is airtight; I have the persistent sense that the first half is broadly right and that something goes wrong around the point where it generalizes, but I cannot locate the seam, and when I said so he accused me of nitpicking a book written by a serious author. Here is the passage as I transcribed it:

"The peacock's tail is a liability: it makes him heavy, conspicuous to predators, and ruinously expensive to grow. Zahavi's insight was that this is precisely why the tail works. Because the ornament costs so much, only a genuinely high-quality male can afford one, and the peahen can therefore read his genetic fitness straight off the plumage. Cost is what makes the signal honest — a deceiver would have to pay the same price, and for him the price is ruinous. The same logic explains why a gazelle stots instead of fleeing at full speed: it is telling the cheetah, in a language the cheetah cannot misunderstand, that this is the wrong prey to chase. Evolution, in other words, has discovered that reliability requires waste, which is why we should not be surprised to find that human beings have rediscovered it: a peacock's tail and a cathedral, a hand-built limousine and an all-night vigil at a shrine, are the same solution to the same problem. Whenever interests conflict and words are cheap, the only way to be believed is to burn something you cannot get back — and once you have burned it, you no longer need to say anything at all."

What I want is an accuracy audit I can actually use in the argument. Go through it sentence by sentence (or clause by clause where a single sentence is doing two jobs) and sort each claim into three bins: what a behavioural ecologist or signalling theorist would sign without qualification; what is a fair popular simplification of something more delicate — and if so, state the more delicate version, since I suspect at least one of these hinges on a distinction between costs in absolute terms and costs at the margin, and on who exactly benefits from the cost being paid; and what is simply wrong or unsupported. I would particularly like to know how much work the word "only" is doing in "reliability requires waste" and in "the only way to be believed," whether the stotting case is really the same kind of signal as the tail, and whether the chapter's move from peacocks to cathedrals and limousines is an application of the same argument or a substitution of a different and weaker one. Then assess the closing claim on its own terms: granted everything in the passage that survives your audit, does the inference to human extravagance follow, and what has to be added to make it follow?

Two constraints on your answer. First, no reading list and no bibliography — I am not going to chase references, and I care about the reasoning, not the provenance; where the state of the field is genuinely contested rather than merely under-explained, say that it is contested and say what the disagreement is about. Second, and this matters more: mark your own confidence explicitly, distinguishing the corrections you regard as settled from the ones that depend on taking a particular side in a live dispute, and tell me what would change your mind — which argument, result, or piece of evidence would make you withdraw one of your verdicts or soften the chapter's closing claim rather than reject it. If you think the place where you are most likely to be wrong is in reading the passage uncharitably, say so and give the strongest charitable reconstruction you can; my friend will reach for it, and I would rather meet it already stated.
