I serve my second term on the board of a small rural water district in the western part of my state — five elected commissioners, roughly 9,100 connections, and a staff of six. I should be candid at the outset about who is asking: I own a hardware store, I have no training in economics or public policy, and everything I describe below I have been turning over by myself for about two weeks, mostly at night. I would like a written opinion I can circulate to the other four commissioners before our regular meeting on the 14th, when we vote.

The situation. The state awarded us a one-time capital grant of $3.1 million, and the award commits us to spend it on a single named project. Our engineer brought two candidates, both of which absorb the grant and cost about the same to operate afterward.

Project A builds a second treatment train and a redundant raw-water intake. Today we have exactly one way of making our water potable, and if it is knocked out — burn debris washing into the reservoir after a fire on the ridge, a tanker rollover on the highway above the plant, a flood that takes out the clearwell — the entire district loses safe drinking water at once, for an estimated five to nine days. Our engineer puts the chance of such an event at about 1 in 40 in any given year.

Project B replaces eleven miles of the oldest main and the laterals off it. It does nothing whatever about the scenario above. What it does is cut the number of localized failures — a block or a few dozen homes without water or under a boil notice for a day or two, with a crew on site within hours — from about 95 per year to about 47.

Staff priced both. They value a day of lost service to one connection at a flat $255, covering bottled water, trucked supply, crew overtime, business interruption, and customer credits. On that basis Project A returns about $406,000 a year in expected benefit and Project B about $404,000. Our written capital plan policy says projects are to be ranked by expected benefit per dollar and is silent on how to break a tie, so on our own governing test the two are even, to within the rounding of the estimates. The engineer's table also reports that the average connection currently loses about 0.52 days of safe water a year, and that either project brings that down to about 0.35. So no ratepayer is better off, on average, under one project than the other. What differs is the shape.

That is where the board has come apart, and it is the argument I need help with.

Doyle, in his third term, says the tie in the spreadsheet is beside the point. A hundred and sixty scattered failures a year, he says, is something a district absorbs: each is over in a day, each touches a few dozen people, the crews know the routine, and the rest of the town goes on. One week in which all 9,100 connections are out together is not the same amount of trouble arranged differently; it is a different kind of thing, and the difference does not appear anywhere in the $406,000. He adds that we should not console ourselves with the thought that it averages out over time. We will make this decision once. There is no second round in which the unlucky years are balanced by the lucky ones.

Vance, who spent thirty years as a loan officer, says Doyle is describing something that never happens to anybody. By staff's own figures, each household's expected loss is identical whichever project we build; the only difference between the two worlds is how the losses line up with one another across households. If we polled all 9,100 ratepayers about their own house, they would shrug. What Doyle says he cares about — the bunched-together-ness of it, the fact that everybody's bad week would be the same week — is a property of the total, and no ratepayer ever experiences the total. Vance's phrase was that we are proposing to spend three million dollars on a picture in our heads.

Marisol, the newest commissioner, teaches high school mathematics and is worried about a third thing. She says that if we adopt Doyle's way of deciding, we will not be able to hold onto it. First, the same project can be written up two ways — as one chance in forty that the whole district goes without safe water, or as 9,100 households each facing a one-in-forty chance — and Doyle's argument only carries any force in the first wording. Second, a rule that lets us prefer the flatter outcome at equal expected value will let people talk us into a sequence of trades, each of which passes the rule, that leaves the district worse off ten years from now than if we had never started. Third, she cannot see how to write the rule into the capital plan policy so that the next board could apply it to a staff table without relitigating this argument from scratch.

Renata, who chairs finance and owns the pharmacy in town, thinks the tie is an artifact and objects to the $255. It is one flat rate for every connection-day, which treats a week without water in July as ninety-five of the same thing as an afternoon without water in October, and it prices only households: it leaves out the two dialysis patients we pump for, the nursing home on the ridge, three days of school closure, the volunteer fire department's tanks, and the possibility that a week-long event costs us our bond rating. She wants the estimate rebuilt. Doyle's answer is that if a re-estimate is what decides the question, then we are only saying the answer depends on how clever we are at pricing, and he wants a way of deciding that still works when we cannot price the thing.

What I am asking for is the opinion itself — call it two pages, in plain English, because it will be read aloud at the meeting and two of us are not readers of technical material. Please have it:

1. Recommend one project, or a definite alternative such as a specific split of the grant or a delay with a stated trigger for revisiting, that we can actually vote on the 14th.
2. Say whether Doyle's reasoning identifies something real or is a confusion. If it is real, say precisely what it is about, given Vance's point that no individual's own odds or own expected harm change.
3. Take Marisol's three worries in order and say of each whether it defeats the rule, merely limits how the rule may be used, or misses.
4. Tell us what to do about Renata's objection: whether the re-estimate must be finished before the vote, and how we should decide if it cannot be ready in time.
5. Give us a decision rule we could write into the capital plan policy and apply to next year's queue from a staff table alone, and say honestly what the rule has to give up in order to be that usable.
6. State the strongest case against your own recommendation as forcefully as you can, and tell me what would have to be true for you to change your mind.

Two further requests. Do not assume I will recognize technical vocabulary; if a term is genuinely needed, introduce it first in a sentence of ordinary language. And do not soften the answer into a finding that both sides have a point. One of these projects is going to be built this year, and I want to be able to say why it was the right one. If the opinion needs information we have not gathered, say what to ask staff for rather than supplying a figure of your own.
