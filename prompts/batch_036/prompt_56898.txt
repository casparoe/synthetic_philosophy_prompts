I'm the evaluation lead at a mid-size private foundation (about $90M a year, mostly housing stability and workforce programs) and I have to approve or reject the outcome indicators grantee orgs put in their proposals, because those indicators are what decide whether they get renewed. Its about 14 of these a quarter and I get maybe two hours of analyst time total across all of them, so whatever you give me has to be fast. The person who actually signs off is our program director — smart, not quantitative, will not read more than one page.

The problem: our current screen is three questions — is the indicator measurable, is it plausibly caused by the grantee's work, and could the grantee hit it without changing what they do? That third one basically never fires, and I'm now pretty sure it's the wrong question entirely, since anything can be gamed if someone's clever enough, so a yes/no on gameability doesn't discriminate between proposals. Meanwhile we keep approving indicators that look fine on paper and then two years out the numbers are beautiful and nothing has changed.

Build me the actual procedure. Concretely I need:

- The questions my analysts ask about a proposed indicator, in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each. Not "consider whether X is robust" — a test they can run and a bar it has to clear.
- Where the procedure terminates in reject, where it terminates in approve-with-a-cap or approve-with-a-monitor, and where it hands the thing to the program director because it's a question about values and not about facts. Be explicit about how the analyst is supposed to tell those apart, because right now they can't.
- It has to keep four failure modes separate, because they need different fixes. (a) The indicator correlates with what we want but the correlation is imperfect for reasons that don't travel, so selecting hard on the indicator also selects for whatever makes it high for the wrong reasons. (b) The relationship holds in the normal range of performance and falls apart at the top end — which is exactly the end we're paying people to reach. (c) Pushing the indicator up causally reduces the thing we care about. (d) Somebody is deliberately optimizing the record instead of the outcome. I don't care what you name them. I care that the procedure tells an analyst which one they're looking at, because (a) gets fixed by changing the measure, (c) might get fixed by killing the incentive, (d) gets fixed by audit, and we currently apply the same fix to all four.
- What to do when nobody can produce a number. Most of our grantees have tiny datasets and "we don't have the power to detect this" is going to be the honest answer more often than not, and the procedure can't just stall out every time.
- Honesty about which steps are judgment calls wearing a lab coat. I'd rather see them flagged than have something that pretends to be mechanical.

Then run it on three proposals and give me the one-page verdict you'd actually hand the program director for each:

1. A workforce org proposing "percentage of participants placed in a job within 90 days of program completion," where the org itself defines what counts as completion.
2. A housing org proposing "number of eviction filings prevented," measured by counting the households they assisted that never show up in county court records.
3. A homelessness org proposing to be scored on "self-reported sense of stability at six months," collected in an interview by the same case manager who's been serving the person.

For at least one of these the procedure should land somewhere the program team will hate — I want to see it say the thing, and I want to be able to judge whether it says it for a good reason or because the procedure is just trigger-happy. Also tell me which of the three the procedure is least well-suited to, and why.

Finally: what can't it catch. The failure modes that would sail through every question on the list and still leave us funding something hollow — the slow drift ones, the ones where every org games identically so the comparison looks clean, the ones that land on people who never show up in anybody's data.
