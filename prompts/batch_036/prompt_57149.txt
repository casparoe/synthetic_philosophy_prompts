my brother joined a group about eight months ago that's convinced something extremely bad happens on a specific date next spring, and I've mostly given up on arguing about the content of the prophecy. what I can't let go of is that a friend of ours — smart guy, does forecasting stuff, super markets — keeps telling me the group is actually epistemically healthy and I'm being dismissive. his argument goes like this: a prediction market is like a group mind. nobody in the market knows much, but all the individual errors are noise, and when you average them out the price lands closer to the truth than any one trader would. so a group of a few hundred sincere people converging on a shared belief is doing the same thing, and if they all independently landed on the same date, that's signal, not noise. "you wouldn't argue with the market price," he says.

here's the thing that makes me want to scream. I got my brother to describe how the group actually settles what it believes, and it's basically a textbook doctrinal paradox — that's apparently what they call it when courts run into it. their date only works if three separate conditions all hold. when the whole assembly votes yes/no on each condition on its own, each one passes by a huge majority, like 70%. but when they take a straight yes/no vote on the actual claim — the thing happening on that date — it loses, something like 70% against. different people are voting different ways on the two questions, so the group's own procedure gives it two incompatible answers depending on whether you go premise by premise or straight to the conclusion. I brought this to my friend as a gotcha and he didn't even blink. he said that's exactly what you'd expect from making people cram their beliefs into yes/no boxes, and that if the group collected a number from everyone instead — a real probability — and averaged them, the average would come out perfectly coherent every single time, the same way the market price can never contradict itself. so in his view the paradox is an artifact of the binary format, and once you fix the format, averaging beliefs is just as safe as averaging money.

can you audit this for me? I want the correspondences spelled out — what in the market story is playing the role of the ballot, the voter, the agenda, the thing being aggregated, the group's output, all of it. then tell me where the mapping actually breaks, and be picky about which breaks matter for the point he's using it for and which are just decoration. specifically: is he right that averaging probabilities can't generate anything like the paradox, or is there a version of the problem that survives the switch to numbers? what does the market picture quietly assume about the group that a bunch of people who have to speak and act with one voice doesn't get to have — does the market ever actually have to commit to anything, or does it just emit a price nobody is on the hook for? and is there something about the paradox being manipulable, about who picks which question gets voted on and in what order, that the market analogy hides? at the end I want a verdict on whether his conclusion survives — "averaging makes the group smarter than its members, so the shared belief deserves deference" — or whether the analogy was carrying the whole weight of that conclusion. and if there's a better comparison for what's actually going on in that basement, give it to me, because I have a lot of time on my hands now and I'd rather understand it than win an argument.
