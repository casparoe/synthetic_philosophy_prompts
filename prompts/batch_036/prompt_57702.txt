For the past two years I have been co-teaching a decision theory seminar with an economist and a philosopher whose disagreement about the independence axiom never resolves, and I have come to suspect that they are not having the same argument. Stated as neutrally as I can manage: both accept the classical Allais choices as data, both agree the empirical record is messy and sensitive to how prospects are described and to what is at stake, and both grant that expected utility is not the only formally coherent framework on the table. Where they part company is over what the axiom is for. The philosopher's camp holds that violations can be permissible — that the certainty of a prize is a genuine feature of a prospect rather than an artifact of its presentation; that representation theorems show only that axiom-obeying preferences admit an expected-utility representation, not that they must obey; and that the money-pump objection fails against a sophisticated agent who plans ahead and declines the second-stage choice, or in any case shows only that pumping someone costs something. The economist's camp holds that violations are symptoms of a badly posed choice — that dynamic inconsistency is a real and measurable cost, that the certainty effect drifts under re-description, and that abandoning independence surrenders precisely the property that made preferences into an object of formal study. Each side accuses the other of begging the question or of changing the subject, and nobody has moved.

My working hypothesis, which I want tested rather than flattered, is that this is not one disagreement but three tangled together. The first is formal: whether any independence-violating ordering can be rendered dynamically consistent for an agent with full foresight, or whether the sophisticated-chooser reply is only a verbal escape from the pumping argument. The second is empirical and, I think, underexploited: what happens when certainty is dialed down rather than removed — replace the sure prize with a 0.9999 chance of it, then 0.999, then 0.99, and locate where, if anywhere, each camp's intuitions flip; if the permissivist's reason evaporates smoothly while the orthodox camp's diagnosis is all-or-nothing, that is itself evidence about which question they are answering. The third is meta-normative: whether axioms constrain the representation of an ordering or the conduct of deliberation, with Savage's deliberative gloss on the sure-thing principle — that it binds an agent who has thought the matter through — cited by both camps in support of opposite readings. Is that diagnosis right, or is the dispute substantive and singular, a genuine clash over what counts as a reason in choice under risk that my three-way split merely disguises? I want the structure of the disagreement, not a verdict on who is correct and not a survey of the alternatives to expected utility.

Answer in exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion: the first naming the kind of disagreement you take this to be (verbal, substantive, two questions under one label, a hidden formal or empirical question doing the work, or an ill-posed one); the second and third giving the strongest evidence for that reading drawn from what each of the two camps actually says; the fourth saying what my three-tangled-disagreements hypothesis gets right and what it gets wrong; and the fifth stating what would count as progress — a theorem, an experiment, a distinction, a change of question — or arguing that none should be expected and why.
