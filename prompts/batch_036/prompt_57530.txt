This term my fourth-year seminar in political philosophy and formal social choice reaches futarchy — Robin Hanson's proposal that a polity should "vote on values, bet on beliefs", with a democratic majority fixing the measure of welfare to be pursued and conditional prediction markets settling which policy maximises it. There are twenty-two students, seated at four tables of five or six, and I have one eighty-minute session in which to make the scheme feel like something other than a slogan.

My students reliably arrive with two errors, and I want an in-class game that dismantles both from the inside rather than by my asserting that they are errors.

The first is that they treat a prediction market as an elaborate opinion poll, and assume that once the outcome is observed the market's accuracy can simply be checked against it. The second arises when manipulation is raised: they repeat the standard reassurance — that a would-be manipulator must buy at the honest price, so pays for the privilege, and that the price snaps back afterwards — without noticing that the reassurance presumes a market that merely reports. Under futarchy the price *is* the decision rule, so moving the price is not asserting something false about the future; it is purchasing the future, at a bounded cost, against an unbounded payoff. Closely related, and just as hard to make vivid, is the asymmetry between the two branches of the comparison: once a policy is adopted, only that branch is ever realised, and the contract conditional on the rejected branch ceases to be traded by anyone who expects it to be rejected. The number the scheme most requires is therefore the number the market is least willing to quote.

Please design the game. My requirements are as follows.

1. The welfare variable must be generated in the room, in real time, by the participants' own behaviour under whichever policy is adopted. It may not be a figure I announce, and it may not be a lottery I draw. Specify two candidate policies, the scalar measure defined over the room's activity, and the productive task that yields it, with enough genuine contingency that the outcome is not obvious from the start.

2. No real money. Use a fixed chip endowment and an explicit payoff function for the policy-conditional contracts, presented as a small printed table that can be evaluated by hand in under a minute. Justify the functional form: it should make honest forecasting strictly profitable in expectation, and — this matters most — the manipulator's arithmetic should be visible on the payoff table rather than engineered into the rules. If the design is honest, the profitable manipulation should fall out of the payoffs as an unwelcome consequence.

3. Include an opening vote on values, taken before anyone knows anything about the policies' prospects, in which the seminar chooses between two candidate welfare measures; the contracts must pay on whichever measure is chosen. Tell me what this costs in minutes, whether the vote is itself strategic, and what to do if it is.

4. Some participants must hold a private stake in which policy is adopted that is independent of the welfare measure — they do better under one policy whatever the outcome turns out to be. State how many such players, how you assign them, and how large the stake must be relative to ordinary forecasting profits for the effect to bite without being spotted immediately.

5. Give an exact timeline in minutes, a full materials list, and rules precise enough that a teaching assistant who has seen none of this could run the session unaided.

6. Provide a debrief that maps particular moments of play onto the two errors above, in the order those moments will have occurred, and that reaches the point at which someone notices that their own trades had stopped being forecasts. Give me the questions to ask rather than a speech to deliver.

7. Be frank about what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run. I expect at least: too little variance in the outcome variable; the conditional market on the losing branch dying completely without anyone understanding why; chip arithmetic bogging the room down; and the privately interested players either blowing their cover or being too timid to exploit it. Offer a pre-emptive fix for each.

8. State honestly which features of futarchy the game cannot reproduce and which I must therefore supply verbally. I suspect the contested character of the welfare measure and the continuous liquidity of real markets are candidates; tell me which you regard as genuinely irreproducible and which as merely difficult.

9. Include a twenty-five-minute fallback version for the event that the session overruns.

I am not after a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind — the design is the deliverable. Please write the whole response in British English.
