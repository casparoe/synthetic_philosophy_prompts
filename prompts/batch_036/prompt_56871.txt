**The question set**

> Is self-deception possible? If it is, say what the mind must be like in order for it to be possible. If it is not, say what our ordinary talk of self-deception amounts to. Address at least one attempt to dissolve the problem rather than to solve it.
>
> (1,200 words; 50 marks)

**The student's answer** (paragraphs numbered for reference)

¶1. Deception, as we ordinarily understand it, is a transaction between two people. One of them knows that p is false and brings it about that the other believes it; the knowledge sits on one side of the exchange and the false belief on the other. Self-deception appears to require that both sides of this transaction be occupied by the same person, and that is where the difficulty begins. The self-deceiver must know the truth in order to have something to hide, and must fail to know it in order to have succeeded in hiding it. If that is right, self-deception is not merely hard to explain but formally impossible, since it asks one and the same mind to stand in contradictory relations to one and the same proposition at one and the same time.

¶2. It is worth separating two versions of the problem, because they call for different remedies. The first is static: how can a person simultaneously believe that p and believe that not-p? The second is dynamic, or strategic: how can a person set out to deceive herself at all, given that the plan she is executing is a plan she must not notice herself executing? A strategy known to its victim to be deceitful is a strategy that cannot work. The dynamic problem is the more serious of the two, because it survives any solution to the static one; a philosopher who has explained how contradictory beliefs can coexist still owes an explanation of how the project of bringing them about gets off the ground.

¶3. The most influential response to the static problem is to deny that the two beliefs belong to a single system. On Davidson's account the mind is partitioned: it contains semi-autonomous structures, each with its own stock of beliefs, and a contradiction is tolerable provided that the offending beliefs are quarantined in different structures and cannot be brought together in reasoning. What I believe about my marriage and what I believe about marriage may conflict; the conflict is survivable so long as no single act of inference draws on both. Davidson is careful to insist that the partitions are not little people. Nothing inside the head is a second agent with its own intentions and its own view of the first; the division is a division of the work that one person's psychology does, not a division of persons. That insistence is what allows him to avoid the obvious objection, since if the partitions were agents we should have to ask how the smaller agent deceives the larger, and then how the smaller agent's own parts deceive it, without end.

¶4. Sartre approaches the same problem from the other direction, and his answer is structural rather than logical. Consciousness, on his view, is divided into levels: there is a level at which the truth is known and a level at which it is not, and bad faith consists in the deception running between the levels rather than within either of them. This is why he thinks Freud was substantially right about the censor and mistaken only about the ontology built around it. The censor knows what it represses, since otherwise it could not select what to repress; the conscious ego does not know it; and the two are not the same thing. Once the mind is understood as two storeys rather than one room, the contradiction dissolves, because no single level ever holds both beliefs at once.

¶5. A more deflationary strategy dissolves the problem differently, by denying that self-deception has the structure of interpersonal deception in the first place. Mele and others argue that no exotic machinery is required: self-deceived people gather evidence in a biased manner, dwell on what is comfortable and hurry past what is not, and arrive, by entirely ordinary routes, at the belief they wanted to have. Nothing here involves an intention to deceive oneself, and nothing involves contradictory beliefs. The welcome consequence is that the paradox turns out to have been verbal all along. If self-deception is motivated belief-formation rather than self-directed deceit, the question whether a person can both know and not know the truth never arises, and the dynamic problem disappears with it, since there is no plan to be executed and therefore no plan to be kept hidden. It follows that self-deception is best understood as a species of weakness of will rather than of deception: the self-deceived person acts against her better judgement, only in the sphere of belief rather than in the sphere of action.

¶6. Two refinements are worth making before drawing a conclusion. First, self-deception need not issue in a false belief about a matter of fact. A person may hold entirely accurate beliefs about her situation and still be self-deceived about her reasons for being in it: about why she stays, why she agreed, what she is angry about. Any account that requires a false factual belief will leave these cases out, and they are among the most important ones. Second, an alternative tradition treats the difficulty not as one about what the person believes but as one about what she avows. On this view the self-deceived person has the information; what she declines to do is to spell it out, to make it explicit even to herself, and so to bring it into the space where she would have to act on it or argue with it. The contradiction lies not within the belief-set but between the belief-set and what the person will say about it.

¶7. My conclusion is that self-deception is possible, but only in the weak sense the deflationists describe. The strong sense, in which a person deliberately deceives herself, is not possible. It requires a part of the mind to be an agent, and once a part of the mind is an agent the regress cannot be stopped: something must deceive the deceiver, and then something must deceive that. Partitioning therefore cannot do the work it was meant to do, and Sartre's levels fare no better. What remains is a picture of a person who wants something to be the case and who manages, by biased attention and by declining to spell out what she already has, to come to believe it. That is unglamorous, but it is possible; and the ungainliness of the alternatives is a reason to accept it.
