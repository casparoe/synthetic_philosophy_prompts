I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the epistemology of disagreement, and in week five we screen Sidney Lumet's 1957 film adaptation of Reginald Rose's teleplay Twelve Angry Men, in a room with no air conditioning, which I like to think is thematically appropriate. My students arrive having done the standard reading on peer disagreement and on higher-order evidence, and they almost invariably come away from the film with the view that it is a straightforward parable of one conscientious man holding out against a prejudiced mob, with the moral that we should all be more like Juror 8. I want to disabuse them of that, because I think the film is doing something considerably stranger and more interesting, but I have not been able to articulate what. I would like a proper critical reading of the film through the philosophy of higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia that I can build a session around.

Here is what strikes me and what I would like you to press on. Juror 8 produces no exculpatory first-order evidence whatever: nothing he offers tends to show the boy did not do it, and everything he offers is instead a reason to distrust the reliability or the confidence of the state's witnesses and of his fellow jurors — the disabled neighbor could not have covered the distance to his door in the time he claimed, the woman across the tracks must habitually wear glasses she was not wearing in bed, the supposedly distinctive switchblade is available in any pawnshop. Is that a coherent dramatic account of higher-order evidence as evidence about evidence, or does the film smuggle it in as though it were first-order? And what does the film's institutional setting make of the conciliation-versus-steadfastness debate: Juror 8 holds out against eleven, yet the secret-ballot bargain he proposes would have him abandon his dissent if the rest of the jury voted guilty without him, and thereafter votes migrate in response to the running tally rather than to argument. Relatedly, Juror 12, the advertising man, switches to not guilty, switches back after Juror 4 insists the eyewitness's account is beyond question, and switches again: is that epistemic akrasia in any sense worth the name, or is it merely a man whose beliefs track the room, and what would have to be true for that distinction to bite? Then there is the ending, which I find genuinely puzzling as an epistemologist. Juror 3's conversion is explicitly not evidence-driven: he tears up the photograph of himself and his estranged son and collapses, and only then votes to acquit. He ends up with the belief the discussion supports, but he seems to acquire it for precisely the wrong kind of reason, and if belief is not under direct voluntary control, it is unclear that he acquires a belief at all rather than merely conforming his vote. Does the film present that as an epistemic success or a failure? And Juror 10, whose tirade about people from the slums leads the others to turn their backs on him, never recants and never revises: he keeps a belief the entire group has abandoned without any change in his evidence. I have been told there is a view, which for the sake of the prompt you can call level-splitting, on which a person may rationally hold a belief while also believing that no reasonable person sharing his evidence would be as confident as he is. Is Juror 10 a case of that, or of something worse, and does the film's treatment of him — silence and turned backs rather than argument — endorse a picture on which some beliefs are simply not answerable to reasons?

What I want back is criticism, not a plot summary and not a syllabus: tell me what epistemology the film actually dramatizes, which scenes I should point the students to and in what order, and where the reading has to stop being honest. I am acutely aware of two places where it strains — the film never tells us whether the boy is guilty, so nothing in it vindicates Juror 8's confidence or makes his dissent anything other than a wager, and the film is at least as much about class resentment and prejudice as about evidence — and I want you to say how far the epistemological reading can be pushed before it starts distorting the movie. Please commit to a position rather than offering me a menu of readings: state what you take the film's implicit epistemology to be, whether an epistemologist ought to endorse it, and what the film gets right about higher-order evidence and akrasia that the technical treatments tend to omit. No bibliography and no list of further reading, please; I want your own view with your reasons, together with a clear flagging of the points where you are leaning on a contested interpretation of the film or of the concepts.
