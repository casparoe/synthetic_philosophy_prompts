ok so I run the philosophy club at my school and every semester I make up a fake "final exam" that people genuinely stress about, which I find hilarious. This semester the section is on whether leaning on AI is quietly costing us understanding, and I need ONE essay question for it plus a model answer and a marking scheme, so I can grade people's answers on something other than vibes.

Here's the setup I already typed onto the handout, so the question has to build on it rather than replace it. I invented two terms:

- **answer-dependence**: you already know what you're asking, and you use the tool to get the answer. Like you've already noticed the analogy in the poem is doing something weird and you just want a second opinion.
- **question-dependence**: you don't know what to ask until it tells you. The tool picks the topic, the angle, the objections worth considering, and you mostly polish the prose.

And there's a paired case. Priya and Jonah hand in the same history essay and get the same grade. Priya wrote a full draft alone, then used AI specifically to attack it, and afterwards she couldn't reproduce the essay from memory — but shown three rival versions she could immediately say which was weakest and why. Jonah barely touched AI while writing and read two whole books, except that he'd had the AI outline his argument for him before he read anything, so he could reproduce the essay near-perfectly and couldn't say what evidence would count against it.

The question should make people commit to who of the two actually understands the material, and it should force them to deal with the extended mind reply — the notebook case, where the claim is that the notebook isn't just a helpful thing the guy owns, it's literally functioning as part of his mind. Because if thats right, then "the person plus the tool" understands, and the whole erosion worry has to get relocated somewhere else, and I want the question to make them say exactly where it lands. I also want a top answer to take seriously the pushback that the notebook is only coupled to his mind rather than constituting it, and the related worry that if you let everything count as part of a mind the idea stops meaning anything — and to say whether those objections bite on Priya, on Jonah, on both, or on neither.

Constraints: 45 minutes, 20 marks total, model answer around 550 words. The question must not be answerable by defining "understanding" and then listing pros and cons of AI in school. I want the crux to be the production/recognition split — Priya can't produce but can discriminate, Jonah can produce but can't discriminate — plus whether understanding is even the kind of thing that lives in a person rather than in a person-plus-tool system. And please don't write "cite two sources" into the question, half the club would just invent them.

For the marking scheme, give the point breakdown out of 20 and name the specific moves an A answer has to make that a B answer won't, plus the mistakes that should cost marks — like treating the extended mind claim as the boring claim that tools are useful, or thinking "Priya couldn't write it from memory" settles the case by itself.

One last thing: tell me honestly whether my two terms are actually doing separate work or whether they collapse into one distinction in disguise. If they collapse, suggest the smallest fix to the setup before I print it.
