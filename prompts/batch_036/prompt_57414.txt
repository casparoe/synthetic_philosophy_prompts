I am a third-year graduate student in a philosophy-of-social-science reading group. Last week we were working through Popper's The Poverty of Historicism, and one member circulated a draft chapter in advance: an argument that, conditional on sustained below-replacement fertility and unreformed pay-as-you-go pension structures, a defined class of sovereigns faces a rising probability of debt crises between roughly 2050 and 2070. The chapter set out its conditioning assumptions explicitly, offered credence ranges rather than point predictions, and was written for a journal whose readership includes market participants and treasury officials. A second member dismissed it with a single sentence: you cannot predict the future course of history, because any prediction changes what it predicts. Several people agreed, the author withdrew the chapter from circulation, and I left convinced that the sentence had been made to carry considerably more weight than it can bear.

I am not asking you to evaluate the forecasts. I want the sentence itself taken apart. Please lay out, as a menu rather than as one favored interpretation, the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making. I can see at least two joints in it — the modality of "cannot" (logical impossibility, conceptual incoherence, or mere practical unreliability) and the scope of "the future course of history" (a single unconditional trajectory for society as a whole, versus any general claim whatsoever about future social states) — but I expect there are further readings, including ones that turn on the second half of the sentence rather than the first, and ones on which the two halves come apart and only one of them does the work. For each reading, tell me what exactly it asserts, whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or simply false, and what a person who adopts it is thereby committed to elsewhere.

Then answer the question I actually care about: which of these readings, if any, would have licensed dismissing the chapter, and which leave it entirely untouched. Please do not assume away the second half of the sentence in order to get a clean verdict — the chapter was explicitly conditional and probabilistic and was destined for an audience capable of acting on it, so if there is a reading under which the reflexive clause does genuine damage to this particular project, say so, and say how much damage it does and to which parts.

One further check rather than an assumption. The sentence is usually credited to the author of the book we were reading. On whichever reading would license the dismissal, is it anything like the position that book argues for, or does that position actually forbid it? I would like the divergence, if there is one, spelled out.

Two requests about how you write this up. First, keep a clear line between what is settled among people who work on this material and what remains genuinely disputed — for instance, whether the argument the slogan gestures at succeeds at all, whether the position it is aimed at was ever held by anyone in the form being attacked, and whether the slogan survives being restated in probabilistic terms. Do not present one contested interpretation of the argument as though it were the consensus. Second, please write the answer in American English.
