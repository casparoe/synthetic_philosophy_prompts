You're a philosopher who works on intertemporal choice and intergenerational ethics, and who's comfortable with the formal side too — discount functions, dynamic consistency, the stationarity results, aggregation over infinite horizons. Please think this through carefully before answering: sketch privately where the chain could break, then commit to one diagnosis.

I built this argument for a reading group, originally to defend the standard line that pure time preference is irrational — the Sidgwick/Ramsey/Parfit line, where Ramsey's complaint is basically that discounting a future just because its the future is a failure of the imagination. But when I actually ran it out, it proved way more then I wanted, and now I can't tell whether the target or my argument is the problem.

1. The weight a benefit deserves in deliberation depends only on how much well-being it is and whose it is, not on where in time it falls.
2. Pure time preference says the weight depends on when as well: an identical benefit counts for less for no other reason than that it comes later.
3. So pure time preference isn't a rival substantive view about how much well-being matters. It's the view that position in time is itself a reason — and "later" is no more of a reason than "farther away" or "happening to someone I'll never meet."
4. (1) isn't just asserted, either. Here's its support: a principle of weighing that lets the ranking of two options turn on an irrelevant feature isn't a principle of weighing at all, it's arbitrary, and arbitrariness is a defect of rationality rather than a difference of taste about values.
5. But apply (4) consistently. A principle that lets the ranking turn on whose benefit it is, for no reason other than that it's mine, is arbitrary in exactly that sense.
6. So if (1) and (4) are right, agent-relative partiality is irrational too — and so is any weighting of the near person over the distant one.
7. And the trouble runs the other way as well. If (1) holds and the future is unbounded, then any policy that permanently raises well-being by even an epsilon has infinite value, and so does any policy that permanently lowers it by an epsilon. Two infinities don't rank. Undiscounted aggregation therefore doesn't give you an ethics of the far future that's merely demanding; it gives you one with no ordering in it at all.
8. The only thing that restores an ordering is a positive discount rate, i.e. (2).
9. Therefore either pure time preference is not irrational but load-bearing — the very thing that makes intergenerational ethics usable — and the Ramsey charge is just my era's taste dressed up as logic; or there is no coherent content to ethics about the far future. Since (7)'s nihilism is absurd, the first horn it is.

I can't accept (9). I'm pretty attached to (1) and (4) — if I have to give something up I'd rather it be (7) or (8), and I'd hate to be forced into (6). But I want to know what I'm actually paying.

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look fine when I wrote it down? Tell me whether the problem is a term doing double duty between two steps, a claim that quietly needs a premise nobody supplied, or a step that's genuinely valid and merely expensive to keep. Then price the alternatives: what does each of (1), (4), (5), (7) and (8) cost me if I keep it and drop something else instead — including whether holding onto (1) really does commit me to (6), and whether (8)'s "only thing" survives contact with the ways people actually try to compare infinite-horizon prospects.
