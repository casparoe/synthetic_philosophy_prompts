I'm in my third year of graduate study and for the past term I've been sitting in on a reading group run by a colleague I'll call R., on future contingents from Aristotle's sea battle onward. R. is not much interested in which label from the literature you attach to a view; she asks for verdicts on cases and then reads the view off them. I have been keeping a list of her verdicts because next week I have to present a case she hasn't seen and predict her response, and I would rather not be wrong in front of the whole group. Here is the list. A fair coin is to be flipped in an hour and nobody has any special information: R. says that "it will land heads" is not true, and that "it will land tails" is not true either, and she is emphatic that this is not an epistemic point, not a claim that one of them is true while we fail to know which. But "it will land either heads or tails," with the disjunction inside the scope of the "will," she calls true, and obviously so; she says anyone who denies it has been misled by a notation. The same disjunction with the "will" distributed — "either it will land heads or it will land tails" — she refuses to call true, on the ground that joining two claims that are neither true nor false with an "or" does not manufacture a truth. A claim that an injection of a fast-acting toxin will kill the patient within the hour, where the objective chance is as high as you like short of certainty: strictly speaking not true, she says, the same strict verdict as for the coin; and when a student pressed her on whether such a claim is then *nearer* the truth than the coin claim, she replied that this was not a question her account was obliged to answer.

Four more. A race was run yesterday and we are about to watch the recording: "the runner in lane four will be ahead at the two-hundred-metre mark" — true, she says, though the "will" is doing no real work there, because there is no fork to speak of. A colleague has secretly already signed an acceptance for a post in Vienna, though nobody in the group knows it: the claim, made today, that she will be at the Vienna conference in March is true; the very same claim made three weeks ago, before she signed, was neither true nor false; and R. insists the difference has nothing to do with what anyone knew at either time and everything to do with whether the world had narrowed. On Thursday she said "it will rain on Saturday"; it did not; asked now, she says flatly that what she said was false and that she was wrong, but asked whether it was true on Thursday that it would rain on Saturday she says no, adding that she is wrong now and that on Thursday there was nothing there yet to be wrong about. And "it might land heads," said before the flip, she calls true — telling a student who objected that this contradicts her verdict on "it will land heads" that his inference was valid but his premise, that "might" is simply the negation of "will," was not something she had granted him.

The case I have to present is this. On Monday two members of the group, Nadia and Tomas, disagree about which city a hiring committee will choose for a post it decides on Friday: Nadia says "the committee will choose Oslo," Tomas says "the committee will choose Lisbon," and they take a bet. A third member, Ines, hearing both, says on Monday: "neither of you is saying anything true." On Friday the committee chooses Oslo. R. will be asked whether Ines's remark was true when she made it; whether Nadia was right on Monday or is only correctly called right now; and — the part I expect to be pressed on — whether Ines's remark and R.'s own account of the coin amount to the same claim, since Ines's remark looks like exactly what R. says about the two coin claims. I know the standard menu well enough to be confused rather than helped by it: Aristotle's gap, the Ockhamist's determinate truth values, supervaluation over branching histories, the Peircean reading of "will" as "in every future," assessment-sensitive truth, and a Łukasiewicz-style view on which unsettled claims have intermediate degrees of truth. My own guess is that R. is some kind of supervaluationist, but her verdict on the distributed disjunction seems to rule that out, and I cannot see what she is instead. So: which rule or rules are genuinely consistent with everything on my list; what does each predict about Ines, about Nadia, and about whether Ines and R. are saying the same thing; which single verdict do you think she will actually give on the Monday case, and why should I prefer that reading to the others; and what further case, one she has not already ruled on, would separate whatever candidates you are left with? Please be explicit about how confident you are in each of your main claims and single out the point in your answer you are least sure of; and if you think one of the verdicts I have reported simply cannot be squared with the others, say which, what you suspect I have misreported or over-read, and how much of the rest of the list survives that correction.
