I'm in a seminar this term on Plutarch and the uses of biography, and for my paper I want to run one real episode through the theory of moral learning from lives that the Parallel Lives assumes. The episode is the Catholic church slowly dismantling its own hagiography. Two stages, roughly: the Jesuit Bollandists at Antwerp from the 17th century on, doing the Acta Sanctorum — reading the saints' lives critically, sorting what could be documented from what was pious invention — and then the 1969 revision of the Roman calendar, which dropped Christopher and a handful of others from universal celebration basically because there was no reliable evidence they'd existed, while leaving local and popular devotion pretty much alone. The Christopher medal bit is what I keep coming back to: people went on wearing them, shrines went on working.

So, map the theory onto it. By the theory I mean the apparatus in the Alexander proem plus the way the Lives actually run — the claim that he's doing lives not histories; that a trifle (a remark, a joke, how somebody handles a small thing) shows character better than the big public events do; the mirror business in the same passage if I'm remembering that right; the paired structure with the comparison at the end; and the fact that he'll also give you men who are *not* worth imitating, on the assumption you learn by avoidance too.

What I want is (a) who the agents actually are in the Bollandist/1969 episode and what each of them thinks the moral work of a saint's life consists in; (b) which pieces of Plutarch's apparatus the episode confirms and which it has no slot for — my suspicion is that an exemplar with no story, keep the feast and the name and delete the narrative, is something his account simply can't generate, and that the reformers were leaning on a mechanism he never describes; and (c) whether his own disclaimer, lives not histories, turns out to be a licence for the purge or an argument against it. My instinct is against — i.e. he'd say you don't get to cancel an exemplar for being undocumented, because the character-revealing detail was never doing evidential work to begin with. Argue both sides properly and tell me if I've got it backwards.

Two things I've been playing with for my own use, tell me if they're junk: call it narrative warrant vs. evidential warrant — a life can carry moral authority either because it happened or because it's well constructed, and Plutarch mostly trades on the second while claiming the first. And related, exemplar decay: how fast an exemplar loses its grip once its historicity is publicly doubted — is that decay total or partial? Does the survival of the medals show it was near zero, and if so, which warrant was doing the work the whole time?

One epistemic ask, and it matters for the paper: seperate out what's actually settled here from what's genuinely contested among people who know this material. I already know that whether lives-not-histories is a genre remark or a claim about truth isn't agreed, and that the motives behind the 1969 removals aren't either — some people would say it was more about de-cluttering the calendar than about historicity at all. So don't hand me a contested reading dressed up as the consensus, and flag the spots where you're unsure instead of smoothing them over. No need for sources or a reading list, I've got those off the syllabus.
