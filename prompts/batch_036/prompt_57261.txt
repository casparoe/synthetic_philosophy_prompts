Bit of an odd request, so bear with me. I spent thirty-four years in metrology — most of it at Teddington, calibrating temperature and pressure transducers for other people's laboratories — and since I packed up I've been reading philosophy rather more seriously than I'd expected to, mostly at night with a whisky and a pencil. I've just come out the far end of Williamson's Knowledge and Its Limits, and the part that has actually stuck to me isn't the knowledge-first business everyone goes on about. It's the anti-luminosity chapter and that cold case: the woman who wakes up feeling cold and warms by degrees across the day, where the assumption that feeling cold is luminous — that whenever it obtains she's in a position to know it obtains — gets squeezed against a margin-for-error premise until something has to give. And the KK material sitting alongside it, the idea that knowing doesn't automatically come with knowing that you know. Here's my obsession, and I'd like you to see whether it survives contact: in my trade there's a standing problem you cannot engineer your way out of. An instrument that drifts by less than its own resolution cannot detect its own drift. So you compare it against a better standard — but that standard has to be checked against a better one again, and all the way up you are always leaning on something you cannot presently verify from the inside. The cold case looks to me like the same problem wearing a cardigan: the subject is her own reference standard, and there's no margin left over for the checking. Whether that's a deep analogy or merely a pretty one is exactly what I want tested, and I don't trust myself to test it in prose. So: a short story, please.

The seed I had, which you are welcome to improve on or throw out. A nose — a perfumer, let's say late sixties, somewhere with a river and old industry near it — who has been losing sensitivity to one particular material over a couple of years, in steps too small to notice: a violet-ish thing, the sort I gather perfumers grumble tires the nose after a few sniffs, though you'll know better than me. She keeps a private calibration routine, Monday mornings, dilution series on blotters from a reference set she built herself thirty years ago, everything logged in a notebook with dates. The wrinkle that matters: the reference set is judged by the nose that's drifting. Now the demand I'd really ask you to honour. The crucial moment must not be the folk version — she must *not* simply have lost the sense while remaining blissfully unaware. I want the harder thing: at the decisive moment she can still smell it, the condition genuinely obtains, and yet she is not in a position to know that it does, because her judgement has no buffer left — a slightly weaker dilution, or the same strip an hour on, and she'd have called it exactly the same way and been wrong. Her reports stay sincere and internally consistent the whole way through, and from the inside nothing feels like a gap. Two further things. Give her a young QA manager or an auditor from some industry body who keeps asking, politely, but how do you know — and let that question be the KK pressure made flesh rather than a debate; and put something real on the line, a batch she has to certify or a reformulation she has to sign off, so it isn't all introspection in a nice room. No character is to explain luminosity, no jargon, no essay paragraphs, and the margin-for-error idea should be felt as a chain of individually discriminable small steps with knowledge needing slack at each one. If a gas chromatograph turns up, it must not rescue her: at most it relocates the problem, since somebody still has to judge the peak and the machine's own certificate is something she can only take on trust. Say 1,800 to 2,500 words, close third person.

On tone: plain, sensory, a bit dry — I have no patience for writing that smells of the writing, if you see what I mean. I'd like the ending to be something that happens which she cannot afterwards be sure happened, and the last line should do work rather than tie a bow. I'll be reading it aloud to my book group — four retired engineers and my sister-in-law who teaches French — so it has to stand entirely on its own for people who've never heard the word luminous in that sense; but I also want a philosopher picking it up to be able to put a finger on the structure. And if you think my instrument-drift analogy is actually a bad one, please build that into the story somehow rather than telling me afterwards — I'd far rather be contradicted inside the fiction than flattered outside it. Just the story, though: no reading list, no bibliography, no author's note unpacking the philosophy. I'd be very greatful.
