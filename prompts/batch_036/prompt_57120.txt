I'm 71, retired after 40 years in sedimentology — deep-sea cores, mostly arguing about what knocked the climate around in the deep past. Since I stopped working I've gotten into philosophy, and causation is where I keep landing, which is unsurprising since it was basically my day job.

There's the counterfactual crowd (Lewis): a cause is something such that if it hadn't happened, the effect wouldn't have. Then there's the interventionists (Woodward): causation is about what the effect does when you wiggle the cause with everything else held fixed. A grad student friend of the family told me at Christmas that on the second view I never made a single causal claim in my whole career, since nobody can intervene on the Eocene ocean or on whether an asteroid hits. I found that irrittating, but I can't tell whether he's actually got a point.

So what are the most important implications of that split for someone in my position? Does the interventionist account really demote my work to correlation dressed up as a story, or does it just mean the interventions are hypothetical ones nobody could carry out? Does it matter that some of my causes were in priciple wigglyable (CO2 in the water column) while others never could be (the asteroid, orbital cycles)? And practically: should I have held those core-based claims at lower confidence than the lab measurements I trusted, and does either account give me a way to tell a real cause from a good yarn? No reading list please — I've got more books than eyesight left. End with a last line beginning "Verdict:" telling me straight whether the grad student is right.
