I ran a protein chemistry lab for thirty-four years and only started reading philosophy after I retired, so excuse the rough vocabulary. I've joined a Tuesday night reading group at the community college and a couple weeks back we landed on personal identity — specifically whether you could have come from a different sperm and a different egg and still be you. Two analogies did essentially all the arguing in that room and I walked out trusting neither of them.

Analogy one, from the member who's read the most Kripke: origins are essential to a thing the way the origin of a table is essential to it. A table made from one particular hunk of wood couldn't have been made from a different hunk of wood, and by the same token you couldn't have come from a different pair of gametes — a different pair and you get somebody else, or nobody at all.

Analogy two, from a younger guy who writes software: no, a person is more like a program. A program is its code, not the machine that happened to run it or which copy of the installer you used; run the same code and you get the same program. So identity lives in the information and the particular egg and sperm are just delivery vehicles. He thinks that settles it.

I want to push on something from my own side of the fence, and I can't tell whether it's a real objection or just a scientist's tic. The genome is not a program, it's closer to a box of reagents, and what comes out depends enormously on the medium it goes into — the egg contributes things the sperm simply doesn't, mitochondrial DNA being the obvious one, plus whatever marks are already sitting on the chromosomes. So "the same code" may not be a quantity you can even hold fixed when you swap the gametes out.

Also, in the lab you always ask what the tolerance is before you're allowed to say two samples are the same, and neither side would give me one. How different can the hunk of wood be before it's a different table? How different can the gamete be before it's a different person? It seemed to me that's exactly where both analogies were leaning without saying so.

And there's an asymmetry nobody raised: even granting that a different pair couldn't have produced you, that doesn't show the same pair was enough to produce you. I'd call that confusing a necessary condition with a sufficient one, and I suspect the table example only ever earns the first half.

Can you audit the two analogies for me? Please use these sections, in this order:

- The mappings — one table, ten to fourteen rows total, with the source element on the left, what it's supposed to correspond to on the right, and a column saying whether that pairing actually holds.
- What holds — around 150 words on the parts of each mapping that survive scrutiny.
- What breaks — around 300 words, and please mark which disanalogies are load-bearing for the conclusion each person wanted versus which ones are cosmetic.
- Smuggled premises — around 150 words on what each source domain quietly imports that persons and gametes lack.
- The tolerance question — around 150 words on the sorites-flavored version: can either analogy survive having to say where the cutoff is.
- Verdict — around 150 words on which conclusion, if either, is still standing, working in the necessary/sufficient asymmetry from above.
- A better analogy — around 100 words, or just say plainly if there isn't one that does better.
