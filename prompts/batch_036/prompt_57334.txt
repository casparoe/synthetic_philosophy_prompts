I should say at the outset who is asking, because the answer I want depends on it. I left school at sixteen and have no university education of any kind; I have never taken a course in philosophy, politics or economics. For nineteen years I have worked in personal-lines insurance, first as a claims handler and for the last eleven as an underwriter, and I know my trade's vocabulary cold: policy wording, schedule, exclusion, loading, excess, conditions precedent, adverse selection, moral hazard, reserving, indemnity, rated groups, subrogation, declinature, and the residual high-risk pool that statute obliges us to maintain for the drivers nobody else will write.

What I have been doing on my own time is chewing over an argument I keep meeting in newspaper columns and podcasts: whether a society should make people bear the costs of risks they chose to run — the motorcyclist without a helmet, the smoker, the person who turned down a cheap preventative treatment and later needed an expensive one. I gather this line of thought has a name, luck egalitarianism; that it turns on a distinction between luck someone opted into and luck that merely fell on them; and that it has forceful critics who call it harsh and defenders who say the critics have misread it. Working through claims all day, I arrived at a slogan of my own before I ever met the term: if it could have been priced, it was chosen; if nobody could have priced it, it happened to you. I want to know whether that slogan is a fair statement of the position or a corruption of it.

So, first: restate the luck-egalitarian position in the vocabulary I actually work in — its central distinction, the reasons offered for it, and the two or three most serious objections raised against it — taking the items one at a time rather than giving me a general gloss.

Then the part I care about most, a residue report. For each item, tell me which of three things happened: it translated cleanly, and the insurance concept and the philosophical claim really are doing the same work; or it translated only with distortion, in which case say precisely what had to be bent to make it fit; or it has no counterpart at all in my vocabulary, in which case say what the missing concept would have to be. And for every residue, give me your verdict on which side the defect lies. Is my framework structurally unable to express something real — an insurance ledger sees rated groups and individual claims, never the shape of a whole distribution, and it always has a bucket of people it declines to cover — or does the difficulty of translating suggest that the philosophical claim was vaguer than it sounded, so that when people say someone deserved their bad luck they are not making a factual claim about the world at all?

Three features of my trade that I suspect will matter, and that I would rather you use than ignore. The statutory high-risk pool, which must accept the risks nobody will write, prices them punitively and funds itself by a levy across everyone else's premiums. Subrogation, under which we pay the innocent claimant first and chase the party at fault afterwards. And the difference between declining a claim for breach — you did an excluded thing — and declining it for non-disclosure — you misdescribed yourself, and nobody chose the loss at all. If these have philosophical counterparts, identify them; if some do not, say so plainly, because that would itself be a finding.

Do not smuggle. It would be easy to describe actuarially fair pricing, announce that this is what the philosophers mean by responsibility, and present the whole exercise as a translation. Where my way of thinking is doing philosophical work of its own rather than carrying someone else's, flag it explicitly, and where you bend a term to make it fit, say that you are bending it.

You may name the thinkers whose views you are restating, but do not give me a reading list, citations, page numbers or publication dates: I cannot check any of them, and I would rather you not guess. Write plainly, for someone with no academic training, but do not simplify the argument in order to be plain. I am not asking to be talked down to.
