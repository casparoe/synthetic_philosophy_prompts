I'm prepping a session for my philosophy of science seminar on discovery and credit, and I want a dialogue I can hand out as a reading — something better than what I can improvise at the board on the day. Write me the dialogue.

The setting: a two-person prize committee, locked in a room, an hour before they have to file a recommendation. This year's prize has to go to one of two labs that announced the same result eleven days apart. Fill in the science however you like — something plausible and non-specific, a catalyst, a bound, an algorithm, your call. But here are the facts the committee is stuck on, and don't fudge them, because the whole argument turns on them. Lab A had the key idea two years ago and then sat on it while running confirmatory work; Lab B didn't have the idea until last autumn, but B's method only makes sense once you have A's framing, and A never thought of it. A announced first. B's result is stronger. Neither lab knew about the other until the week of the announcement. One committee member wants to split it; the other thinks splitting it would be an act of bad faith and wants to give it to one lab outright. They disagree for philosophical reasons, and they know it.

Member one holds that discoveries are functions of the state of a field: given the tools, the data, the questions already in circulation, results are effectively guaranteed to show up, and the near-simultaneity in this year's case is exactly what you should expect. On this view the whole priority institution is a machine for converting moral luck into desert, and the split is the only honest recommendation. Member two holds that discoveries are achievements of particular people, that a field being ripe tells you nothing about whether any given person was replaceable, that A's two years of sitting on the idea was itself a kind of work, and that splitting the prize launders a real asymmetry between the two labs into a feel-good compromise. Give them names, and make sure each of them actually has resources — arguments, not slogans. The second member in particular needs more than "genius matters."

Now the part I really care about, and I want you to build the argument on it rather than nod at it in passing. Have them seperate two claims that get collapsed into one every single time this comes up — in the multiples literature my students will have read, and in the room. Call them the substitution claim and the identity claim. Substitution: would the result have been found within a few years by somebody else, had our two winners never existed? Identity: is what the two labs produced one discovery or two? My own hunch, and I want the dialogue to test it rather than assert it, is that member one needs only substitution and keeps smuggling in identity, while member two has been assuming she needs to deny substitution when the thing she really needs is to deny identity — that what A had and what B had were not the same discovery, so there was nothing to split in the first place. If they notice this, it should change what each of them is arguing for by the end.

Also make them fight about the two historical cases, and make them fight about whether those cases point the same way, because they don't. The calculus business is a dispute about who owned a notation: two men built genuinely different tools, the one who published first is the one whose symbols everybody still uses, and the society's 1712 report was written in secret by the man it vindicated. The 1858 reading is the opposite shape: the contents were near-identical, the backing was wildly uneven — one man had twenty years of pigeon breeding, barnacles, and an essay he'd written in 1844, the other had a fever on a island and a hunch he'd picked up from reading Malthus — and they were bundled together anyway, with results we're still arguing about. I want the committee to disagree over whether those two cases support opposite conclusions about this year's prize, and I want at least one of them to think the historical parallel is doing no work at all.

Ground rules. Neither of them is a strawman, and neither is allowed to win on a technicality. They answer each other's actual points rather than trading prepared speeches — if one makes a move, the other has to say why it fails, not just restate. Each concedes at least one thing that genuinely costs them something. No name-dropping: they may not cite philosophers, sociologists, or historians, and they may not appeal to the fact that science is socially constructed as a way of ending an argument. Around a thousand words, alternating turns, and it must not end in agreement and it must not end with them solving the prize. End with an impasse that has a shape: two or three sentences between them in which they say what the remaining crux is, the one thing they'd have to settle in order to settle everything else.
