I've been going in circles for a while about how much an agent is allowed to trust its own reasoning, and I'd like to find out whether my gut verdicts add up to a principle or whether some of them have to go. No bibliography needed, I just want the reasoning.

Background I'm assuming, tell me if I've got it wrong: Löb's theorem says that if a system proves "if P is provable in me then P," then it proves P outright. So you never get anything *new* out of generally trusting your own proof-finding — that trust is only ever licensed for things you could already prove. That's the wall I keep running into.

My verdicts, roughly in the order I collected them:

1. A calculator I've tested on a few thousand sums whose answers I already knew. It reports a product. Fine, I trust it.
2. My own longhand multiplication of the same product, done twice, same answer both times. Fine, but less fine than 1, because the second run isn't really independent of the first.
3. A proof assistant's tiny kernel checking a big machine-generated proof, where I've actually read the kernel. Fine. This is my favourite kind of fine.
4. That same assistant vouching for itself in the ordinary way — I've never read the kernel or the checker, I just take "the proof was checked" at face value because it basically never gets it wrong. In practice fine, and everyone does it. But by my own lights it should be the same shape as the thing I reject in 6.
5. A friend says "I checked your lemma, it's right." Fine. My confidence in her doesn't rest on anything she says about her own checking — it rests on a track record.
6. My own future self, faster and better-informed, says "I thought about this much harder than you did and the answer is X." Not fine. I don't get to bank that in advance. Yet my *friend's* future smarter self saying X would be fine, and I can't articulate the asymetry except by hand-waving.
7. A chess engine's deeper search confirming the top move from its shallow search. Fine, obviously. Except this is a system trusting its own reasoning about its own reasoning, which 6 says I'm not allowed to do.
8. The toy agent that only acts when it can prove the action is good. I think it's broken, and broken in a way no amount of self-trust fixes: it can't prove its proof-searcher is consistent, so it can't rule out proving that something awful is good, and adding a blanket "my future proofs are trustworthy" rule seems to make it strictly worse.
9. Me declining to think hard about some medical decision on the grounds that "if I think about it I'll talk myself into something and then I'll be committed to it." I feel oddly sympathetic to this. Refusing to run the computation seems sometimes rational, which doesn't fit anywhere in the above.
10. A system reporting "I have a proof that you won't press the button." If I check the proof I'm fine to use it, but then the report added nothing; if I don't check it I can't use it. I think that's not a bug, that's just what the word "proof" costs.

My tentative principle, which I want you to attack: a source of justification is admissible only if my confidence in it bottoms out in something whose reliability I can establish *without running the reasoning in question*. Calculator → tests I ran myself. Kernel → my own reading of it. Friend → track record. Future self → nothing, because the only evidence available about future-me's reliability is future-me's reasoning. Call it well-foundedness: no loops in the trust graph.

Known problems, which you should push on: it seems to condemn 4 and 7, which I want to keep; it might condemn trusting induction or testimony generally, which is way too much; and 9 looks like a different subject entirely — not trusting computation but refusing to compute — and I don't know if a principle about the first should say anything about the second.

So: is there a principle, mine repaired or yours, that gets all or nearly all of these? I'd rather have one clean principle that overturns a verdict or two of mine than a baroque one that saves everything, but show me what the baroque version costs so I can compare. And tell me what your principle says about a case I haven't listed — ideally one where an agent has to decide whether to accept a modification to its own goals, since that's where I actually started and I chickened out of writing it down.

Format: prose first, stating the principle clause by clause with the case that motivates each clause. Then a JSON array, one object per case, with exactly these fields: "case" (short label, e.g. "kernel"), "my_verdict" ("fine" / "not fine" / "mixed"), "principle_verdict", "agrees" (true/false), "clause" (which clause of your principle does the work, a few words), "cost" (what dropping this verdict would cost me, or null if your principle keeps it). Then the new case as one extra object with "case": "new". Then a single line naming which of my verdicts you'd force me to give up and why.
