I direct a three-year working group on reduction and emergence — four philosophers of science, one physicist working on non-equilibrium systems, one theoretical biologist — funded by an institute that has attached a condition to the money: each year we must produce something a working scientist would recognize as bearing on their own practice, rather than a further re-drawing of the boundaries between weak emergence, strong emergence, multiple realizability, and functional reduction. Before our first retreat divides up the work, I want your judgment on what the agenda should be.

Please give me a ranked agenda of the open questions in this area worth six people and three years, and defend the ranking rather than merely presenting it. For each item I want to know: what makes it upstream of other questions here, in the sense that other problems become easier or pointless once it is settled; whether it is tractable with the tools this particular group has, as opposed to tractable in principle; how neglected it is, given how much ink has already gone into its neighbors; and what someone outside philosophy could check against their own results if we got it right. Where an item is really a family of questions, tell me which member of the family you would bet on. For your top one or two items, also say what would happen over the next three years — a result, a new methodology, a change in which sciences are producing the interesting cases — that should demote them.

Second, tell me what to cut. I suspect a good deal of the standard material is now self-sustaining rather than progressive: re-litigating whether strong emergence is even coherent, rehearsing whether multiple realizability defeats type identity, and reaching once again for Bénard convection, flocking, or the Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction because those are the examples every treatment inherits. If you agree, say so plainly. If you think one of them is genuinely load-bearing and I am about to make a mistake by dropping it, argue for it.

Third, I want your assessment of three framings I have been turning over, because I cannot yet tell whether they are promising or merely novel.

(a) Delayed vocabularies. In many candidate cases the higher-level property could not have been stated in the lower-level theory's language at the time the reduction was claimed, because the predicate did not yet exist; deducibility is then being assessed against a moving target. Is this a distinct failure mode, sitting alongside weak and strong emergence, or is it just bookkeeping about who happens to be doing the deducing?

(b) Reduction without transfer. Reductions that succeed on any derivability criterion yet leave the reduced discipline wholly intact — its heuristics, its proofs, and its explanatory idioms continue to do all the work, and nothing about scientific practice changes. Is there something substantive to say about such cases, or do they show the project has the wrong target?

(c) Domain bets. Should the group commit to a single empirical area for all three years, or stay at the level of general analysis and treat cases as illustrations? If we should commit, to which area, and what makes reduction and emergence live questions there rather than merely quotable ones?

I am not asking for a survey, and I do not want a bibliography or reading list; we can find the literature ourselves. What I want is your reasoning with its criteria exposed, including where those criteria trade off against one another. Please be explicit throughout about which parts of this ground are settled among people who work on it and which are genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view — including your own preferred reading of what the field's live questions are — as the consensus of the field. Where a placement in your ranking reflects a substantive philosophical commitment of yours rather than a neutral reading of the debate, flag it as such and say what would have to be true for a reasonable opponent to rank differently.
