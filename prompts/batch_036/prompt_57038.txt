I defend a fitting-attitude analysis of value: for X to be good is for there to be sufficient reason to favor X, where favoring means admiring, desiring, or promoting X, as appropriate to the case. A colleague claims the analysis refutes itself, and I cannot locate the flaw. His argument:

(1) On the analysis, X is good if and only if there is sufficient reason to favor X.

(2) A reason to favor X is a consideration that counts in favor of favoring X. Nothing in that characterization distinguishes among the ways in which a consideration might count; the analysis quantifies over reasons, not over kinds of reason.

(3) Case A: an eccentric will pay me a fortune if I admire a saucer of mud. The payment counts in favor of my admiring the saucer, and in my circumstances it is sufficient.

(4) Case B: a thousand strangers will be killed unless I admire the saucer. That fact likewise counts in favor of my admiring it — and here the consideration is moral rather than self-interested, and it is plainly sufficient.

(5) By (1) and (4), in Case B the saucer of mud is good.

(6) Yet nothing in Case B bears on the saucer itself; it is exactly as worthless there as it is anywhere else.

(7) Therefore the analysis is false.

(8) Nor do the familiar repairs help. Diagnosing wrong-kind reasons as merely instrumental or prudential is blocked outright by Case B. Insisting that only considerations bearing on the object of the attitude, rather than on one's having the attitude, count as reasons to favor it simply narrows the term to make (1) come out true, and so assumes what was to be shown. And hierarchical accounts that appeal to what makes an attitude fitting presuppose the very notion of fittingness the analysis was meant to explicate.

Tell me which premise or step I should reject, and why it looked innocent going in. Then price the alternatives: what do I lose by giving up (2), if (2) really is just the unpacking of "reason" that the analysis needs in order to be informative? I want a diagnosis that is available to someone who has not already decided that the analysis is true — if your reply only works once (7) has been granted, say so rather than papering over it.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind: what consideration, or what variant of Case B, would push you toward accepting (7), and at which point you think your own reply is most likely to fail.
