I am drafting the requirements section of a specification for an automated allocation system that must rank proposed interventions, including proposals whose payoffs are unbounded — some promising benefits of astronomical size at probabilities so small that the system cannot usefully represent them. Before I survey candidate decision rules, I want the requirements themselves settled. The deliverable I want from you is therefore not a verdict on the candidates but a specification of what any of them must achieve.

Write the list of desiderata that a theory of choice under unbounded payoffs must satisfy to count as solving the St. Petersburg problem, where the theory is to serve this purpose: producing a determinate ranking that a bounded agent can actually apply, in cases the agent cannot decline to decide. For each desideratum, state the consideration or the case that motivates it, and mark whether you take it to be non-negotiable or to be a commitment of a particular camp — bounded utility, truncation of small probabilities, risk-weighting, growth-rate maximization, the denial that expectation is the right criterion, and so on.

Pin the requirements down against the following five prospects, which your list must collectively handle or explicitly disqualify. In each, a fair coin is flipped until heads first appears on toss n, and the payoff is as given.

1. The classic gamble: $2^(n−1). The expectation diverges because every term contributes 1/2.
2. $2^(n−1)/n. The expectation diverges again, now as a scaled harmonic series — the same verdict reached by a different route, with payoffs that grow more slowly.
3. (−1)^(n−1) · 2^n / n dollars: a gamble of the sort Nover and Hájek introduced as the Pasadena game. Its expectation terms form the alternating harmonic series, which converges to ln 2, but only conditionally, so the very same outcomes rearranged can be made to sum to any real number one likes. Your list must say what is demanded of a theory confronting a prospect whose value depends on the order in which its outcomes are enumerated.
4. As in 3, except that heads on the first toss pays $3 rather than $2. This prospect pays at least as much as 3 in every possible outcome and strictly more in one. Any list that permits a theory to rank it below 3, or to declare the comparison undefined while still claiming to guide action, should be flagged — and identify which desideratum the failure violates.
5. A single-shot prospect whose payoff is so large and whose probability so small that the agent cannot compute the product with any confidence, though a proposer can always name a pair whose product exceeds any bound the agent has so far accepted — the structure exploited in Pascal's-mugging cases.

Two further matters belong in the specification, and your list should take a position on both. First, say which distinctions an adequate solution must keep apart and which it may collapse: for instance, the value of a prospect versus the price an agent should pay to enter it — and add any others you think the surrounding literature has blurred. Second, state whether the output "this prospect has no determinate value" is admissible for an adequate theory. If it is, my system has no rule for prospects 3 and 4, and I need to know whether that gap is fatal or honest.

Also tell me whether it is a desideratum that the theory explain what real agents actually pay for prospects of this kind, or whether that datum is beside the point, and why.

Finally, identify any subset of the desiderata you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, and explain why the tension is substantive rather than a matter of phrasing — if in some region the list is itself an impossibility result, that is the part I most want to see. Say which single desideratum does the most work in eliminating candidates. Score the main families of solutions against the list only briefly, at the end.
