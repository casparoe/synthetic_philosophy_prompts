I'm sitting in on a decision theory seminar this term, mostly for fun, and a couple weeks back we did the standard example of the driver who can't remember which intersection she's at. I built my own version to annoy the TA and it backfired — he wasnt much help, and now I can't tell whether I've got a good argument or a broken one. Here's the version. Marta is driving a one-way service road with two unmarked turnoffs. Turning off at the first is worth 0 (construction, dead end); turning off at the second is worth 8 (her depot); blowing past both is worth 2 (she loops home empty). She's been awake about thirty hours, the two turnoffs are visually identical, and she knows both that she won't be able to tell them apart and that she won't remember whether she's already passed one. Standing at a turnoff, she reckons she's at the first or the second with credence 1/2 each. Turning is then worth 4. Going straight is worth 2 — because if she's at the first she'll arrive at the second in exactly the same epistemic state and, being the same person with the same information, make the same call, so she'll blow past both. So she turns, and ex ante that plan is worth 4. Her wide-awake twin, who can tell the turnoffs apart, drives straight past the first and turns at the second, and gets 8.

Now the argument I built out of that, which is supposed to be a reductio and which I've started to suspect is one, just not of what I meant:

(1) What an agent rationally does at a moment is whatever maximizes her expected utility given the information she has at that moment.
(2) A plan is rationally adoptable by an agent only if she'll still find it rational to carry out at every point where she has to carry it out; a plan she'll rationally drop when she gets to it isn't a plan she'll execute.
(3) So the plans Marta can rationally adopt are exactly the ones that are turnoff-optimal.
(4) Since turning is worth 4 and going straight is worth 2, "turn at whatever turnoff I reach" is the unique turnoff-optimal plan, and it's worth 4 ex ante.
(5) Her wide-awake twin faces the same road and the same payoffs, and for her "straight at the first, turn at the second" is turnoff-optimal at both, and worth 8.
(6) So the whole four-unit gap between the two Martas comes from exactly one thing: at the moment of choice, Marta can't tell which choice she's making.
(7) And that generalizes. Whenever an agent ends up with less than she could have had, the shortfall traces to a failure to discriminate, at the moment of choice, between situations that call for different actions — had she discriminated perfectly she'd have done the right thing in each, and there'd be no shortfall.
(8) So a fully rational agent, one with no shortfall, is one who always knows at each moment of choice which moment of choice she's at. Perfect recall isn't a technical convenience the theory could do without; it's what (1) turns out to demand once you spell it out.
(9) No human being has that. I can't always tell whether this is the first or the second time I've been asked the same question in a meeting, and my grandmother can't tell whether she's taken her pill.
(10) So no human being is a rational agent — not "somewhat irrational," but outside the theory's jurisdiction, the way a rock is outside the jurisdiction of chess.
(11) But (10) followed from (1) plus unremarkable facts about human memory. If (1) is the core of expected utility theory — and I don't know what else would be — then that theory isn't a normative theory of human choice at all. It describes a creature that can't exist, and its only available verdict on every actual person is "irrational," which isn't a verdict but a misuse of the word.
(12) Therefore (1) is false.

Two things before the ask. First, I know someone will go for the 1/2. Somebody in the group objected that what Marta ought to believe about which turnoff she's at depends on what she's planning to do at turnoffs, and I couldn't follow the rest of it. I don't think it matters here: she turns for any credence above 1/4, and the gap in (6) is positive for any credence below 1, so the argument goes through with any number you like in between. If you think it dies there anyway I want to hear why, but I'd rather not be handed that exit, since it feels like the cheap one. Second, I ran (7) on a case that doesn't feel paradoxical at all and that's what's really bugging me. My grandmother doesn't know whether she's already taken her morning pill. She should work out the odds of a double dose against the odds of skipping a day and act on them — that's just (1), and nobody would say she's outside the theory. But she's also failing to discriminate, and by (7) she's bleeding value, and by (8) she's a defective rational agent. So either (1) and (7) come apart somewhere and I can't see where, or the pill and the turnoff are the same case and I owe an account of why one feels like plain good sense and the other feels like a reductio.

Where I want to end up, if I can get there at all: keeping (1), because I genuinely don't know what else "rational" could mean, and not having to say my grandmother is a rock. I have no attachment to (2) — it's always smelled like a stipulation to me — though I'm not sure dropping it rescues anything, since Marta can't seem to execute a plan that says different things at two turnoffs she can't tell apart, no matter how sincere her commitment. So: which step do you actually reject, why did it look innocent going in, and what does rejecting it cost me? And if your view is that the argument is valid and the only question is which plausible premise is cheapest to give up, say that instead and price the options.
