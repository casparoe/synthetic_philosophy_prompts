I'm in a reading group on the epistemology of markets — half econ grad students, half philosophy people who wandered in — and last week I tried to argue that the market is systematically underpricing something (a regulatory risk facing a company that a couple of us own shares in; I'll leave it vague becuase it isn't really the point). One of the econ guys cut me off with "if that were true, it would already be in the price," and the rest of the hour was basically me repeating my argument and him repeating that sentence, and I left feeling like we'd talked past one another without either of us knowing where the gap was.

Here's my problem: I'm pretty sure the sentence isn't meaningless, but I also think it's doing at least four or five different jobs depending on who says it, and some of those jobs can't all be done at once. Can you take it apart for me? I want the distinct precise claims it could be expressing, ranging from the ones that are trivially true or maybe just definitional — something like "prices are where public information ends up," which I'm not sure says anything at all — up to the strong epistemic reading on which the price is literally a better guide to the truth than my own reasoning, and on which the correct response to my having an argument is to conclude my argument is wrong somewhere and go find where. For each reading, say what has to be true for it to hold: assumptions about who is trading, what they already know, what it costs to dig information up, and whether the person on the other side of the trade has different beliefs from mine or just different reasons to want cash right now. And for each, give me a verdict — plausible, false, or true only in a way that would not have helped him at all that afternoon.

Three things I especially want you to push on. First, the self-undermining worry: if enough people believe the sentence it seems like it should stop being true, since nobody would bother finding out the stuff that's supposed to already be reflected in the price — is there a version of the slogan that survives that circularity, and a version that clearly doesn't? Second, the slide between "priced in" and "correct": there's obviously a gap between a price being the best estimate available to anyone and a price being right, and I suspect he was moving between those two without noticing. Third, when is the sentence a legitimate way to end an argument, as opposed to a dressed-up way of saying "you're not special"? I ask because I've used it myself to swat down people's stock tips and it felt obviously correct in that setting, so I'd like to know what's different about those two cases. Finish by telling me which reading, if any, I should have conceded to on the spot, and what I could have asked him that would have forced the readings apart in front of everyone. Prose only please — no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables — just write it the way you'd email a friend, taking the readings one after another in paragraphs.
