I'm a postdoc in a small cognitive science group. Last term I piloted an analogy task I'd built for a modeling project, and the results set off an argument between two colleagues that I was never able to settle: Nadia, who builds Copycat-style architectures (slipnet, workspace, codelets, the parallel terraced scan), and Owen, who works on methodology and the philosophy of psychology and is by temperament a deflationist. I'll describe the task first, since I want their reasoning checked against what the data actually were rather than against the usual textbook gloss, and then the argument. Please take no side by default; I genuinely don't know which of them is closer to right.

The microdomain is mine, built in the spirit of the classic letter-string puzzles but not one of them. A string is a row of one to four flags, read left to right. Each flag has two independent features. Its color is crimson, amber, or teal, arranged in a cycle in that order, so the successor of crimson is amber, of amber is teal, of teal is crimson, and the predecessor relation runs the other way. Its pattern is plain, striped, or dotted, also a cycle in that order, but with one deliberate asymmetry: dotted is terminal, so a dotted flag has no pattern-successor, in the way that there is nothing after the last letter of the alphabet. "Advance the pattern" means move one step along the pattern cycle; "advance the color" means move one step along the color cycle.

Every participant saw the same base pair: crimson-plain, amber-plain, teal-plain maps to crimson-plain, amber-plain, teal-striped. Note that the base admits several descriptions that all fit it equally: the flag that changed was the rightmost one, and also the teal one, and also the one that completes a run through the color cycle in forward order; and what happened to it can be described either as "its pattern advanced" or as "it became striped." Item 1's target was crimson-plain, teal-striped, amber-plain. Here the descriptions diverge. Of 40 participants, 24 gave crimson-plain, teal-striped, amber-striped, which is what you get by advancing the pattern of the rightmost flag; 11 gave crimson-plain, teal-dotted, amber-plain, which is what you get by advancing the pattern of the teal flag; 3 gave crimson-striped, teal-striped, amber-plain, which is what you get if you notice that the target's colors walk the color cycle backwards — crimson, then teal, then amber — and transfer the change to the flag that starts that backwards walk; the other 2 answers were idiosyncratic. I should add, since it matters to the argument below, that the instructions always named color before pattern and the flags were always presented left to right, and I never counterbalanced either.

Item 2 kept the same base pair but used the target amber-dotted, teal-dotted, crimson-dotted, where the description that drove the majority answer on item 1 is blocked outright. Here 30 of 40 gave amber-dotted, teal-dotted, amber-dotted — the rightmost flag's color advanced, patterns untouched; 6 gave amber-dotted, teal-dotted, crimson-striped, reversing the operation on the same flag; 2 gave amber-dotted, teal-dotted, deleting the rightmost flag; the rest scattered.

The argument went roughly like this.

(1) Nadia said item 1 shows what a concept's shape looks like from the outside: "rightmost" plus "advance the pattern" is the default mapping, and the 24/11/3 split is its graded periphery showing through in the response distribution. Owen said a frequency table of guesses is not a picture of a concept: the split is manufactured by the wording of the instructions, by which feature people happen to encode first, and by presentation order, and if you re-ran the same item with the features named in the other order the numbers would move while nothing about concepts had changed.

(2) Nadia said item 2 is the real evidence, because there the slip has a direction rather than a location: 30 of 40 kept the position and moved to the other feature dimension, instead of reversing the operation or destroying an object, and that ordering is what a spreading-activation account predicts — under pressure the mapping slips to the nearest available concept, not to an arbitrary one. She compared it to the familiar letter-string case in which the element the obvious rule targets has no successor, and people who initially give the blocked answer end up preferring the one that applies the inverse operation to the element at the other end of the string: once the blockage is seen, the odd answer feels obviously right.

(3) Owen said that is redescription, not prediction. Whatever people answer, you can draw the neighborhood map afterwards so that the observed answer counts as the nearest available concept — advance, reverse, delete, and switch dimension are all neighbors of something. Unless the distances in the slipnet are pinned down independently of the response data, the account cannot be wrong.

(4) Nadia said it can be wrong, because the model commits before seeing the item: codelet priorities and activation structure are fixed once, so the distribution over answers on held-out items is a genuine prediction, and our replication on a dozen new items tracked the model's distribution closely enough that she would stake the account on it. Owen said "closely enough" is doing all the work, and that the temperature schedule, the codelet selection rule, and the stopping condition are free parameters, so a model that reproduces twelve response distributions has been fitted, not confirmed.

(5) At this point the argument changed level. Owen said that even granting everything, "concepts are fluid" is a confused way of stating a fact about the task: the base pair underdetermines the mapping, many completions are equally licensed, and people simply pick among them, so the looseness is in the puzzle and nothing slips in the mind. Nadia said relocating the looseness to the task leaves the asymmetries unexplained — some equally licensed completions strike people as better than others, and people revise their answers once they notice the blockage and then find the revision compelling rather than arbitrary, which a view on which all licensed completions are on a par cannot capture. Owen's closing move was that compellingness is just fluency: the completion requiring the least revision of the initial parse feels right, so the normative ordering Nadia appeals to is processing cost in a nice hat.

Please adjudicate this move by move rather than giving an overall winner. For each of (1) through (5), say who is right about that specific point, what they get wrong even when they're right, and how confident you are. Then answer four questions directly: does Owen's instruction-and-order objection to the frequency data land against my pilot as actually run, or does the general conclusion he draws from it overreach? Is the redescription charge in (3) fatal to a slipnet-style account, and what would an independently fixed neighborhood structure have to look like in order to answer it — concretely, what would have to be settled before the data came in? Is (4) a fair criticism of a process model's replication, or does it demand a standard that no model of this kind could ever meet? And in (5), is Owen right that the looseness belongs to the task rather than to the concepts — if both readings can be made precise, what would distinguish them, and is there any part of their exchange where they are simply using "concept" at two different levels and talking past each other? Finish with what you think both of them are missing, and with the smallest change to the pilot design that would make their dispute decidable by data rather than by temperament. No bibliography and no reading list, please — I'll supply references myself, and I'd rather have the adjudication than a survey.
