I design and operate automated market maker pools for a decentralized exchange. Constant-product curves, concentrated liquidity ranges, fee tiers, slippage, impermanent loss, total value locked, and routing across pools are my entire working vocabulary, and I am fluent enough in it that none of these terms needs explaining to me. I have no training in classical philosophy. But I repeatedly encounter people invoking Aristotle's hostility to making money out of money — his distinction between managing a household and the boundless art of acquisition, his claim that coin exists by agreement rather than by nature, his insistence that a thing's use differs from its value in trade, and his charge that interest is the most unnatural way of getting wealth because it makes currency generate more currency instead of doing the work it was invented for — as though this were an objection to the systems I build. I want to test that claim properly rather than dismiss it.

Restate Aristotle's position on money and exchange in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can manage. Where the natural counterpart is fee accrual to liquidity providers, use that; where it is a lending market or a yield-bearing stablecoin, say so instead; where it is the fact that a pool's price is nothing but a ratio held together by the consent of whoever trades against it, say that. Do not select whichever mapping makes him look most foolish, and do not select whichever makes him look most wise.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Which of his claims translate cleanly into pool mechanics? Which translate only by being bent, and precisely what gets bent — for instance, if a claim about what a thing is *for* has to be rendered as a claim about what maximizes a quantity, say so explicitly rather than hiding the substitution. And which have no counterpart at all in a framework built from curves, depth, fees, and realized returns? For each of those, tell me whether the absence is a limitation my framework could in principle repair by adding machinery, or a sign that the claim was never doing the work it looked like it was doing.

I hold a standing hypothesis, and I want it attacked rather than accommodated: that everything Aristotle is really saying reduces to a complaint about rent extracted without productive contribution, and that a pool earning fees from trades it intermediates is exactly the case that defeats him, since the return is payment for a service rendered rather than money breeding money. Tell me whether that hypothesis survives the translation intact, or whether it only survives because the distinction between productive and unproductive return is something I have imported from him and then congratulated myself for deriving. Flag every point at which you suspect the translation is working only because you have described my system in his terms rather than in mine.
