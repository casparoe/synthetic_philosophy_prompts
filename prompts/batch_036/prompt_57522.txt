I work in the secretariat of a national equality commission — small country, just call it the Commission — that issues non-binding opinions on whether proposed quota schemes in public and publicly funded institutions square with our equality statute. The statute is maddeningly short: it permits measures aimed at substantive equality so long as they are appropriate, proportionate and time-limited, and then it stops. Ten years of opinions have built up a body of precedent, but the Commission has never written down the rule its actually running, so every new file restarts the same argument from scratch. I have to draft a note for Thursday and I want to know what we should say to be consistent with ourselves rather than with our politics.

Our record so far, compressed as best I can:
1. Approved a floor requiring roughly two fifths of seats on state-owned company boards to go to each gender, enforced by a hard sanction and a two-year compliance window. No end date.
2. Rejected reserving a third of promotions into the senior civil-service grade for members of a long-excluded group, on the ground that entry-level posts for that group were already reserved and the shortfall at the senior grade had largely closed.
3. Approved a rule requiring party candidate lists in closed-list elections to alternate genders all the way down.
4. Rejected a municipal fire brigade's plan to reserve a quarter of entry posts for residents of two named districts, even though applicants from those districts passed the same physical test at the same rate as everyone else, because the plan had no end date and no review.
5. Approved with conditions a scholarship fund for first-generation university students, capped at 15% of the fund, reviewed every five years against whether the gap had closed.
6. Rejected a conservatoire's proposal to reserve 20% of its places for applicants from the poorest fifth of postcodes, where admission is by audition, saying the measure would interfere with assessing the very thing the institution exists to select for.
7. Approved a tie-break: where two candidates land within one point of each other on the same exam, preference goes to the one from the group underrepresented at that grade, and the rule lapses automatically the moment representation reaches parity.
8. Rejected a requirement that employers above fifty staff reserve 5% of all vacancies for people with disabilities, enforced by a levy, noting that a levy lets an employer buy its way out so the reserve wasnt binding on anybody.

The new file. A public medical school, funded by us, admits through a single national exam that ranks every applicant. It wants to reserve 25% of its places for applicants who grew up in three regions that are badly under-doctored. Those regions supply about 24% of the applicant pool but hold 8% of the places, and their applicants score within a couple of points of the national median on that same exam. The school's stated aim is not compensation for anything anyone did to those regions — it's that graduates from there go back and practise there, and it wants to attach a bond that waives tuition for reserved-place students who take up a post in the region within five years of qualifying. There's a seven-year sunset with a mandatory review at year four. The people displaced are individually ranked applicants who would otherwise have had a place, and one of them has already written to us; separately, applicants from a fourth region that is served just as badly but is not on the list say they've been left out arbitrarily.

So, what should we say? I want (a) the candidate rules that are each consistent with all eight precedents — I can think of a "was there identified discrimination by the state" rule, an "is the shortfall unexplained among people who have already cleared the same bar" rule, a "does it end" rule, and something about how many named individuals get displaced, but tell me which of those genuinely survive the whole list and build better ones where mine don't; (b) for each surviving rule, what it predicts for the medical school file, as a table so I can see exactly where they come apart; (c) your recommendation, with the single closest precedent named and an honest account of what has to be distinguished to get there; and (d) whichever way we go, the principle we would be committing ourselves to, phrased so that we couldn't quietly reverse it next year without someone noticing. Also tell me which of the eight is the outlier we'll eventually have to explain away, and give me one further hypothetical file — concrete enough that I could actually table it at a meeting — that would split the surviving rules from each other. My colleague Marta thinks it's a clear approve because of the sunset and I think she's underweighting #6, but I could be wrong about that as well. No need for foreign case law or a reading list, just the reasoning worked through.
