I cover neuroscience and ideas for a magazine, which mostly means consciousness, which means I've ended up with a small stable of regular sources. One of them is a computational neuroscientist who's a fully signed-up partisan of integrated information theory — not a curious bystander, a believer. She talks about phi, intrinsic cause-effect power and the exclusion postulate the way other people talk about their kids. I've interviewed her four times in three years and I'm about to call her about a new result, and I want to guess her answers before I do, partly so I can tell when she's giving me the theory and when she's giving me herself.

Her track record, roughly in her own terms as I've got them in my notes:

- A single-pixel photodiode, she says, has "a speck of something" — a tiny bit of experience — while a 12-megapixel camera sensor has none, because that's a million independent little sensors and not one subject. Discriminative power isn't the point.
- The cerebellum holds most of the brain's neurons and, in her telling, contributes almost nothing, because it's modular and feedforward: lots of copies of the same circuit, very little irreducible whole.
- Under propofol or in deep sleep the brain is still doing stuff but there's nobody home. She leans hard on her lab's pertubation measure — you hit the cortex with TMS and score how complex and spread-out the echo comes back. No complex echo, no experience.
- Ask her about split-brain patients and she'll go further than most people will: the whole's phi falls below the parts', so for some purposes there are two of them.
- Degrees are real for her. Mouse: yes, less than us. Honeybee: yes, much less. Nematode: "a sliver, if anything."
- Current large language models: no, firmly — but she's given me a different reason each time. Once it was that a forward pass through a big model is essentially feedforward and between tokens theres nothing happening at all. A year later it was that whatever the software looks like, the physical machine doing the computing is a serial digital computer and that system has essentially no phi of its own. On a panel she got asked whether a heavily recurrent network would change the answer, and she said it depends on the physical system rather than the software — "an empirical question."
- Cortical organoids in a dish: yes, but "almost nothing there, a million neurons on a plate isn't a subject."
- When I put the standard objection to her — that on the theory's own math a big enough array of simple logic gates doing something like error correction would come out with more phi than a human brain — she said she wasn't going to pretend she's happy about that implication, and left it there. She never said whether she accepts it.

Now the new thing. A group has built three objects, put out a preprint, and I'm calling her about all three:

1. An analog neuromorphic chip — actual physical silicon, no clock, no software — whose units' dynamics reproduce a slice of recurrent cortical activity.
2. A bit-exact software emulation of that same chip on a GPU cluster. The authors claim it's dynamically identical, and they've applied simulated pulses to it and report getting back the same kind of complex, differentiated-but-integrated echo that the perturbation measure scores highly in awake humans.
3. As a control, a much bigger array of the same basic units wired up to compute an error-correcting code. It does nothing interesting and has no behaviour worth mentioning, but by their own phi calculation its integrated information dwarfs both of the others.

What does she say about each of the three? Give me her most likely verdict and her most likely stated reason for each. Then do the part I actually care about: work out which rules her track record is compatible with. State them properly, in the theory's own machinery — is she tracking irreducible cause-effect power in whatever physical system is actually there; is she tracking recurrent, integrated architecture regardless of what it's made of; is she really running the perturbation measure as the criterion; is there a wetware preference hiding underneath all of it — and for each say which of her past verdicts it leans on and which ones it strains to explain. Then tell me which rule you'd bet on for the chip-vs-emulation pair and why, and whether object 3 changes your read on what she's actually running.

And: is she already contradicting herself, or does one rule cover everything she's said? If there's a real inconsistency in there I want to know precisely which two remarks can't both survive, because that's the story. Finish with the single follow-up question I should put to her — the one where each candidate rule gives a different answer, so her response tells me which rule she's on. No need to point me at papers or give me a reading list, I just want the reasoning.
