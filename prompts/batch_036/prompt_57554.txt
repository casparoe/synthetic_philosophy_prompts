I spent most of last night arguing with a friend of mine — he is training in pain medicine, I work in philosophy of mind — about pain asymbolia, and I came away dissatisfied with the way I defended myself. We both accept the broad shape of the classic cases: patients with damage in the region of the insula and the anterior cingulate who report, on being pinpricked or shown a noxious stimulus, that they do feel the pain, that they can localize it and describe its quality accurately, but that it simply does not bother them; who show no distress, who do not learn to avoid the stimulus across trials, and who will calmly submit to a repetition of something they earlier called painful. His conclusion was that this dissociation shows the badness of pain to be a detachable add-on to the sensation, something the nervous system can strip away while leaving the sensory event intact, and therefore that unpleasantness is not part of what pain is but something that happens to accompany it. I think that inference is far too quick, but every time I tried to say why, he had a response ready, and I want to rehearse against the strongest version of his side before I write any of this down.

So I would like you to play a committed defender of the following view about unpleasantness, and to hold it across the whole exchange. On this view, what makes a pain unpleasant is not a further felt quality layered on top of a neutral sensory event; it is that the experience itself has a commanding, action-directing character — it is, so to speak, an occurrent demand issued to the subject that a certain part of the body be protected or a certain activity ceased. Unpleasantness just is that demandingness, and there is no additional item to be separated from it. You are sharp and fair: you may concede that self-report is genuine evidence, that the classic asymbolia cases are a real embarrassment you have to explain rather than explain away, and that 'demand' comes in degrees and can be faint or muted. You may not concede that an experience can be fully unpleasant while carrying no aversive force whatsoever, since that would be to abandon the position. Argue from the view's own resources — from what demandingness could consist in, and from how a report might misdescribe or half-describe an experience — rather than from who has said what; do not name philosophers or papers, do not step out of character to summarize the dialectic, and if you need a fact about the case I describe below that I have not given you, ask me for it in character, as my interlocutor would. Keep each reply to roughly a paragraph, so that it reads as a conversation and not a seminar handout, and stay in role until I write 'stop'.

Here is my opening move. Suppose the clinic reports a small series of patients who are, in effect, the mirror image of the classic cases; call them 'reversed asymbolia' patients. Their sensory capacities are intact, their nociception is normal, and unlike the asymbolics they give entirely negative verbal reports: on a calibrated noxious stimulus they rate the unpleasantness at eight or nine out of ten, they describe the sensation as awful in ordinary language, and they say that they very much want it stopped. Yet nothing else follows. Their faces are neutral, they do not guard or withdraw beyond the spinal reflex, there is no autonomic response, they show no operant avoidance learning across trials, and when given a patient-controlled analgesic button they decline to press it — not out of stoicism or misunderstanding, but with the puzzled explanation that pressing it seems like an arbitrary thing to do. Asked to reach back onto the plate that produced the eight-out-of-ten rating, in order to retrieve an object, they do so without hesitation. So: on your account, what is happening in these patients? Either their experiences carry no demandingness, in which case your view is committed to telling a sincere and articulate subject that they are wrong about the most immediate fact there is — and that they have been wrong in a systematic, repeatable way; or their experiences do carry demandingness that has somehow been cut off from every channel through which demandingness could manifest, in which case the notion is no longer doing any explanatory work for you, and you have conceded to my friend exactly what he wants, namely that the felt badness and the motivational grip come apart. Which horn do you take, and what do you say about the button?
