A friend and I spent most of last night on Bell's theorem and the pilot-wave interpretation, and we parted at the usual impasse: each of us persuaded that the other is using one and the same phrase to mean two different things. I am writing because I should like the disagreement settled on its merits rather than by exhaustion. I should say at the outset that I am not looking for references or a reading list — I have read enough of the secondary literature to know that it will not decide this. What I want is an assessment of whether the inference set out below is sound, and, if it is not, precisely where it breaks.

My case, as I put it to him, ran as follows. Bell's theorem shows that no theory can reproduce the observed correlations between spacelike-separated outcomes while also screening those correlations off by a complete specification of what is physically present in their common past. The pilot-wave theory evades the theorem, as he is always glad to remind me, only by letting the velocity of each particle depend on the instantaneous configuration of the entire system. Yet he insists that this does not amount to any physical influence crossing the gap between the particles, because the dependence can never be put to work: systems are always encountered in quantum equilibrium, with configurations distributed as the squared amplitude of the wavefunction, and so the nonlocality, in his formulation, is real but inoperative. When I pressed him further he added that the wavefunction is not, on his preferred reading, a physical field at all but something closer to a law, and he appeared to think this strengthened his position rather than undermining it.

I maintain that the phrase "real but inoperative" concedes the whole argument. The hidden variables of his theory are — and on this point he agrees with me — quantities whose exact values we can neither know nor control. What is nonlocal, then, is a dependence between values that are in principle inaccessible, and a dependence that becomes manifest only relative to what can be prepared and what can be measured. If whether a signal may be sent turns on which distributions we are able to prepare, the constraint in question is a constraint on our epistemic access, not a structural feature of the world. It would follow that Bell's theorem establishes something considerably weaker than is usually advertised: not that nature is nonlocal, but that no epistemic reconstruction of the correlations can be signal-local. Nature may be perfectly local; it is our descriptions that are not.

His reply was that this cannot be right, on account of the result — due to Antony Valentini, I believe — that a system prepared out of quantum equilibrium would permit superluminal signalling, and indeed measurements violating the uncertainty principle. But that seems to me to argue my side. That the possibility of signalling should depend on the possibility of preparation is exactly my point, since preparation is an epistemic notion. A dependence of what is physically possible upon what can be known or arranged is a dependence of physics upon description.

So: which of us is misusing the term hidden variable? Is he entitled to hold that a single theory can be at once epistemic about its hidden variables and physically nonlocal, or is he sliding between two senses of the phrase without noticing? And if the combination is coherent after all, which step in my argument is doing the illegitimate work — the assimilation of the hidden variables to quantities we are merely ignorant of, the move from inaccessibility to unreality, the treatment of preparability as an epistemic rather than a physical notion, or the claim that a law-like wavefunction cannot bear physical relations at a distance? I would much rather be told flatly that one of these is a mistake than be given a survey of the available positions.
