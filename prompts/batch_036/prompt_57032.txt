I'm a junior who helps run my school's history club, and after our meeting on twentieth-century famines two seniors kept arguing in the hallway while the rest of us listened. Neither would give ground. I've tried to reproduce their moves faithfully, in order.

(1) Nadia began from the entitlement analysis of Bengal in 1943: aggregate rice availability that year was not dramatically below normal, yet two to three million people died, so what killed them was distribution and policy — the wartime denial measures imposed on coastal districts, war-economy inflation, and a War Cabinet that repeatedly turned down requests to divert shipping. That, she said, makes it a political crime rather than a natural disaster.

(2) Owen accepted every one of those facts but said "crime" was carrying too much weight. Crime implies intent. The Cabinet was callous, prejudiced in how its members reasoned about India, and grossly negligent, but it did not aim at mass death; and once the word stretches from genocide to a botched relief operation, it stops distinguishing anything.

(3) Nadia answered that this misdescribes criminal law, which has long punished gross negligence and reckless indifference without purpose — only conscious disregard of a grave risk — and that the same government which found shipping for other purposes was reading reports of starvation while it deliberated.

(4) Owen conceded that, then said the concession proved his worry, because the standard now sweeps in most famines under central planning, where the mechanism isn't entitlement failure at all. In the Great Leap Forward there were no markets for entitlements to collapse in; what killed people was a breakdown of information, with local officials inflating harvest figures and the center procuring grain on the strength of numbers that everyone now agrees were invented.

(5) Nadia said this concentrates responsibility instead of diluting it: where the state is the only agent able to move grain, and the misinformation is itself produced by officials competing for favor, there is nobody left to hold accountable except the state.

(6) Owen's final move was that if every famine turns out to be a political crime, the thesis cannot be wrong, and that Nadia was sliding between two different questions — what would have prevented these deaths (institutions, information flows, a free press) and who deserves blame — treating the second as a substitute for the first.

Please adjudicate this move by move: rate each of the six from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification of every rating, and then say who was more right overall. I would also like your view on whether the real dispute was about the meaning of a word, about causal mechanisms, or about something neither of them named, and on what both of them left out.
