I'm in the third year of a philosophy PhD, working on theories of well-being, and the chapter I'm drafting defends a position I'll call tranquilism: on the experiential side of a life, what matters is the absence of suffering and the absence of craving, and positive experience has value only insofar as it quiets wanting or forestalls harm — a life in which nothing hurts and nothing is lacked cannot be improved by adding pleasures, however intense. My supervisor is broadly sympathetic but presses a single image at me in every meeting. Craving, she says, is thirst, and satisfaction is drinking. The goodness of a glass of water is entirely borrowed from the thirst that it answers; to someone who is not thirsty, water is neither good nor bad but merely neutral; and the ideal condition is therefore not continuous drinking but no longer being thirsty. She also likes pointing out that the Pali term usually rendered as craving literally means thirst, which she takes to suggest that the oldest version of this ethic already thought in these hydraulic terms. Her conclusion from the image is that tranquilism is not the bizarre denial of pleasure's value that its critics say it is, but the modest recognition that pleasure's value is relief-shaped: it is always payment against a deficit, never a surplus.

At a workshop last month a commentator turned a different image against me. He said that the thirsty model, taken seriously, makes the tranquilist ideal indistinguishable from sedation: a life without craving looks like a life on a sedative, or like a pond that is perfectly calm only because nothing is alive in it. Either tranquilism cannot distinguish its ideal from a pathological flattening, he argued, or it must concede that contentment is itself a positive felt state doing the real justificatory work — in which case the view collapses back into a hedonism about a particular kind of feeling. I have my own candidate counterexample to the thirst picture, which is the unbidden pleasure: music overheard from an open window that I had no desire whatsoever to hear, or a cool breeze on a day I hadn't noticed being warm. These seem good, and good in a way that isn't the cancellation of any prior lack, and I can't see how to fold them into the hydraulic story.

Please take this on as a philosopher of well-being and of the ethics of desire, and think the mapping through carefully rather than reaching for a verdict — I would rather you began with a short, explicit brainstorm of the candidate points of contact and mismatches before organising anything. You may assume I'm fluent in the standard taxonomy of hedonism, desire-satisfaction, and objective-list accounts, so no exposition of those is needed, and please don't hand me a bibliography or a reading list; I want the argument, not the literature. What I need is, first, the correspondence table for the thirst analogy: what plays the role of thirst, of water, of drinking, of quenching, of not being thirsty, and of the fact that thirst reliably returns — and, importantly, whether "satisfaction" in tranquilism is supposed to map onto drinking or onto the extinction of the thirst itself, since those are different targets. Second, please separate the disanalogies that genuinely threaten the intended use from those that are merely cosmetic, and say which is which and why: for instance, thirst is homeostatic, object-specific and self-limiting in a way craving arguably is not, since craving seems to proliferate and to attach to novel objects; whether that difference damages the analogy or actually strengthens the case for eliminating craving is something I'd like you to argue rather than assume. Third, tell me what features of the source domain the image quietly imports into the target — in particular whether "relief-shaped value" is smuggled in by the choice of a thirst as the example rather than established by it, so that the analogy is doing the arguing instead of illustrating a conclusion reached elsewhere. Fourth, given all of that, does my supervisor's conclusion survive: is tranquilism entitled to present itself as a modest recognitional thesis about the structure of pleasure's value, or does the analogy, once audited, leave the view with the revisionary burden its critics assign it? Fifth, run the same audit in brief on the sedation and still-pond image, and tell me whether the tranquilist can answer it from inside the thirst framework or must change analogies to do so, and whether my unbidden-pleasure case and his sedation case are really the same objection wearing different clothes. Finally, if you judge that neither image will carry this weight, propose a better one and be explicit about what it can and cannot support, and give me one discriminating test case — a scenario on which the rival analogies deliver opposite verdicts — that I could put to a reader to settle which of them is tracking the phenomenon.
