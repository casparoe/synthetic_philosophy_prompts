My novel's whole structure hangs on one conceit: it opens in 1952, first H-bomb test, and every section after that is a layer of sediment above the line. A geologist I know says that's basically right — the Anthropocene is like the boundary that killed the dinosaurs, one global datable marker layer (the plutonium spike they tried to pin at Crawford Lake), everything above it a different world, and people who want to date the thing to farming, or to the 1610 CO2 dip after the Americas emptied of people, just haven't understood how a timescale works. An environmental historian in the same room says that's a category error: the Anthropocene is a ramp, not a spike, more like a fever curve than an impact layer, and the comittee voted the formal proposal down in 2024 partly because the marker is too recent and the record too thin.

Can you audit the impact-layer analogy for me before I commit to it? I want the mapping laid out — what plays the role of the iridium, the extinction, the suddenness, the after — and then the disanalogies sorted into the ones that actually matter for an opening chapter set on a single date and the ones that are just noise. I especially want to know what the analogy quietly imports from the dinosaur case that the Anthropocene doesn't have; my suspicion is something about being external, instantaneous, and finished. And then: does the 1952 line survive carrying the weight I'm putting on it, or is the historian right that it's a threshold nobody ever crossed? If it fails, what's a better analogy I could build the book on instead?
