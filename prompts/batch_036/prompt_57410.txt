So I'm on sabbatical this year and, as punishment for writing op-eds about public memory, I got drafted onto our city's Commemoration Review Panel — nine of us, mostly retirees, one county archivist, and a councilmember who has never once spoken. We take up petitions to remove, rename, or contextualize public honors, and we now have eight decisions behind us. Condensed, but I think fairly:

1. Statue of a Confederate colonel in a park, erected 1911, the same session the state rewrote its voting rules; the city's Black churches petitioned against it at the time and were ignored; his only claim to remembrance is the uniform. Removed — and we specifically voted down keeping it with an interpretive plaque, the chair saying a plaque on that one is just slower removal.
2. Street named in 1953 for a surgeon who in the 1840s operated on enslaved people without consent, which a handful of his contemporaries called monstrous at the time; his technique is still taught and still saves lives. Renaming would have hit about 4,000 addresses, and the residents who'd inherit the paperwork — majority Black, and against it almost unanimously — came to us. Kept the name, markers at both ends naming the people he operated on.
3. 1908 statue of a suffragist who also campaigned hard for Chinese exclusion and said exactly why; that politics had nothing to do with the work she's honored for; erected by a women's club; the local Chinese-American historical society told us they wanted it to stay with real context rather than go. Kept it, funded a proper companion installation.
4. Middle school named in 1962 for a segregationist board member whose entire public record is resistance to desegregation — not a thinker or a builder, just an organizer of delay; the school is now 94% Black; nobody protested the naming in 1962; the alumni association wrote in asking us to leave it alone. Renamed, over the alumni.
5. Bridge named for a railroad man who used armed strikebreakers and people died; legal, routine, and barely remarked on in its day; almost nobody knows the bridge carries his name; the unions asked for a labor-history marker, not a renaming. Marker added, name kept.
6. Courthouse bust of a judge who upheld convict leasing, erected 1936 by the state bar, decades after a federal report had already called the system slavery by another name; his own opinions say the quiet part out loud. Descendants of leased men asked for it to go and the bar agreed. Removed.
7. Park named in 1975 for a pediatrician; in 2019 letters surfaced showing he'd sent two gay patients to conversion therapy in the sixties and written ugly private things about them. The community group that came to us asked for an acknowledgment, not a renaming. Kept the name, issued the acknowledgment; I'm still not sure we did right.
8. Fountain dedicated 1899 to a city founder's wife for her charity hospital work; in 1898 she'd written against vaccinating the "tenement classes," a common view then, which killed nobody traceably; the hospital she built is still a clinic. Kept, no action.

Now the live one, and I've been staring at it for a week. In 1897 a subscription of businessmen put up a monument in a small plaza to the quartermaster who rebuilt the city's water works after the great fire — the cholera numbers before and after arent in dispute, the man plausibly saved thousands of lives, and the system is still in service. He also paid the Irish immigrant laborers who dug it below the going wage, fought any compensation for the families of the eleven who died in the trenches, and wrote about those men in terms published in his own lifetime that nobody then thought disqualified him. The Catholic parishes sent a protest letter in 1897 and it was ignored. The inscription praises him for finishing the works "under budget," and the parishes read that phrase, in 1897, as a boast about the wages. Our local Irish-American heritage society says leave the figure alone, they'd much rather the city build something for the laborers; the labor council wants it out; the plaza is small, removal is cheap, and the water department declines to have an opinion.

What I want from you isn't a vote. I want (a) the three or four candidate rules that each fit all eight precedents — I keep oscillating between something about what the honoring act asserted at the moment it was performed, something about whether the celebrated achievement can be seperated from the wrong it rests on, something about whether the honoree was condemned by his own contemporaries, and something about deferring to the people the harm actually landed on, and I genuinely can't tell which one the panel is running because we haven't disagreed enough yet; (b) what each of those rules says about the quartermaster, so I can see exactly where they come apart; (c) your best single guess at the verdict, plus which of the eight you'd call the closest analog and why; and (d) the one hypothetical case we should invent and slip onto next quarter's docket whose verdict would actually discriminate between the rules, since I have some influence over the agenda and I'd like to spend it. If the record genuinely underdetermines the outcome, say so plainly and tell me what principle we'd be adopting with each option, rather than pretending the precedents decide it for us. And please write in American English — the minutes go out to council and I'm going to paste chunks of this straight in.
