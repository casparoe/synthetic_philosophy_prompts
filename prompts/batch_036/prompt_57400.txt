Please take the role of an economist or philosopher who works at the intersection of information economics and social epistemology — someone equally comfortable with the formal no-trade results and with the older debates about what a price is supposed to know. Begin by working through the argument below and writing out, for every step, the list of assumptions that step quietly needs; use that list to organize everything that follows, and let me see it.

Some context. I am a high school junior and I run my school's economics society. For our spring meeting I wanted to present a tidy argument for the claim that nobody can out-think a market price, so I assembled the following out of popular summaries I had read. The trouble is that the conclusion is far stronger than anything I actually believe, and when I showed it to my faculty adviser she said she could not locate the flaw either but was certain there was one. Here it is:

(1) Consider two traders who begin with the same prior probability distribution over the eventual payoff of a single asset and who revise that distribution only by conditionalizing on whatever information each of them receives.

(2) If a proposed trade between them is not driven by different attitudes toward risk, different existing holdings, or a need for cash, then it must be driven by different posterior estimates of that payoff; otherwise at least one of them would be surrendering something he values for something he values less.

(3) An offer to trade is public in the relevant sense: whoever accepts it knows that it was made, and knows that it would not have been made unless the maker's posterior favored it.

(4) So in accepting, each trader learns something about the other's posterior and updates on it.

(5) Agents who update in this way cannot end up holding posterior estimates that differ and are commonly known to differ — this is the agreeing-to-disagree result, if I have stated it correctly (Aumann, 1976).

(6) If their posteriors cannot commonly be known to differ, then the updating described in (4) can never generate a trade both sides accept; and since nothing in that reasoning depended on which asset or which pair of traders we picked, prices cannot move at all.

(7) Prices move constantly, and sometimes enormously.

(8) Therefore (1) must fail for at least one party to every actual trade: whoever trades is either irrational or misinformed.

(9) Hence a market price is the estimate of whichever participant happened to be right, and anyone who believes she has reasoned her way to a better estimate than the price is, by (8), automatically one of the irrational ones.

What I would like from you:

1. Take (1) through (9) in order and say of each whether it is a genuine theorem, a substantive but defensible assumption, or a slide from one claim to a different claim. Where it is a slide, state the two claims separately and precisely, and say what additional assumption would have been needed to make the step legitimate.

2. Identify the smallest set of steps I would have to reject in order to block the route to (9). For each candidate set, price it: tell me what I lose by abandoning the common prior in (1) as against abandoning the publicity assumption in (3), and whether one of those choices leaves markets looking epistemically remarkable while the other leaves them looking merely convenient.

3. Tell me what survives. I would much rather keep (1) through (5), which I believe are real results, than keep (9), which I do not believe. Is there a weaker and defensible conclusion in the neighborhood — one that distinguishes the kinds of trade the results genuinely rule out from the kinds they say nothing about — that a careful person could actually endorse?

4. Close with something I could put on a slide: a plain-language explanation of why (7) does not carry the destructive weight I gave it, naming the sorts of trades that happen every day in real markets and that (2) and (6) were never designed to cover.

Please be rigorous rather than encouraging, and do not assume more mathematics on my part than one year of AP Statistics — define each technical term the first time you use it, but do not soften any of your conclusions on my account.
