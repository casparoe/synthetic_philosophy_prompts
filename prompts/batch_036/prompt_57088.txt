im teaching a first-year philosophy of art seminar, unit on "what is art" — we've done Dickie's institutional theory, some Beardsley, Levinson's historical account, and a bit of Tolstoy on art as infection. For the last session I want to put Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go on the list, because it increasingly looks to me like the novel is secretly a philosophy-of-art text: the entire justification for Hailsham rests on the claim that the students' drawings and poems prove they have souls.

Could you read the book through the definition-of-art debate for me? Some things I specifically want worked through rather than gestured at:

- The Gallery. Madame takes the best pieces away and hangs them somewhere the students never see. Is that an artworld conferring candidacy for appreciation in Dickie's sense, or is it something else entirely — a courtroom exhibit, an advocacy file? The collecting is purely instrumental (evidence for the outside world), which seems to pull against every aesthetic definition we've covered. Does institutional theory have the resources to handle an institution whose whole purpose isn't appreciation?
- The Sales and the tokens, where the students price each other's work and there's a pecking order to what counts as good. That looks like an artworld in miniature, with its own market and juries. But does any of it count as art if the makers have no standing as persons in the world outside the fence?
- Miss Emily's explanation, late in the book, that the art was taken because it showed the students had souls — paraphrase that, don't put words in her mouth. That's expression theory running the show. Except the reveal is that the expression theory was deployed as testimony in a political campaign, and the campaign lost. Does the novel endorse it, or is it showing what happens when you stake art's worth on its ability to testify about its maker?
- Tommy's animal drawings and the deferral application. Here art is asked to do a job none of our definitions assigns it: certify that two particular people are genuinely in love. Which theory, if any, can make sense of that function, and what does it mean that by the time they try, there's nobody left with the standing to adjudicate it?
- And where my reading breaks. Kathy seems almost completely indifferent to aesthetic quality, and the artworks themselves are barely described. An honest critic could say Ishiguro isn't doing aesthetics at all and I'm projecting. How strong is that objection, and does the book resist being reduced to an illustration of any of this?

What I most want at the end is the thing the novel gives me that the papers don't. My hunch is that every one of these definitions quietly assumes the maker is already somebody — a candidate for appreciation presupposes a candidate for moral standing — and the book is what makes you feel that assumption. If you think that's wrong, or true but not what the novel is doing, say so plainly.

Please close with what would change your mind: which of the claims above you think is most likely me over-reading, what consideration or argument would push you to a different verdict, and flag any scene you're reconstructing from memory rather than actually being confident about. I've got the book, I can check.
