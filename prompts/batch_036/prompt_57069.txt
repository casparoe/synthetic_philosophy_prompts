Our department's speaker series is hosting a philosopher who takes the unfashionable line that the repugnant conclusion is not merely unavoidable but true, and that our horror at it is a prejudice to be outgrown: on her view a sufficiently enormous population of lives that are only barely worth living really is better than a smaller population of excellent lives, and nothing matters but total welfare. Write six or seven questions for the discussion period that put pressure on the position instead of letting her rehearse it. Please include one question on what she says makes a life barely worth living, and on whether those marginal lives must still contain goods like friendship or achievement for her comparison to run at all; one on whether she accepts that enough marginally good lives outweigh the intense suffering of a few, and what resources she has for resisting any lexical threshold; one on the asymmetry between our reluctance to create miserable people and our indifference to creating happy ones, asking whether she is committed to treating the omission of a possible child as a wrong of the same kind as making someone suffer; one on whether she preserves the transitivity of betterness or is pushed toward incommensurability somewhere in her sequence of populations; and one that tests whether her embrace survives contact with an actual decision, such as how much weight to give a vast far-future population in present policy. For each question, supply the answer you expect from her and a follow-up that makes that answer expensive.
