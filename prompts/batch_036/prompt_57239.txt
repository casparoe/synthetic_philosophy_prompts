For twelve years I directed a small biophysics group; I retired this spring and now sit on the board of the society that funds our field. Each autumn the board selects one fellowship recipient, and the custom is that at the open meeting members announce their preferences in order of seniority, each hearing everything already said, with the chair speaking last. This year the two most senior members endorsed a candidate whose work I know well and consider overrated, and a third promptly agreed with them — though she told me afterwards at lunch that she had read none of the dossiers and was simply going by the first two. When my turn came I said nothing, and the choice went through as it was already heading. I had a genuine reason to prefer another applicant: I have reviewed two of her papers and know the substance of what she has done, which nobody else in the room did. I have only lately taken up philosophy and lack the vocabulary, so put it plainly.

Two questions. Was my silence rational or merely comfortable? If deferring to an apparently unanimous panel is just what the evidence in front of me supports, what precisely goes wrong when the unanimity has been assembled rather than gathered — and does that difference put any obligation on me personally, or only on whoever designed the procedure? And what should I do next autumn: speak out of turn, request a written ballot in advance, announce an abstention aloud, or press the chair to have members speak in reverse order of seniority?

Give your verdict on each of those four options as a table with the columns: option; effect on the information later members actually receive; whether it is defensible on epistemic grounds, procedural grounds, or both; principal risk; and recommendation (adopt / adopt only with modification / reject). Then tell me which parts of your answer are settled among people who study this and which remain genuinely disputed — I have no patience for a contested claim presented as consensus.
