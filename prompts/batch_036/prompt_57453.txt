Its a short argument about what Lewis's triviality results actually prove, and I think I'm onto something, but I also know this is exactly the kind of thing where you can talk yourself into a mistake that looks like an insight. Be blunt.

---

The familiar story goes like this. Adams taught us that the probability you ought to give an indicative conditional — if the butler did it, he had an accomplice — tracks the conditional probability of the consequent on the antecedent, rather than the probability of anything. Stalnaker read the same equation the other way round: the conditional does express a proposition, and the equation holds because conditional probabilities just are the probabilities of those propositions. Lewis's triviality results are usually summarised as a refutation of Stalnaker's reading, and the summary then gets cashed out as a general argument that indicative conditionals have no truth conditions at all.

I want to argue that last step is too quick, and that the theorem is really aimed at a third thing nobody mentions: the ratio analysis of conditional credence.

Look at what the shortest derivation actually uses. Suppose conditionals express events in your algebra, and suppose the Adams/Stalnaker equation holds not just for your credence function P but for the functions you get by conditionalizing P. Assume nothing is zero, to keep the notation clean. Split the event A→C at C: its probability is P(C) times P(A→C | C) plus P(¬C) times P(A→C | ¬C). Now apply the equation to the conditionalized functions. Under P(·|C), the conditional probability of C on A is 1, so P(A→C | C) = 1. Under P(·|¬C) it is 0, so P(A→C | ¬C) = 0. Everything collapses to P(A→C) = P(C), which together with the equation says P(C) = P(C|A) for every A whatsoever. Independence of everything from everything. Triviality.

Notice where the work got done. You do need A→C to be an event in order to split it; I'm not claiming otherwise. But splitting alone gets you nowhere. The collapse comes from the two values, 1 and 0, and those come from the fact that P(·|C) and P(·|¬C) are defined as quotients of unconditional credences. On the ratio analysis, that a fully centered conditional credence is 1 is a theorem, not a substantive assumption. That's the engine of the whole argument, and it's inherited from the first page of any probability course rather than argued for anywhere.

So there's a third option besides keep-the-equation-drop-truth-conditions and keep-truth-conditions-drop-the-equation. Drop the ratio analysis. Treat conditional credence as a two-place state that isn't a quotient of one-place states — roughly the picture Jeffrey wanted, where you can have definite conditional probabilities without definite unconditional ones. Then the derivation stalls at the step that needs P(·|C) to be a ratio conditional of P.

Two obvious replies. First: you only need the equation to hold for the actual agent's credences, not for all probability functions, so the universality I'm blaming isn't there to be dropped. But the functions the derivation uses are P(·|C) and P(·|¬C) — the same agent's credences conditional on things she might learn. Restricting to actual agents doesn't obviously buy you anything.

Second: any coherent primitive framework will still validate the centered values, so nothing is gained. This is the interesting one, because it concedes that what's needed is an extra principle linking the two-place state to the one-place state — a centering principle. On a primitivist account that principle is a substantive constraint that can be questioned, not a definitional truth. Deny it and you can have truth-conditional conditionals together with the Adams equation, at the cost of allowing that an agent certain of A∧C might not be certain of C given A.

Is that cost too high? Compare it with what the non-cognitivist pays. The centering denial is a claim about the internal structure of a doxastic state, and we already know that idealized credal states are fictions — nobody's actual attitudes are a single coherent probability function, let alone a closed family of them. Truth-conditionality, by contrast, is a claim about the semantic type of a huge class of ordinary English sentences. Giving that up costs you embedding, negation, disjunction, agreement and denial, and the fact that if A, C seems to be the kind of thing that can be true in the same way A and C is. One of these costs is paid in a currency we already treat as approximate. The other isn't.

My conclusion: triviality is a no-go theorem about a package, and the package quietly includes an assumption about what conditional credence is that does most of the work.
