I would like to know whether a certain structural pattern in theodicy has a name, and, if it does not, what it ought to be called. Let me describe it as I keep running into it, rather than in the vocabulary of the literature.

A theodicy says that God permits some evil E because E is required for a good G that could not otherwise be had — a soul-making good, or the good of a world whose regularities are stable enough to be reasoned about, or the good of creatures whose choices really issue in consequences. Now suppose I am persuaded by the theodicy, and suppose further that I can tell of a particular case in front of me that the theodicy covers it. Then the theodicy seems to tell me to leave that case alone. My intervention would destroy G, and G is precisely what made E permissible; so a person who fully believes the theodicy and recognizes this instance as an instance of it appears committed to standing by, and even to treating his own mercy as a small frustration of a justified plan.

The defenders I have read do not accept that conclusion, and their reply is familiar: we are not God; we cannot see whether G really is obtainable only through E; we cannot survey the whole economy of goods; our office is that of neighbor and physician rather than of designer. So the very reason that licenses God's permission licenses our prevention. Very well — but look at the shape of that reply. It is not simply that the reason is indexed to its holder, the way my reasons concerning my own children are indexed to me. The reason changes direction. Held from God's position it points toward permitting E; held from mine, once I know it, it points toward preventing E; and the reversal is produced by the content of the theodicy together with a difference in knowledge and power, not by any independent principle about the limited role of human beings. What results is a justification that can do its work only for an agent to whom it cannot be addressed: the theodicy exculpates God on terms that, were a creature to come to know them, would condemn that creature's ordinary duties.

Two instances, to mark out what I mean. First: a man is beaten in an alley, and the free-will defense says God permits it because a world in which such choices are genuinely effective is worth more than one in which they are quietly overruled. I am standing at the mouth of the alley. I accept the defense, and I know that this beating is a case it covers. The defense appears to counsel me to keep walking — which no one, least of all its proponents, will say. Second: a landslide takes a village, and the regularity theodicy says that removing this rock would mean removing the dependable order that makes deliberation, science, and promise-keeping possible. I am the engineer who could have built the retaining wall. Again the theodicy I endorse seems to advise against the wall, in exactly the case where everyone agrees the wall is required.

So: does this configuration have an established name or a settled treatment? I have several candidates, and I suspect each is nearby without being it; I would like you to say how close each comes and exactly where it comes apart. Reasons that are agent-relative, or agent-relative permissions. Sidgwick's esoteric morality, where an act is right but it would be bad for people generally to believe it right. Raz's exclusionary reasons, where one reason functions by ruling others out of deliberation. And the general idea of a theory that defeats itself once its content is widely known, or that must efface itself to succeed. I would also like to know how the thing I am describing differs from the older and more rhetorical complaint that theodicies are obscene, or that they console us into acquiescing in the status quo — because my difficulty is not about tone or ideology but about a logical property that the justification acquires when it is transferred between agents.

If none of these is the thing, then coin a term and define it sharply enough that I could apply it as a test: what structural features must a justification have in order to reverse direction across a change of agent? And please give me the edges. I want at least two cases that look as though they have the property but do not — I have in mind something like a deterrent threat, whose force depends on its not being carried out, and something like a parent who arranges a manageable failure for a child's sake and ought not to be second-guessed by a teacher who has learned of the arrangement — with an explanation of why each differs. And I want at least one case entirely outside theology that plainly has the property.

Finally, if the diagnosis survives that test, tell me whether it discriminates among theodicies. My suspicion is that it does: that a defense on which the good consists in the sufferer's own response to the evil, and can survive the evil's being cut short once that response has occurred, escapes the reversal, whereas a defense on which the good consists in the occurrence of the evil itself, or in the maintenance of an exceptionless order, does not. If that is right, say what feature of a theodicy settles which side of the line it falls on; if it is wrong, say so and give me the correct sorting. I am not after a label. I am after the carving, and after a criterion I can apply to the next argument of this kind I meet.
