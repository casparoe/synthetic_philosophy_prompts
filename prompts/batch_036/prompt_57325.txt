I am marking the final examination for a second-year module on the ethics of aggregation, and before I assign a mark I would like an independent assessment of one script. The question, as set, was:

> Taurek argues that when you can save only one of two groups, you are not required to save the larger.
> (a) Reconstruct his argument, identifying the premises it depends on.
> (b) Explain the role the coin toss plays in his view and why he thinks it is called for.
> (c) Assess one of the following replies: (i) that fairness requires giving each individual an equal chance of being saved; or (ii) that the reason to save the many lies not in aggregation but in the fact that some would otherwise be worse off than others.
> (d) Would the argument change if the losses were unequal — five people each facing the loss of a hand against one facing death?

The candidate wrote:

> Taurek's target is the assumption that the value of outcomes is additive across persons. He denies that the deaths of five are five times as bad as the death of one, on the ground that a death is a loss to the person who dies, and there is no subject — no aggregate entity — to whom the combined loss of five could be a loss. Losses, on his account, do not pool. That is why the familiar step from "each of these five has a reason to be saved" to "there is a stronger reason to save the five" fails: the second claim requires a sum that Taurek says does not exist.
>
> It matters that Taurek does not conclude that saving the five is wrong. His claim is that it is not required. Either rescue is permissible, and the coin toss is introduced not as a device for bringing about a better outcome but as a way of showing equal concern for each of the six: it treats each claim as counting for something rather than allowing the many to be absorbed into a total.
>
> For the same reason the toss is fair in the obvious sense — each of the six ends up with an equal chance, one in six, of being saved, which is just what equal respect for persons demands when their claims are equally strong. This also explains what unites the two standard replies to Taurek. Those who argue for equal individual chances and those who argue, like Otsuka and Voorhoeve, that the reason to save the many is that five deaths are a worse state of affairs than one, are making the same objection in different vocabularies: both hold that what ultimately matters is how much harm is prevented overall, and both therefore reject Taurek's denial of summation. If he is right that harms do not sum, both replies collapse; if he is wrong, both succeed. The dispute is consequently a single one, and it is about the structure of value.
>
> On (d), Taurek's argument is untouched by the change in magnitudes. It rests on a claim about the structure of value — that losses belong to persons and cannot be pooled — and says nothing about how large any particular loss is. The coin toss therefore remains the appropriate response even where the one would die and the five would each lose a hand, since the aggregation it refuses is exactly as unavailable there as in the symmetric case.
