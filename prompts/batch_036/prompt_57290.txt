Slightly odd request, and I should probably explain myself. I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine, mostly neuroscience and AI — and out of nowhere my old comprehensive in Sheffield has asked me to give the address at prize day in July. Sports hall, about six hundred people, pupils aged eleven to eighteen in the front rows, parents behind them, staff along the sides. Eight minutes, ten at the very outside.

Here's the wrinkle. Its the last year the school gives out the Character prize. They've decided to drop it — something about not wanting to grade personality — and I've been asked both to say a few things about why the award existed and to hand over the final one. The girl getting it is in Year 10. The citation the head sent me says she is reliable: never in trouble, always gets her work in, helps without being asked, never once seen to loose her temper.

I want to write the speech around the difference between a fink and a mask, and I want to check I've actually got it right, because I'm working from memory of a book I read about fifteen years ago. As I understand it: a disposition is a standing property that shows up only under particular circumstances — the glass is fragile even while it sits untouched on the shelf. A fink is a case where the triggering circumstance arrives and something intervenes to remove the disposition itself at exactly that moment, so the test never happens the way you'd expect; the standard example is a live wire rigged to a device that renders it non-conductive the instant something touches it. A mask is different: the disposition stays exactly where it is, fully intact, but something gets between it and its manifestation and blocks the showing — the fragile glass packed in bubble wrap, which is still fragile, and still gets struck, and doesn't break. Tell me plainly if I've mangled that.

The thing I actually want to say to that room is this: nobody in it has ever met the circumstances that would reveal most of what they believe about themselves. What a school can see and reward is behaviour under a very narrow, very sheltered set of conditions, which means a great deal of what we call good character in a fourteen-year-old is packing material — real, useful, protective, but not the same thing as the glass. And I want that said without turning a prize-giving into a demolition, and without humiliating a kid who is sitting three metres from the microphone having done nothing wrong.

The hard constraints:

- No jargon and no namedropping. Don't use the words disposition, fink or mask as technical terms in front of the eleven-year-olds — the argument has to be carried entirely by ordinary language and one or two images they'd remember next week. But it has to be rigorous enough that a classmate of mine, who now lectures in philosophy in Edinburgh and will be in the fourth row, could reconstruct the real distinction from what I say. She's the reason I'm bothering.
- One joke, and it mustn't be at anyone's expense, including the school's.
- The school's motto is carved over the hall door — a King James sort of phrase about proving all things and holding fast to what is good. I'd like the speech to turn on that, since it's the one bit of the building that's already on my side.

Then three cases I want dealt with, in the speech if you can do it without bloat, or flagged afterwards if you can't: the person who has never been cruel because they've never been tempted — is that a masked virtue or no virtue at all; the person whose disposition is entirely real but who has been wrapped in cotton wool by their family and will never find out; and me, someone who has spent fifteen years forming very confident opinions about strangers from press releases and edited transcripts.

Deliverable: the speech itself, around a thousand words, written for the mouth rather than the page — say where I should stop and let a line land. Please write the whole thing in British English, spellings and idiom included. Then a note of about a hundred and fifty words after it telling me where you've bent the philosophy to make it speakable and what a specialist in that fourth row would object to; I don't need any references or a reading list, I can find those myself. And if you think a finks-and-masks speech is a genuinely bad idea for a July afternoon in a sports hall, say so in one line at the top, then write it anyway.
