I'm a philosopher of social science and next month I'm giving a talk at an archaeology conference — the session is something like "Beyond Collapse: Reorganization and Continuity in Complex Societies" and I'm the token humanities person on the panel. My argument: claims that a society collapsed are never just readouts of the evidence, they smuggle in a criterion for when a society is still *the same* society, and once you drag that criterion into the open my three case studies (the Terminal Classic Maya, Rapa Nui, the eastern Mediteranean around 1200 BCE) come out differently depending on which continuity you're weighting.

The machinery, briefly: I split social persistence into two relations — institutional continuity (offices, succession rules, record-keeping, ritual calendars getting taken up again by somebody) and demographic-ecological continuity (roughly the same people on the same landscape with the same subsistence base) — and I argue, borrowing a move from Parfit, that what matters in "survival" talk is continuity-plus-connectedness of that sort rather than the numerical identity of "the Maya" or "Mycenaean palatial civilization." So collapse isn't a one-place property that either obtained or didn't; it's an interest-relative verdict about which continuity you're tracking, and my cases are fission-style examples where the two relations come apart. I'm explicitly *not* saying collapse never happens. I'm saying the question has no answer until the weighting gets supplied.

The room: three or four regional specialists (two Mesoamericanists, one Bronze Age eastern Med person) who have spent their careers on the actual sequences and get twitchy when a philosopher says their vocabulary is confused; a paleoclimatologist who works with speleothem and lake-core proxies and thinks the drought record settles it; one resilience ecologist fluent in adaptive-cycle and panarchy language who's perfectly happy calling collapse a phase transition; and a community-engaged archaeologist who works with descendant communities and is going to worry out loud that "the Maya didn't collapse" is either a denial move or an erasure of real suffering. Nobody there does philosophy of identity and most of them wont have read Parfit.

So prep me for the question period. The eightish objections this particular room will raise — not the ones a philosophy audience would — in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way each of these people would phrase them, including the resilience ecologist's jargon and the paleoclimatist's impatience with anything called "interest-relative." Flag how damaging each one is. For each: a reply I can say out loud in about ninety seconds, what I should concede before it lands, and an honest note on where my reply is soft or where I'm quietly changing the subject. Then tell me the one reply that absolutely has to land for the talk to survive, and name the question I'm praying nobody asks — the one about my own criterion of persistence that I don't have an answer to yet.
