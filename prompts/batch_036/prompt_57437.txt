The question I set her (600 words max):

> Explain the Doomsday Argument. Say which assumptions it actually needs, work through one version of the reasoning with real numbers, and then say whether the self-indication assumption defeats it. Give your own verdict at the end.

I gave her this marking scheme, which I made up: 5 marks for stating the argument and its central idea, 5 for naming the assumptions it depends on, 5 for the numerical example (does it work, and does it do the job she says it does), 5 for the self-indication assumption part. Out of 20.

Her answer:

> The idea comes from Brandon Carter in the seventies and was worked out properly by John Leslie. Richard Gott came up with his own version independently, and his is the one people usually tell the story about. It's an argument that you can say something about how long humanity has left just by thinking about where you sit in the line of everyone who will ever live.
>
> The main assumption is the self-sampling assumption: you should reason as if you were a random sample from the class of observers you belong to. This makes sense for the same reason the Copernican idea does. You're not in a special place and you're not at a special time. If you don't know when you live, you should assume you could be anywhere in history, and the earlier you turn out to be, the shorter history probably is. Given that, your birth rank is like drawing a ticket out of a hat with N tickets in it, where N is the total number of humans who ever live.
>
> Toy version. Say your birth rank is about 70 billion, which is roughly the number of people who have ever lived. Two hypotheses. Short Future: the total is 100 billion, we die out fairly soon. Long Future: the total is 100 trillion, we spread out and last millions of years. If Short Future is true, the chance you'd draw the ticket numbered 70 billion is 1 in 100 billion, because any of those ranks was equally likely to be yours. If Long Future is true, it's 1 in 100 trillion. So your rank is evidence for Short Future over Long Future by a factor of about a thousand. Notice that ratio doesn't depend on what you thought beforehand: whatever your priors were, they shift by the same factor. If you started at 50/50 you should now be at about a thousand to one. So most of us are living near the end.
>
> The argument never predicts a date. It doesn't say we're finished in 2500. It just moves probabilities around. Gott's version is slightly different again — he doesn't use a prior at all, he just says that with 95% confidence you're in the middle 95% of the sequence, so the total is within a factor of a few dozen of your rank in either direction.
>
> Now the objection. The self-indication assumption says that the more observers a hypothesis contains, the more likely it is that you exist at all, so hypotheses with more people in them get a boost before you look at any evidence. That boost exactly cancels the doomsday shift: a hypothesis with a thousand times more observers in it gets a thousand times more weight, and then the thousand-to-one against it from your rank brings you right back to where you started. I think the self-indication assumption is correct and so the argument fails. And the standard worry about it — the presumptuous philosopher, who supposedly gets to know there are tons of observers just by sitting in an armchair — isn't a real problem. How many observers there are is an ordinary empirical question. You point telescopes at exoplanets, work out how many could support life, and you find out. So it isn't smuggling in free knowledge, it's just saying take seriously that there might be more of us than you assumed, and let the evidence about how big and how old the universe is settle the rest. Also the complaint about the reference class is overdone: whether you count humans or mammals or observers generally, you get roughly the same ballpark.
