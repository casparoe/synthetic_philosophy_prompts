ok so context: I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background at all, and I've somehow ended up as the tie-breaker-ish member of the data standards committee at the conservation nonprofit where I work. We run a platform that ingests citizen-science observations — photos, GPS, volunteer IDs — and publishes species counts on regional dashboards. Those counts now feed a state grant formula and a couple of corporate biodiversity reports, so the numbers move actual money.

By the end of the quater the committee has to decide what a "species" is in our database. Concretely: do we commit to one concept, or do we store several per record?

Where it gets ugly:

- Our schema has species_id as the primary key on the observation table. One row, one species. If the same population is a full species under one concept and a subspecies under another, the schema literally cannot represent that. I floated a join table tagging each taxon with (concept, accepted name, source, version) and making every published count carry a concept parameter. Response was "so now the headline number is undefined."
- The upstream checklists disagree with each other and get revised. We've had cases where a widespread "least concern" species got split in two and one of the daughters instantly has a tiny range and qualifies for protection. So which concept we pick changes what gets funded. Feels like we're legislating reality with a foreign key.
- The geneticists want the phylogenetic species concept (monophyly, diagnosability). The field biologists want something closer to Mayr's reproductive isolation idea because that's roughly what they can see with their eyes. Nobody's concept is even defined for our metagenomics arm, which is mostly bacteria swapping genes horizontally the whole time. And there's the salamander ring species thing I read about, where "reproductively isolated from the others" is true at the two ends of the range and false in the middle.
- One of the ecologists said something that stuck with me: species aren't definitions at all, they're individuals — one big sprawling historical thing, a lineage, where membership is a fact about ancestry rather than about matching a checklist of traits. I think that's Ghiselin and Hull? If she's right, then storing an intension — a set of criteria — is the wrong shape entirely, and what we want is something more like an append-only ledger of lineage membership. Closer to how a git branch stays "the same branch" through a thousand commits than to how a class stays the same class.

And the strongest argument against my instinct, which I want the memo to actually wrestle with instead of strawmanning: our head of field programs says a count that changes when you change the concept isn't a count, its a mood. Grant reviewers and the public will hear "the county lost 40 species" and no footnote survives that headline. She'd rather we adopt one upstream authority wholesale, publish "species as defined by X, version N," and stop pretending we have a view of our own. That is... not obviously wrong.

Can you write the memo I'd circulate before the vote? What I need in it: a clear recommendation — one concept, concept-tagged counts, defer-to-authority, or some fourth option I haven't thought of — with the concrete schema/rollout shape and a timeline that lands before end of quarter; decision rules the ingestion pipeline can actually execute when two sources conflict; an honest section on what the field-programs objection gets right and what it costs us to swallow it; and one paragraph making the individuals-versus-kinds point land for a room full of non-philosophers, because I think that's the real crux and I can't articulate it myself. Keep it to about two pages, everyone will read it on a phone. And don't give me citations or a reading list — nobody on this committee will read them and I couldn't check them anyway.
