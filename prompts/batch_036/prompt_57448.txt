I spent the best part of three hours last night arguing with my flatmate, who is training to be a solicitor, about whether there is any such thing as a rule governing what one may assert. She has read none of the literature and does not care; her position is that the only thing anyone is ever entitled to demand of a witness is that what they say be true and that they not have been careless in saying it, and that any supposed rule which makes an honest, carefully conducted, well-evidenced assertion improper is not a rule but a philosopher's tic. Her sharpest case was the true lottery proposition: a juror who says the defendant did it on nothing but sheer statistical odds has said something true, and on her account has done nothing wrong as an asserter — merely gambled — whereas the anxious expert who refuses to state what her own impeccable evidence supports is, on my account, being improper, which she finds absurd. What I could not answer was her observation that the disagreement is structurally one-way: if knowing entails truth, then a knowledge-based rule can never license a falsehood, so the extra demand over a truth-based rule only ever bites in cases where nothing goes wrong for anyone, which is at least a hint that the extra demand is doing no work at all. I want my students to feel both sides of that rather than hear me summarise it.

I convene an interdisciplinary second- and third-year seminar, "Testimony, Evidence and Authority", eighteen students, roughly half philosophy and half law, meeting once a week for one hundred minutes in a room with movable tables. We are in week four; they have encountered Gettier-style cases and have a working idea of what it is to know something, but nobody has met the norms-of-assertion debate and I would like them not to meet its vocabulary until the very end. Design an in-class game that makes them run into the conflict between candidate rules for assertion — one keyed to what the speaker believes, one keyed to what the speaker is justified in believing, one keyed to what the speaker knows, one keyed simply to whether the claim is true — and, ideally, that also makes them feel the separate question of whether the rule shifts with practical stakes. Six groups of three; printable materials that fit on two sides of A4 plus a pack of index cards and some counters (we have no budget, so no real money); a hidden state of the world that only I hold, so that speakers must decide what to say without knowing whether their claim is in fact true; and a scoring system with three separate streams, one rewarding truth-conduciveness for the hearers who must act on the testimony, one rewarding the speaker's own epistemic standing, and one recording whether the group blames the speaker afterwards — the last two should be allowed to come apart, because the possibility of an improper but entirely blameless assertion is exactly what my flatmate denies exists. Crucially, do not rig the game to vindicate my side: build in at least one round in which strict adherence to the knowledge rule paralyses a group that must act on time-sensitive unverified information, and at least one round in which hearers are catastrophically misled by an assertion that was true but could not have been known, so that each rule wins a round on its own terms and the students have to say which cost matters more. Include a stakes dial, so that the same evidence package appears in a low-stakes and a high-stakes version, and let the debrief raise the objection that a rule one cannot tell whether one is satisfying cannot guide anyone. Tell me also what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I expect the law students to argue about admissibility rather than propriety, and I expect someone to try to game the hidden truth values — and be frank about which feature of the debate the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply by hand.

Please write the whole thing in British English, including the wording that will appear on the cards. No citations, no bibliography, no further-reading list and no named papers: everything the students need must be self-contained in the materials, and the in-game language should be neutral and invented (talk of warrant cards, the tribunal's rule, credibility chits) rather than the technical vocabulary, which should surface only in the debrief. Deliver it in three parts. First, the activity plan in prose under the headings Setup, Materials, Roles, Round sequence with minute-by-minute timings, Scoring, Debrief, and Predictable failures. Second, a table of the full card deck with exactly these columns: Card ID; Proposition asserted; What the asserter has (the evidence package); Stakes (low or high); Hidden truth value; Proper under the belief rule?; Proper under the justified-belief rule?; Proper under the knowledge rule?; Proper under the truth rule?; Where the rules diverge and why, in one sentence. Use "yes", "no" or "indeterminate" in the four rule columns. Third, immediately after the table, a single JSON object summarising the session with exactly these fields: "session_minutes", "group_size", "materials" (an array of strings), "rounds" (an array of objects, each with "name", "minutes", "mechanism" and "what_participants_should_notice"), "scoring_streams" (an array of objects, each with "stream", "what_it_rewards" and "which_rule_it_proxies"), "predicted_failure_modes" (an array of objects, each with "symptom", "cause" and "fix"), "irreproducible_feature" (a string), and "facilitator_must_supply" (a string).
