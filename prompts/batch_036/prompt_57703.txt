Rough draft of something I've been chewing on — comments welcome, preferably the kind you'd give at a whiteboard and not a referee report. It's about satisficing. My claim is basically that the word covers two theories that don't belong together, and that a lot of the bounded-rationality literature is unstable because it slides between them. Sorry, its messy.

WORKING TITLE: two satisficings

1. The domesticated one. The version you see in most formal work: an agent evaluates options sequentially, evaluation costs something, so she stops when expected marginal gain drops below marginal cost. Add the constraint that she can't recall passed-over options and you get optimal stopping, with the 37 percent rule as the poster child. Two things about this: it's routinely introduced as a vindication of satisficing, and it isn't one. It's maximizing over a richer outcome space where search effort is itself an argument of the utility function. Nothing structural has been given up — the agent still represents the whole problem, has complete preferences over (outcome, cost) pairs, and picks the best. Only the computational assumptions got realistic. I want to call this constrained maximizing and stop calling it satisficing.

2. The other one. Simon's aspiration-level idea, which I think is a genuinely different animal. Here the threshold isn't derived from any comparison of costs and benefits; it's carried in, and it moves — up when success has been coming easily, down when the search is going badly. The distinguishing features aren't that the agent is lazy or uninformed, they're (i) the threshold is endogenous to the realized sequence rather than fixed by the model in advance, and (ii) the agent never represents the choice problem as a whole, so there's no utility over (outcome, cost) pairs for her to maximize. On this picture satisficing isn't a solution to an optimization problem with frictions, it's a procedure that replaces the problem.

3. An empirical wedge, which is the part I'm least sure of. The two should come apart in the lab. Under optimal stopping with a known option distribution, the stopping rule depends on what's left, not on the quality of what you've already seen. Under aspiration dynamics it depends on the latter: the lucky searcher raises her bar and keeps going, the unlucky one lowers it, with the same number of draws remaining. So the design would be: known distribution, no learning, matched histories, vary realized draw quality. Any history-dependence in the threshold is then aspiration dynamics and not Bayesian updating. I can already see the objection — real subjects never really treat a stated distribution as known, they infer it from the draws, so the "no learning" condition may be a fiction and the confound survives my fix. I don't know how to close that, and I suspect the paper lives or dies here.

4. Objections. The standard sequential-rationality complaints — money pumps, sure-loss sequences — assume a fixed menu with stable descriptions across the steps. Aspiration adaptation moves the threshold between steps, so the pump doesn't get going. But the rescue is expensive: if the agent has no single preference over the sequence at all, she has no standpoint from which to judge that she should have kept looking, and ordinary people do make exactly that judgment, about the choice and not about their threshold. Meanwhile in ethics, Slote's satisficing consequentialism took the self-frustration hit from Pettit: a view that aims at the good but systematically produces less of it than it could isn't a rival consequentialism. My claim, which might be too cute: these are the same objection in different clothes. Both say a procedure that doesn't track the value it's answerable to isn't a theory of rational choice but a description of a failure. And both get the same answer from the aspiration theorist: the procedure isn't answerable to that value, it's answerable to a threshold the agent maintains. Which forces the real question — is that a theory of rationality, or a piece of psychology wearing rationality's clothes?

5. Conclusion. Bounded rationality is currently a banner under which two projects travel, one that keeps the maximizing structure and drops the computational assumptions, one that drops the structural assumption and keeps the psychology. Because they aren't seperated, any suboptimal choice can be redescribed either as optimizing-under-costs or as threshold maintenance, and the program as a whole stops risking anything. I want to end on that unfalsifiability charge.
