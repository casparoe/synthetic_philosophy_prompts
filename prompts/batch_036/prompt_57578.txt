I am revising the first chapter of a general-audience book on behavioural genetics, and I want to pressure-test an explanation before it goes to print. The point at issue is the familiar slogan that heritability is a property of a population raised in a range of environments, not a property of a trait. My worry is that the usual presentations of that slogan run together two quite different sources of population-relativity, and I want to know whether the separation I propose holds up.

Call them:

- **The variance-partition source.** Broad-sense heritability is a ratio of variance components, so it moves whenever the distribution of environments moves, even if nothing whatever changes about how any individual organism develops.
- **The interaction source.** Genotype-by-environment interaction, in the rank-changing sense, means the ordering of genotypes is itself environment-relative, so no single number can capture how much of the trait's variation is attributable to genotype even when the variance components happen to be stable across settings.

Please address the following, in order.

1. State the difference between the two sources in a way that does not merely restate the slogan. In particular, say which of them is a claim about arithmetic and which is a claim about development, and why that distinction matters for the inferences people want to draw from a heritability estimate.
2. Give a concrete, fully worked hypothetical case in which the interaction source is present but the variance-partition source is neutralised — identical variance components in both environments, reversed genotype ranking — and a second case in which the variance-partition source is present but the interaction source is absent, via strictly parallel reaction norms.
3. Say whether the two sources undermine heritability to different degrees. My working intuition is that the interaction source threatens the coherence of the quantity, whereas the variance-partition source threatens only its portability. Tell me whether that intuition is right; if it is wrong, tell me what the correct asymmetry is.
4. Suppose a trait turned out to exhibit no genotype-by-environment interaction anywhere in the relevant range of environments, with parallel reaction norms throughout. How much of the population-relativity of heritability survives in that case, and what is left of the slogan?
5. Tell me anywhere you think my two-way cut misses the joints — for instance, if there is a third source of population-relativity that belongs in neither bucket.

Please structure the response with exactly these sections, in this order:

- **Bottom line** (about 120 words): the answers to 3 and 5 in miniature.
- **Two sources, or one?** (about 350 words): items 1 and 5.
- **Two worked cases** (about 350 words): item 2, with the numbers or the reaction-norm geometry actually spelled out rather than gestured at.
- **The asymmetry** (about 250 words): item 3.
- **The no-interaction limit** (about 200 words): item 4.

No bibliography and no citations, please; I will supply those myself. Aim the explanation at a reader who is comfortable with the definition of broad-sense heritability as Vg/Vp but who has never opened a quantitative genetics textbook.
