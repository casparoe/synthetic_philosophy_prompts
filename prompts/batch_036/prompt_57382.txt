Fifteen years of reading rationalist blogs and their successor forums, and one sentence turns up in them often enough to count as house doctrine — roughly: when you have no information bearing on a question, the possibilities are all equally likely. It is typically deployed as a rebuke to whoever has just produced a confident number out of thin air, and typically treated as settling the exchange.

Two uses of it in a thread last week have left me unsure it was even the same sentence twice. The first came up in an argument about when some novel capability would first appear. One participant said that because we have no data on the arrival time, every decade is as likely as any other; when it was pointed out that spreading evenly over arrival decades gives a different answer from spreading evenly over years-to-arrival, and a different answer again from spreading evenly over the reciprocal of the wait, he called that quibbling — the state of ignorance is identical in all three formulations, and ignorance is what the principle tracks. The second use was in a Fermi-paradox argument, where someone wanted to put equal weight on each possible number of technological civilizations in the galaxy; since those values are countably many and no such assignment can exist, he waved it off as a technicality about infinite sums that has nothing to do with whether the reasoning is sound.

I am not asking for a verdict on the principle. I want the sentence taken apart. Lay out the distinct precise theses it might be standing for: a requirement on what a rational agent's degrees of belief must look like; a claim that symmetries in the problem ought to be mirrored in the credence function; a claim that some assignment is uniquely free of arbitrary choices; a claim about how to act under ignorance rather than what to believe; a claim that indifference is only the default state from which evidence moves us. Do not treat that list as exhaustive or as correct, and state each thesis in a form that could actually be false. For each one, say whether it is a platitude, substantive and defensible, or false, and say how it fares in the two situations above — including under the first participant's insistence that the three parameterizations describe one and the same ignorance, and under the second's appeal to technicality.

Then answer two questions directly. Which thesis, if any, entitles the sentence to the rebuke it is usually used to deliver — is there a reading on which producing a confident number without evidence really is the mistake being alleged? And is there a version that survives both the parameterization problem and the countably infinite one? If there is, state exactly what it obliges you to settle before it will output a single number, and what it gives up relative to the slogan; if there is not, say so, and say what the careful Bayesian in that thread should have said instead.
