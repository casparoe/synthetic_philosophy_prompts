So last night I got into a roughly three-hour argument with my officemate — she's a semanticist, I'm not — about the thing that's basically the second chapter of my dissertation, and I'm now pretty sure she's going to show up to the talk and reopen the whole thing in front of an audience. Which is fine except I need to be ready.

Here's the situation. Next Thursday I'm presenting at our department's language-and-mind reading group. The room is mostly formal semanticists who do modals and conditionals in a broadly Kratzerian way — modal base plus ordering source, that whole machinery — plus two or three epistemologists who mostly care about knowledge and evidence, and one psycholinguist who runs experiments on how people reason with if-sentences. Nobody in the room is a decision theorist and nobody is going to be sympathetic to purely philosophical hand-waving about what we "mean" by if.

The thesis I'm defending: there is only one if. The difference between "If Oswald didn't shoot Kennedy, someone else did" and "If Oswald hadn't shot Kennedy, someone else would have" is not a difference in the meaning of the conditional connective and not two lexical entries for if — it's a difference in what evidence you're allowed to look at when you evaluate it.

The argument sketch I'll give them, so you know what they'll be aiming at:

1. Start with an Adams pair. The indicative version is something I can happily assert; the subjunctive version is at best unsupported. Standard accounts either give if two lexical entries, or make the mood shift the similarity ordering on worlds.
2. Neither actually explains the felt contradiction in asserting both together. If they're just two different connectives, or the same connective with two freely-varying world-orderings, then there's no reason at all why I can't say both. The fact that it sounds like I've contradicted myself is left as a brute fact.
3. My proposal: a conditional always expresses the same operation relative to an information state. Grammatical mood isn't part of the conditional's semantics, it's an instruction about which information state to run it on. Indicative: use your own current evidence, and the antecedent has to be live in it. Past subjunctive: step back to an earlier information state that's been frozen shut — the evidence as it stood at some contextually salient past moment — and evaluate there.
4. That gives the contrast without two ifs. The Oswald pair isn't true-and-false, it's supported-and-unsupported relative to different evidence, which is exactly what it feels like. And you get the constraint on asserting both for free, because there's only one evidence-driven operation and mood is just telling you when to switch it.

What I need from you is a prep sheet, not a defense of the view — I already think it's right, I just need to not get run over. Give me the eight or so objections this particular room will actually raise, ordered by a mix of how likely they are to come up and how much damage they'd do, and say roughly when each one hits (opening questions, mid-talk, or the pile-up at the end). For each: a reply I can actually deliver in about two minutes standing at a podium, a flag on where the reply is soft, what I should concede immediately to save time, and what I absolutely have to hold onto.

I specifically want you to cover: the compositionality complaint (where does this information-state switch live in the logical form, what's its type, how does it interact with tense and with embedding under attitude verbs and epistemic modals); the charge that Adams pairs really do differ in truth conditions and not just in what you can assert; whether relativizing everything to information states makes me eat a triviality result or lose propositions altogether; negation and compounds ("it's not the case that if A, B"); and what I say to the psycholinguist about the fact that people reason differently with indicatives and subjunctives in selection tasks.
