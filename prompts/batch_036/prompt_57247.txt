My sister got me to tag along twise to a reading group she's in at the community college — a bunch of retirees and one or two younger people working through Peirce and James on truth. I'm an ER nurse, so I mostly went for the free coffee, but the second night turned into a real argument and I couldn't follow half of it. Two people got locked in: Ines, a retired high school chemistry teacher, and Theo, a guy in his thirties who does something with geographic data. Someone had put a case to the group — 1790s navigators computing a ship's position from tables that assume the earth is a perfect sphere; the assumption gets them safely into harbor on schedule, and two centuries later investigators know the earth is an oblate spheroid, flattened at the poles. Was the navigators' belief true? Ines read out a line from a dictionary entry Peirce wrote in 1901, where he says "Truth is that concordance of an abstract statement with the ideal limit towards which endless investigation would tend to bring scientific belief, which concordance the abstract statement may possess by virtue of the confession of its inaccuracy and one-sidedness, and this confession is an essential ingredient of truth." Then she and Theo went at it for an hour, and here's roughly the sequence as I wrote it down:

(1) Ines: the whole James-versus-Peirce fight the textbooks stage is fake, because they're answering different questions. Peirce is saying what truth consists in — the settled opinion at the ideal limit of inquiry. James is saying when a thinker who's only partway through an inquiry is entitled to treat a belief as true. Two questions, no contradiction, and the navigators don't separate them.

(2) Theo: they do separate them, because the two give opposite verdicts on exactly the same claim. Peirce has to call the spherical earth false, since further investigation is bound to reject it. James has to call it true for those men, because it worked — it got them to port, and it led them on to further experience that panned out. Same proposition, opposite truth values; that's a contradiction, not a division of labor.

(3) Ines: that's the cartoon version of James, the one James himself complains about. For him a belief's working isn't a payoff or a comfortable feeling; it's that we can assimilate it, corroborate it, verify it — the idea carries us through experience without getting us stranded. On that reading the navigators' belief does work in James's sense, and Peirce can agree it works while denying it's where inquiry ends. So they agree about the case and are only fighting over which label to stick on it.

(4) Theo: if that's all working means, James has nothing distinctive left. He's now describing ordinary justification — having good grounds — and calling it truth. The claim that made him worth reading is the other one: that truth is something that happens to an idea, that it gets made true by events along the way rather than being fixed by where the whole investigation eventually lands. Her charitable reading buys compatibility by deleting James's actual thesis.

(5) Ines: then look at Peirce, the one Theo is treating as stable. If truth is whatever the ideal limit settles on, then either there is such a limit — an enormous metaphysical bet — or there isn't, and nothing is true yet, including arithmetic and including Peirce's own theory. Worse, on his account whether my belief about some fact is true right now depends on what investigators who don't exist yet would conclude, which gets the direction of explanation backwards: today's truths would hang off the future instead of the future hanging off today's facts. So he's in no position to call James vague.

(6) Theo: she's assuming you have to reach the limit to use it, which nobody claims. The view just says truth is what inquiry aims at and converges toward, and I can have every reason to think a claim will survive without knowing that it survives; and since approximation is built into Peirce's account, he can say the navigators' belief was true insofar as it would have survived further investigation and false insofar as it wouldn't. Besides, her position is self-undermining: if the two theories never disagree about any case, then choosing between them is just picking a favorite word, and she's already conceded that James isn't offering a theory of truth at all.

I honestly coudn't tell who was winning and they never resolved it — the group moved on to Dewey the following week. Could you act as a professional philosopher who works on classical American pragmatism and has taught this exact dispute many times, and referee it properly? Think carefully. Before you rate anything, brainstorm out loud for a few sentences: state in your own words what each of the two says truth consists in, and say what would actually have to be the case for them to genuinely conflict rather than just differ in emphasis or in what they're doing with the word. Then go through moves (1) to (6) one at a time and give each a verdict — clearly right, mostly right, mixed, mostly wrong, clearly wrong — with two or three sentences saying why, and naming the specific flaw or the specific insight in that move; don't let a move slide just because the person was right two moves earlier. Finish with who is more right overall, plus one or two things both of them assumed or ignored that a referee should have called out. Please don't split the difference just to be even-handed, and don't give me a bibliography or a reading list — I want the reasoning, not the scholarship.
