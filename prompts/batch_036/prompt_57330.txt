I'm ghostwriting a long-ish essay for a local magazine about identity after a diagnosis (my dad's early dementia is the personal hook, the editor liked it), and one section leans on Dennett's idea of the self as a center of narrative gravity. I built that section out of a bestselling pop-neuro book with a whole chapter on the topic, and I want to know before I file whether I've been building on sand. Here's the book's paragraph, retyped so it fits:

'Your self is a story your brain tells. Dennett calls the self a center of narrative gravity: just as a physicist posits an invisible point inside a spinning object where all its weight is concentrated, the brain posits a point inside itself where all its experiences come together. There's no little man in there watching the show, but there is a storyteller — a process that weaves everything you see, feel and remember into a single autobiography with you as its hero. That's why the self feels so solid even though it's made up: a made-up story can still do real work. And the upshot is liberating. Since the self is a narrative, you can edit it. Rewrite the story and you rewrite the person, which is why Dennett's picture is the philosophical foundation of narrative therapy.'

Then I sent the draft to a friend who's a philosophy grad student and he sent back a takedown instead of notes, which I also want assessed, because I can't tell if he's catching real problems or just being a snob about a popularizer:

'The center of narrative gravity is a cheap trick. Dennett tells you the self isn't there in the ordinary sense, then buys back all the comfort of the ordinary view by renaming the hole an abstraction — a fiction he insists isn't fictional. And the account is empty: if the self is just whatever coherent story gets told about a body, then two people telling wildly different but equally coherent stories about the same brain are equally right, which makes selves a matter of narrative fashion rather than fact. The dissociative cases prove it. If one body can host several centers of narrative gravity, then the posit multiplies whenever it's convenient, and a posit that multiplies at will isn't tracking anything real.'

So go through both passages claim by claim and tell me which bits a Dennett-sympathizer would nod at, which are the harmless sort of simplification you'd expect in a magazine, and which are actually the opposite of what he argues. Specifically I want (a) whether the center-of-gravity analogy is being used the way he uses it, since that's the load baring sentence in my whole section, (b) whether "made up but still does real work" is fair, or whether it quietly turns an abstraction into an illusion, which I'd think he'd hate, (c) whether my friend's two objections land or are just the standard misreading dressed up as a refutation, and (d) the practical one — can I keep the "you can edit your story" line at all, or does it not follow from anything in the view? If the honest answer is that the self is revisable but not at will, say what constrains the revision, and say what that leaves of the idea that people can be flat-out wrong about who they are, because that matters alot for the dementia framing and I don't want to write something that sounds like my dad's self is just whatever story the family settles on.
