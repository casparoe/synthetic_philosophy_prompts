You are a philosopher of computation, equally at home with the recursion-theoretic definition of computability and with the conceptual and physical debates around it. I am drafting a chapter on the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis, and before writing it I want to know what I actually think, judged by my verdicts on cases rather than by my professions. Treat the verdicts as the data. Work through several candidate principles privately before answering, and send me no references or reading list: I want the reasoning, not the literature.

Throughout, a counterexample to the thesis means something that computes a function no Turing machine computes. My verdicts:

(a) A quantum computer that factors numbers beyond the reach of any classical machine. Not a counterexample. It is fast rather than strong: every function it delivers is still recursive, and speed belongs to a different question.

(b) A Turing machine with an oracle symbol wired into it, in Turing's own sense. Not a counterexample. The extra power sits in the definition rather than in any mechanism, and the device starts a hierarchy instead of breaking one.

(c) A machine that takes its first step in a minute, its second in half a minute, its third in a quarter, and so on, finishing infinitely many steps in two minutes. Not a counterexample. Nothing could be built to accelerate like that, and there is no determinate state for the machine to be in at the instant the run completes.

(d) A computer riding a worldline of unbounded proper time — one orbiting a rotating black hole, say — every event of which lies in the past of a single event on a second worldline, that of an observer who falls inward and receives the computer's signal there, by which time the machine has been through every step of an ordinary Turing computation and can report whether it halted. A counterexample, in principle. I grant that the inner horizon is believed to be unstable and that the recipient's prospects are poor; neither seems to me to matter, since what is at issue is what general relativity licenses rather than what engineering permits.

(e) A recurrent network one of whose real-valued weights happens to be Chaitin's halting-probability constant, so that the network's dynamics yield the halting function. Not a counterexample. The answer has been smuggled in through a constant nobody could set; the network is a container for it, not a computer of it.

(f) A device that emits guesses about the halting problem, changing its mind only finitely often on each input and settling, at no knowable time, on the right answer. Not a counterexample. Nobody is ever in a position to know that the answer they hold is the settled one, so nothing has been computed.

(g) A detector that records the outcomes of genuinely indeterministic quantum events as an endless bit string. Not a counterexample. With probability one the string is not computable, but no function has been evaluated; randomness is not a way of getting an answer.

(h) A mathematician who, presented with any consistent formal system, reliably sees that its Gödel sentence is true. Not a counterexample. The phrase ‘reliably sees’ is an idealisation I have granted to nothing else on this list, and the argument needs a soundness claim the mathematician could never establish for themselves.

Two pairs bother me. Cases (c) and (d) both require infinitely many operations to be got through, and I reject one and accept the other. Cases (d) and (h) both rest on an idealisation that no finite procedure could ever discharge, and again I accept one and reject the other.

The case I keep going back and forth on: countably many ordinary Turing machines, each finite in its resources and each running an ordinary finite computation, scattered through space, with a collector that pools their reports — no acceleration anywhere, no exotic geometry, no uncomputable constant, only an infinity of unremarkable devices, enough between them to settle any halting question.

Answer in British English, as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion:

1. the principle that best fits all eight verdicts, stated as a test I could apply to a case I have not thought of, together with what it makes the thesis — a conceptual truth, an empirical hypothesis, or neither;
2. the strongest rival principle, and the one case on which it and yours come apart;
3. which of my verdicts you would have me give up and why, or, if you think all eight can be saved, what the saving costs;
4. what your principle delivers for the infinity of ordinary machines, and whether my wavering is justified;
5. one further case, of your own devising, that would settle the choice between the principle in item 1 and the rival in item 2.
