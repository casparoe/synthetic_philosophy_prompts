**Who has to justify what**

Everyone arguing about the length of the working week — 35 hours vs 50, overwork as cultural pathology vs personal choice — assumes the interesting part. They assume the disagreement is about how much work there should be. I think the real disagreement is about which side of the ledger starts out holding the burden of proof.

**1. Weber's move.** Weber's Protestants did something odd. In most of the societies they came out of, work was what you did because you had to, and the good life was what you did once you didn't have to. Leisure was the default for anyone who could afford it; work was the interruption. The ascetic sects flipped this. Idleness became the sin and labor became the sign — the way you discharged an obligation you couldn't otherwise check on. The point about Franklin, on Weber's reading, is that by then time itself had turned into a standing debt: an hour not worked was an hour taken. So work is the default position, and not-working requires justification.

**2. What the 35-hour week was actually for.** Read this way, the Aubry reforms that took France to 35 hours around 2000 weren't mainly about four fewer hours. They were an attempt to flip the default back by statute: 35 hours is where you start, and every hour past that is an exception that has to be paid, negotiated, and accounted for. The mechanism was the RTT day — you bank the excess as time off rather than as money, so the exception gets converted back into the default. Whether it created jobs is an empirical question I'm not going to touch. The interesting failure is that it flipped the default on paper and left it standing in practice: taking your RTT days still felt, to the cadres I've talked to, like asking permission for something faintly disreputable.

**3. Karoshi as the limit case.** Karoshi is what the flipped default looks like when the flip is total. The first reported case is from 1969, and by 1988 there were national hotlines — and the striking thing about the early hotline data is that most callers weren't the workers but their wives. That's exactly what you'd predict if rest has to be justified and nobody inside the system is in a position to do the justifying. Overwork here isn't a quantity that got too big. It's a structure in which stopping has no standing.

**4. The claim.** Call it the *direction of the default*. A work regime isn't defined by how many hours it demands but by which side has to speak first. That predicts things hours-talk can't: two places with identical average hours can feel completely different depending on direction; a law that cuts hours without changing direction produces resentment rather than relief; and reforms that change direction without changing the headline number — an enforceable right to disconnect, a real norm of part-time work for everyone rather than for mothers — may do more work than the number suggests.
