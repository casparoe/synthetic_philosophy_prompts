You're a Holocaust historian who also reads the obedience-and-conformity literature in social psychology closely, and you've taught the Browning/Goldhagen argument enough times to be a bit sick of how it usually gets staged — each side quoting the same passages at the other. Help me out. I'm TA'ing a fourth-year seminar on the historiography of perpetration, and for the last session I've invented a fake archival case purely as a thinking device. Please treat everything in it as made up by me, not as real evidence about any real unit, and don't go looking for parallels in the actual record.

The case: a reserve police battalion of about 480 men, summer 1943, somewhere in occupied eastern Poland. They're ordered to shoot several hundred Jews rounded up from a nearby labour camp. Before the action the commander tells them twice that anyone who doesn't feel up to it can step out and take guard duty instead; he mentions no consequences and none follow. Eleven men step out at the announcement. Over the next six weeks another thirty-odd get themselves reassigned by grumbling to their sergeants or pleading illness — two get ragged for it, nobody is punished. Around sixty letters home and a couple of diary fragments survive. Almost none mention the victims at all, and not one contains antisemitic language, which is strange because these same men's letters are full of crude remarks about Poles, about their officers, about the food and the weather. What does show up: the physical strain, the heat, the smell, the drinking afterwards, pride in the unit, homesickness. Eight letters contain some version of "I did my duty" or "I held out" without ever saying what the duty was. Three describe thier own hands or nerves as the thing that let them down.

Deliverable: a dialogue between two historians in the seminar room after class, both looking at this case — one arguing it confirms the situational/conformity reading, one arguing it confirms the cultural/ideological reading. Both are equally sharp and equally honest; neither wins by the other being dense, and no strawmanning. The thing I most want them to fight over is that both sides can use the same facts in opposite directions: the free exit is proof for one that the killing was uncoerced and therefore willing, and proof for the other that group pressure was near-total when 97% stayed in line despite a real way out; the silence of the letters is evidence for one that ideology wasn't doing the work, and evidence for the other of how unremarkable the act was inside their moral world — you don't justify what nobody around you thinks needs justifying. I want at least one clean hit landed by each side and at least one real concession each, plus an explicit argument about what evidence would actually settle the dispute, because that's usually where they talk past each other.
