Thirty-five years doing probabilistic seismic hazard assessment, retired four years ago, and since then I've been reading history and philosophy of science rather badly and enjoying it. Lately I keep running into this cliodynamics business — Turchin, secular cycles, elite overproduction, the idea that you can build a "political stress index" out of wage data, degree inflation and state finances and then say a society is in for a rough decade.

Every time I see it attacked as unfalsifiable fortune-telling, the defenders reach straight into my own discipline. The line is roughly: seismologists can't tell you the day or the magnitude either, but we can say this particular segment is loaded and hand you a probability over a thirty-year window, and that's enough to write building codes and insure things, and cliodynamics is the same species of science just at an earlier stage of development. Fair?

I'd like a proper audit rather than a shrug in either direction.

Start with the mapping, table is fine. What plays the role of accumulating strain, of the fault segment itself, of the rupture, of the magnitude scale, of the recurrence interval — they seem to want both a 200–300 year cycle and a shorter fifty-ish year one riding on top of it, which I dont think has a counterpart in seismology at all — of foreshocks and aftershocks, of the seismic gap. Where there's no honest counterpart, leave the cell empty and say so instead of forcing something in.

Then the disanalogies, and this is the part I actually want: sort them into the ones that do real damage and the ones a defender can absorb without surrendering the conclusion. Two I've come up with myself, so tell me whether they're as strong as I suspect or whether I'm just flattering my old trade. First, in hazard work the loading rate is measured by something independent of the earthquakes — geodesy, creep meters, paleoseismic trenching — whereas here the stress variable seems to be reconstructed out of the same historical record the model is then credited with predicting. Second, the statistics: I get many hundreds of mapped segments and a magnitude-frequency relation that holds across the whole range of event sizes we can instrument, and they get a couple dozen societies whose crises have an annoying habit of arriving at the same time as each other's.

Which brings me to their showcase: a forecast made in 2010 that the following decade would be turbulent in the US, now counted as a hit. In my business a single hit against a high base rate is worth roughly nothing, and the first thing we'd ask is what the forecast beat. Does the earthquake analogy hand them a way to answer that, or does it make the problem worse by importing standards they can't meet?

Finish with a verdict on the building-code question — is it legitimate to act on these things the way we act on hazard maps — and if seismology is the wrong comparison to be defending with, tell me which analogy would be the right one and what it would and wouldn't license. No bibliography needed, I just want the reasoning laid out.
