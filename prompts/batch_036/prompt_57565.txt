I've been going down a rabbit hole about how insurance pricing actually gets regulated and there's something I can't resolve.

An 18-year-old guy pays a lot more for car insurance than an 18-year-old woman, on average, and the insurers are right that the two groups differ. In the EU they're not allowed to do that anymore — sex got banned as a rating factor — and by all accounts young women's premiums went up and young men's came down, so nothing about the underlying risk changed, the cost just moved. Meanwhile the same regulators, and most American ones, are perfectly happy letting insurers rate on age (the single biggest factor in life insurance, and it discriminates way more brutally), on smoking, on occupation, on marital status, on your ZIP code, and — where it's permitted — on your credit score. Every one of those correlates with race and sex and class. The ZIP code one is the weirdest: everybody agrees ZIP correlates with race, using race directly is illegal, using a variable *because* it stands in for race would be illegal too, but using it because it predicts theft is fine. As far as I can tell the only difference is the insurer's motive, which isn't a thing anyone can inspect from the outside.

So what is this practice committed to? My first guess was "you can only be priced on what you chose" — smoking yes, sex no. But age isn't chosen. Then I tried "you can be priced on whatever genuinely predicts, and we ban mere proxies" — except a rating factor *is* a proxy, that's the whole job of one. Someone once told me the difference is that everybody will be old someday but nobody changes sex, and I can't work out whether thats an argument or just a slogan that feels like one.

There's also a thing I half-remember from a stats philosophy class, the reference class problem — Reichenbach's rule, roughly that you should assign an individual the probability belonging to the narrowest reference class you have reliable statistics for. Which sounds like it should settle all of the above, and obviously doesn't, because "narrowest" already assumes you know which similarities matter, and there's always a narrower class (18-year-old men who drive red hatchbacks after midnight in this particular county) with no statistics at all. Someone later flipped the advice around and said use the broadest class that's homogeneous with respect to the outcome, which I think makes it worse rather than better.

Can you reconstruct what this practice actually presupposes — about fairness, about evidence, about what sort of thing a person is for the purposes of a risk pool? Specifically: which bits are load-bearing and which just happen to fit? Is banning sex while permitting age consistent, or does the whole scheme only hang together under one particular theory that it then violates somewhere else? And where is it underdetermined, so that two or three quite different theories would license the same rulebook and you couldn't tell from the practice which one it's running on? Then tell me what would have to change if we swapped the implicit view for either extreme — pure individual assessment with no group factors at all, or fully Bayesian pricing with every statistically relevant variable thrown in.
