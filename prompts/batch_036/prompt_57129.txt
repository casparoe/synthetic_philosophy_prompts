I am in a seminar on Marx's critique of political economy and its reception, and I am drafting a paper that I want to be more disciplined than the usual gesture at "Marx and automation." Could you help me work through a specific historical case rather than a survey of the theory?

The case is the Luddite disturbances in England, roughly 1811 to 1813. So that we are working from the same picture, here are the features I take to be salient: the movement was not one trade but three, in different regions and at different technical stages — framework knitters in the Midlands working in domestic outwork, many of whom owned or rented their own frames; highly skilled croppers and other woollen workers in the West Riding resisting gig mills and shearing frames; and handloom cotton weavers in Lancashire resisting power looms. The grievances were mixed: unemployment and food prices, but also the quality of goods produced on the newer "wide" frames, and the removal of the statutory regulation of the trade, which the knitters petitioned to have restored before Parliament declined. The violence was selective — frames belonging to masters who met the workers' terms were often left standing — and the movement framed itself in a legalistic, even constitutional idiom, with letters signed in the name of a mythical "General Ludd." The state response escalated sharply: frame-breaking was made a capital offence in 1812 over the opposition of, among others, the young Lord Byron in his maiden speech in the Lords; large numbers of regular troops were deployed; there was the failed night attack on Rawfolds Mill and the murder of the mill owner William Horsfall; and the York assizes of early 1813 ended in a batch of executions and transportations.

Against that, I want the analysis to engage two distinct pieces of Marx's own apparatus, which I think are usually collapsed into one another. The first is the machinery chapter of Capital, Volume One, and in particular Marx's insistence on separating machinery as such from its capitalist application — machinery as a means for producing surplus value, as a weapon for repressing strikes, as a force that deskills and enlarges the reserve army of labour. Marx's own verdict on the Luddites is that their form of struggle was immature, and the passage I keep coming back to is this: "It took both time and experience before the workpeople learnt to distinguish between machinery and its employment by capital, and to direct their attacks, not against the material instruments of production, but against the mode in which they are used." The second is the so-called fragment on machines in the Grundrisse, where the picture is quite different: the worker no longer inserts himself as the chief actor into the production process but stands beside it as its regulator, the general intellect becomes a direct force of production, and because labour time ceases to be the measure of wealth, production resting on exchange value is supposed to collapse.

Please run the case through both, and treat the following questions as the real work.

First, the mapping. Who counts as the agent in Marx's categories? The Midlands knitters were not factory proletarians under real subsumption; several owned their means of production. Does Marx's account of machinery presuppose a phase the Luddites had not reached, and does that mean the framework misdescribes the episode or merely arrives early?

Second, explanation and failure. Which features of 1811–13 does the machinery chapter actually predict — the direction of violence toward the instrument, its selectivity, the state's alignment with the mill owners, the deskilling logic behind the croppers' resistance — and which does it get wrong or leave silent? Here is the crux I cannot resolve: the Luddites seem to have been drawing precisely the distinction Marx says they had not yet learned, since they attacked only frames "worked in a fraudulent and deceitful manner" and left compliant masters alone; yet they concluded from that distinction that the remedy was the restoration of legal regulation and petition, not the transformation of the mode of production. Does that rescue Marx's diagnosis by showing the distinction without its political consequence, or does it invert the diagnosis, since the distinction was available and simply did not point where he says it points?

Third, the verdict on the fragment. Given that the Grundrisse passage is about a much later phase, does the Luddite episode bear on it at all? If it does, is it as a first instalment of the transfer of skill into the machine — an expropriation of craft knowledge rather than the objectification of science — and what does that difference cost the fragment's argument?

Finally, tell me which of the competing readings of Marx on automation the episode best supports and why: the claim that automation erodes the measure of value and so undermines capitalism's own foundation; the claim that machinery is chiefly a disciplinary instrument with no such tendency, since displacement is reabsorbed; the claim, associated with Postone, that value survives into a phase in which it is increasingly anachronistic rather than disappearing; or the labour-process reading descended from Braverman, on which machines are chosen for control rather than efficiency. Say plainly where the case underdetermines the theory or where the theory has nothing to say, rather than forcing a fit.

I do not need references, a bibliography, or a reading list — only the argument, laid out as a mapping section, then the assessment running in both directions, then your judgement among the four readings.
