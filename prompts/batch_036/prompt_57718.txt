hey — I've got quals coming up on the nature of pain and my committee asked me to write down where I've actually landed so they can attack it properly. Here's the list. I genuinely don't know which of these are fighting each other and I don't have a favourite I'm trying to protect, so please don't guess at one — just tell me where the damage is.

(1) Unpleasantness is in the experience. When a pain is bad it's bad because of what it's like to have it: there's an affective component with a felt character of its own, sitting alongside the sensory "sharp, throbbing, left hand" stuff. It's not that the feeling is neutral and then I take up an attitude toward it.

(2) So the badness of pain is intrinsic to the feeling. Anything with that affective character is bad for whoever has it — whether or not they mind it, whether or not they want it to stop, whether or not they do anything about it.

(3) Asymbolia is the case I actually care about: the patients who'll tell you yes it hurts, then shrug, won't pull the limb away, sometimes seem faintly amused, and will let you repeat the stimulus. I take those reports at face value. They're really in pain. Not confabulating, not performing, not just preternaturally stoic, and not misdescribing their own state to the neurologist.

(4) But what they have isn't bad for them — or isn't bad in the way ordinary pain is. Their indifference isn't an error or a failure of judgment; it's tracking a real absence. Something that normally makes pain bad is genuinely gone in them, and I don't want to be pushed into saying they just fail to notice it.

(5) I'm also not willing to phrase (4) as "they're in pain, but their pain isn't unpleasant." That sounds like saying they have a pain that isn't a pain. I think pain is essentially unpleasant: strip the unpleasantness out and what's left is a pressure or a tingle, not a pain.

(6) Desire-type accounts are wrong for a seperate reason anyway. Someone can want a pain to continue — the hurts-so-good massage, some masochism, the runner who welcomes the burn — and it's still unpleasant; and someone can be rendered indifferent by suggestion or by drugs while what's left is still awful for them. So wanting-it-to-stop can't be what unpleasantness is.

(7) One more, which I suspect is the weirdest thing on the list: a pain can be bad for someone even if it leaves no trace on them at all — had under sedation, never recalled, never complained about, no behavioural residue. The amnesia doesn't rescue them.

So: which pairs actually collide, and how badly? Rank them rather than listing them all as equally worrying — I want to know which collision would blow up the whole package in a viva and which ones are only superficially uncomfortable and dissolve once you look. For each real collision, say what has to be given up and what it costs me to give it up (not just "you could drop (5)" but what I lose by dropping it). Then tell me which single revision buys the most consistency per unit of cost, and whether there's a version of this package that keeps the thing I actually care about — that asymbolia teaches us something about pain rather than nothing at all — without the contradiction.
