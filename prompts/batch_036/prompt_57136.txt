I work as a senior automation engineer at a mid-sized cold-chain logistics company, and I have been asked to write a two-page internal note that will be read on Friday by people who have never heard the phrase "nonmonotonic reasoning," so every technical term I use has to earn its place in a parenthetical. The occasion is a post-mortem. About six weeks ago we swapped in a new task-planning layer — a learned planner that sits on top of our older symbolic action model and proposes command sequences for our dock crews — and on its fourth day in production it scheduled a pallet of temperature-sensitive biologics into a staging bay while simultaneously scheduling that bay's door to be held open for an inbound forklift. Nothing was malfunctioning. The bay's compressor had been logged as down for maintenance two days earlier in a ticket the system had read access to; the door-hold was licensed by an ordinary traffic rule; and the two facts simply never met. We lost the pallet and nearly lost a customer. The post-mortem split into two camps, and I have to explain the split without taking a side I cannot yet defend. My manager's view is that this was a data gap: more logged examples, a retrieval fix, and a guardrail, and the class of error goes away. A colleague of mine who did a philosophy degree says it was the frame problem, and that no finite pile of additional examples touches it, because the difficulty concerns which of the things a system knows are treated as bearing on the action it is about to take. I took a logic course years ago and remember enough to know that the narrow technical problem is supposed to have been largely solved by now, which I suspect is part of why the two of us keep talking past each other. What I want for the note is not a literature review or a history of the debate — nobody will read it — but a single designed case that makes the disagreement sharp and adjudicable.

So: build me a thought experiment to specification. The case has two agents, operating in one shared, realistic industrial or clinical setting, with the same sensors, the same actuators, and the same body of information about the world. Agent A is a symbolic planner whose domain theory contains an explicitly written-out, finite, and correct set of frame rules or successor-state-style axioms for that domain. Agent B is a learned world-model planner that predicts the next state by induction over a large corpus of operational logs from that same domain. The scenario should consist of one decision point at which A takes the safe action and B takes the unsafe one. Constraints: no ticking bombs on wagons, no Yale-shooting-style toy domains, no adversarial saboteurs, no omniscient predictors, no exotic physics, and the whole scenario in two paragraphs at most — the analysis that follows may run as long as it needs. The failure may not be a perceptual failure; B registers everything A registers. B must be credited with knowing, in some form it can access, every fact a competent human supervisor in that role would know, so its error has to come from how it treats what it knows rather than from any gap in what it knows. A, meanwhile, may not win by being handed an axiom that simply encodes the answer to this particular situation — nothing of the form "never hold the bay door open while the compressor is down." A's rules must be general domain rules that a designer would plausibly have written before the incident, and they should be spelled out compactly enough that I could paste them into a slide. Finally, B's failure must be stateable to a machine-learning person without their immediately replying that it is just an out-of-distribution artifact that more data fixes; if the only way to make B fail is to push it outside its training distribution, then say so plainly and tell me what that concession costs the argument.

After the scenario, I need the demonstration that it works. First, a compact trace of each agent at the decision point: what it represents, which default or learned prior it applies, what it infers, and what it does. Second, a constraint-by-constraint check against the specifications above — an actual check, not an assurance. Third, name the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and say what happens to both verdicts if that feature is weakened or removed. Fourth, give me the strongest attack from the losing side: what someone convinced that learned world models dissolve this whole issue would say to discredit the case, and how much of it survives that attack. Fifth, classify the trouble — is this the frame problem in the narrow sense, the qualification problem, or the ramification problem, and does the classification matter for the practical question my manager and my colleague are actually fighting about? The case exists to adjudicate between two positions: that the incident reflects a data problem, and that it reflects a structural problem about relevance and default reasoning. That fixes what counts as cheating. A case that smuggles in the verdict by stipulating that B "just fails to consider" the relevant fact is worthless to me, and so is a case where A wins only because its designer already knew the answer and quietly wrote it down. And if your honest assessment is that these specifications cannot all be satisfied at once, tell me which one has to go and why — for the purposes of the note, that would be just as useful as a case that holds together.
