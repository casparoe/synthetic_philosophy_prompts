Before I came to journalism I spent about eleven years trading derivatives — first market-making rates and energy options, later running a book of bespoke structured trades. What I actually think in is contract language: the underlying and how observable it is, the fixing and who performs it, settlement date and settlement agent, counterparty and clearing arrangement, margin, position limits, who is obliged to quote and what happens to liquidity when nobody is, adverse selection, basis risk, thin and orphan markets, squeeze and manipulation dynamics, and knock-out or otherwise conditional payoff features. My political philosophy is secondhand at best.

I am writing a long piece on governance by prediction markets — the proposal associated chiefly with Robin Hanson that a polity should, in his slogan, vote on values but bet on beliefs: elected representatives formally define and maintain an after-the-fact measurement of national welfare, speculators trade on which policies would raise it, and the operating rule is that when a betting market clearly estimates a proposed policy would increase expected national welfare, the proposal becomes law. Proponents also rely on conditional contracts — Hanson's called-off bets — to compare welfare under adoption against welfare under non-adoption. I want the critics in the frame too: thin markets, manipulation by interested parties, the impossibility of measuring welfare well enough to settle on it, the charge that legitimate authority cannot be handed to a price, and the objection that money-weighted trading is not anything like a vote.

Please restate that entire position, and that entire dispute, in my vocabulary — as faithfully as you can manage, contract spec by contract spec. Treat the proposal as if it had landed on my desk as a term sheet, and describe what it would actually take to structure and clear it: what the deliverable is, who fixes it and when, who stands on the other side, what the payoff is denominated in, what happens to the price when the parties best informed about the underlying are also the parties most affected by the decision. Do not give me a primer on prediction markets and do not recite the literature's own summary of itself; I have read both.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Sort the claims on both sides into three piles: those that translate cleanly into contract and market structure with no loss; those that translate only under distortion, where you should name precisely what gets bent to make them fit; and those with no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. Be disciplined about not smuggling: my framework carries assumptions of its own — that a price is a forecast, that efficiency is the default, that liquidity aggregates better than deliberation, that someone always takes the other side, that a clearinghouse exists — and if you import any of those and present them as translation, say so explicitly at the point of import.

Test the translation on two concrete cases before you generalise. First, a city council that delegates a corridor upzoning to a market settling seven years out on some composite proxy for resident welfare, where the landlords, developers and long-term tenants who will do most of the trading are also the ones with the sharpest information about the outcome. Second, a decision to impose or lift sanctions where the market's own price feeds back into the calculations of the government being sanctioned, so that the contract changes the probability of the event it is written on.

Finally, tell me where you come out. Is the residue evidence that this proposal is missing something structural — no settlement asset, no counterparty, no source of authority — or evidence that its critics' objections are less substantive than they sound, once restated as ordinary and soluble contract-design problems? And is there anything my own vocabulary makes visible that the political-philosophy debate appears not to have noticed: about conditional payoffs with no one to declare the condition, about a price being a capital-weighted average rather than a consensus, or about what a market does when the underlying is a decision the market itself helps to make? I am not asking for references or a reading list. I am asking for the translation and an honest account of what did not survive it.
