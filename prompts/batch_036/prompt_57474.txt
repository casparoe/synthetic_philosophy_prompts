I run an evening reading group on Stoic ethics — half the room has come to it via the modern self-help revival, and they've all absorbed the dichotomy-of-control framing, which is not quite what the ancients said but never mind. Last week my most combative student turned up with this, and he's rather pleased with himself:

(1) The Stoic thesis is that virtue is the only good and vice the only bad; everything else — health, reputation, pain, including other people's pain — is indifferent.
(2) Others' suffering is therefore an indifferent, and its removal or continuation makes no difference to my own good.
(3) If something makes no difference to my good, then it cannot itself furnish me with a reason to act; only the virtuousness or viciousness of an act can do that.
(4) So the only reason I can have to relieve someone's suffering is that relieving it would be virtuous of me.
(5) But if that is the only reason, the suffering itself contributes nothing to it. All the value sits in the response, none in what is responded to.
(6) It follows that the relief I give has whatever value it has only because the suffering is there to be relieved. The suffering is the material of my virtue.
(7) Occasions for virtue are worth having — that's the whole point of the Stoic line about difficulties being training for the soul.
(8) Hence a consistent Stoic should be glad about other people's suffering, and should want there to be rather a lot of it.
(9) Nobody can want that. So Stoic ethics is either monstrous or it does not in fact hold what (1) says it holds.

He also reckons the whole thing can be short-circuited at (3): if the only reasons are reasons of virtue, then virtue is a skill whose successful exercise leaves the world exactly as it was, and he asks what's supposed to distinguish the virtuous response from a well-meaning delusion. I think that's a real worry but I don't think it's the crux.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Tell me why that step looked unobjectionable on first pass — I want the diagnosis, not just the verdict. Then price the alternatives. I'd rather hold on to (1) and (5) if I can, and I'm certain (9) has to go somewhere, but I can't work out how to get rid of (8) without giving up something in (5)–(7). Two specific things I'd like answered along the way: does appealing to the archer analogy — the one where hitting the target is what the archer does everything to achieve without its being the good — actually block the argument, or does it concede that the response is aimed at the material after all? And does the whole thing depend on running together two different things the argument keeps calling "value"?

Please write this in British English. And be candid about the state of play: separate what's settled among people who work on Stoic ethics from what's genuinely contested, and don't dress up your own preferred reconstruction as the consensus. I have a suspicion the room is split between people who think preferred indifferents solve this and people who think they paper over it, and I'd like to know whether that suspicion is right before Thursday.
