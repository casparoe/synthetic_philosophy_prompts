We're doing a unit on human enhancement in my ethics elective and my teacher is convinced there's a clean line between therapy and enhancement — fixing a disease versus upgrading someone who's already healthy — and that the line is what makes enhancement sketchy. I tried to argue him out of it and I think I blew up my own side. Here's the argument:

(1) A medical intervention counts as therapy only if it restores normal functioning.
(2) "Normal functioning" is always relative to some reference class — a species, an age group, a population.
(3) Which reference class you use isn't settled by the body: growth hormone for a really short kid is therapy measured against kids below the first percentile and enhancement measured against the average kid.
(4) So whether something is therapy depends partly on a choice we make, not just on facts about the drug.
(5) So the therapy/enhancement line isn't a real joint in the world, its just wherever we happen to draw it.
(6) And if the line is only a drawing exercise, the objection that enhancement is wrong because it goes beyond healing can't be doing any work, since it would sweep in glasses, vaccines and learning to read, all of which push people above some reference class.
(7) So there's no principled way to object to enhancement.

He read it and said (7) is obviously false — some enhancements are exactly the ones worth objecting to, like rich parents buying their kid a better memory while everyone else just queues — and that if the conclusion is false, one of my steps has to be broken. I'd much rather keep (3) and (5), those are the interesting ones. So which step do I give up, or which inference, if the steps are all fine on their own? Why did it look harmless when I wrote it, and what do I actually lose by dropping it — does my teacher get his bright line back, or only a fuzzier version of it that can't support the weight he puts on it? And if you think nothing's broken, tell me which conclusion I'm supposed to swallow instead.
