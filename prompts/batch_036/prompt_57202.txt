**Section 3: joints you can only cut with a knife you made**

The debate about natural kinds is normally staged as one question asked at one level: either there are groupings the world itself licenses, or we do the grouping. I want to suggest both sides share an assumption that makes the argument unwinnable, namely that a candidate kind either is or isn't a joint, simpliciter. What's missing from the setup is a variable for the discriminating power of the apparatus doing the looking.

Call a *resolution regime* a pair: (a) a set of measurement capacities — which differences are detectable, at what precision, over what samples — and (b) the inferential license a discipline attaches to those detections, i.e. what you're permitted to conclude from a difference you can measure. Then:

(R1) A grouping is *forced within a regime* if, given that regime's discriminating power and sampling, the boundaries fall out rather than being chosen. Internal to the regime there is a fact about where the cluster stops.

(R2) A regime sits on a *stable rung* if moving one rung finer or one rung coarser doesn't dissolve the grouping into noise but refines or coarsens it along the same axes.

The view: kind-hood is forcedness plus ladder-stability, and it is regime-relative in exactly the amount (R2) permits and not one bit more. This is meant to be a third option rather than a compromise, because it asserts two things that pull against each other — the boundaries aren't ours to set, and they aren't regime-independent either.

Why think anything like this is true. First case, and the one I trust least but find most suggestive: mineral species are individuated by composition and crystal structure, and there's a formal approval process for new ones. What interests me is the direction of travel. As analytical technique improves — detecting trace substitution, resolving ordered from disordered arrangements in a lattice — material that was one species gets split into several, and sometimes a split is later walked back when the distinguishing feature turns out to be an artifact of the method: contamination, beam damage, that sort of thing. (Flag it if I've got the shape of this wrong — I'm going on a general impression, not case studies.) If it's roughly right it's a ladder in the literal sense: rungs correspond to discriminating power, boundaries at each rung are forced, and stability is what seperates a genuine split from a technique-induced ghost. Note that the realist can't say the old lumping was simply false, because the old regime's boundary was forced too; and the anti-realist can't say the new split was stipulation, because it wasn't up to anybody.

Second case: the gap in the radius distribution of small transiting exoplanets — a relative scarcity of planets somewhere between roughly one-and-a-half and two Earth radii (treat those numbers as suspect, I'm writing from memory and will check them), which is treated as a real feature and given physical explanations about atmospheric loss rather than filed as bookkeeping noise. But whether there's a joint there depends on the detection regime, since transit surveys sample unevenly in radius and period, and there's a live argument that at least part of the deficit is selection rather than formation physics. Here's the part I care about: if it's a selection effect, the gap is not a *false* joint. The sampled distribution really does have that shape, and inferences drawn from it will keep working for further sampled planets. What fails is the license to extrapolate to the unsampled population. That is precisely what a rung is: forcedness relative to a regime, bundled with a bounded inferential license, so that the artifact/joint boundary is a fact about where the license runs out. And this, I think, is a pattern pure realism struggles to accomodate — on a realist picture a selection-effect gap ought to support nothing at all, yet it clearly supports something.

Third case, and the one I most want to make work because it's the standard example everyone reaches for: biological species concepts. The biological and the phylogenetic concepts give incompatible boundaries for the same organisms. On my account that does not automatically make them two rungs of one ladder, and this is where the view earns its keep, since it can say when regime-relativism stops. If the schemes stand in a systematic refinement relation — each phylogenetic species a sub-unit of some interbreeding population, or the reverse — they're rungs and both are forced at their level. If they cross-cut unstably, with no systematic refinement in either direction, then at least one of them isn't tracking a mechanism and the question 'which concept is the right one?' is well-posed after all. So the account doesn't dissolve the species debate, it relocates it: the substantive issue becomes the shape of the ladder rather than the choice of concept.

*Objection 1.* This is interest-relativity with extra steps: purposes pick the rung, so purposes do all the work, and forcedness-at-a-rung is just the trivial observation that once you fix your interests you get determinate answers. Reply: interests enter twice, and only once do they do metaphysical work. Which rung you climb is interest-driven and, fine, conventional; where the boundary falls once you're on the rung is not, and it's the second thing that makes a grouping projectible. The interest-relativity view has no room for that asymmetry, which is why it can't explain why some interest-relative groupings support counterfactuals and others don't. (I'll concede that interests may also shape what counts as 'the same axes' in R2, and I don't yet know how much damage that concession does.)

*Objection 2.* This is the one I can't currently answer. Ladder-stability is a local relation between adjacent rungs. So A–B can be compatible, B–C compatible, and yet A–C incompatible: a chain of individually unobjectionable refinements that adds up to a change of subject. If that's possible, (R2) doesn't do the work I need, because whether a grouping is stable depends on how far along the ladder you look and there's no principled place to stop looking. Two moves are available. Bite the bullet and make kind-hood graded, which defangs the view considerably. Or argue that the failure case is detectable in principle because at some rung the axes themselves change — you stop refining along the same dimensions and start measuring something else — and a change of axes is a sharp-ish event rather than a gradual one. I lean toward the second but I can't shake the feeling that it just restates the problem in nicer vocabulary.
