I sit on the Methods Review Panel of an interdisciplinary social-science institute, and I would like you to draft the written opinion I will circulate to my colleagues before Thursday's meeting. Please approach the task as a philosopher of social science with expertise in the methodological individualism/holism debate and in interventionist accounts of causal explanation; think carefully, and begin with a short brainstorming section — not part of the memo itself — in which you lay out the candidate decision rules that are jointly consistent with our record and what each of them implies for the application at hand.

Our panel does not assess feasibility or policy relevance. Its sole charge is whether the causal-explanatory vocabulary in a project's methods section is admissible. Our rulings to date: (1) we rejected a proposal to explain regional variation in suicide rates by social facts — integration and regulation — said to constrain individuals from without, with individual motives treated as downstream symptoms; (2) we approved a proposal explaining seventeenth-century accumulation behavior by Protestant asceticism, on the ground that the macro-level ideational claim was cashed out in beliefs about a calling and about salvation that entered each actor's practical reasoning; (3) we approved unconditionally an account of the emergence of a medium of exchange in which the explanans is nothing but the unintended aggregate of individual substitutions; (4) we split on Marx, rejecting a paper that explained the persistence of legal and religious ideas by their function in stabilizing class rule — treating a class as an entity with needs and strategies — while approving a companion paper on the same phenomena that derived those outcomes from individual jurists' and officeholders' career incentives; (5) we recorded but declined to adopt the peg-and-board argument, on which macro-level facts about rigidity and dimensions explain an outcome that no micro-description would explain except at prohibitive cost, noting that the example involves no agents and leaving open whether a social analogue could exist.

The application is an agent-based model of a liquidity crisis. Every entity is a trader or a fund governed by individually specified rules — margin constraints, redemption terms, fire-sale pricing, imitation of observed positions — and no collective agent is posited anywhere; the two macro variables in the paper's explanation, aggregate market depth and system-wide interconnectedness, are computed from those same individual states. What is unusual is the intervention study: many micro-rulesets differing sharply from those observed in the real market produce the same crash once the two macro variables are held fixed, whereas no modification of the micro rules within a fixed macro envelope averts it. The authors conclude in prose that the macro variables do the causal work and the micro specification merely sets initial conditions. One colleague insists this is precedent (1) in new clothes; another insists it is precedent (5), finally arrived in our own domain.

The memo should run to roughly eight hundred words and should recommend approve, approve with specified conditions, or reject; identify the controlling precedent and explain why it controls better than the closest competing analog; engage the strongest objection to your recommendation from the individualist side of the panel rather than caricaturing it; and state the general principle the panel would be committing itself to by ruling as you advise, in terms specific enough that it would also decide a future application in which the macro variables are not defined as functions of the simulated individuals at all.
