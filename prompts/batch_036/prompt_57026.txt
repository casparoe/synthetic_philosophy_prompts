So — I'm an actor, mid-thirties, mostly regional theatre, trained in a conservatory program that was basically Meisner with a lot of Stanislavski underneath it. A few weeks ago I was playing a role where my character's kid drowns offstage, and during the third preview I lost it for real: actual ugly crying, had to turn upstage so nobody could see my face, and my scene partner had to carry the beat until I could get a line out. Afterwards my girlfriend, who's two years into a philosophy PhD, told me over dinner that what I'd just done was irrational, because I knew none of it was real, and that there's a whole literature on this called the paradox of fiction. I want to understand the argument she was invoking, but every summary I've found is written in a language I don't own — belief, judgment, propositional attitudes, whether the object of an emotion exists. That is not how I've ever thought about what I do at 7:30 on a Tuesday.

Here's what I'm after: restate the whole debate in the vocabulary I actually work in, as faithfully as you can manage, and then give me a residue report on what didn't survive the trip. My vocabulary, so you know what you've got to work with: given circumstances, the magic if and the "as if," sense memory and affective memory, substitution, repetition, objectives and obstacles, playing actions and verbs, the fourth wall, public solitude, psychological gesture, inside-out versus outside-in approaches, breaking, and the fact that it has to happen eight times a week on a schedule whether you feel like it or not. Take the puzzle premise by premise — the one saying we have genuine feelings for people we know aren't real, the one saying a feeling requires believing its object exists, the one saying nobody who takes something to be fiction also believes it's real — and for each of them tell me what it sounds like rendered into those terms, or whether it can't be said in them at all. Then do the same for the standard answers: the one that says we don't literally feel anything but a quasi-version of it that we imagine to be real, the one that says imagination on its own is enough and no belief is required, the one that says we're partly under an illusion while we watch, and the one that just denies emotions need belief to begin with.

The residue report is the part I actually care about, so don't skimp on it to get to the translation. For each chunk: does it map cleanly, does it map only if you bend it (and tell me how far you bent it), or is there no expression for it in my framework whatsoever? Two specific questions. First — does actor training have any way of saying a feeling is genuine but unjustified? We say truthful, repeatable, connected, appropriate to the given circumstances, but I have never once heard anybody in a rehearsal room ask whether an emotion was warranted, and I want to know if that's a real hole in my framework or a sign that the philosophers' question is smuggling in belief-desire machinery that isn't load-bearing here. Second — the debate seems to be about audiences, people sitting in the dark recieving something, and I'm at the other end of it, generating the thing live, on cue, with the possibility of failing to, in a body that still has to hit its marks while it grieves. Tell me which premises survive that swap and which ones are quietly assuming a spectator, and whether "we know it isn't real" is even an accurate description of what an actor knows mid-scene. And while you're in there: our own tradition has Diderot's paradox of the actor, the claim that the great performer feels nothing at all — is that the same puzzle in costume, or a genuinely different one, and what gets lost translating between the two? Two ground rules. Don't import the philosophy's assumptions into my vocabulary and then score that as a successful translation; if a term like given circumstances is doing real work in the mapping, say what work it's doing rather than waving at it as a metaphor for belief. And flag every place you fudge — the fudges are more interesting to me then the clean mappings. No bibliography or references, I'm not writing a paper. Please write in American English.
