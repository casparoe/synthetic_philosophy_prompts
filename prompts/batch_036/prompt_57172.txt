I'm in my third year of a PhD in political philosophy and I'm applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship. The application asks for a proposal of roughly fifteen hundred words, and the reading committee is mixed — two philosophers and a political scientist who works on legislative bargaining — so it has to be legible to someone who doesn't read metaethics for a living. My supervisor's verdict on the draft I showed her was that it is either too broad to finish or not really philosophy at all, and I'd like to find out whether it can be made precise enough to survive that objection.

The half-formed idea is this. A good deal of the discussion of compromise proceeds on the model of an exchange: two parties with conflicting positions find a deal that leaves each better off than continued conflict, and the moral work is done by the fact that both of them consent to it, because the gains from trade justify the compromise. What I keep running into is that this picture quietly assumes something like a settled pre-trade endowment. It assumes each party morally owns what it is trading away, and that what it gives up is a loss in the same currency as what it receives. In the cases that actually motivate the literature, both assumptions look shaky. When a minority agrees to the state's funding or toleration of a practice it regards as a grave wrong, in exchange for a majority concession on some unrelated matter, the minority is not giving up a preference; it is tolerating what it takes to be a wrong, and the gain it receives is denominated in interests while the cost it pays is denominated in commitments. There may be no common metric in which the deal is a net improvement for that party, even though the party would sign it and even though everyone agrees it is better than the alternative. And separately, the situation only looks like a trade at all because of a prior allocation of who gets to decide what — an allocation the parties disagree about, and disagreeing about which is part of what the dispute is. So the framework's central explanatory device, mutual gain, may be borrowed from a picture of bargaining that already presupposes the dispute's resolution.

Please turn that into the proposal. I want a precise statement of the research question — roughly, for which moral disagreements can gains-from-trade reasoning justify a compromise without presupposing a settlement of the disagreement itself, and what does the justification of compromise look like where it cannot — followed by an account of why the existing positions leave this open: the mutual-advantage tradition, the dirty-hands and integrity-based objections to compromise, and the treatments of compromise as a second-best device under conditions of reasonable disagreement. Then the approach, and here I want you to be specific rather than programmatic. I have in mind a small set of worked cases (a religious exemption arrangement, a climate burden-sharing agreement, a transitional-justice deal in which amnesty is traded for truth), together with a semi-formal model that keeps the two currencies distinct instead of collapsing them into a single utility scale, used to test whether the model returns the verdicts we want. Say why that rather than the obvious alternatives — pure casuistry, a fully formal bargaining model, or a conceptual analysis of the word 'compromise'. I also want the proposal to address a worry I can't shake: that the two-currencies point is just the old incommensurability objection dressed up in bargaining vocabulary, and that the project needs a way of telling whether it is saying anything more than that. State what a successful result would look like (my guess is a characterization of the class of disagreements in which trade talk is legitimate, plus a diagnosis of what goes wrong elsewhere, which I suspect turns on complicity rather than on loss), what would count as failure — for instance, if the model collapses back into a single-metric picture, or if my distinction turns out not to change any verdicts about any real case — and where I should cut to make it feasible for one person in twelve months.

Don't produce a bibliography, a literature review by citation, or a list of further reading; I'll attach the references myself. Where you do gesture at positions in the literature, describe them rather than attributing them to named authors or works. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold, and no tables — the way one would write a letter to the committee.
