I am a software engineer, twelve years in, and for the last three I have worked on the trust and safety side of a mid-sized video platform. We are currently drafting a written escalation policy for the moderation teams, and I have been asked to contribute the section on when a moderator should defer to somebody more senior rather than make the call herself. Before putting anything into policy language I tried to set out what I actually believe about relying on other people's moral judgements, on the grounds that a document should not quietly contradict the views of the person who wrote it. A friend of mine who did a philosophy degree offered to attack the draft, but she is away for three weeks and I would like a check now. I have no formal training, so I have deliberately written everything in my own words rather than in vocabulary I have picked up second-hand; please treat any resulting clumsiness as mine and not as a hidden objection, and do not assume I mean something more sophisticated than what is on the page.

Here is the list as it currently stands.

1. Testimony is not a second-class way of forming beliefs. If somebody reliable tells me something and I have no reason to doubt them, I can come to know it, and I cannot see what is supposed to be different about moral claims: being told that a certain practice exploits people looks to me no worse, epistemically, than being told what happened at last night's incident review. I am unpersuaded that there is anything peculiar about being told that an action is wrong.

2. That said, knowing and understanding strike me as different achievements, and I am content to say that somebody can know a thing is wrong without having any idea why. A colleague of mine can tell you, correctly, that a particular notification design preys on loneliness; she could not tell you how it does so, or what precisely the user loses. That combination seems to me perfectly coherent, if a little unsatisfying.

3. Credit for doing the right thing tracks the reasoning, not the result. I think an agent deserves praise only in so far as the considerations that make the action right actually figured in how she arrived at it. Ending up in the right place by accident, or by obedience, is not the same achievement as seeing your way there.

4. A concrete case. Last year a junior moderator escalated a video because a senior colleague had told her to, saying only that the person appearing in it had been pushed into doing so. The junior had not watched the whole thing and could not have said what the pressure consisted in. Afterwards I said, and still think, that she did exactly the right thing and that she is owed just as much credit as the colleague who spotted it unaided.

5. Another case, in which I said the opposite and cannot see the difference between them. Some years ago I was deciding whether to join a team whose product I suspected was net-harmful. I asked a friend whose judgement I trust; she said no; I did not join. I never learned her reasons and could not now reproduce them. At the time I told myself that I had merely landed on the right answer and that there was nothing whatever to my credit in it.

6. Circumstances matter. Where I have the time and the decision is genuinely mine, I think I owe it to the situation to work out the reasons myself rather than ask somebody; where something is live — an incident in progress, a queue backing up — I think the responsible move is to defer to whoever has handled it before, and I do not regard that as a moral cop-out.

7. I do not, however, believe in moral experts. There is no certification, no track record anybody can audit, and the people I most admire in this area disagree with one another as often as anyone else does. What I do believe is that some people are reliably better at these judgements than others, and that it is worth real effort to be around them.

8. Finally, I think there is something faintly contemptible about outsourcing one's moral thinking wholesale — a failure to take the subject seriously. I would rather my children argued with me than agreed with me on somebody's authority.

What I want is an audit rather than a summary. Go through these and tell me which of them genuinely pull against each other and which merely look as though they do; my expectation is that a good part of the list is more consistent than it felt while I was writing it, and that one or two places are considerably worse than I have noticed. Rank the real tensions by severity instead of presenting them as equally bad, and for each say what would have to be given up or restated and what the restatement costs me — I would rather surrender something I care about than paper over the problem. Where a tension can be dissolved by a distinction, tell me whether that distinction is already doing work elsewhere on the list or whether you have had to supply it yourself, and what supplying it commits me to that I have not already accepted. If a single revision would clear up more than one of the problems at once, identify it and state the price. I am not attached to any individual item, and I have deliberately not indicated which ones I would defend first, because I do not know. Two further requests: do not simply restate my views in better language, and end by telling me whether whatever survives the audit could coherently underwrite a written policy instructing moderators to defer in urgent cases, since that is what I have to draft on Monday.
