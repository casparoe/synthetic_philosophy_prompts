I'm in the second year of a neurology residency, and I co-run a small philosophy-of-mind reading group at our hospital, mostly attended by trainees looking for an excuse to argue after clinic. Last month an attending from the chronic pain service came to present, and the spine of his talk was an analogy he also uses in patient education: pain is a smoke alarm. On his telling, the alarm's sole job is to report that there is a fire somewhere in the house; its loudness is not part of the message it carries, since a discreet indicator light on the wall would convey exactly the same information to anyone paying attention; and much of what reaches a pain clinic is the alarm going off because somebody burnt toast, which is a defect in the alarm rather than a reason to conclude that alarms are intrinsically bad. He pushed the analogy a long way. Because the aversive component is, on his view, a redundant amplifier bolted onto a perfectly serviceable signalling channel, he argued two things: that pain is bad only instrumentally, in virtue of being unreliable and disabling, and not in virtue of how it feels; and that a hypothetical drug — he called it the asymbolia pill — which left nociceptive discrimination fully intact while abolishing the suffering, would be an unqualified improvement, delivering the information and discarding the noise. As evidence that the two components really come apart he cited the actual syndrome of pain asymbolia: patients who, typically after lesions involving the insular cortex, can identify a noxious stimulus as painful yet show no withdrawal and no emotional response to match, remain unconcerned about their own deficit, and fail to learn protective or escape behaviour. To this he adds a distinction he leans on throughout — signal failure, where the alarm rings and there is no fire, versus receiver failure, where there is a fire and the alarm rings correctly but nobody minds it. The pill, he says, produces only receiver failure, and receiver failure is benign so long as the information arrives.

Our group pushed back from two directions and the discussion got stuck. On the syndrome: some philosophers have argued that the asymbolic patient does not feel pain at all, that what survives the lesion is a bare, non-painful bodily sensation which the patient can classify because classifying does not require suffering — in which case the syndrome is not an instance of information without bother but an instance of the channel itself having been destroyed, and it cannot be recruited as evidence for the pill. On the analogy: a smoke alarm addresses an agent who already does not want the house to burn down, so it supplies information to a standing desire; whereas there is a live view on which pain does not inform a pre-existing desire to avoid bodily damage so much as constitute the motivation, its content being something closer to a demand to stop what you are doing than to a report about the state of the body. If that view is right, the alarm model mislocates the unpleasantness, treating it as a volume knob on a message when it may be the only thing that makes the message a message about the body at all.

Could you audit the smoke-alarm analogy properly? I would like an explicit correspondence table — fire, sensor, wiring, the sound and its loudness, the person who hears it, the toast, disabling the unit, the house — and then a separation of the disanalogies that actually bear on his two conclusions from the ones that are merely decorative. In particular, I want to know what the analogy quietly imports that pain may lack: for instance, that a message can be delivered equally well over any channel, that the recipient's caring about the content is supplied from somewhere else, that there is a designer whose intentions fix what the device is for, and that the badness of a faulty alarm is a badness of unreliability rather than a badness of the experience it produces. Does either conclusion survive once those imports are on the table, and does the signal-failure/receiver-failure distinction really isolate a harmless case, or does it beg the question about whether aversiveness is separable from the signal? Finally, if you think there is a better analogy available, propose one and put it through the same test: hunger, itch, nausea, grief, and a bodily command are all candidates, and I want to know which of them can accommodate both the asymbolic patient and the pill, and what the choice of analogy ends up committing you to about why pain is bad in the first place.
