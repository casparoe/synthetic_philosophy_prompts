So next month I'm giving a departmental colloquium — 45 minutes plus a long Q&A — at a place that's very strong in metaphysics and only okay in ethics, and the organiser has warned me that maybe a third of the room won't work in value theory at all. My paper defends an account of the procreation asymmetry that I've been calling the custodial view, and I need a prep sheet for the question period rather than another defense of the paper. Sketch of the argument so you know what the objections should be aimed at:

1. Reasons of beneficence aren't freestanding. They only arise inside an actual relation of custody over a subject — a relation in which that subject's welfare is *yours to look after*. Call this the custody condition.
2. A merely possible person stands in no such relation to anyone, so there's no welfare that is anyone's to promote. Hence no reason to bring about a happy life. Not because happy lives aren't good — I'll grant in the talk that they're good impersonally — but because a good that isn't anybody's to look after doesn't generate a reason for anybody in particular to produce it.
3. Procreating is the act of *entering* a custody relation, and you can't permissibly enter a relation you already know you won't be able to discharge. A life of severe, unrelievable suffering is exactly that, so there's a decisive reason against creating it.
4. The asymmetry then falls out with no person-affecting restriction on value, and without having to claim that misery is bad-for-someone while happiness is somehow not good-for-anyone.

The thing that keeps me up is that the machinery in (1) and (3) is mine and there's no existing defence of it I can lean on, so unlike the standard asymmetry views I can't gesture at somebody else's replies when I get stuck.

Give me the eight or nine objections I'll actually get from *this* room, in roughly the order they'll come, and phrase each one the way that subgroup would phrase it — the metaphysicians are not going to put it the way the population ethicists would. I specifically expect a necessitist to go after step 2: if possible people exist as non-concrete objects, isn't there already a subject for the custody relation to latch onto, and doesn't my view smuggle in contingentism as an undefended premise? Tell me how bad that actually is as opposed to how bad it sounds at the podium. There's also a health economist in the department who models climate damages and will want to know whether I've just given people a licence to discount the wellbeing of anyone who doesn't exist yet, plus someone in reproductive policy who'll ask about existing children — adoption, fostering — and whether my view makes procreation merely permissible or actively second-best, which is a political fight I do not want to have in a colloquium. And somebody will try to collapse me into Benatar while somebody else tries to collapse me into the older person-affecting asymmetry; say which of those two collapses is the real threat and give me the cleanest wedge.

For each objection: how damaging it is, on a scale from "I have nothing" to "two sentences and we move on"; the two-or-three-sentence reply I should give in the room; and a blunt note on where that reply is soft. Flag which ones I should concede up front in the talk to buy goodwill, and tell me the single reply that has to land for the paper to survive the afternoon. Finish with the question I'm praying nobody asks. Please don't send me a reading list or citations — I know the literature, its my sense of which of these actually bites that I dont trust.
