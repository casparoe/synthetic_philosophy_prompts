Some background so you know where I'm coming from: I spent about forty years doing condensed matter theory, mostly phase transitions and the renormalization group, and I retired three years ago. Since then I've been reading philosophy of biology as a way of staying useful, and I'm auditing a philosophy of science seminar at the state university, where the younger people have been very patient with me. I've got a set of notes for an essay that I'm supposed to circulate to the group in a couple weeks, and I'd like a second pair of eyes on them before I embarrass myself.

The notes push one big claim, which is that the units of selection debate — genes versus individuals versus groups — isn't a substantive disagreement at all but a disagreement about coarse-graining. In a critical system you can ask what the degrees of freedom are, and the answer changes depending on the scale you look at. At a fixed point the microscopic detail becomes irrelevant and you get scale-invariant effective descriptions; away from it, which description counts as the right one is a function of your measurement scale. I want to say selection works the same way. Asking whether the gene or the group is the unit is like asking whether the correct description of a magnet is spins or domains: both are true, they answer different questions, and neither is more fundamental in a way that changes what you can predict. Someone has probably said something like this before and I don't care. I want to know whether it's right.

Here's the arguement as it currently stands. Dawkins's replicator/vehicle distinction is a criterion for picking out a privileged level, the one with high-fidelity copying and long lineage persistence. Godfrey-Smith's criteria for Darwinian individuality — the usual variation, heredity, differential fitness triad, plus things like reproductive bottlenecks and integration — are, as far as I can tell, the same criterion in different clothes: they're the conditions under which a level stops being sensitive to what's going on underneath it, which is exactly what a fixed point is. So the two frameworks agree with each other, and they both say the interesting cases (aspen clones, siphonophores, Dictyostelium, biofilms, those snowflake yeast lineages from the lab evolution work) have no fact of the matter about their level, because there's no scale at which the description stops depending on the level below. Hence the whole fight is verbal.

Where I most want pushback is the standard objection that it can't be verbal, because the two sides make different predictions, or at least have different tractability. I've heard the Price equation accused of being nothing but bookkeeping while trait-group models are said to be genuinely empirical, and I've also heard that inclusive fitness and multilevel formalisms are only equivalent under restrictive assumptions like additivity and weak selection. My answer is that when two models are intertranslatable and never differ about what happens next, calling one of them the real unit adds nothing — but I'm aware that intertranslatability is doing an awful lot of work in that sentence, and I haven't earned it.

So: tell me whether the physics analogy is load-bearing or just decorative, flag anywhere I've gotten the biology or the philosophy wrong, and say whether the last step actually follows from the earlier ones or whether I've quietly slid from "the level is scale-relative" to "the level doesn't matter." And if you can, give me one concrete case where a gene's-eye and a group-level description would disagree about what happens next — an actual prediction, not a redescription of the same prediction. If you can produce that, my essay is in trouble and I'd rather find out now than in front of the seminar.
