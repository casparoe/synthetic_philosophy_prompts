I am a doctoral candidate in the philosophy of economics writing a chapter on Keynes's *Treatise on Probability*, and I have hit a distinction that secondary writers keep collapsing. I would like a careful, self-contained explanation of what Keynes's notion of the weight of an argument is supposed to be doing, and of why it resists the standard modern translations of it.

First, the internal account. Keynes treats probability as a logical relation between a body of evidence and a conclusion, and weight as a further property of arguments — roughly, the amount of relevant evidence standing behind that relation. Please explain: (i) why two arguments can carry the same probability while differing in weight, with a concrete case; (ii) what weight is supposed to be doing in practical reasoning, given that on Keynes's own account a weightier argument need not shift the probability balance at all; and (iii) why he denies that weights can always be compared or measured, and what he thinks follows for the kind of theory probability admits of. Here I want a tension addressed that I cannot resolve myself: on some readings weight is monotonic in the quantity of evidence, while on others it tracks relevance or informativeness, and these come apart, so that further evidence might leave weight unchanged or even reduce it. Which reading is defensible, and can both be held at once?

Second, the translation question, which is where my project is stuck. Three identifications are routinely proposed and I suspect all three are too quick: that Keynesian weight is what imprecise-probability theorists capture as a set of probability functions rather than a single one; that it is the same notion as Knight's unmeasurable uncertainty, given that *Risk, Uncertainty and Profit* and the *Treatise* appeared in the same year; and that it is simply second-order uncertainty — uncertainty about one's own probabilities. My working formalization is that weight measures the spread, or cardinality, of the set of probability functions compatible with the evidence, and that ambiguity aversion is the behavioural shadow of low weight. Say precisely where this goes wrong, if it does. In particular: can a Keynesian argument be both maximally weighty and numerically sharp, and can an argument be very weighty while yielding an interval rather than a number? And since Keynesian probability is a relation among propositions rather than a state of an agent, is "weight" even the kind of thing that can be identified with an agent's imprecise credence? Do the same for Knight, whose contrast is drawn inside an account of markets and insurable risk rather than inside a logic of inference.

Third, Ramsey. Explain what his objection to Keynes amounts to — that no such objective logical relations exist, that Keynes never connects them to degrees of belief, or both — and then give the strongest Keynesian reply: the sense in which Ramsey is charged with changing the subject from the warrant an argument supplies to the coherence of an agent's betting dispositions. I want to know whether that reply is a genuine rebuttal or a restatement of the disagreement.

Finally, the continuity question. Does weight of argument survive into the *General Theory*? The conventions and animal spirits of Chapter 12 look psychological rather than logical, and Keynes's 1937 article restating the argument for the *Quarterly Journal of Economics* marks off uncertain knowledge from merely probable knowledge by appeal to cases where no calculation is possible at all. Is the later material the *Treatise*'s weight applied to long-term expectation, or a different notion doing similar work — and what turns on the answer?

Two constraints. I do not want a bibliography or a list of references, and I would rather you paraphrase than quote; I have the texts in front of me and am interested in the argument. Where the interpretive literature is genuinely divided, say so and say which side you are arguing from, rather than writing as though there were a settled reading.
