I work as a software engineer on scheduling and constraint-satisfaction systems, and for the past year I have been sitting in on a reading group that a colleague runs out of her house: seven of us, every other week, one of whom is an intensive-care nurse and none of whom has any formal philosophy training, myself included. We spent the last several months on whether genuine moral dilemmas exist, by which the group means cases where an agent is morally required to do each of two things and cannot do both, and we have gone around the same circle perhaps eight times without anyone moving. The deniers press what I gather is the classical line: a rational moral system cannot issue contradictory demands, and since one ought to do only what one can do, an irresolvable pair of all-things-considered obligations is not a tragic feature of the moral life but a sign that the case has been misdescribed. On this view the conflict is always between merely prima facie duties, in something like W.D. Ross's sense, which everyone concedes can conflict, and never between duties proper; and where a conflict looks genuinely irresolvable, as in the old rationalist treatments I was pointed to by one of our members, the appearance of perplexity is supposed to be traceable to the agent's own prior fault. The affirmers reply that this explains away precisely the data that made the question interesting. They lean on Bernard Williams's complaint that the consistency requirement loses the phenomenon it was meant to account for, and on Ruth Barcan Marcus's argument that the moral 'ought' simply does not agglomerate, so that nothing incoherent follows from being under two obligations you cannot jointly discharge, together with the point that the surviving guilt, the refusal of consolation, and the sense that something is still owed after the choice is evidence that neither obligation has evaporated. Both camps have worked through the same cases, from Sartre's student to Agamemnon at Aulis to Sophie's Choice, and both concede the cases are tragic. That is what puzzles me: they seem to agree about everything checkable and still not to be having the same argument.

My working hypothesis, which I would like tested rather than flattered, is that this is not a substantive disagreement about morality at all but two different questions run under one label. One side is asking whether a normative system can be consistent by construction, which is a formal question about which principles the operator expressing obligation is supposed to satisfy and about how strong a principle of agglomeration one is entitled to; the other is asking what an agent is left owing after an irreparable choice, which is a question about blame, regret, and the practices that surround them. If that is right, the impasse is not evidence that the question is hard but evidence that nobody in the room has said which question they are answering, and further case-mongering will never close it. An alternative I have not managed to rule out is that the disagreement is genuinely verbal, turning on whether the phrase 'morally required' is meant to name a single all-things-considered verdict or a family of considerations that resist combination into one; a third possibility, which our nurse argues for forcefully and I cannot answer, is that the two sides simply differ about what counts as evidence, one treating the structure of a moral theory as authoritative and the other treating the reported experience of people who have made such choices as authoritative.

What I want is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Please say which kind of disagreement this is, defend the diagnosis by reference to what the two sides actually argue, and tell me honestly if the debate has stalled for a duller reason than any of my hypotheses, such as the two sides having never agreed on what a moral principle is. Then tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis: a theorem about the principles of obligation, a new species of case, a distinction that has been missed, a change of question, or nothing at all, in which case please say why the group ought to stop meeting about it. Finally, I would like you to be blunt about an analogy I keep reaching for. In my day job an infeasible constraint set and a cost function with conflicting terms are different objects with different remedies, and soft constraints, which can be violated at a price, behave nothing like hard ones. Whether morality contains anything resembling a soft constraint that leaves a residue no choice discharges strikes me as the real engine of this argument, but I suspect the engineering vocabulary may be distorting the issue rather than clarifying it, and I would rather be told so. Please write the answer in British English.
