I'm a junior and I sit on my school's peer mediation board — six of us students plus one teacher who signs off on whatever we agree to. We get two or three cases a month: someone cheated and got a friend to lie for them, a screenshot from a group chat, a team doing some humiliating thing to a freshman. We're supposed to get a real apology out of the person instead of just a punishment, and we get maybe 40 minutes in a room with everybody.

Our advisor says we keep accidentally making people feel ashamed instead of guilty, and that those are two totally different moral emotions — guilt is about what you did, shame is about you as a person, shame makes you hide or lash out, guilt makes you try to fix it. But she also says some philosophers think shame is the deeper one because its tied to who you want to be, so its not even obvious which of the two we should be aiming at. She won't tell us how to steer it.

Can you write us a procedure we can reuse every time: what to work out before the session, what to say and not say in it, how to read from the person's reactions which of the two emotions is actually showing up, and the point where we should stop and hand it to an adult because it isn't ours to decide. Then run it on two cases — the cheating one and the team hazing one — and don't soften it if one comes out an answer we're going to hate. Mark which steps are just judgment dressed up as a checklist. And separately: can a school actually decide as a policy to run on guilt instead of shame, or is that not something a culture gets to choose?
