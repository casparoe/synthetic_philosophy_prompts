You are a professional philosopher whose specialty is the theory of well-being — in particular hedonism, the nature of pleasure and pain, and the relation between desire and value. I am a doctoral candidate writing the central chapter of my dissertation on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism, and I am about to circulate a statement of my own position to my supervisory committee. My commitments are these.

1. Experiential hedonism about welfare: how well a life goes for the person living it is fixed entirely by the balance of pleasant over unpleasant experience within it. Achievement, knowledge, friendship, and virtue matter only insofar as they produce that balance.
2. What makes a pleasant experience good for its subject is intrinsic to the experience — a matter of how it feels, not of any belief, judgment, or desire the subject takes toward it.
3. I take the heterogeneity problem to be a genuine difficulty rather than a pseudo-problem. Warm bread after a cold walk, the resolution of a chord in a Bach chorale, the shock and release of open-water swimming, the click of a proof falling into place: I can find no phenomenal ingredient these share, and the familiar reply that pleasure is a single raw feel, differently toned, has always struck me as a bare assertion.
4. My working solution is to identify the pleasant experiences as those the subject, while undergoing them, does not want to stop and would want more of. This gives the hedonist a principled and unified extension without requiring a shared quality.
5. I reject desire-satisfaction theories of welfare. What decides it for me is unnoticed frustration: the spouse deceived for years without suspecting anything, the promise broken after the promisee's death. Nothing bad happened to those people in the welfare sense, so the satisfaction of a desire cannot be what is good for its subject.
6. I want a strictly quantitative hedonism. Nothing answers to irreducibly higher and lower kinds of pleasure; what Mill's competent judges are really tracking, I think, is that certain pleasures tend to be more intense, longer-lasting, and less exposed to satiation. Talk of quality is confused talk about quantity and availability.
7. I give the experience machine no dialectical weight against hedonism. Reluctance to be wired up expresses a prejudice about authenticity together with ordinary fear of the unfamiliar; and in any case what people want is not evidence of what is good for them.
8. Methodologically, I hold that theories of welfare are assessed chiefly by how they handle concrete cases, and that considered verdicts about cases outrank theoretical economy.
