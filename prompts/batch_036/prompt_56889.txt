I am a TA for a second-year proofs course, and in three weeks I have a single seventy-five-minute section on non-constructive existence proofs. In lecture the students will have just met the two-case argument that some pair of irrationals has a rational power, without anyone saying which pair, and the six-people Ramsey argument; my section is supposed to consolidate the idea that a proof can establish that something exists while leaving you unable to point at it. I have decided to build the entire session around the strategy-stealing argument in positional games rather than treating it as one more example in a survey, because it is the case where the gap between knowing and doing is most vivid. There are twenty-six students. All have done induction, contradiction, contraposition, and the pigeonhole principle; none has taken game theory or graph theory. Two of them play serious competitive chess and one runs the board-game society, so I expect a couple of them to arrive confident that they already know what a winning strategy is and how you would find one. I have a whiteboard, a document camera, and about thirty printed 7×7 Hex boards with two colours of counter. What I want from you is not an explanation of the argument — I know it — but a plan for explaining it, and I want the plan to make the dependency structure explicit: which pieces genuinely cannot be stated until some other piece is in place, as opposed to a list of topics arranged in the conventional order. Please do not send me any readings, references, or suggested sources; I only want the pedagogy and the conceptual sequencing.

On ordering and on the example. My instinct is to have them play in pairs for ten minutes before I say anything at all, so that they feel the impossibility of constructing a winning strategy before being handed a proof that one exists; but I worry that pairs will finish convinced the game is a draw, and the fact that Hex cannot be drawn is exactly the thing I cannot prove to them at this level. Tell me whether to open with play, with a game they already know cold, or with an explicit failed construction attempt on the board, and tell me what to concede openly about the no-draw property and what to simply assume in front of them. Then: which single game should carry the whole session, and what must that example have and lack in order to do the work? I have been going back and forth between Hex, ordinary 3×3 tic-tac-toe, where the argument yields only the weaker conclusion that the first player can force at least a draw, and Chomp, which appeals to me because the opening move that gets stolen is intuitively a terrible one. But I am not sure Chomp is running on the same rail at all: in Hex the licence seems to be that an extra stone of your own can never hurt you, whereas in Chomp the stolen move is the corner square, which is swallowed by every possible reply, so the extra material is never a genuine liability in the first place. Settle that for me, say whether those are two species of one argument or two different arguments wearing the same name, tell me which example to commit to, and identify the smallest change to that example that would make the argument fail. I would also like your view on whether the swap rule that real Hex players use to offset the first-player advantage is such a breaking change, or is itself a fresh application of the same trick.

On the misconception map, which is the part I most need. I am already predicting these, and I want to know at which point in the sequence each should be headed off rather than corrected at the end: that the argument tells you how to play, so that a student who has followed it should be able to answer the question of where to put the third stone; that the strategy is literally copycat, with the whole thing collapsing the moment the mirrored cell is already occupied, because they do not see that the fallback of playing any free cell is available and, more importantly, harmless only under the monotonicity condition; that the conclusion is the first player wins at tic-tac-toe, conflating the second player has no winning strategy with the first player does in a game that admits draws; and that the argument is symmetric, so that the second player could equally steal the first player's strategy — I want a crisp way to show them exactly where the asymmetry is being used, since I suspect the free extra turn is doing all the work and they will not notice. I also expect someone to say that we were told a proof has to construct its object, and someone else to want to apply the argument to backgammon or poker. Give me a rough minute allocation across the session, one moment of genuine surprise, because my supervisor wants this section to function as recruitment for the combinatorics option, one closing question that will tell me whether the idea landed rather than merely entertained them, together with the two or three distinct wrong answers it might draw and what each of those would reveal about where the sequence failed, and finally a list of what I should deliberately leave out and announce as deferred — the topological argument that Hex cannot be drawn, determinacy for finite games of perfect information, Maker–Breaker theory, the complexity of finding the strategy, and how large a board an explicit winning strategy is actually known for — distinguishing the items worth naming and deferring from the ones I should simply not raise.
