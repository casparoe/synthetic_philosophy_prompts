I'm three years into a novel whose central character is a New Testament historian, and her crisis is methodological rather than moral: she gradually stops believing in her own tools. To get the texture of her discipline right I've worked through a fair amount of the literature on the criteria of authenticity — Käsemann's 1953 restatement of dissimilarity and the confidence that hardened around Perrin, Meier's careful staging of embarrassment (including the fact that he credits the label to Schillebeeckx, who apparently never used it, and the longer pedigree running back to Schmiedel at the end of the nineteenth century), Theissen and Winter's proposal to trade authenticity for plausibility, Porter's history of the whole apparatus, and the memory turn around Allison, with Rodríguez and Kirk pushing back on just how malleable he takes recall to be. What has struck me, though, is less the content than the shape of the argument. It has been running for seventy years, the objections are almost as old as the criteria themselves — Hooker's point that dissimilarity presupposes a knowledge of first-century Judaism and of the early church that nobody actually has was made early on and, so far as I can tell, never really answered — and still nothing settles. Both camps work from the same texts, both claim the same sayings, and the field can't even hold its own periodization steady, which is roughly Ferda's complaint about the conventional three quests. My character's despair is supposed to be intellectually respectable, not merely temperamental, so I need to know what she is actually despairing of.

Here is my working hypothesis, and I want it tested rather than flattered. My suspicion is that the criteria are not independent tests whose verdicts happen to conflict, but a partition of the evidence: multiple attestation privileges material the tradition had reasons to keep, dissimilarity privileges material it had no reason to keep, so the two are structurally anti-correlated, and any saying that scores well on one is almost guaranteed to score badly on the other. If that is right, then the disagreement between the camps is not about Jesus at all but a suppressed empirical claim about the preservation dynamics of oral tradition that nobody has ever measured. A second, related suspicion: the criteria are borrowed evidentiary rules — the textual critic's preference for the harder reading, the common lawyer's rule about statements against interest — that work in their home domains only because of scaffolding the Jesus tradition lacked, a fixed text with a copyist's economy of error, or a witness under oath facing cross-examination and penalty; in which case the whole quarrel is about transfer conditions, not about authenticity. A third: the fight between atomists, who apply the criteria pericope by pericope, and holists, who want recurrent patterns and characteristic impression, may be a dispute about what the object of reconstruction even is, conducted under a single word that one side treats as a binary verdict, another as a probability, and a third as a claim about the direction of the tradition. And a fourth, least charitable: the criteria function as a protective belt, so that a criterion's failures are always absorbed by invoking another one, and the resulting verdicts track the portrait the scholar already favored — Cynic, apocalyptic prophet, sage — with the criteria supplying the weighting after the fact.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a survey: I know what the criteria say, and I do not need them re-explained or a bibliography appended. Tell me which of my four hypotheses survives contact with what each side actually argues, and which of the familiar categories best fits the impasse — a verbal dispute over "authentic" and "historical," a difference in standards of evidence, a disagreement about which cases count as data, two groups reconstructing different objects under one heading, a hidden empirical question doing all the work, bedrock intuitions about what counts as a respectable historical inference, or simply a very hard problem that is not stalled at all. Argue for your diagnosis from the moves the participants make: what they treat as decisive, what they concede, what they refuse to count. Then tell me what would constitute progress on your account — a distinction, a formal or probabilistic treatment of the criteria's diagnostic power under stated assumptions about transmission, a change of question, new evidence of some describable kind — or why no progress should be expected and the field is rationally at rest. And because this is for a novel, end with something practical: three claims my character could make at a faculty dinner that would be true and would land, and three she could not make without being simply wrong about her own discipline. About a thousand words, and please resist the temptation to conclude that everyone is partly right.
