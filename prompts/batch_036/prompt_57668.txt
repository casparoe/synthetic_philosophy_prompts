I am the technical staff lead for a model fire and life-safety code maintained by a nonprofit standards developer. Every three years we open a revision cycle, and in each cycle we receive three to four hundred public proposals to delete or simplify an existing provision. They almost all run the same argument: no recorded rationale can be found, the requirement is redundant with something newer, it costs money, and nobody has shown it prevents anything. The committee that votes is roughly twenty volunteer engineers, fire marshals, and builders who get about four minutes per proposal on the floor; I get about thirty minutes per provision to prepare the half-page dossier they see. There is an external appeals route for anyone aggrieved by a decision to retain, and a provision we delete is not fully recoverable at the next cycle, because our code applies prospectively and construction completed in the intervening three years stays permanently grandfathered under the deleted text.

What I want to encode is Chesterton's fence — the parable, usually credited to Chesterton, about the reformer who declares a fence across the road useless and the traditionalist who replies that until the reformer can say what the fence is doing, he has no standing to tear it down — fused with the Burkean thought that a long-standing requirement is a deposit of knowledge nobody wrote down. I do not want the slogan. I want a procedure my staff can run and hand to a volunteer who has four minutes.

Write that procedure. It must satisfy the following.

1. Open with the ordered questions a staffer asks of a deletion proposal, and for each one state what counts as a sufficient answer, what counts as a failure, and what to do when the answer cannot be obtained in the time available. Include an explicit stopping rule for the search itself: when the audit has cost more than the deletion is worth, the procedure should say so and return a verdict.

2. Return one of four verdicts — delete, retain as written, re-found (keep the requirement but replace its stated rationale and possibly its scope), or hold to the next cycle — and say what each commits us to and when the fourth is merely an evasion.

3. Handle the problems that make this domain hard, without my having to name them all: that the outcome a safety provision is meant to produce is a non-event, so absence of incident data is not evidence of absence of function; that the reason a provision was adopted and the work it does now can come apart in both directions; that provisions are cited by other provisions, inspection checklists, training curricula, insurance underwriting, and licensing exams, so a deletion can silently orphan things nobody tracks; and that reversibility here is weaker than it looks, given the grandfathering rule above.

4. Treat the case of a provision whose origin is documented and ugly — adopted at the urging of a trade association whose stated aim was to exclude a class of workers from the trade — as a distinct branch, not a footnote. Tell me whether an illegitimate origin is decisive, and if it is not, say precisely what work it still does in the reasoning.

5. Mark every step that is a judgment call wearing procedure's clothes.

Then run it on three proposals.

A. A requirement, dating from the 1950s, for a manually operated alarm pull station at each exit of a class of storage occupancy. Sprinklers and automatic detection are now mandated throughout the same buildings by a newer chapter. The original committee report is missing; the requirement is cross-referenced by three other sections, appears on a widely used inspection checklist and a state licensing exam; and no adopting jurisdiction reports a single fire in twenty years in which a pull station alerted occupants — though none tracks that either.

B. A requirement that a particular exterior wall joint be sealed with material of a specified fire-resistance rating. The proposer is a manufacturer whose product does not qualify. The entire file is a one-line 1978 rationale reading that it was recommended by the committee, no incident data, and a letter from one retired inspector asserting that it matters.

C. The exclusionary-origin provision described in point 4, which is still doing real safety work and for which the qualified workforce is thin.

For at least one of these, the procedure must return a verdict I will not like, and it must not soften it. In case B, show what the dossier says when the audit stalls rather than inventing findings.

Close with the failure modes: where the procedure will misfire, its built-in tilt toward retention and what to do about a code that has accreted hundreds of dead provisions, and which of the four verdicts is most likely to be abused.

Begin with a single sentence stating the operative decision rule — what makes a provision stay and what makes it go — then give the procedure, then the three worked cases, then the failure modes. No preamble, no restatement of my situation, no closing summary, and no citations or bibliography; invoke distinctions in prose if you need them.
