Ok so this has nothing to do with philosophy technically but I think it does, and I want a document, not an opinion.

I work on infra. Our CI suite takes ~40 min and maybe 2% of runs fail on something that passes on a plain rerun. We built a little service, call it the flake ledger: every test execution gets a row (test id, sha, region, shard index, machine family, time of day, pass/fail, was-it-rerun). The service spits out a number per test and that number now does real work — release gating ("if a test's failure probability is under 2%, a red run doesn't block the release"), auto-filing bugs, and deciding which tests get quarantined.

Two of us have been fighting for months about what that number is.

- One of us thinks a probability just is a long-run relative frequency in a genuinely repeatable setup. On that view the ledger is measuring something real, "the probability this test flakes" is a fact about the world, and writing down 0.03 for a test that has never run in a new region is nonsense — there's no such setup, so no such frequency, so no such number. Also single-case talk ("what's the chance THIS run fails") is at best sloppy shorthand.
- The other thinks probability is degree of belief that has to be internally coherent or you can be Dutch-booked, that frequencies are evidence you condition on rather than what the word means, and that the first view can't get a decision off the ground — the gate has to fire on this run, right now, including for tests with no history at all.

It keeps leaking into design. Cold start is the worst: new region, zero rows. The frequency person wants the gate to abstain and block conservatively; the belief person wants a prior, shrunk toward the fleet-wide base rate until real data shows up. Then somebody came back from a conf talk about a method that outputs a threshold with a guarantee that the true failure rate is covered at least 95% of the time — proven, apparently, without any distributional assumption, just that the sequence of runs is exchangeable with whatever the future ones come from. Dov says that's obviously a constraint on what you're allowed to believe; Maya says it's a frequency guarantee wearing a costume, and the exchangeability condition is doing quiet, load-bearing work.

Write the joint statement these two should have produced after a real adversarial collaboration, where they're both smart and both arguing in good faith:

1. A numbered list of claims both could sign without wincing — about what's actually in the ledger, about the math both accept, about what the gate has to do operationally. Not mush. Each claim should say who it costs something to accept, if it does.
2. A table of the disagreements that survive. Columns: the disagreement stated in wording the frequentist would sign for their own side, the same stated in wording the subjectivist would sign for theirs, and a column saying whether it's substantive or just verbal — with a one-line reason.
3. The cruxes: the sub-questions where flipping one of them would actually move somebody. For each, give a concrete test or thought experiment both would accept as bearing on it — something you could genuinely run against the ledger is much better than a hypothetical — and pre-register each side's prediction.
4. What each of them would count as evidence against their own view, and whether there's anything at all both agree could move the needle.
5. One paragraph at the end: once you strip out the fights that turn out to be about words, what would change in the service — the estimator, the abstention rule, the cold-start behavior, the wording of the alerts.

Don't give me a bibliography or a reading list, we can't go look things up. Naming the actual positions and the standard arguments is fine and wanted. But the interesting part is the drafting: finding formulations that aren't vacuous and don't smuggle in one side's vocabulary, and working out which of these fights is real. If some chunk of it turns out to be purely verbal, say exactly which chunk and why.

And be honest about which one of the two actually matters for the system we're shipping, versus which one we should just stop bringing up in design review.
