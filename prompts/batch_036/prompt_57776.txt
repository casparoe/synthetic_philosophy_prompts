I run a small epistemology reading group — six of us, two philosophers, one psychiatrist, the rest just nosy — and this term we're doing self-knowledge, which means we've hit Moore's paradox. We got through the usual rounds: the conjunction can be true and isn't inconsistent, so the trouble can't be the content; it's something about asserting it or believing it; the commissive and omissive forms might not need the same treatment; and there's the linking-principle move where an account of the belief-level weirdness is supposed to carry over to assertion for free. Everyone could recite it by the end of week two. So for next session I want to hand them something they can't pattern-match, and I want it built to order rather than lifted out of a paper.

Here's the build. One realistic scenario — the scenario itself should be a single paragraph, no sci-fi, no drugs, no brain lesions, nothing that needs a philosophy-of-mind apparatus to make sense of — in which an ordinary, well-informed, mentally healthy speaker sincerely and correctly utters a commissive Moorean sentence: the p-but-I-don't-believe-p form, with the first conjunct flat-out true and the second a true self-ascription of a missing belief. And crucially, saying it has to be the *right* thing for her to say in that situation, not merely forgivable — she should be able to explain articulately and without irony why she doesn't believe the thing she just said. The point of the case is to separate the two levels, so it has to be generated by the situation rather than smuggled in by the wording. Please honor these bans explicitly: no tense or indexical tricks (yet, now, anymore, at the time), no quotation or reporting of somebody else's belief, no exclamatory amazement reading, no sliding between belief-that and belief-in or between believing and mere acceptance-as-a-premise, no self-deception, no repressed or unconcious belief, no dissociation or amnesia, no splitting group belief off from individual belief. My hunch — only a hunch, tell me if it's bad — is that the one place with real traction is higher-order evidence: a speaker who has genuinely conclusive first-order evidence for p and, seperately, good evidence that the belief hasn't formed in her, for a reason that doesn't make her irrational. If you can find a better route, take it.

Then show me the design works. Run the case past at least three of the standard explanations — the assertion-side family (assertion expresses belief, plus the knowledge-norm variant of it), Shoemaker's linking-principle line, and Rosenthal's denial that there's any problem at the level of belief at all — and for each give the verdict on my speaker: absurd, improper-but-excusable, or perfectly fine, with the reasoning. Then tell me which single feature of the scenario is doing the work, what happens to those verdicts if I delete it, and whether the case collapses into an utterance everyone already agrees is unremarkable. And give me the best counterattack from whichever account comes out worst — specifically the version that says she wasn't really asserting the first conjunct at all, and whether you think it lands. Last thing: an honest note on whether my constraints are jointly satisfiable. If they aren't, say which one has to go and what we lose by dropping it, rather than quietly bending one of the bans to make the case come out. No bibliography and no page cites, please — I can't check them anyway and I'd much rather you described the views than guessed at titles.
