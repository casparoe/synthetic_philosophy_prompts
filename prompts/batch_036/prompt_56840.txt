My grandad died in March and for some reason the family has decided I'm the one to write up his life for the memorial booklet — not an obituary, more like a proper two-page account of who he was that people will actually read. He was a good man but he wasnt easy: he and my dad didn't speak for about fifteen years, and only made it up near the end. My aunt thinks that whole stretch should be left out because a memorial isnt the place for it. I'm not so sure, and I've been circling the question for weeks, mostly by rereading the Plutarch lives I had on the shelf from college (they're short, and I vaguely remembered him making a point about how some throwaway remark or joke tells you more about a man than his famous victories — the small unguarded moments doing the work that the big set-pieces don't).

But before I decide anything about my grandad I want to know what I actually think about this kind of thing generally, going by my own reactions. Here they are, and they dont hang together:

- A glowing account of a missionary who never once lost his temper and gave away everything he had: admired it for about a day, changed nothing. It felt like being handed a mountain and told to climb it.
- A life of a surgeon who saved hundreds of people and was cruel to his wife and children: I think about that one constantly, especially when I'm tired and snapping at my own kids. But I dont want to be him, so I'm confused about what it's doing to me.
- A memoir where the writer was savagely honest about his own selfishness: taught me a lot, but I liked him less by the end and I didnt entirely believe the honesty either — it read like a pose.
- A life written by the subject's own daughter, obviously adoring, obviously airbrushed, and everything in it probably true: I resented the book the whole way through.
- A saint's life I picked up in a used bookshop, where the miracles and the perfect patience are just stated as fact: I expected to dismiss it and instead found it weirdly useful, like it was aimed at something other than my judgment.
- And the one I keep flip flopping on: a quiet account of an ordinary decent person, no achievements, no drama, who just kept showing up for fifty years. I couldn't finish it. Bored me. Which embarasses me, because that's exactly the person I'd most like to be.

So: is there a principle, or a small set of competing ones, that predicts when a written life actually makes the reader better — not when it's enjoyable, and not when it's accurate, but when it changes anything? State it precisely enough that each clause is tied to the case that motivates it. Then tell me which of my verdicts it can't keep and has to overturn, and what it predicts about cases I havent listed — e.g. whether a life that is knowingly flattering might beat a truthful one for some readers, whether a bad life can be instructive without being admirable, and what happens when the reader is related to the subject. Finally, apply whatever you land on to my grandad, and say whether leaving out the fifteen years makes the thing less useful or is exactly what the genre requires.
