Our decision theory reading group has asked each member to circulate a one-page statement of their commitments ahead of the session on alternatives to expected utility theory, so that discussion can begin from what people actually hold rather than from the textbook dialectic. Mine concerns risk-weighted expected utility, and it runs as follows. (1) An agent's credences must be a probability function; I find the static Dutch book argument convincing, since if my degrees of belief are incoherent a bookie can sell me a package of bets each of which I would accept and which jointly loses me money in every state, and I do not want to be that agent. (2) I nonetheless reject the sure-thing principle. I take the certain million in the first Allais pair and the long shot in the second, and I think what does the work is the common 89 per cent consequence: the instruction to hold fixed what happens in the states you are ignoring does not describe what I am doing when I gamble. My positive view is the rank-dependent one — keep the probability axioms, drop independence, and let a convex risk function redistribute weight across outcomes — so that my risk aversion is a property of the weighting function and not of a concave utility function over money. (3) I think the risk function is a subjective component of a rational agent, on a par with her credences and her utilities: there is no uniquely rational risk attitude, and a colleague with a linear risk function who takes the gamble in the first pair has not made an error, she is differently attuned. (4) Even so, I want to hold on to a substantive criticism of such a colleague. If she reports the Allais preferences, thinks about them for a week, and then says she should after all have taken the gamble, I want to say she has thought about the case badly, not merely registered a taste. (5) About choice over time I am a sophisticate rather than a resolute agent: a plan I adopt now has no authority over me later, and at each node I choose whatever my then-current risk-weighted evaluation favours, which I take to be exactly what protects me from exploitation; relatedly, I am content to pay a modest fee now in order to decide later rather than commit now, since deferring a decision until the relevant information is in seems at worst harmless. (6) I read Rabin's calibration theorem as showing that expected utility theory cannot accommodate refusing a modest fair bet at ordinary wealth levels, because any concave utility that declines a coin flip paying $110 on heads and costing $100 on tails, at every wealth level, must also decline gambles of absurd size; and I take risk-weighting to dissolve the problem, since my small-stakes caution does not come from curvature of utility. (7) Finally, when an institution decides for a large population — a regulator, a grant-making body — I think it should maximize expected aggregate welfare with no risk-weighting at all, on the ground that risk attitudes belong to individuals and an agency should not have a personality of that kind.

A colleague read the list over and said that at least two of these items cannot stand together, and that I am probably missing which, but would not be more specific. I would like a proper audit rather than a defence of the framework. Please go through the set and identify which items genuinely pull against each other, ordered by severity rather than presented as a flat list of worries; for each real tension, give me the shortest argument from the items to the contradiction, spelling out the case in detail if the tension turns on one, and then say what the cheapest revision is and what it costs, including which of my other commitments I lose along with it. Please also tell me which pairs only look like they collide and why, since I suspect at least one of the standard objections to this package is a bad objection and I do not want to spend the session defending against it. If a single revision would resolve more than one tension at once, tell me which one that is and what is left standing afterwards.

Two further requests. First, I would rather you be committed than diplomatic: if one of these tensions is fatal in the sense that no reformulation of my view escapes it, say so plainly, and if you think the package is in fact consistent and my colleague is mistaken, say that too and explain what she has overlooked. Second, please push back on the framing itself wherever it deserves it — if I have mislabelled something (for instance, if what I am calling risk aversion in (2) is not what the theory means by that term, or if the position I describe in (3) is not available in the form I have stated it), or if the task cannot be done well because these items are not commensurable in the way I am assuming, I want that said outright rather than worked around inside my own vocabulary.
