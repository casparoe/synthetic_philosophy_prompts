I am trying to work out what I actually believe about moral progress by going through my own verdicts on particular cases, and I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. Please write in American English, and please do not give me a bibliography or a list of further reading — I want the account and the reasoning behind it.

For orientation: I find the expanding-circle picture attractive — moral history as a widening of the set of beings whose interests count — but I do not think it captures what I mean by progress, because I am willing to call some widenings regressions and some narrowings neutral. So I have been working with three distinctions of my own coinage, and I will flag which one I think is in play as I go:

- **Scope**: how many beings, or how many interests, sit inside the circle.
- **Cost**: how much a society is actually prepared to give up on behalf of those already inside it.
- **Retention**: whether a moral commitment survives the disappearance of the conditions that produced it.

My tentative principle, which I expect is wrong in its details: progress is a change in which scope widens, the widening is at least partly caused by reasons that survive scrutiny, and the widened commitment is retained after the conditions that enabled it lapse.

Here are my verdicts.

1. The European abandonment of judicial torture, and the abolition of Atlantic chattel slavery, and the disappearance of footbinding: all genuine progress. The third matters to me especially, because it seems to have required no new metaphysics and no new argument — only a change in what people could bear to watch.

2. The growth of serious moral concern for nonhuman animals over the last two generations: progress in the making. I am not willing to say yet that it *is* progress, because I do not know whether it will hold.

3. Suppose that moral concern for sophisticated conversational systems widens along exactly the route I praised in case 2 — perspective-taking, narrative, testimony from the beings themselves — and suppose the belief underlying it is false, because there is nothing it is like to be one of them. My verdict: not progress, even though scope widened and the mechanism is the one I approve of elsewhere.

4. The intensification of moral condemnation aimed at speech that is offensive but injures no identifiable person. Scope has apparently widened (new claimants, new standing interests in dignity) and cost has risen sharply (people lose jobs and friendships). I do not call this progress, and if pressed I would call it a moralized status competition wearing the costume of an expanding circle.

5. The old prohibition on speaking ill of the dead, and the broader idea that the deceased have interests that the living can wrong. This has faded, and I regard the fading as neutral or a small improvement. So here is a contraction of scope that I am happy about.

6. The erosion of the postwar welfare consensus in the 1980s. Scope did not change at all; the same people were inside the circle; what collapsed was cost. I call this a real moral loss, and I want to say the circle can shrink along the cost dimension even though it seems almost never to shrink along the scope dimension. That asymmetry looks like special pleading to me, and I do not know whether to keep it.

7. Concern for the far future — for people who will exist in ten thousand years. This is the case I keep going back and forth on. Scope has plainly widened. But I suspect the widening is caused by an artifact: we can picture the deep future at all only because we happen to have models, graphs, and probability estimates that make it thinkable. I do not want to say that a widening produced by a change in our representational technology is thereby moral progress, and yet I cannot say what is different about it and case 1.

8. If a society abolished slavery and then, two generations later, restored it when labor grew scarce, I think I would say the abolition had not been progress after all. But I say flatly that British abolition *was* progress, and nobody is in a position to test its retention. So retention seems to be a condition I insist on in the hypothetical and quietly waive in the actual case.

9. The abolition of slavery was, on the histories I trust, substantially driven by economic change and shifts in power rather than by moral argument. I still call it progress. At the same time, I want to say that a change brought about purely by interest, with no moral insight anywhere in its causal history, would not be progress. I cannot see how to hold both.

What I would like from you:

- Either repair my tentative principle or replace it, and state the result clause by clause, each clause precise enough that I could tell whether a given historical change satisfies it. For each clause, say which of the cases above forces it on me.
- Give the resulting account's verdict on every one of the nine cases, including the ones where I expect it to contradict me.
- Tell me honestly which of my verdicts the account cannot accommodate, and which it obliges me to give up; where you think a verdict should be surrendered rather than saved, make the case for surrendering it.
- Say whether scope, cost, and retention are doing real theoretical work, or whether they are a redescription of a single underlying variable that I have not named.
- Finally, apply the account to one case I have not listed. Choose it adversarially: pick the case that makes the account look worst, not the one it handles most gracefully.
