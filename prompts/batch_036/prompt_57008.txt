I teach 26 second-year epistemology students for 75 minutes; they have had conditional probability and Bayes' theorem but no measure theory and no statistics. Design an in-class game that makes them collide with Bertrand's paradox before anyone names it: equal ignorance turned into flatly incompatible probabilities for what they will insist is a single question, where the disagreement turns out not to be arithmetic.

Constraints: no computers — every randomizer must be buildable in five minutes from card, string, pins, dice, coins, a ruler or a hand-cut spinner. The surface framing should be ordinary (a wager, a machine, a shipment, a fair) rather than a chord in a circle, so nobody in the room recognises the puzzle, with the reveal at the end that what they built is the classical trio of chord-generating devices. Three groups build different devices that each of them would describe as picking a chord at random, and the pooled outcomes from all three go up on the board as one dataset. Score with integer tokens under a rule the groups can apply in their heads, where a confident wrong answer costs more than sitting out.

The design problem I most need solved: "your devices are just different experiments, so nothing contradicts" is both the obvious escape and the correct dissolution, and a group that finds it in minute ten never feels anything. So the rules must extract, before any cross-group data is revealed, a written and token-backed commitment from every group that any fair device answers the same question theirs does — the escape hatch should exist only once they are on record. I also want a route by which a group that judges the question ill-posed profits by declining to bet; tell me whether that lets a sharp group exit before the lesson lands, and how to price it so it cannot.

Deliver the framing story, a materials list and self-readable instructions per device, the scoring rule, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief tying particular moments of the run to the three classical answers, the ill-posedness response, the invariance response, and the generating-process response, so each is something that happened in the room rather than something I assert. Then say what will predictably break on a first run — including where a few hundred physical trials will muddy the point instead of sharpening it — and what the activity structurally cannot deliver that I have to supply in words.

Finally: which of the two candidate lessons does your design actually secure — that indifference is relative to a parameterisation, or that a probability claim is incomplete without a specified generating process? What would I have to see in the room to be persuaded it taught the other one, and where is your own reasoning about this design weakest? Last line exactly: Verdict: parameterisation / process.
