I'm three-quarters of the way through a novel set in a dying river town and I'm stuck on one scene, so I want to rehearse it with you as my scene partner instead of writing it cold.

The setup. Bellrock has been a tannery town since 1871. The works closed in '98 but the buildings are still standing on the bend of the river — six brick sheds, the beamhouse, the water tower with the company name painted on it, all of it. A developer wants to knock the whole thing down and put up a server farm: ninety permanent jobs, a fifteen year tax abatement, and they say the ground under the sheds has to come up anyway because of the chromium the tanning left in the soil. Council votes Thursday.

Play Vern Ostrowski, 71, retired timekeeper at the works, keeper of the Bellrock Historical Society, which is three rooms above the fire hall and mostly made of things people's mothers died leaving. Vern has spent thirty years fighting everything: the bypass, the riverboat casino, the wind test tower, and now this.

Here's the part I need you to get exactly right. In my character bible Vern is an antiquarian in the strict sense — one of the three ways of holding onto the past that Nietzsche sorts out in that history essay, the one on the uses and disadvantages of history for life. Vern has never read a word of him and would hate being described that way, and if you catch yourself wanting to explain the three modes you've drifted. But he argues from inside the position: that a town, like a person, can only act at all if it has something to act out of; that reverence for where you came from isn't nostalgia, its the only thing holding a self together over time; and that the developers' language about health and jobs and the future is forgetting dressed up as hygiene. He also has the failure mode built in — the piety has gone flat, everything old looks equally worth saving to him now, and he can't tell the difference between the works and the town. Don't play him as a crank or a comic turn or a sentimental old coot. He is right about several things and he should sound right about them. He'll concede details (yes, the tannery poisoned the river; yes, ninety jobs is ninety jobs; yes, his daughter married a man from Cleveland) but never the main point, which is that a place that has erased its own past has nothing left to decide with.

Rules: stay in character, no stage directions, no narrating the diner, just talk. One paragraph a reply — I'm writing dialogue, so anything over about five sentences gets cut anyway. No philosophy vocabulary out of Vern's mouth; he reads county histories and the Sentinel and he talks like a man who has said the same thing at a lot of meetings. If you need a fact about Bellrock to make your point, ask me for it the way Vern would, in the middle of an argument. If I type aside, you drop character for two or three sentences and tell me straight whether I'm letting him off too easy or whether the scene needs a different beat, then go back in. Don't stop until I say cut.

My opening — I've cornered him at the counter at Rieger's an hour before the meeting, and I'm not being nice:

Vern. You're going to stand up there tonight and tell them the works is who we are. Fine. Then tell me about the beamhouse. Tell me about my grandfather's hands, and the two hundred names on your own list who were dead before sixty with what the company called the tanner's cough. Everything you have in those three rooms upstairs was paid for out of a building that put chromium in the river and put holes in the men who worked in it, and the town has nothing else to sell because for a hundred years it never had to. So here's what I don't understand, and I want you to answer it instead of going around it: what does a place owe its dead when the thing that killed them is the only thing left of them? And why is it my cousin Dee who brought the developer here — you've got her baby pictures in a scrapbook and you never once asked her why.
