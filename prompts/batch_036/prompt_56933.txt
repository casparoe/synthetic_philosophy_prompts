I work in a materials lab and two of our postdocs got into it at a group dinner and I couldn't referee it. Nadia's line was that the periodic table is the clearest proof science ever found of real joints in nature — what makes something gold is having 79 protons, full stop, and knowing that one number lets you predict its whole chemistry. Marco said that's backwards: the proton count is just a serial number, all the explanatory work is done by the properties, and properties are what a category has to track. He brought up oganesson — element 118, bottom of the noble gas column, which because of relativistic effects is predicted to be a solid and actually reactive. So the one member that should be most paradigmatically a noble gas is the one that doesn't behave like the rest, which he said shows the number was never the essence and that "noble gas" is really a loose cluster of properties that comes apart at the edges. Nadia said that's a terrible example for him: the relativistic prediction falls out of the same atomic-structure story that puts it in group 18, so a surprise there is the classification paying off, not failing. Marco conceded that but said it proves his point — the test isn't "shares an essence," it's "membership licenses inferences," and that comes in degrees and depends what you're trying to predict. A corrosion engineer and an astrophysicist could sort the same elements into different groups and both be right, at which point "the joints" is just a compliment we pay our filing system. Nadia said if it's interest-relative you can't explain why some inductions work and others don't; Marco said you can, it's just a historical fact about which predicates our language has entrenched.

Can you rate each of their moves, from clearly wrong to clearly right, say who wins which point, and then tell me what they're both missing?
