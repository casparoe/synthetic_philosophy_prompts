I cover AI for a science magazine and once a year I run a weekend seminar for our new interns — bright, no philosophy training, and at the end they get one 50-minute essay worth 20 marks. Write that essay question on what counts as deception in a language model, plus a model answer of about 500 words and a marking scheme that says what an A answer does that a B answer doesnt.

The thing I want it to turn on is a distinction I keep seeing fudged in coverage. Our fact-checkers sort bad outputs into two piles. Pile one: probe the model's internals and they line up with the truth, but it says what the user wanted to hear anyway. Pile two: no settled internal state either way, it just produces a confident claim that turns out false. A definition built around systematically causing false beliefs in the user convicts pile two of lying when it might merely be confused, and it convicts pile one only because of what the user happened to do with the output. A definition built around the model's own beliefs has to say what a belief is here, and every candidate I've seen smuggles in something about training or design. Make the question force them to pick one and defend it against the pile it handles worst.

The question has to be self-contained — they've read none of the literature, so describe both piles inside it, and dont build the model answer on name-checking researchers. In the marking scheme, seperate out answers that slide between saying the model deceived and saying the output was misleading, since that slide is exactly the move the fudged coverage makes.
