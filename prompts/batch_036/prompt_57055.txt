I am writing the seminar paper for a module on Nietzsche, and I have built an argument against the second Untimely Meditation that I cannot refute, though its conclusion looks self-defeating.

(1) On the essay's own criterion, history serves life only where a living being has the plastic power to draw a horizon around what it knows — to bound its attention and to forget whatever it cannot use; where that power is absent, the identical historical content ceases to be nourishment and becomes a poison.

(2) The essay's diagnosis of its own age is that this power has been spent: the modern person can no longer forget, and therefore can no longer act, and merely carries the past about with him.

(3) That diagnosis is itself historical content, and heavily documented content at that, resting on an account of how German historicism, Hegel's philosophy of history and the seminar system produced the type it describes.

(4) By (1), historical content nourishes only those able to assimilate it; and by (2), the essay's intended readers are just those who cannot.

(5) So the essay cannot do for its readers what it promises: for them it is a further indigestible portion of the past, and reading it deepens the malady it describes.

(6) Nor can the failure be local, since any critique of historical culture must be delivered in the medium it condemns; therefore no critique of that kind can succeed.

(6) is what I refuse to accept: it abolishes the genre, and my seminar paper with it. If forced to choose, I would sooner abandon (2), the claim I find most convincing, than accept (6). Tell me which step to surrender, why that step looked entirely respectable when I set it down, and what the essay must pay once it is gone — and say whether the argument can be patched into something sound rather than merely abandoned.
