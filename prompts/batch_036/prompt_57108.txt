I'm setting the final for a third-year personal identity course and I want one essay question on uploading that students can't answer by reciting the standard gradual-replacement story or by summarising a psychological-continuity view. Here's the case I want the question built around — treat these stipulations as granted, you don't need to make them plausible:

The Tandem Protocol. In a neuroprosthetics trial, each of a patient's neurons, once its input–output profile has been mapped, gets a synthetic counterpart wired in parallel with it. Nothing is removed. Eighteen months in, every function of the patient's brain is duplicated in silicon; both layers are integrated and running together and the patient reports nothing unusual. Then phase two: the layers can be decoupled from one another, and when they are, each one on its own sustains a fully functioning person with the same memories, temperament and self-reports up to the start of the trial.

The question should ask what happens to the patient — one person or two, survival or not — and then push on which of the familiar positions about gradual uploading actually gets a grip on this case and which one loses its footing. The specific thing I want students to catch is that the usual argument against gradual replacement (swap them one at a time, there's no moment at which consciousness could switch off, and if you'd notice a change you'd have noticed it) is aimed at *replacement*. This case involves no replacement at all, no subtraction, so that argument doesn't transfer — and I want them to explain why rather than just assert it. Likewise with the biological/animalist line: the organism never loses a neuron, so the "your body is still here" reply has to say something about the second layer, and I want the question to force that.

Deliverables:
- the question exactly as it would appear on the paper, with a word limit (1200 words) and mark allocation (30 marks)
- a model answer of about 700 words
- a marking scheme that splits an A from a B by naming the specific moves each band has to contain — in particular what a student must do with the decoupling phase to get into the A band, since almost everyone will just call it fission and stop there
- three concrete mistakes that should cost marks, plus a line on which of the three is most common and why

Constraints: no sci-fi machinery beyond what the case already grants, no escaping via souls or brute-fact mysterianism, and don't let the question be answerable by picking a camp and cheerleading — the marking scheme should dock a student who never states the strongest objection to their own view. It's closed book and I don't allow quotations, so nothing should turn on remembering exact wording from any text, and please don't ask them to cite anything.

One more thing: the model answer shouldn't just be the view I happen to favour (I lean animalist, for what that's worth). Write it as the answer a very good student would give whichever way they land, so it demonstrates the structure of a good response rather than a conclusion.
