For four years I've been one of three people on the jury for a regional open-submission show — anybody can enter, no CV, no gatekeeping, we look at the work and the entry form and take a vote. Our record so far, since it probably constrains things:

We accepted a floor piece of forty identical unglazed factory plates laid out in a grid, where the one-page statement said the arrangement was the work and any plate could be swapped for another off the same shelf without changing anything.

We rejected a rocking chair — genuinely gorgeous, walnut, hand-cut joints — after the entry form said he'd built it for his granddaughter and only submitted it because his wife kept at him.

We accepted a twenty-minute video of a pair of hands erasing a photocopy of a drawing until the paper went grey and then started to tear.

We rejected a framed piece of unmodified sea glass entered by a hobbyist who said he wasn't an artist, he just thought it was pretty and the show let anyone in.

We accepted three hundred bound pages of a handwritten invented language by a seventeen-year-old who'd never been inside a gallery, shown with a reading desk and a lamp.

We rejected a proposal that consisted of the artist announcing he would think about the exhibition every day for its duration and report nothing to anyone.

This year's problem. A curator friend has entered, on behalf of a 78-year-old retired state hydrologist, sixty hand-inked stage-discharge charts the man drew for one river gauging station between 1971 and 1994. The agency was clearing out flat files in the early nineties and he carried them home. He never thought of them as anything but records. He's alive and easy to reach, and he told the curator that they aren't art, they're his work; that he doesn't mind them being hung; that he'll come to the opening; and that if anything sells he wants the money to go to a river trust. The curator has re-ordered the sheets by water year instead of by the date they were drawn, which makes the flood years leap out in a way they never did in the file, and has written the wall text.

Honestly, hung that way they are extraordinairy. You can lose an hour in sixty years of one river.

So: which way do we go, and what are we actually running when we go that way? I want the candidate rules first — give me at least three that fit all six earlier verdicts without strain, in your own words, and say which of the six each one would have had the easiest time with and which it nearly breaks on. Then run each rule on the charts and tell me where they seperate. Then your best guess at our vote with the reasons for preferring that rule over the others. Then which of the six is the closest analog to the charts and exactly where the analogy gives out. And if we accept, whose work is it — the hydrologist's, the curator's, ours, for conferring on it — and does that answer change depending on which rule got us to yes? Finally, tell me what principle we'd be committing ourselves to by accepting and what different one we'd be committing ourselves to by rejecting, and describe a single hypothetical submission for next year that would cleanly split the rules that happen to agree here.

Please write it all as continuous prose, the way you'd write an email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. Just paragraphs.
