**The Flagged Two-Bitser: Normal Conditions, Self-Maintenance, and the Line the Continuum Blurs**

**§1. The distinction I want to reopen.** Searle's distinction between original and derived intentionality is usually read as a claim about kinds of substance: marks and noises have content only in virtue of being used as contentful by agents who genuinely have minds, and since the states of a computer are just such marks, any intentionality they have must be borrowed. Dennett's reply is usually read as an attack on that reading, mounted by way of a continuum. Consider the simplest sort of coin-detecting device, the two-bitser, installed in a vending machine, whose internal state we naturally describe as reporting that a quarter is present. Now transport the very same device, unchanged, into an environment containing a look-alike coin it cannot discriminate from a quarter; the identical state can be described, with equal naturalness, as reporting the local coin instead, or as reporting a disjunction of the two, depending on where the device sits and what one wants to predict. On Dennett's telling, as I reconstruct it, there is no further fact about which description is correct — and if there is no fact of the matter even here, at the bottom of the scale, then there is nothing for the original/derived distinction to be a distinction between. Any line drawn on the continuum is drawn by us.

**§2. Two jobs the environment is being asked to do.** I want to argue that this continuum runs together two roles that the environment plays in fixing what the two-bitser's state is about, and that separating them is enough to restore a joint worth cutting.

The first role is extension-fixing. What the state covaries with is settled causally by the geometry of the slot, the tuning of the sensor, and the distribution of coins in the surroundings. Change the distribution and you change what the state covaries with. Nothing here is contentious, and nothing here touches the original/derived question, since covariance is symmetric between device and environment.

The second role is error-enabling. Content, I assume, requires the possibility of misrepresentation: a state that cannot be wrong about anything is not about anything at all. But whether a given triggering counts as a mistake rather than as a correct report depends on which conditions have been designated as normal. When the device fires at a look-alike coin in the machine room where it was installed and calibrated, we call that an error. When the transported device fires at the local coin, we are inclined to say it is now correctly detecting that coin, not misreporting a quarter. Both verdicts invoke exactly the same physical facts. What differs is which set of conditions has been nominated as the baseline relative to which departures are departures.

That nomination is not made by the physics. It is made by an interest — typically the interest of whoever installed, calibrated, purchased, or is now predicting the device. So the second role of the environment is really the role of an interested party wearing the environment's clothes.

**§3. What this does to the distinction.** If that is right, the two-bitser's intentionality is derived — but not for the reason Searle's opponents assume. It is not derived because some interpreter reads its state as a symbol in her own language; states can have content that nobody bothers to read, and the vending machine does not consult the two-bitser's output as a text. It is derived because the baseline of normal conditions, which is what makes error and hence content possible, is fixed by an interest that is not the device's own.

This yields a naturalised version of Searle's line that does not cost him his biology, or at least not in the way he fears. Say that a system has original intentionality when its normal conditions are fixed by its own persistence conditions, and derived intentionality when they are fixed by an outside interest. The line is then not between the physical and the mental, nor between the symbolic and the non-symbolic, but between systems whose standards of correctness are self-maintained and systems whose standards are on loan.

**§4. The flagged two-bitser.** Now modify the case. Add a second bit: a flag that latches whenever the machine's transport sensor registers that the device has been displaced from its calibrated environment without being recalibrated, and that gates the output, so that when the flag is set the device withholds or discounts its report rather than passing it downstream.

The device now has, in a minimal but non-trivial sense, a stake of its own in which conditions count as normal. It carries information about the provenance of its own readings and uses that information to decide which of them to stand behind. The baseline is no longer purely loaned: the device enforces one, and enforces it because doing so is what keeps it in the state its downstream operations presuppose. If that is right, the continuum is not smooth. Between a bare two-bitser, whose standards of correctness are wholly external, and a system that withdraws its own commitments when its provenance information tells it they are unreliable, there is a difference in kind and not merely a difference in the number of bits — and it is very nearly the difference the original/derived distinction was groping after, recovered inside a thoroughly mechanical setting.

**§5. What I am not claiming.** Nothing here vindicates the Chinese Room. The flagged two-bitser is a syntactic engine from end to end. If the friend of my argument thinks that flagging plus gating amounts to the beginning of self-maintained standards, that is a reason to doubt the sufficiency claim about syntax, not a reason to embrace it. My conclusion is deliberately narrow: the two-bitser, as deployed, does not blur the line between original and derived intentionality, because it never raises the question of who fixes the baseline of normal conditions. Once that question is raised, the answer admits of a non-biological and non-Cartesian distinction, and two bits' worth of modification is enough to put something on the far side of it.

**§6. The problem I have not solved.** Regress. The flag bit is itself a physical state whose content, on my own account, is fixed by a baseline of normal conditions — and where does that baseline come from? If it comes from the designers' decision that displacement, rather than thermal drift or vibration, is what the flag is about, then the device's stake is a stake on loan, and §4 collapses back into §2. I suspect the way out is to observe that once a system's outputs feed back into the maintenance of the system itself, the interest that fixes the baseline becomes endogenous; but I have not managed to say this without either appealing to a selecting process, which reintroduces an interested party external to the system, or appealing to a teleology I have not earned.
