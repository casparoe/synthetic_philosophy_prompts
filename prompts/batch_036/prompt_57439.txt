I'm reading a chapter in a recent anthology on the philosophy of machine learning. Most of it is careful, but one section argues that the no-free-lunch theorems settle Hume's problem of induction, and I want to know how much of it to believe. Here is the passage as it appears:

'The no-free-lunch theorems, proved by David Wolpert in the mid-1990s and extended to search and optimization jointly with William Macready, are the closest thing mathematics has come to a formal version of Hume's argument. The result is disarmingly simple: average an algorithm's performance over all possible objective functions, and every algorithm comes out exactly the same. Any method that excels on one class of problems must pay for it with degradation on the rest, so the distinctions we draw between learning algorithms — between an inductive bias that assumes smoothness and one that assumes nothing — do not survive scrutiny; they are artefacts of which problems we happen to be looking at. What Hume could only argue, the theorems prove: nothing licenses the inference from observed regularity to unobserved regularity, and the fact that a method has succeeded so far is not evidence that nature is uniform but a measure of how narrow our sample of problems has been. Philosophers who insist that machine learning's successes refute the sceptic have simply mistaken a lucky draw from the set of all functions for a fact about the world.'

The chapter adds, in a footnote that I find harder to dismiss, that the theorem does not depend on the uniformity assumption after all: it continues to hold for any distribution over objective functions that is left unchanged by permuting the points of the search space, and the author takes this to show that the result is not hostage to an implausible idealization.

I am inclined to think the sceptical conclusion is too strong — surely the fact that much the same smoothness-preferring or gradient-following methods work across physics, vision, and language is evidence of something — but I worry that this is precisely the reply the author has already dismissed in the final sentence.

Could you audit the passage for me? Going sentence by sentence: which claims would a researcher who works on these theorems endorse as stated, which are defensible simplifications that lose something load-bearing, and which are simply wrong? In particular I would like to know (a) whether the hypotheses of the theorem have been stated correctly, and what the omission of any of them costs the argument; (b) whether the gloss conflates the version for supervised learning with the version for search and optimization, and whether that conflation matters here; (c) whether the footnote's permutation-invariance claim is accurate; and (d) whether the inference from the theorem to Humean scepticism about induction survives once the theorem is stated properly — or whether the direction of that inference is in fact backwards.

Please also tell me how confident you are in these verdicts, and single out the one place in your audit where you are least sure of yourself: the point at which a specialist could plausibly say you have it the wrong way round.
