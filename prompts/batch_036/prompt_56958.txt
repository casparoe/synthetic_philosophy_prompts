I've been playing a little side game with a junior colleague here — call her P — who works on rational choice in the absence of preference. The game is that I hand her cases, she hands me verdicts, and I try to run the inference backwards to whatever principle she's actually operating with. We started where everyone starts, with the ass: she's adamant that the starving-between-two-haystacks story never gets off the ground for a *rational* agent, and she likes to note (correctly, I think) that the ass itself doesn't turn up anywhere in Buridan's extant writings even though he did take up exactly-equal alternatives in his Aristotle commentaries and found a way to let the will off the hook. Her track record on my cases so far:

1. Two unmarked twenty-dollar bills on a table; you take one, the other gets donated. Verdict: grabbing either is fine, full stop — no coin, no suspension, and no mark against you that you can't articulate why you took the left one.
2. Two therapeutically equivalent drugs that differ only in capusle colour; the patient takes the blue one because it's blue. Verdict: rational, and not in the weak "excused anyway" sense — colour has zero normative bearing and still does the work.
3. The ass case itself, but with a reflective agent in place of the donkey. Verdict: doesn't starve. She says the paralysis reading gets things backwards; nothing about a perfect tie licenses standing still.
4. Two routes home you have no basis whatsoever for preferring. Verdict: take either, and — this is the part I keep coming back to — don't spend twenty minutes hunting for a difference you have no independent reason to think is there.
5. Two careers, neither of which is better and which aren't equally good either, just genuinely hard to compare. Verdict: here she does *not* say "just pick." She says you commit, and that the committing is part of what eventually makes the chosen life the better one. She treats this as structurally different from (1) and (2) but wouldn't say in what respect.
6. Flipping a coin in case (1). Verdict: permitted, but "cheating," her word — she thinks the coin only looks like it's supplying a reason where none was wanted.
7. An agent who knows two options are exactly equal, refuses on principle to break the tie arbitrarily, and so ends up with neither. Verdict, hedged to the point of uselessness: "not irrational exactly, but I wouldn't call it a success of rationality either," and then she changed the subject.

Here's the case I want to spring on her next, and I'd like your read on it before I do. You're back in a (1)-type situation, only bigger: two job offers, two departments that are so far as you or anyone else can tell exactly equally good — pay, teaching load, colleagues, city, you've compared everything comparable. You take A arbitrarily, no coin, no reason you could give. A year later you learn something small that was true all along but which nobody could have known at the time: B's chair turned out to be a monster, or B had a slightly better leave policy. Three questions. First, was the original choice *irrational* — retroactively, now that we know there was a real difference — or was it rational relative to what you had and merely unlucky? Second, does P think you should have treated the apparent exact tie as a standing reason to keep looking, since this pattern (a micro-difference surfacing later) is predictable in advance? And third, is her answer to (1)-(4) the same machinery as her answer to (5), or has she quietly got two different theories of "no preference"? Lay out which candidate rules are actually consistent with all seven verdicts, what each of them says about the new case, and which one you'd bet on and why; then name the next case I should give her that would separate your top two candidates — ideally one where they come apart cleanly rather than just probabilistically. Finally, tell me what would change your mind here: which additional verdict or which argument from her side would make you drop your best guess, and where you think your own reconstruction is most likely to be going wrong — e.g. if you've attributed her a distinction (subjective vs. objective rationality, say) that she'd reject as a philosopher's artifact rather than a real joint in the phenomenon.
