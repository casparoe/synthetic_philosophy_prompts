I am a fourth-year undergraduate writing the final paper for a seminar on moral psychology and the emotions, and I need help designing the central case for it. My thesis is that the standard division of labour between guilt and shame cannot be sustained: guilt is said to take an act as its object, to presuppose an identifiable victim, and to issue in confession, apology and reparation, whereas shame is said to take the whole self as its object, to depend on an actual or imagined audience, and therefore to be self-absorbed, unreliable as a moral motive, and best confined to the private sphere. On that picture — the one defended by Nussbaum's arguments against appealing to shame in public life, and echoed in the empirical guilt-proneness/shame-proneness literature — guilt is the distinctively moral emotion and shame is its primitive, socially toxic cousin. My paper concedes the intentional-object distinction (it is the position I find most convincing in Williams's *Shame and Necessity* that shame can survive without a punitive morality of blame) and instead argues that the distinction implies a class of situations in which guilt is structurally unavailable while a shame-response is nonetheless appropriate, non-self-absorbed, and the only response that generates the right kind of moral work. My seminar is mostly analytic philosophers who think that claim must be either trivially false or a re-description of guilt.

Please construct the case for me to specification, and then demonstrate that it meets every specification.

**Case A — shame without guilt.**

1. A realistic contemporary setting in an ordinary institution: a firm, a workshop, a hospital ward, a school, a sports club, a family, a parish, a municipal office. No science fiction, no dystopia, no war crimes, no torture, no genocide, no trolley-problem machinery.
2. One to two paragraphs of scenario, written concretely enough that the reader can see what the agent does and knows, not schematically.
3. Guilt must be unavailable *for principled reasons generated by the case*, not stipulated. The agent must have committed no act or omission that is wrongful on any plausible account: no negligence, no recklessness, no culpable ignorance, no failure of due care, no promise broken, no role obligation violated. If a guilt-theorist can show the agent was at fault even a little, the case collapses.
4. The agent must nonetheless be genuinely implicated in a harm or a wrong — not a mere bystander who happens to feel bad, and not a victim herself. Implication must be real: something about who the agent is, what she benefits from, what she was made into, or what she is now positioned to see.
5. The shame must pass what I will call the no-audience test in the paper: it must remain appropriate on the stipulation that the agent is certain nobody will ever learn the relevant facts, that no community of witnesses exists, and that she will never see her own reflection or be described to herself by another. Do not smuggle in spectators, publicity, exposure, or discovery.
6. The shame must not lean on disgust, on the body, on nudity or physical exposure, on degradation, or on any of the stigmatising features that the guilt-primacy camp identifies as shame's real content. It has to be shame about a self, not revulsion at a body.
7. It must also resist the obvious reduction: the response may not be re-describable as guilt about a character trait, as guilt about earlier acts that made the agent the person she is, or as remorse without fault. If your case works only by shifting the object of guilt from act to disposition, it fails, because that concedes my opponents' point that one emotion with a flexible object will do all the work.
8. The shame must be *productive*: it must issue in some determinate response — an action, a disclosure, a refusal, a re-orientation of the agent's standing commitments — that guilt's action tendency cannot generate in this case, and you must say precisely why it cannot.

Please avoid the examples everyone reaches for: no citizen of a wealthy country reflecting on historical injustice, no clinician who failed to report something, no survivor of an accident who feels responsible, no heir to a family fortune with a guilty secret, no soldier returning home. I want a domain that is unfamiliar as a moral-psychology example but utterly mundane as a piece of life.

**Case B — guilt without shame, as a mirror.** In the same kind of setting and in three or four sentences, a case in which guilt is available and outperforms shame: a situation where the agent who responded with shame would be doing something morally worse, because the shame-response would turn attention back onto her own standing and away from the victim. Case B exists to show that my asymmetry is not a general preference for shame, and it should make Case A's structure sharper by contrast.

**The check.** For each case, run through the two emotions explicitly and separately: the intentional object, the appraisal conditions the emotion requires, the characteristic action tendency, the verdict, and the reasoning that gets from the structure of the emotion to the verdict. The verdicts must fall out of those structures and the facts of the scenario; a case that produces the divergence by announcing how the agent feels is worthless to me.

**The honest part.** Finish with: (a) the single feature of Case A that is doing the work, named as precisely as you can; (b) what happens to the verdict if that feature is removed or weakened — for instance, if the implication is made more tenuous, or if the no-audience stipulation is dropped; and (c) the two or three best attacks a defender of guilt-primacy would run on the case. I expect candidates to include the claim that the appropriate response is really agent-regret or integrity rather than shame, that a shame which survives without an audience is just a dispositional self-assessment and so not shame at all, and that the productive response in Case A could have been motivated by a virtue rather than by any emotion — but tell me which attack is actually strongest and where my case is softest, even if that is a different one.

Format: both scenarios first, each followed by its analysis under short labelled headings, then the closing section on (a), (b) and (c). Roughly 1,100 to 1,400 words in total. I do not need references, a bibliography, or any suggestions for further reading — the case and the reasoning are the deliverable.
