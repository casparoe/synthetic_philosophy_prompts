So I'm TAing a seminar called "Philosophy and Psychotherapy" — about a dozen second-year clinical psych students — and next Tuesday it's my turn to run the 75-minute section on Sartre's bad faith. The catch is that they're three weeks into their CBT placements, so they arrive with a very specific toolkit and they will absolutley try to file Sartre under it. Two failure modes I can already see coming: one group will cash out bad faith as basically a cognitive distortion, i.e. a wrong belief about yourself that dissolves once someone shows you the evidence against it; the other group will treat it as lack of insight, i.e. the client is repressing the truth and therapy is just getting them to self-report honestly. Neither is dumb, and I don't want to simply announce that both are wrong — I want the sequence itself to make the wrongness visible to them.

Plan the section for me rather than explaining bad faith to me; I know the material, what I don't have is an order of attack. So: what has to be in place before bad faith is even statable — I'm assuming facticity and transcendence, plus something about consciousness not being a thing that has properties, and I'm fuzzy on how much of that machinery I can get in without losing them — and what depends on what, so I'm not borrowing a concept three steps before I've earned it. Then which case should carry the whole thing. The café waiter is available and they've half-heard of it, but I worry it reads as cute people-watching rather than as freedom collapsing on itself; should I use it as a warm-up and then retire it, or is there a better single case, maybe drawn from their placement world, since every one of them has a client who says "I'm just an anxious person, that's who I am"? Tell me what that carrying case has to have and what it has to lack — my instinct is it can't be a case where the truth is obvious on reflection, or the paradox just disapears.

And then the misconception map. For each predictable wrong turn — bad faith as ordinary self-deception in the hiding-a-belief sense (and how do I handle the problem that you'd have to know exactly what you're hiding?), bad faith as mere hypocrisy or cowardice, radical freedom as "you can be anything, so just choose differently," authenticity as brutal honesty or sincerity-as-cure, and the big one where they conclude Sartre is blaming people for their circumstances — say where in the sequence I head it off and what I actually do at that moment so it doesn't feel like a teacher correcting a student. Finish with one thing I can put to them in the last ten minutes that would genuinely discriminate between "bad faith landed as a claim about the structure of freedom" and "bad faith landed as a fancy synonym for being in denial," and tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level and announce that I'm refusing it — I'm guessing the full for-itself/in-itself ontology, and probably the Other. American English, please, and be concrete: rough timings per block would help, because 75 minutes goes fast.
