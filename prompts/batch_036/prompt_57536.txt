Been trying to pin down where I actually stand on the lottery/preface stuff before I present it to a reading group, and I'd like someone to check whether the package hangs together. Here's the list:

1. I'm a Lockean about belief: to believe p just is to have credence in p above a threshold, and I set that threshold high — call it .99.
2. Belief has to work as a batch. If I may believe p and may believe q, I may believe p and q together. Otherwise I don't see how my beliefs could be the pool my reasoning draws on.
3. Fair lottery, 10 millon tickets, I hold one, draw hasn't happened. I may not believe my ticket loses — and not "it's a close call," flatly impermissible.
4. What makes a belief justified, for me, is a safety condition: a belief is okay only if it isn't easily wrong, i.e. in the nearby worlds where I hold it, it's true.
5. Preface: an author who checked her sources and argued carefully may believe each individual claim in the book, and may also believe that at least one of the claims is false. Both attitudes are legitimate.
6. As far as any single proposition is concerned, the lottery player and the preface author are in the same evidential shape, and what you may believe shouldn't swing on what else you happen to be thinking about at the time.
7. Also, I'm perfectly happy to act on the lottery odds — I wouldn't buy a second ticket, I wouldn't refuse a bet that pays me a dollar if I lose. That feels like it sits badly with (3) but I can't articulate why.

Go through it as an audit rather than a lecture. Which pairs genuinely pull against each other, and how badly — rank them, don't just list them all as equally bad. Which ones only look like they conflict and come apart once you press on them. For the real conflicts, what's the cheapest thing to give up, and what does giving it up cost me somewhere else in the list. Then say which single revision would clean up the most damage. I don't have a favorite among these yet, so don't assume I'm protecting any of them.
