I'm on the participant-protection subcommitee of a large state retirement system's defined-contribution plan — about 400k members who pick their own allocations — and Thursday we vote on a vendor tool. I've been asked to circulate a short written opinion beforehand and I'd rather it be mine in substance than a press release, so: can you draft it for me? I'll sign my name to the reasoning, which means I need it to actually take a position, not split the difference down the middle.

The setup. The vendor runs a "risk-tolerance questionnaire" at onboarding. Two of the items have this shape, with the amounts scaled to retirement money:

Item 1 — take $40,000 for certain, or a ticket that pays $200,000 with probability 10%, $40,000 with probability 89%, and nothing with probability 1%.

Item 2 — take a ticket that pays $40,000 with probability 11% and nothing otherwise, or a ticket that pays $200,000 with probability 10% and nothing otherwise.

If a member takes the certain $40,000 on item 1 and the 10% shot on item 2, the tool flags them as "incoherent" (their word) and, instead of recording the member's election, substitutes a reccommended moderate-growth allocation behind a screen saying a specialist reviewed their answers.

Facts I have, all from the vendor's own pilot, so treat them as self-serving but it's all we've got: 31% of members who completed the questionnaire were flagged. Of the flagged members re-surveyed two weeks later, 88% gave the same answers again. Flagged members whose election was overridden were, over the next year, less likely to log in, less likely to change their contribution rate, and more likely to move their whole balance out of the plan when they switched jobs.

The vendor's pitch as I understand it: the two items differ only in a common consequence, so a member who reverses between them has no single consistent answer to "what is your certainty worth," and therefore there is no coherent preference sitting there to be respected. They add that you can walk such a person through a sequence of trades that leaves them worse off holding exactly what they started with, which is why they say this is a defect rather than a lifestyle. They're careful to insist they're not claiming expected utility is the true theory of good decisions — only that it works as a coherence test, and that a plan shouldn't act on preferences that fail it.

Our two dissenting members say: the reversal is stable and predictable, and 88% is about as stable as anything in survey data ever is; the sequence-of-trades argument assumes someone keeps standing there offering the trades, and a plan that simply never runs the sequence leaves members un-drained; and the moment we let an administrator decide which preference orderings count as real, the axioms have stopped being a test and become a licensing regime. They'd rather print a plain-language warning and let the member re-affirm.

What I need in the memo:

- a bottom line up front — adopt, reject, or adopt with specific conditions, and if it's conditions, make them ones someone could actually check compliance against rather than aspirational language
- the reasoning, engaging the vendor's technical point head-on: I want the common-consequence structure handled properly, and I want the sequence-of-trades argument either defeated or conceded, not stepped around
- the strongest thing the losing side has going for it, stated as forcefully as they'd state it themselves, and then dealt with rather than noted
- a short section on what general standard this vote commits us to for the next vendor tool, since we'll get three more this year
- about two pages, and no references or bibliography, nobody on this committee reads footnotes

One more thing. My co-chair thinks the whole dispute is fake — that it's really just a fight over who gets to design the default, and that both sides would swap seats instantly if the tool defaulted people into the aggressive allocation instead of the moderate one. I don't think he's right, but I can't wave him off in a single line either, so work in something that takes that seriously.
