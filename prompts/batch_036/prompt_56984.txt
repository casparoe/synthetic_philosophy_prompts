I'm a novelist and I'm stuck on a scene in the third book of a series set in a drought-bitten farming county, and honestly the fight I had about it is now more interesting to me than the scene itself, so I'd like you to referee. Setup: a rep from an outfit called Caldera Ag comes to the town of Kessler selling a soil treatment that either rescues yields or does nothing whatsoever. Farmers decide one at a time whether to sign, in rough order of who runs into who. Each one can run a cheap test plot first and gets a private read off it. Every signing is public — Caldera nails a little tin sign to the mailbox. The first three sign on the strength of their plots. The fourth, Vern, stands up at the Grange hall and announces he's signing before he's bothered to run anything, pure chest-thumping, and everybody in the room knows exactly what that was. The fifth, Ellis, runs his plot, the read comes back bad, and he signs anyway. After that the whole county signs inside a month. The treatment is worthless, but that doesn't come out for two more growing seasons, so nobody inside the book ever finds out.

I ran this past my sister Dev, who's finishing an economics PhD, and Ray, who spent twenty years in agricultural sales and reads alot more than anyone I know. It went badly. (1) Ray said the scene as written is dishonest, because Ellis is just caving to his neighbors and dressing that up as "rational herding" is a polite word for spinelessness. (2) Dev said that's wrong on the theory: by the time Ellis looks up there are three signs plus Vern's on the road, and that public evidence genuinely outweighs one weak test plot, so a perfect Bayesian in Ellis's shoes signs too — if anyone is irrational here its the county as a whole, not the man. (3) Ray said if that's true then the town's belief is getting better with every signature, since more evidence is more evidence, so a hundred signs is a hundred votes for the product and I've got no business writing that the product is a fraud. (4) Dev said it's the reverse: once the cascade starts the later signatures are information-free, because they'd have happened whatever the plot said, so a hundred signatures is worth maybe three or four votes and the crowd's supposed wisdom has just evaporated. (5) Ray pounced on that — if the later signatures carry no information, Ellis couldn't rationally have followed them either, since he knows as well as she does that they're hollow, so either they're evidence or they're not and Dev can't have it both ways. Dev said he can't know they're hollow, that from inside Ellis has no idea whether Vern followed a plot or was showing off, and that uncertainty is exactly what makes following rational at every single step. (6) Ray said then the whole thing is unfalsifiable, since a cascade looks identical from the outside whether people are reasoning beautifully or panicking, so it isn't a theory, it's a vibe with math attached. Dev said there is a testable difference: cascades are fragile, and one piece of solid public information — say a county extension agent publishing real soil results — flips the entire town overnight, which no amount of private doubt could ever do.

Can you go through the exchange move by move and tell me who's right at each step, with a reason for each rating, rather than just declaring a winner at the end? I'd also like to know where Dev is overclaiming, if she is, and whether Vern's grandstanding in front of witnesses changes anything about whether this should even count as a cascade — Dev treated it as just one more sign on the road and I'm not sure that's fair to the scene. And finally, what are the two of them missing that I ought to be building in? Please answer in American English. Sorry for the run-on in that second paragraph, I typed it in a hurry.
