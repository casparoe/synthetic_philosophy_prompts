I'm 72, three years out of the lab, and I spent about thirty-five of them doing protein crystallography. Since retiring I've sort of fallen into philosophy of science — a neighbour lent me a book and then another one — and the disorienting thing is that the entire method I built a career on turns out to have a name in that literature and a whole argument attached to it. Fine. But there's one objection to it that I have not been able to shake, and I've decided it wants a poem rather than an essay, so I'm asking for the poem. The objection, roughly: you are allowed to rank the explanations you've got, and ranking is pretty easy to defend — fit, simplicity, how much you get for free, whether it points somewhere new to look. Nobody ever promised you that the true one was on your list. In my own field this wasn't a hypothetical. To solve a structure you need phases and the detector only ever gives you intensities, so the phases are simply gone, and the ordinary way to get them back is to borrow: find an already-solved structure similar enough to yours, fit it into your data, use it to phase, build your model into the map that falls out. Which means the standard method can only hand you back an answer that resembles something somebody already knew. Novel fold, no borrowed starting model, no structure — you have to go get the phases the hard way instead, with heavy atoms, and even then you're still building into a map and guessing at the parts of it that don't look like anything. I spent a career ranking lists I did not write. There's also a distinction I liked, between the explanation that would be best if it were true and the one that's actually most likely to be true, and my strong sense from the bench is that you only ever get to rank the first kind, so the winner is the prettiest, not the truest.

So here's what I want. A poem of about 18 to 22 lines that is literally a ranked shortlist: four or five candidate explanations of one and the same set of observations, presented in order, one stanza each, each scored against the usual virtues. Make the stanzas uniform — four lines each, a settled rhyme scheme, a steady metre, roughly the same rhythmical weight — so the whole thing reads like a tidy procedure being correctly followed, because the tidiness is the trap. Then break the shape in the last stanza or the closing couplet: drop to two lines, or one, or refuse the rhyme, or run the metre over. The break should be the argument. The ranking was never the weak link, the list was, and the reader should feel the floor give way under the fifth stanza rather than be told about it. I'd like the true explanation to appear only as an absence — never nominated, never scored, not even placed last, just not in the room while we were arguing about the room. Don't resolve it, and please no consolations about how science self-corrects over the long run, or how we do better than coin-flipping; leave the unease standing, that's honestly where I got to.

Two ground rules. No philosophers named and no jargon in the poem itself — don't use the words underdetermination, bad lot, or the name of the inference rule; a reader who knows the debate should be able to say precisely which objection the poem is making from the shape alone, and a reader who doesn't should just get a good poem about being confidently wrong. And keep it concrete rather than abstract: one real image from a bench or a detector doing the work. Something like an electron density map that's smooth and interpretable for four fifths of its length and then has a stretch of it that the winning model simply doesn't account for, blobby and unbuildable, the part you stop looking at. That's the truest version of my worry I've found and I'd like the poem to end on it. My reading group meets in my living room on thursdays and I want to hand this out at the start of the session before anyone says anything clever, so it should stand alone without an introduction — no title that explains the joke, and don't append a note telling me what it means.
