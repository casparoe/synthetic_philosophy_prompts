I'm a third-year PhD student in mechanistic interpretability, and my project has stalled on a question I cannot make precise: what makes two circuits the same mechanism? I have accumulated verdicts from my own interventions and thought cases, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to. I'm not looking for a literature survey and I don't want citations; I want a criterion.

My verdicts:

1. Induction in a two-layer model versus induction in a twelve-layer model. In the small model the whole thing is one previous-token head feeding one induction head by query composition. In the large model prefix-matching is distributed across three heads in layers 4 through 6, and the copying is performed by an MLP that reads their output. The two agree on every corrupted prompt I can construct, including held-out prefix lengths and novel prefix tokens. Same mechanism.

2. A lookup table that passes the same evaluation. In a third model, the same benchmark is handled by memorized (prefix, continuation) pairs stored in MLP weights; it matches the induction models on the entire evaluation set and diverges only on prefixes I generate deliberately to break it. Different mechanism, even though it is behaviorally indistinguishable on everything my benchmark measures.

3. Shared components versus dedicated ones. In model A, the head doing prefix-matching is also load-bearing for an unrelated document-completion task; in model B, the functionally identical circuit is built from components that do nothing else. Under my full intervention set the profiles coincide. Same mechanism — entanglement with other tasks doesn't break identity.

4. A role swap. In model C the prefix-matching is done by an MLP and the copying by attention, so the sub-roles are exchanged relative to A and B. Every intervention I can run — activation patching, path patching on each edge, component knockout — gives the same result as in A and B; the abstract causal graph is isomorphic under relabeling of node types. Different mechanism. I am aware this sits badly with 3 and with 1, and I cannot say why I hold it.

5. Redundant copies inside a single model. I find two sub-circuits, in layers 5 and 9, each of which produces the same comparison-of-two-numbers behavior; ablating either costs partial accuracy, ablating both destroys the task, and their profiles match on every input I test. One mechanism, implemented twice — not two mechanisms. But when I find a third, structurally similar sub-circuit in layer 11 that fires only when the two operands have different digit counts, I call that a different mechanism, even though it looks like a descendant of the same one.

6. Dictionaries of different granularity. Two sparse autoencoders trained on the same layer's activations, one wide and sparse, one narrow and dense. Where the fine dictionary has two latents whose directions approximately sum to a single coarse-dictionary latent, and their activations never dissociate on my test set, I say: same feature, just described at two resolutions. Where the coarse dictionary has one latent covering what the fine dictionary separates into two latents that do dissociate — one tracking a syntactic pattern, one tracking a topic — I say the coarse latent is not the same feature as either. Splitting preserves identity; merging does not. I cannot justify the asymmetry.

7. Across tokenization schemes. A character-level model and a byte-pair model both contain prefix-match-and-copy machinery. Same mechanism, notwithstanding that the objects being matched are not even the same kind of thing.

8. A component that only shows up under mean ablation. In one model, mean-ablating a particular attention head substantially degrades the task, but patching it from a corrupted run into a clean run does nothing, because its contribution is essentially the same in both runs. Not part of the mechanism — background, not signal. Conversely, a component that shows no effect under mean ablation but a large one under resampling from an off-distribution baseline I count as part of the mechanism.

And the case I keep going back and forth on. In the twelve-layer model, an automated edge-pruning procedure gives me a minimal sufficient subgraph of four edges that reproduces the clean behavior when everything else is ablated. But there are nine further edges carrying measurable signal, each individually unnecessary. Is "the mechanism" the minimal sufficient subgraph, or the whole signal-carrying set? My answer seems to change depending on which of the verdicts above I am trying to protect, and I suspect that is a symptom of not having a criterion at all.

The squeeze I can see is this: any criterion built purely out of agreement under a library of interventions appears forced to call 4 the same mechanism, and any criterion sensitive to the substrate that realizes the sub-roles appears forced to call 1 and 7 different mechanisms. I don't want either.

What I'd like:

(a) State one criterion, or a small number of competing candidates, precisely enough that I could check it given a finite library of interventions and a finite set of components. If it needs to be stated in terms of a structure-preserving map between two implementations, say exactly what structure the map must preserve and over which interventions the match must hold — and what the criterion says when my intervention library is incomplete, which it always is.

(b) For each candidate, say which of my eight verdicts it delivers and which it overturns. Tell me in particular what to do about 3 against 4: I don't see how to keep both without letting identity turn on substrate in a way that looks arbitrary, and I don't see how to give up 4 without giving up 1.

(c) Diagnose or repair the asymmetry in 6, and say whether it is really an asymmetry or an artifact of how I described the cases.

(d) Rule on the flip-flop: minimal sufficient subgraph or full signal-carrying set. Must a criterion choose, and if it chooses, which of 1, 5, and 8 does that choice destabilize?

(e) Give me verdicts on three cases I have not listed that you expect any defensible criterion to be pressed on. For at least one of them, predict where I will resist the verdict and say whether you think my resistance is principled or just conservatism. A base model and its instruction-tuned descendant, where the circuit survives fine-tuning with reweighted edges, is one such case — say what your criterion gives there and whether historical continuity is doing any independent work, given that I already call independently trained models the same mechanism in 1.

(f) Tell me honestly which of my verdicts cannot be accommodated by any criterion that isn't gerrymandered, and whether same-mechanism should be binary at all or graded — and if graded, graded in what respect, since 5 and 8 suggest there may be more than one dimension along which two circuits can fail to coincide.

Please begin with your bottom line in the first sentence — the criterion you would actually defend, stated in one sentence — and then give the reasoning. No preamble, and no restatement of my question or my cases before you get to the substance.
