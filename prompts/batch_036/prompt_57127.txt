I am the mayor of a city of roughly 140,000 people, and next Tuesday I open the first session of what our charter amendment calls the Citizens' Review Panel — a standing body, not a one-off. One hundred and ten residents were drawn at random from a merged frame built out of voter registrations, utility accounts, and public-library cards. They serve a single, non-renewable fourteen-month term. They issue recommendations on the annual budget, on council district boundaries after each census, and on any ordinance the council refers to them. Under the amendment the council may reject a recommendation, but only by recorded vote and only with a written statement of reasons published within thirty days.

Three facts about the room complicate the opening address.

First, of the 620 people originally sampled, 510 declined or never responded. The 110 who turned up are noticeably older, more home-owning, and more college-educated than the city as a whole. Our own consultant flagged this in a report that two of the members have read, and they have told the clerk they intend to raise it on day one: they want to know whether they are a fair sample of the city or a self-selected group of people with time on their hands.

Second, sitting among the sampled members are two people who ran for council and lost, and one former council member who served three terms and was drawn this year like anyone else. She has said publicly that she intends to be useful and that she does not plan to keep quiet.

Third, the other half of the seats belong to elected officials, department heads, and union representatives who are required to attend. Several of them hold that a body nobody voted for has no business instructing a body everybody voted for. I am among the few people in the room who is not ambivalent about the panel, but I am also, plainly, a career politician: this is my third term, term limits bar me from a fourth, and I campaigned for the amendment in part because I knew it would outlive me. Some people in the room think that is the whole explanation of my enthusiasm, and they are not entirely wrong.

Please write the opening address. Six to seven minutes spoken, so roughly 800 to 950 words, in plain sentences a tired person can follow at four in the afternoon. It needs to do four things at once, and I would rather it did them honestly than smoothly.

It must give the sampled members a warrant for deciding that does not rest on their being experts — they are not, and they will not become so in fourteen months — nor on their being a statistically accurate mirror of the city, which they are not, nor on their being better people than the officeholders they are advising, which they are not either. Find what is left after those three are set aside, and build on it.

It must tell the elected half why they should take the panel's recommendations seriously, on grounds that are neither flattery nor threat. The rejection-with-written-reasons mechanism is the lever here, but the speech should explain what that mechanism actually does to a council member's incentives rather than merely reciting it.

It must deal with the refusal-rate problem in the open, in ordinary language, without either conceding that the panel is illegitimate or papering over the gap between the city and the room. If the honest position is that the panel represents the city in some respects and not in others, say which respects, and say why the ones it has are worth anything.

It must handle the former council member without naming her, in a way that simultaneously tells the two unsuccessful candidates what their situation is here. One of these three people is in the room by design and two are here by the accident of the frame; the speech should make that difference visible to them without humiliating anyone.

Two things I will not say, and neither should the speech: that this is democracy in its purest form, and that the panel's authority comes from its members having no ambitions. They do have ambitions. The design makes some of those ambitions harmless, which is a different and less flattering claim. If the address can explain that difference in concrete terms — what a term you cannot renew, a stipend you cannot campaign on, and a constituency you do not have actually do to what a person is willing to say out loud — it will have done most of what I need.

Finally, draw whatever distinction you think makes the panel's authority its own rather than borrowed from the council, but draw it in words a sixteen-year-old would understand. No jargon, no Latin, and nothing that requires the audience to have read political theory to follow along.
