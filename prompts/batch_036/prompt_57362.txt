I am assembling a piece of fiction for a community reading in my hometown, and I would like your help writing it. Some background first, because the story has to grow out of something that actually happened.

I grew up in a country that lost its currency twice before I turned twenty. The second time, my grandmother's savings — a shoebox of banknotes she had counted and rearranged for years, and whose denominations she could recite in her sleep — became the paper we used to start the stove. Within a year the town had a credit circle of its own: a woman at a desk with a ledger, stamped cards, and paper notes good at eleven shops and nowhere else. Half the town took them. My uncle did not. He kept a tin of the old hard money under his bed and would not touch the cards, on the grounds that they were not real money and had never been a real thing.

A few years ago I came across a pamphlet that I have not been able to put down since: *The Querist*, by George Berkeley — the Anglo-Irish philosopher, by then Bishop of Cloyne — first published in 1735, its three parts later issued together, written from beginning to end as a run of numbered questions with no answers offered. He wrote it about Irish poverty and about whether the shortage of coin was really the trouble. What stopped me is how precisely it describes my grandmother's shoebox and my uncle's tin. Here are the queries that matter to me, quoted so that you have them exactly:

- Query 1: "Whether there ever was, is, or will be, an industrious nation poor, or an idle rich?"
- Query 4: "Whether the four elements, and man's labour therein, be not the true source of wealth?"
- Query 5: "Whether money be not only so far useful, as it stirreth up industry, enabling men mutually to participate the fruits of each other's labour?"
- Query 6: "Whether any other means, equally conducing to excite and circulate the industry of mankind, may not be as useful as money."
- Query 20: "Whether the creating of wants be not the likeliest way to produce industry in a people? And whether, if our peasants were accustomed to eat beef and wear shoes, they would not be more industrious?"
- Query 22: "Whether, therefore, less money swiftly circulating, be not, in effect, equivalent to more money slowly circulating?"
- Query 23: "Whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, or as being a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, as is variously suggested by writers? And whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?"
- Query 26: "Whether the denominations being retained, although the bullion were gone, things might not nevertheless be rated, bought, and sold, industry promoted, and a circulation of commerce maintained?"
- Query 32: "Whether there be any vertue in gold or silver, other than as they set people at work, or create industry?"
- Query 33: "Whether it be not the opinion or will of the people, exciting them to industry, that truly enricheth a nation?"

A little further on he asks what makes a wealthy people and whether mines of gold and silver can do it, observing that those who live among the gold sands of Africa are themselves poor and destitute.

What I want is a short story of roughly 2,200 to 2,800 words that thinks these claims through as events rather than as arguments.

Setting: invent it. A small inland town, any century you like provided it is pre-industrial enough that nobody can send for money elsewhere and no outside institution will rescue anybody; a harvest that has partly failed; and a stranger of some standing — clergyman, surveyor, assessor, whatever fits — who arrives asking questions and does not answer them.

Requirements:

1. No character may state the theory. Nobody announces that money is a token or delivers a speech about circulation. Keep modern vocabulary out of the dialogue entirely: no currency, monetary, liquidity, inflation, credit rating, fiat, or economy. The reader should be able to reconstruct the view purely from what happens.

2. At least four of the quoted queries must be load-bearing in the plot — that is, if the story's world did not work the way the query says, the scene would come out differently. In particular I want the story to dramatize: tokens that keep working after the metal they were issued against has gone away (26); the gap between a large stock of money that does not move and a small stock that moves quickly (22); somebody made poorer by being richer, the tin under the bed, which follows from what Berkeley says about money's use lying in its stirring up industry; and an appetite that has to be manufactured before anyone does any work at all (20).

3. The querist figure must not be a hero. Let him be once wrong, or too late, or unable to persuade the single person who matters. Berkeley makes a nation's wealth depend on the opinion and will of its people, so a story faithful to that has to let opinion turn the other way as well. I would rather the ending be unresolved than triumphant, and I would rather the town's paper fail for human reasons than succeed by clever design.

4. If you wish, you may break the prose once with a short run of the stranger's unanswered questions — the pamphlet's own device — but those questions must not explain what has just happened.

On the writing itself: American English throughout, in the narration and in everything else you produce; period-appropriate diction; no breadline, no riot, no bank run as the climax; and please avoid the sentimental ending built around a child and a coin. Do not attribute any line to Berkeley that is not among the queries quoted above. If you need him to make some further point, let the character put it in his own words — I have no way to check quotations, and one plausible-sounding invention would ruin the piece for me.

After the story, add a note of no more than 400 words in two parts. First, a plain-prose, scene-by-scene account of which query each key scene is doing work for. Second, an honest paragraph naming the one place where you had to bend or simplify Berkeley to keep the story alive, and what that bending cost the argument. I am not asking for sources, references, or a bibliography, and I would rather you not guess at any.
