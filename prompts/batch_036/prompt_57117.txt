Last Saturday evening I found myself in a long and rather bruising argument with a friend of mine, a statistician by trade and a committed Bayesian by conviction, about whether Keynes ever had a coherent theory of probability or merely an elegant muddle. Her case, delivered with some force over the remains of a roast, was that the entire apparatus of the Treatise — probability conceived as an objective logical relation between a body of evidence and a hypothesis, the "weight" of an argument, the suggestion that some probabilities resist numerical comparison altogether — can be dissolved without residue into priors, likelihoods, and a second-order distribution over how much evidence one might yet collect. Ramsey made the same objection, she pointed out, and made it better. My case, which I defended badly and am still annoyed about, was that what survives the dissolution is precisely the interesting part: that a judgement resting on a thin body of evidence differs in kind, and not merely in confidence, from the identical judgement resting on a comprehensive one; and that in the matters Keynes came to care about in the thirties there is no balance of evidence to be had at all, only a convention adopted so that action can proceed.

She has now, with what I take to be considerable malice, asked me to give the retirement speech for her doctoral supervisor, Dr Helen Ashworth, who spent the whole of her career in the long-range group of a national forecasting agency. The dinner is at the end of next month: roughly ninety people. A third are econometricians, actuaries and government forecasters who will recognise every move I make, including the ones I do not intend to make. A third are her former analysts, now scattered across treasuries and central banks. The remaining third are family — a husband, two daughters, a brother — who have heard a great deal about forecast rounds and nothing whatsoever about epistemology, and who are entitled to be spoken to as human beings rather than as an audience for a set piece. Six minutes, give or take: about nine hundred words.

The following constraints matter, and they are not decorative.

- No philosopher may be named. Not Keynes, not Ramsey, not anybody. If an idea cannot be carried in ordinary language before an audience of which a third has never met it, it is not to be carried at all.
- The speech must not contain a single numeral or spelled-out number. Not her years of service, not the year she joined, not a percentage, not a joke about error bars. The room is full of people whose livelihood is quantities, and the omission has to feel like a choice rather than a deprivation — which is the hardest thing I have asked you to do.
- The distinction between the balance of evidence and the weight of it must do real structural work, not appear as an aside. Two of Keynes's sentences may be used, unattributed: "New evidence will sometimes decrease the probability of an argument, but it will always increase its 'weight.'"; and, from the chapter on the measurement of probabilities, "If the barometer is high, but the clouds are black, it is not always rational that one should prevail over the other in our minds, or even that we should balance them, though it will be rational to allow caprice to determine us and to waste no time on the debate." Helen will know exactly where both come from; the room will hear only two good images. Neither may be introduced as a quotation from anybody.
- She was never a convert, and the speech must not imply that she was. She was a calibrationist to her bones: she thought the honest thing was to commit to a number and be scored on it, and that a forecaster who hid behind vagueness was a coward. The speech may locate the point at which her practice and my argument genuinely touch, but it may not enlist her. It must not read as a victory lap, and it must not read as a rebuke.
- Use the following material, all of it true. When a central projection of hers had to be withdrawn and reissued mid-round, she kept the superseded version in her desk drawer, annotated in pencil, and handed it to every analyst who joined the group afterwards without explanation. Her standing question to juniors was not what the model said but what would have to be true. She objected, unsuccessfully and in private, to publishing a spread around one class of very long-horizon projection, on the grounds that the fan would be ornament rather than information. And she kept on her office windowsill a brass barometer that had not given a reading since long before I met her, which she would gesture at when a visitor asked how she knew.
- There must be one genuinely funny moment that the forecasters will recognise instantly and everyone else will enjoy anyway.
- The last minute belongs to the family, and must not be a philosophical peroration.

Please write the speech. Then, separately and in no more than a hundred and fifty words, tell me where the philosophical commitments actually constrained the wording — which sentences exist because of the distinction between weight and balance, which exist because of the absence of numbers — and which of the constraints above you found hardest to satisfy.
