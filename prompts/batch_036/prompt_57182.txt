I teach a 14-person senior seminar called "Computation and the Mind" — half philosophy majors, half comp-sci kids who signed up thinking it would be easy — and I've been putting off writing the final for way too long. It's a three-hour sit-down with two 20-mark essays; I've already got one on Turing's imitation game and Lovelace's objection, and for the second I want to build the whole thing around von Neumann's unfinished Silliman lectures, the little posthumous Yale book where he compares the nervous system to the computing machines of his day. The reason I like it for an exam is that it's full of claims that cut against the popular reading of the guy: neurons look digital to him because of their threshold, all-or-none behaviour, but the memory side of things looks associative and statistical rather than addressed and sharply localised; the nervous system in his telling gets by on arithmetic of very low precision while a machine needs long, precise arithmetical chains to do anything worth doing, which is the source of that contrast he draws between how deep the arithmetic has to be and how deep the logic has to be; and then he turns around and says the nervous system doesn't speak anything like a formal mathematical language, and that the analogy with automata may actively mislead us. Plus the last lecture's business about there being a threshold of complication below which self-reproducing automata degenerate and above which they don't.

So here's what I need from you: write the essay question itself, a model answer of around 650 words, and a marking scheme that adds up to 20 marks, broken into five must-hit points with the marks for each and one line per point saying how candidates typically fumble it. The question has to be impossible to pass by writing "von Neumann thought the brain was a computer" — I want the top answer to notice that his comparison is deliberately hedged and limited, and I want it to force a stand on something my TA and I argued about over lunch: whether the low-precision point actually undercuts the analogy or deepens it. That is, if the nervous system really runs shallow arithmetic with tons of redundancy and statistical coding instead of exact calculation, it starts to look precisely like the kind of noisy digital device an information theorist would design, which would flip von Neumann's own conclusion on its head. Make the question require them to engage at least one of the later-lecture moves (the non-mathematical language point or the complication threshold) so nobody can hide inside lecture one.

A few constraints. The marking scheme should separate an A from a B by naming the specific moves, not by vibes, and it should give zero credit for the cheap stuff I fully expect to see: hauling in the Chinese Room, announcing that the brain is "wetware", Penrose-style appeals to quantum effects, "he basically predicted deep learning", and any gesture at what modern neuroscience has since found as though it settled a conceptual question. Also, penalise pure description — my TA is convinced a third of the class will recite the all-or-none business and then stop dead. Students had no required secondary literature on this, only the three lectures, so don't make the model answer depend on anything they couldn't have gotten from the text and our seminar discussions. Department wants the paper from me by Friday, so please keep the whole thing tight and self-contained.
