I run training in a breast imaging unit and we're trying to write down what our four best mammographers actually do when they read a film, so we can teach residents insted of hoping they pick it up by osmosis over five years. The plan: expert narrates out loud while reading, we record it, transcribe it, and turn the transcript into a decision rule with explicit thresholds.

I worried the whole thing was pointless, since Ryle showed knowing how and knowing that are different kinds of knowledge and you can't just cash a skill out in propositions — that's the force of the regress argument, right? But a colleague pointed out that narration gets around it: the skill lives in System 1, and making them talk slowly while they look recruits System 2, which converts the know-how into know-that on the spot. That feels right to me, and it also explains why our experts are so useless when a resident asks 'why did you call that back?' mid-session — they just haven't slowed down yet.

One snag. Two of the four narrate totally differently for the same finding. One says she's hunting for architectural distortion from the first second; the other says she never consciously looks for that, the density just 'reads wrong' before she knows what's wrong with it. If System 2 is faithfully reporting on System 1, I'd expect their transcripts to converge a lot more than they do.

So how should I design the sessions to get the most complete extraction — live at the workstation, or afterwards from a replay of where they clicked and zoomed?
