I am a novelist, and my new book — set between a Bordeaux trading house and a plantation in Saint-Domingue across the years of the Revolution — advances a thesis I must now defend in public: that the Enlightenment did not merely fall short of its universalism in the colonies, but that this universalism was among the instruments through which domination was administered. My argument, in brief, is that a single intellectual kit — classification, quantification, the abstract rights-bearing individual, and the vocabulary of improvement and of a civilizing exception — made emancipation thinkable and the cargo ledger thinkable at once; that abstraction rather than hypocrisy is the culprit; and that the Convention's abolition of slavery and its reversal under Napoleon are two outputs of one machine rather than a betrayal of principle by principle.

Next month I appear on a festival panel where my interlocutors will be a historian of the eighteenth-century French Atlantic economy, a philosopher who works on the foundations of universal human rights, and a critic serving as moderator, while the room itself will be general readers with graduate students in history and in postcolonial literary studies occupying the front rows. Prepare me for the question period. Give me the eight objections or so I should genuinely expect, ordered by how much damage each would do, and phrase each in the idiom of the person likeliest to raise it — the historian's will not sound like the philosopher's, and neither will sound like the graduate students'. For every objection, say how much of it I ought to concede from the podium, sketch a reply I can deliver in under a minute, and mark precisely where that reply is soft. Then tell me which single answer must land for the thesis to survive the hour, and name the question I am privately hoping nobody asks.
