For eleven years I wrote the explanatory memoranda that accompany proposed rules at a state agency — the documents that tell a legislative committee what a regulation will cost, whom it will reach, and which alternatives were considered and rejected — and for the past three I have done comparable work for an organization that files comments on those rules from the outside. Last spring a book group of mine took up Orwell's 1946 essay on politics and the English language, and I found myself in the odd position of agreeing with every sentence of it while thinking that it described something adjacent to, rather than identical with, the thing I actually encounter all day. Orwell's diagnosis, as I read it, is fundamentally about texture: vagueness, worn-out imagery, diction inflated to sound impartial, the refusal to call a thing by its name, and the insincerity that generates all of these. What I keep running into has almost none of that texture. The documents I wrote and the ones I now criticize are clean, precise, and largely free of pretentious diction; their authors are competent and honest people who could define any term they use, and most of them would pass every one of Orwell's stylistic tests. And yet positions that ought to be stateable in that language turn out either not to be stateable in it at all or to be stateable only in a form that misrepresents them.

Two instances. First: in our memoranda every countable noun denoted an intervention the agency could administer — a bed-night, a placement, a service unit, an enrollment, an episode of care — and every axis of comparison ran between such things: cost per unit, utilization, rate of completion. At a hearing on rules governing emergency shelter, a commenter argued that the people in question should be given money outright, with no program attached and nothing administered. She said this in plain English, it went into the transcript, and nobody interrupted her. But the memorandum summarizing the comment could only represent it as a request for a new program category, a form of direct financial assistance, which was then scored against the incumbent programs on the incumbent axes and lost badly. Nothing had been suppressed and no word had been smeared; the proposal had simply been absorbed by being translated into the only distinctions the framework marked. The question she was actually raising — whether a program was the right unit of analysis to begin with — had nowhere to be recorded, and any attempt to raise it inside the framework came out sounding like an argument for one more program. Second: I now sit on a hospital committee that reviews patient-safety reports. The reporting form's categories are all indexed to what the institution does — an event counts if it occurs after admission, a harm counts if it traces to an identifiable act or omission by an identifiable staff member, and anything left over goes in a residual box labeled 'other.' The consequence is that a familiar kind of bad outcome cannot be entered at all: the one produced by nobody's error and by nothing done inside our walls, but by the fact that a patient was discharged into circumstances in which the care plan could not be followed. Everyone uses the form fluently and in good faith, every box is neutrally defined, and there is not a euphemism anywhere on the page.

So I want to know whether this has a name. People I have described it to reach for neighbors that do not seem to fit. It is not the case of a group lacking words for its own experience, because here the vocabulary is rich rather than deficient and everyone is fluent in it. It is not euphemism or doublespeak, because the terms are accurate as far as they go and are not doing any concealment. It is not the strong thesis that language bounds what can be thought, because the woman at the hearing thought and said the thing perfectly well; what she could not do was make it register as the kind of claim it was. It is not framing in the ordinary sense either, since framing is something a speaker does to an audience, whereas this is a standing property of the medium that binds the person doing the framing just as tightly as it binds everyone else — I wrote those memoranda, and the framework foreclosed my own alternatives before it foreclosed anyone else's. Orwell's invented language of total surveillance in his last novel comes closest in spirit, but as I understand the design, it works by deleting words and by welding approval and disapproval onto the ones that remain; here nothing is deleted and nothing is slanted, and the entire effect comes from where the joints of the classification fall, which distinctions are marked and which are not, and what the residual box does to whatever lands in it.

Tell me whether an established concept covers this, and if there are two or three candidates in the neighborhood, tell me which of them the description actually falls under and precisely where it falls outside each — I want the boundaries much more than I want the label. If nothing fits, coin a term, define it so that it cannot collapse back into ideology, framing, or some slogan about media and messages, and specify the edges of the phenomenon for me. In particular: are the hearing and the hospital form the same thing, or does one involve a distortion that the other merely omits? Does a case in which the missing distinction is only awkward to express — you can say it, but it takes a paragraph and reads as a digression — count as an instance, or does the concept require that the attempt to state the position actively turn it into a different position? And is the phenomenon properly located in the vocabulary, in the forms and metrics built on top of it, or in the habits of the people using them?
