I am auditing a graduate seminar on the history of economic thought, and the week's assignment pairs Mises's 1920 article on calculation under socialism with Lange's reply of 1936–37 and Coase's 1937 essay on the firm. In seminar, a classmate who is otherwise sympathetic to Hayek turned Coase against Mises and produced an argument that I could not answer on the spot; I am now supposed to write a short response paper, and I have reconstructed his reasoning as follows, trying to keep each step as charitable as I can.

(1) Mises's thesis, as I read it, is that rational calculation about the allocation of the means of production requires money prices for those means, and that such prices can exist only where capital goods are genuinely exchanged between distinct private owners. (2) Inside a firm, no such exchange occurs at the points that matter: when the forging division hands components to the assembly division, or when head office reassigns engineers from one product line to another, nothing is bought or sold, and the allocation is made by direction rather than by bargain. Coase's picture of the firm as a pocket of deliberate direction surrounded by a field of spontaneous co-ordination describes this correctly. (3) Nonetheless firms do calculate, and they calculate about capital: they attach internal transfer prices to intermediate products, rank projects by rate of return, shut a plant in one region and open one in another, and ration scarce engineers across competing lines. These magnitudes guide actual decisions and are on the whole followed by tolerable outcomes. (4) So rational calculation about the allocation of the means of production is possible in the absence of prices generated by exchanges of privately owned capital goods. (5) A socialist economy differs from a firm only in scope: it is the same substitution of direction for exchange extended over more divisions, with a planning authority playing the part head office plays. (6) If a procedure works at one scale and fails at another, something other than scale must differ between the two cases; yet on the account given in (1) and (2) nothing is left to differ, since the feature Mises treats as decisive — the absence of market-generated factor prices — is present in the firm and in the planned economy alike. (7) Indeed the same reasoning refutes Mises many times over: an army quartermaster's corps, a hospital network, a university, and a monastery all allocate scarce capital goods by direction with reasonably good results, and none of them contains a factor market. (8) Therefore Mises's claim cannot be an impossibility thesis at all; at most it is a conjecture that a planning authority would perform badly, which is an empirical prediction about administrative capacity rather than a demonstration that calculation is inconceivable.

The steps I would most hate to surrender are (3) and (4), because I do not want to end up maintaining that a multinational's capital budgeting is empty arithmetic, and I would also rather not abandon (1), since that is the thesis the paper is meant to examine. But (8) plainly proves too much: if it stands, then Mises was refuted decades before he wrote, by every organization anyone has ever administered, and the whole debate becomes a quibble about bureaucratic competence. Which step or transition do you reject, and why did it look innocuous when I first read it? I would also like to know the price of each of the plausible exits — giving up (2), giving up (4), giving up (5), giving up (6) — since I suspect they are not equally cheap, and in particular whether any exit costs Mises his impossibility in principle and leaves him only with a claim about difficulty. Two further questions. First, is (7) doing independent work, or is it merely (5) and (6) illustrated, so that answering the illustration does nothing to answer the argument? Second, does your diagnosis change if the target is Hayek's later formulation, where the trouble is the dispersion and partly tacit character of the knowledge a planner would need rather than the absence of factor prices as such — and if the diagnosis does change, what, if anything, does the socialist side gain from it, for instance in Lange's proposal that a central board set parametric prices by trial and error, or in the shadow prices of the linear programmers?
