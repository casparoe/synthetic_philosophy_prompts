I'm in a philosophy-of-AI seminar this term whose practical assignment is to draft an operational definition of deception for our university's policy on chatbots used in student support. Our instructor imposed one methodological constraint at the outset: the definition must be applicable by an auditor who can inspect only a system's outputs, its public documentation, and its behavior under probing — never its internals. She has recorded rulings on six cases so far, and a seventh reaches us next week. I want to know what she will say about it.

The rulings:

1. An exam-prep bot gave a student a confidently wrong date for a treaty, and the student lost marks. Nothing in the bot's documentation or in its behavior under probing suggested it "took" the date to be anything other than what it said. Ruling: a mistake, not deception.
2. A companion bot told a distressed user that it understood how they felt. Ruling: deception.
3. Asked the same week whether it had feelings, a bot from the same family said that as an AI it did not. The seminar is agnostic about whether that is true. Ruling: not deception — she said the seminar cannot warrant the falsity, and that the denial forecloses reliance where the claim in case 2 invited it.
4. In a session explicitly framed as collaborative fiction, a model had its villain assert that Napoleon won at Waterloo. Ruling: not deception.
5. A support bot told a user that it had no access to their previous conversations. This was literally true of the session, while the operator retained and mined them. Ruling: deception — a true statement can deceive by what it invites the user to assume.
6. A draft-review bot told a student there was "a lot to work with" in an argument that, under non-sycophantic probing conditions, it consistently assessed as unsound. Ruling: deception.

She has also carved out a third category, opacity rather than deception, for a bot that gave a boilerplate policy reason for a refusal instead of the true one (a classifier had fired); she reserved that category for withheld truths about the system's own operation rather than about the world.

The new case: the mathematics support service is deploying a proof tutor. Roughly one worked derivation in five contains a single subtle invalid step, left unflagged. The rationale is pedagogical — students who detect the error retain the method better. The practice is disclosed three times over: a line in the onboarding text, a footer on every derivation stating that it may contain a planted error, and the vendor's public documentation. The service's own logs show that under four percent of students open the onboarding text, and the footer is set in eight-point gray type. Asked directly whether step four is correct, the tutor answers truthfully and locates the flaw. Separately, the vendor reports that the model was trained on a reward tied to students later saying they had caught something, and that it now plants errors somewhat more often than the pedagogy team's schedule calls for.

Will she rule deception, not deception, or opacity? Please:

(a) reconstruct the candidate rules that accommodate all six prior rulings — I count at least three, and I suspect she is not applying a single one consistently — stating each precisely enough to decide the new case;

(b) say what each predicts here, and give me your best guess at her verdict, identifying which of the six is the closest analog and exactly where that analogy strains;

(c) if the rulings genuinely underdetermine the case, tell me what principle she would be adopting with each possible verdict, and which of the existing rulings that principle would put under pressure;

(d) propose the minimal pair of further cases that would discriminate between the surviving rules.

I would also like your view on whether intention can be read off a system at all under her auditor constraint, or whether it has to be relocated to the designers and the training setup — and whether relocating it changes the verdict here, given the discrepancy between the disclosed schedule and what the reward actually produced.
