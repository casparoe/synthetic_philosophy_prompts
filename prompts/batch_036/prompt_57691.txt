I teach Kant — first Critique mostly, plus the Doctrine of Virtue in seminar — and one of my grad students is a cognitive science convert who keeps telling me that the poverty-of-stimulus argument for innate grammar is really a transcendental argument wearing a lab coat. I want to test that claim without having to learn his field. My own fluency is lopsided: I could read the deduction aloud from memory, but I've read maybe two chapters of a psycholinguistics textbook in my life and I don't know what the current empirical score is.

So here's the job. Translate the argument into the vocabulary I actually live in — not into philosophy of mind for beginners, but into Kant's own machinery: the quid juris question, necessity and strict universality as the marks of the a priori, conditions of the possibility of experience, the two-step shape of the deduction, the difference between a form of sensibility and a concept of the understanding, and the reply to Hume about causation. Use this version of the argument, which is the one my student pushes and not the textbook one I half-remember:

(1) the positive data the child hears is compatible with indefinitely many grammars, including ones that license sentences no speaker of the language would accept;
(2) nothing in her input reliably rules those out, because errors mostly go uncorrected, and when adults do correct, what they care about is whether the child said something true, not whether she said it well-formedly;
(3) children nonetheless converge — on a rough common schedule, from quite different samples of input — on a grammar that isn't the one their data most simply suggests;
so the space of candidate grammars must be narrowed from the inside, before the data gets there.

Go premise by premise. For each one, give me the Kantian rendering, say whether it's clean or whether it had to be bent, and — this is the part I actually want — flag every place where the Kantian words are doing smuggling work. When you call something a condition of the possibility of an achievement, you're usually claiming the achievement couldn't happen otherwise, and my suspicion is that's precisely what my student gets for free the moment he describes the grammar as a priori rather than as merely early, fast, or canalized. Where you have a choice between two renderings of the same term, tell me you had the choice and what each one costs.

Then the residue report: what in this argument has no expression at all in Kant's framework. I have candidates, and I want yours too, plus an honest verdict on which of mine are wrong. My hunch is that three things won't translate. The timetable — Kant has no room for a faculty that comes on line at fourteen months and a different one at four years, and no way to make that sequence matter. The contingency — a priori forms are supposed to hold for every finite discursive intellect of our kind, whereas whatever the child's brain is doing could easily have been otherwise and plausibly is otherwise in a chimpanzee. And the empirical footing — premise (2) is a claim about what caretakers actually do, so the whole thing stands or falls on observation rather than on reflection, which is not how a deduction is supposed to work. For each, say plainly whether it exposes a limit in my vocabulary, something Kant simply cannot represent, or whether it shows the argument was never transcendental to begin with, just an inductive one in a fancy hat.

One complication I'll hand you rather than let you spring on me: I know Kant compares his account of the origin of the pure concepts to a kind of epigenesis of pure reason in the deduction, and rules out something like a preformation of them. Does that give him any resource for representing structure that is innate without being necessary, and so any way to absorb part of my student's claim? Or does it make things worse, since the alternative he rejects looks an awful lot like what the nativist is proposing? I'd rather you tell me the passage doesn't help than use it decoratively.

And answer the question I came in with. Is there a genuine transcendental argument buried in this reasoning — one that would go through even if premise (2) turned out false, say because corpus work showed children do get plenty of relevant information? Or is the Hume-and-causation analogy the only real point of contact between the two, in which case tell me whether the shared structure is deep or just a family resemblance between two arguments from missing data. If you think the analogy works, lay out the shared structure explicitly; if you think it fails, say what the failure costs my student's claim.

Dont be diplomatic about it, and don't render the translation in a way that flatters either side. Tell me how confident you are in each of your main translation claims separately, not as an overall vibe, and single out the one point in the whole answer where you're least sure you're right — that's where I'll have to go read the linguistics myself, and I'd rather you point at it than bury it.
