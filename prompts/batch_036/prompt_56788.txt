A friend in my epistemology reading group has an argument against van Fraassen's Reflection principle and I genuinely can't tell whether it's any good. As I've reconstructed it:

(1) Reflection says that my present credence in p, conditional on my future self having credence x in p at some later time, should itself be x — i.e. I'm required to treat my future opinions as an expert's testimony.
(2) Deferring to an expert is rational only if I take that expert to be likely right about the matter at hand.
(3) So Reflection commits me to taking my future self to be likely right about p, for every p and every future time.
(4) But taking my future self to be likely right about p just is having no reason to expect to revise my present view of p; expecting to revise is expecting that my future self gets it wrong.
(5) I do expect to revise — I've signed up for the seminar, booked the archieves, and I fully expect my views on p to move a lot over the next year. That's what inquiry is.
(6) So Reflection implies that a rational inquirer can't expect to learn anything, which is absurd.
(7) Therefore Reflection is false.

He says the upshot is that Reflection is a dogmatism principle wearing a coherence costume. If I have to choose I'd rather keep (5) than (1) — expected opinion change seems non-negotiable — but I suspect something slid somewhere between (1) and (4) and I can't name it. Which step do you reject? For every premise you think is worth attacking, tell me why it looked innocent going in and what giving it up actually costs, and say whether the argument is a case of a bad step or a case of valid reasoning from premises that can't all stay. Then finish with whether Reflection can be cut down to something that still does the work van Fraassen wanted from it — the coherence/dutch-book style motivation, and licensing deference to my own better-informed later self — without delivering (6), and what the restricted version loses.
