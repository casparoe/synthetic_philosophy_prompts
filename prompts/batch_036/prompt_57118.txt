A friend and I argued for an hour about slot machines and got nowhere, so I'd like a more disciplined answer than either of us produced. The regulatory practice runs like this: a machine is licensed on the strength of its arithmetic rather than its history. The manufacturer files a worksheet giving the theoretical payback percentage, the testing lab examines the pay table and the number generator, and the machine goes onto the floor. Everyone agrees its realized payout over any realistic stretch will diverge from the filed figure, sometimes widely, and that this is expected; but if the divergence is large and persistent, the machine is treated as malfunctioning and pulled — not as evidence that the filed figure was mistaken. Meanwhile the same industry spends heavily on destroying information: continuous shufflers, early shoe cuts, and the ejection of card counters, all so that no player can acquire evidence bearing on the odds of the next hand.

What account of chance does this presuppose? It looks like a single-case propensity built into the device before it has produced any outcomes, plus Lewis's Principal Principle as the bridge from chance to credence. Yet a machine's generator is a deterministic algorithm, so the filed figure is really a calibrated measure of the player's ignorance — which suggests these are ideal credences rather than objective chances, and that the industry's evidential hygiene is a tacit admission of it. Take the three readings (propensity, best-system frequency, idealized epistemic probability for a specified kind of agent) and say which the practice could actually run on. Which features does each license, and which does each leave inexplicable — in particular, why the divergence test pushes toward "broken machine" rather than "wrong chance," and why a counter is ejected instead of simply out-bet. Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably, mark where it is underdetermined between rival theories, and say what would have to change in the certification regime if we abandoned the Principal Principle and instead required credences to track total evidence.
