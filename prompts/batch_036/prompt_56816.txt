So I sit on my professional association's classification committee — the body that decides what goes into our diagnostic manual and what comes out — and I'm trying to write up my own thinking before next month's meeting, because I can already tell we're going to argue in circles. What I want from you is a precedent-consistent recommendation, not a general essay about diagnosis. Please write in American English, and don't bother with references or a reading list; I just want the reasoning.

Our record over the last decade, roughly as it appears in the minutes:

1. Nine years ago we approved a brand-new category for a dissociative presentation that clinicians had already been coding under a catch-all residual code for about ten years. The stated rationale: the thing was already being recognized in practice, and giving it a name meant people could get treatment covered and stopped being told it was all in their heads.
2. At that same meeting we rejected a proposed "mild" subtype of an existing mood category. Rationale: we couldn't draw the threshold tightly enough, and we expected a mild subtype to swallow the parent category.
3. Six years ago we approved a purely nominal change — swapping a term that had curdled into an insult for a descriptive one — with zero change to the criteria. Nobody argued.
4. Also six years ago we declined to give its own category to a distress syndrome reported mainly in one region, but we did add a paragraph about it to the cultural-considerations section. Rationale, more or less: the people who brought it to us wanted recognition, not a new box.
5. Three years ago we rejected a proposal to create a category for a behavior pattern that had spread extremely fast through short-video platforms, even though we had a large petition from people saying the label had changed their lives and got them help. Rationale: we asked how many of these cases would have shown up absent the online spread, nobody could answer, and we didn't want to ratify a prevalence figure we couldn't interpret.
6. Same year we *approved* a category for a presentation seen almost entirely in people who can't report on their own experience at all — late-stage dementia. There was an objection that the classified would have no voice in it. Rationale: no name meant no care pathway, and there was no plausible mechanism by which being classified would change what it's like to be that patient.
7. Somewhere in there we attached a cautionary note to an established category, flagging that reported rates had climbed steeply and that clinicians should consider whether they were capturing normal variation. People inside the association credit that note with flattening the curve. Worth knowing: a naming intervention of ours has demonstrably bent a prevalence trend before.

Now the live proposal, which is the one I actually need help with. It concerns an existing category we created about twenty-five years ago for what was then thought to be an uncommon post-traumatic presentation. Current state of play: prevalence in the clinics that report to us is something like thirty times what it was at introduction; the criteria have been broadened twice, each time partly in response to organized advocacy; most new cases now arrive having already self-identified through online communities before they ever see a clinician; a substantial minority of the clinicians who write to us say a lot of present-day cases wouldn't have met the original criteria; two well-organized groups run by people carrying the diagnosis have filed statements saying that narrowing or removing it would be catastrophic, would strip them of school and workplace accommodations, and would be experienced as the association telling them they were never really ill; and we have no data whatsoever on how many of today's cases would have presented at all if the label had never existed. The options on the table are: leave it alone, narrow the criteria, attach a cautionary note like in item 7, demote it to a research appendix for conditions needing further study, remove it, or rename it.

Here's what I want:

- Tell me what we should decide if we want to be consistent with our own record, and make the case for it against at least two of the other options rather than against a strawman.
- Name the closest analog in the list above, and — this is the part I care about most — name the precedent that *looks* like the closest analog and would actually mislead us if we leaned on it. Say why the resemblance is superficial.
- Reconstruct the implicit rule or rules we've been running. I suspect more than one fits everything we've done, and I suspect at least one of them says voice and self-organization matter while another says they don't, because items 5 and 6 point in opposite directions on that. Lay out the candidate rules, mark which ones our record genuinely can't distinguish, and propose one concrete hypothetical future proposal that would tell them apart — a case where the rules give different answers.
- State flatly the principle we'd be adopting by whichever option you pick, especially as it concerns the two advocacy groups. If we narrow the criteria over their explicit objection, on the grounds that the category may have helped produce the very experiences they're reporting, what are we thereby committed to about the evidential weight of the classified people's own testimony? And is there a version of that commitment we could live with, or is it fatal to the whole enterprise?
- Flag anywhere in the seven items above where we were inconsistent and didn't notice, or where the rationale we wrote down is doing work that a different rationale would do better.
- Finally, tell me which part of the upcoming fight you think is about words rather than substance. My hunch is that "narrow the criteria," "demote to a research appendix," and "remove" get treated by both camps as three different acts when from the point of view of the person holding the diagnosis they might be one act, but I could be wrong and I want the wrongness checked.

Please use headers and a short table comparing your recommended option against the main alternatives under each candidate rule you identify. Don't pad it out, and if you think the honest answer is that our precedents genuinely underdetermine this and we're just choosing, say that plainly instead of dressing up a coin flip as a derivation.
