I am three-quarters of the way through a novel whose narrator is a historian seconded to a national commemorations board, where he rules on the inherited myths of defeat. He never states his criterion, and this is deliberate: he believes a principle announced in advance is a species of cheating. The chapters already drafted give his rulings. Masada, inadmissible. The Vidovdan cult around Kosovo, inadmissible. The Alamo, inadmissible. Dunkirk, admissible — although he spends forty pages on the French rearguard left on the beaches and does not permit it to move his verdict. The Foreign Legion's annual commemoration of Camerone in Mexico, admissible, invasion notwithstanding. Verdun, admissible.

In the chapter I have not yet written he must rule on Gallipoli as Australians keep it: a defeat commemorated as a nation's birth; an evacuation carried off with almost no loss and given almost no mention; the opposing commander folded into the mourning by a consolation attributed to Kemal and carved at the cove and in two southern capitals, whose attribution my researcher cannot pin down; Indigenous men who served in the first contingents and were for decades afterwards kept out of the associations of returned soldiers; and a day that has come to carry the weight of every subsequent war the country has joined.

Tell me how he rules, and on what principle you take him to be proceeding. Then give me at least one further principle that fits every ruling I have listed and reaches a different verdict on Gallipoli, so that I can see what I have accidentally committed him to. Finally, tell me which myth I should bring before him next if I want an attentive reader to be able to discriminate between those principles.
