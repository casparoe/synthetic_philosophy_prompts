**§2. The compression reading and why I want to replace it.** Dennett's criterion, as I read it in "Real Patterns" (1991) and in the later restatements, is that a description picks out a real pattern when it captures a regularity that would otherwise be visible only at enormous cost, or not at all, in a rival (typically lower-level) description: the higher-level description earns its keep by compressing information accessible through the rival. The standard interpretive consequence is a deflationary realism — beliefs, centers of mass, and designs are all real, in the same sense, because all of them are compressive, and the disputes between realists and instrumentalists about these levels are disagreements about nothing. My paper argues that compression is doing less work here than the interpretive tradition assumes, and that the substantive content of the criterion is modal. I propose to gloss it as follows: a higher-level description picks out a real pattern just in case the predictions licensed by that description survive a specified class of perturbations of the underlying description that leave the higher-level parameters fixed. I will call this "perturbational robustness," and it is my coinage, not Dennett's. Three considerations motivate the substitution. First, compressibility is too easily satisfied: any dataset can be compressed by a suitably engineered encoding, and gerrymandered predicates can be made to compress the particular data in hand, so compressibility alone cannot discriminate genuine explanatory levels from bookkeeping devices. Second, the compression relation is indexed to a choice of rival description, which makes the reality of a pattern hostage to a comparison class that nothing in the account tells us how to select. Third, and most importantly, the cases where our intuitions about reality and compressibility diverge are exactly the cases where robustness under intervention is what is at issue.

**§3. Perturbation classes, and the standoff it is supposed to dissolve.** The trouble, which I think is also the payoff, is that there is no privileged perturbation class. Consider three patterns and the interventions they survive. The center of mass of a gas survives any rearrangement of the molecules that preserves the mass distribution, yet it survives no intervention that shifts that distribution, and — this is how I read Dennett's own treatment of centers of gravity — it is not plausibly a locus of causal powers; robustness here buys us a summary statistic and nothing more. A functional description of a heart survives physical perturbation but not redesign: swap the organ for a pump with the same throughput and the description still holds, whereas a change of function defeats it even when the physical substrate is nearly untouched. An intentional description of an agent survives both physical perturbation and rational redesign, but fails under irrational perturbation, which is why the stance is both astonishingly powerful and systematically fragile. If realism about a level is relativized to a perturbation class, then the apparent inconsistency in Dennett — the realist who insists that beliefs are real patterns alongside the instrumentalist who insists that the stance is a mere predictive device — dissolves, because the two claims are made relative to different classes and are not in competition. What is left is not relativism about existence but a graded realism indexed to intervention type.
