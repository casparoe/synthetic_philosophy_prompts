So — I've got a small research network funded for two years, six of us, on origins and biological individuality, and I'm trying to work out what the first workshop should actually be about. Half the group are metaphysicians (modality, essence, the usual), two are developmental biologists, and one is an ethicist who works at a fertility clinic, which was a slightly impulsive invite of mine and is turning out to be the most useful thing I did all year.

The nominal topic is origin essentialism — the Kripkean line that you couldn't have come from a different sperm and egg. But the more I sit with it the less sure I am that the sorites problem everybody reaches for is the real crux, and I want your honest take on where the effort should go rather than a map of the terrain.

Things I'm weighing:

- The tolerance budget. If origins tolerate a bit of variation, iteration seems to walk you to somebody with wholly different origins, and nobody has a principled cutoff on offer. Is that fatal to the doctrine, or does it just show that origins are vague rather than that they aren't necessary?
- Twinning, in the direction people usually skip. Monozygotic twins come from one zygote, so essentialism read as a sufficiency claim looks like it says they're the same person. Does the essentialist have to relocate "origin" off the gamete pair and onto the zygote, or the process, or the whole developmental trajectory — and if she does, does the doctrine survive the move, or does it just stop being interesting?
- The thing my clinic colleague keeps pushing, and which I've started calling the protocol-origin problem for want of a better name: in IVF, which sperm meets which egg is settled by a technician's pipette, a culture dish, a lab protocol, a vitrification schedule. On the essentialist picture a person's identity is then hostage to lab procedure. Is there a distinct metaphysical problem there, or is it just a vivid repackaging of the old one? I genuinely can't tell.
- Chimerism and mitochondrial donation, where the origin isn't a tidy pair of gametes at all. Do those cases generalize into a real objection, or are they exotic enough that a theory is entitled to bracket them?
- Whether the debate is mis-specified to begin with — i.e. whether essence should be read modally at all, in which case "could you have come from different gametes" is the wrong question to be spending two years fighting about.

What I want back is a prioritized agenda for this particular group over two years: what's upstream of what, which of these a mixed room of metaphysicians and biologists can actually make headway on together versus which are solo-philosopher questions that will just stall in front of an audience of embryologists, and which one I should quietly deprioritise because settling it wouldn't change anything downstream. Be explicit about the criteria you're ranking by — how many other questions hang on it, tractability with the people I've actually got, neglectedness, whether the biologists have anything distinctive to contribute — and about the trade-offs between them. Then tell me what evidence over the next twelve months would move something up or down the list.

No survey and no reading lists, please; I've read most of it, more or less, and a bibliography is exactly what I can't use here. I want the ordering argued for.

Finish with a line beginning "Verdict:" giving the single question you'd have us work on first, in twelve words or fewer.
