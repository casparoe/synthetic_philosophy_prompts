I have spent the better part of a term going round in circles on Kavka's toxin puzzle with a small reading group, and I have arrived at a diagnosis that I now want properly stress-tested rather than merely admired. My position runs as follows. Intending is blocked by a doxastic condition: one cannot intend to φ unless one takes oneself to be going to φ, or at least regards it as a live possibility one is committed to. In the billionaire's case, I know full well that once the money has landed there is nothing whatsoever to be gained by drinking the toxin at the appointed hour and a day of misery to be lost, so I cannot believe I will drink it; hence the intention is simply unavailable to me. On this reading the puzzle is not about whether it would be rational to intend — the reward plainly makes intending worth having — but about the limits of what can be intended at all, and the correct general moral is something I have come to call the bootstrapping prohibition: no incentive conditional merely on possessing an intention can ever directly purchase that intention. Incentives can only ever work indirectly, by altering what the agent expects of herself — through precommitment devices, hiring someone to make the act unavoidable, self-deception, deliberate character change, or the cultivation of a standing policy that already covers the act. I hold that this is not a quirk of Kavka's eccentric billionaire but a structural feature of practical rationality, and that every apparent counterexample will turn out, on inspection, either to be a case of indirect self-management or to be a case in which the agent did not really intend but merely wished, pretended, or resolved in a sense that carries no commitment.

What I want from you is a map of this view's exposure: what would have to be true for it to be false, and how much of it is genuinely at risk. Please work through the following, and rank the resulting defeaters from fatal to merely awkward rather than listing them indiscriminately. First, is the doxastic condition a claim about the concept of intention or an empirical hypothesis about human psychology, and does the answer change what could count against it — in particular, could the condition be true by stipulation in a way that makes my whole position unassailable and therefore uninteresting? Second, take seriously the resolute agent of the Gauthier variety, who insists that once the intention is formed the later deliberation is constrained by it: does my view have to deny that such an agent can form the intention at all, or can it absorb her, and what is the cost of either answer? Third, consider the variants that seem designed to embarrass me — the billionaire who pays for intending to try rather than intending to drink, the agent who is told she will later be given a sufficient reason to drink, the agent with a small independent reason (a wager with a friend, a point of honour) already in place, the agent offered a second payment conditional on the drinking itself — and say for each whether it defeats the bootstrapping prohibition or merely relocates it. Fourth, my reading group contains a psychologist who argues that the implementation-intention literature shows people routinely form plans precisely in order to secure future behaviour they do not currently expect of themselves, and that self-prediction is systematically poor; tell me what such evidence would have to establish to genuinely threaten the view, what it would not establish, and whether the distinction between forming an intention and forming a plan about circumstances is one I am entitled to. Fifth, the puzzle grew out of deterrence theory, where a state must make credible the intention to do something it would have no reason to do once the moment arrives: if my view is right, does it follow that rational states cannot form retaliatory intentions, and is that a reductio of my view, a vindication of it, or a reason to think the analogy between states and agents is doing more work than it can bear? Sixth, tell me what my position predicts that its rivals — resoluteness, dispositional accounts of threatening behaviour, and the view that the puzzle simply dissolves once we stop treating intentions as available to choice — do not predict, and where those predictions could be checked even informally.

I should be candid about what would move me most, so that you can aim at it rather than around it: a description of a case in which an agent plainly forms the intention, correctly understands that she will not act, is neither self-deceived nor confused about what intending involves, and yet has it — so that the blockade is bypassed rather than worked around; and, alongside that, a principled account of what separates intention from pretence that does not smuggle in expectation. Finally, I want an honest verdict on whether the view has been qualified into invulnerability. If it has, say so plainly, and then say whether that is a serious defect for a thesis of this kind, given that part of what it claims is conceptual rather than empirical, or whether some immunity is exactly what a claim about the structure of practical reasoning should be expected to enjoy. Distinguish throughout between what a defender of the doxastic condition ought to concede as counting against her and what she is entitled to dismiss, and do not soften the conclusion if the position turns out to be badly exposed on one flank and unassailable on another.
