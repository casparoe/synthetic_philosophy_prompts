I maintain a ranking service at work that scores proposed reliability projects by expected loss avoided — probability of the bad incident multiplied by an estimate of what it would cost us. Last quarter one row sat at the top of that list for six weeks running: a failure mode nobody had ever observed, carrying a probability estimate around one in ten thousand, multiplied by an impact figure someone had extrapolated from a hypothetical. The arithmetic was never wrong. I checked it three times. What I could not explain to my team, in any vocabulary I had, was why we all nonetheless felt certain the row was lying to us — and why the feeling got stronger, not weaker, every time someone tried to argue that the probability was implausibly low. I went looking for whether this had a name and found that it does: something called expected-value fanaticism, and a thought experiment usually known as Pascal's mugging, in which a person with no weapon asks a stranger for five dollars in exchange for delivering an astronomically large benefit, and wins the argument by naming a larger number each time he is told the odds are too small. I want a poem about this, not an essay, and I want it in a specific form.

Write a sestina: thirty-nine lines, six stanzas of six lines and a three-line envoi, unrhymed, with the six end-words rotating by the form's fixed rule — each stanza takes the previous stanza's last end-word first, then its first, then its fifth, then its second, then its fourth, then its third, applied over and over until the rotation comes home. I want this form because it is the only poem I know that is literally an algorithm, and because the thing that unsettled me about my ranking service was precisely that the procedure does not care what you feed it: the same six inputs, recombined by the same permutation, produce the same verdict whether the subject is a mugger on a street corner or a spreadsheet row with a reasonable-looking pedigree. Use these six end-words, in this order, for the first stanza: count, odds, save, worth, sum, number. Each of them has an ordinary sense and a decision-theoretic one, and I would like every word to be used in both senses at least once across the rotation, so that the drift between the two — rather than any argument in the lines — is what makes the repetition feel like motion. Let save carry its computing sense at least once, the sense of writing something to disk so it survives; that is the sense in which my team and I used the word for months while the row was winning.

Here is the shape I have in mind, though I would rather you surprise me within it. Open on the street: a request for five dollars, an ordinary disbelief. Let the second stanza be the moment the numbers get written down and stop being absurd. Let the third belong to the man who hands the money over and cannot afterward say what convinced him. The fourth should hold the standard repairs — cap the payoff so no benefit can be arbitrarily large; distrust a promise whose magnitude costs more words to state than the thing it promises — but hold them as images, not as a list of objections, because my audience will not know them as objections. The fifth should move, without announcing the move, to somewhere respectable: people doing careful, uncontroversial good with distant strangers, using exactly the machinery the mugger used, and nobody calling it a mugging. The sixth should land on the rotation itself, the recognition that the permutation is the argument. The envoi must take in all six words and must not decide whether the man who paid was a fool or the only honest person in the poem. Two accuracy constraints, since I will be reading this to a room full of engineers at the end of a twenty-minute internal talk and one of them reads philosophy: the poem must not imply that the problem is simply that the probability is too small, because the whole force of the case is that for any probability above zero there is a larger payoff, and it must not let the mugger be dismissed as a crank while the identical calculation goes unexamined in the room where it is respectable. Plain diction throughout, no philosopher's name anywhere in the poem, and no footnotes — I would rather it be right than be learned.
