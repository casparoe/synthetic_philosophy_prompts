I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy of mind, and the chapter I can't get past concerns a suggestion Hofstadter develops in *I Am a Strange Loop*: that a person's "I" — the self-symbol, the strange loop that constitutes them — is not confined to the skull in which it started, but is also instantiated, at varying degrees of precision, in the brains of the people who know them well. I know the occasion for the view: his wife's death from cancer in 1993 and the sense he describes of her persisting in him and in their two young children. I also know that he treats the matter as one of degree rather than of kind — instantiations attenuate as they are passed along and outward, so that a self gradually dilutes across a widening circle of intimates, acquaintances, texts and institutions, and eventually dissipates, rather than being switched off at a moment. What I don't know is how much of this can survive being made explicit.

For the chapter I've tried to give the view a spine it usually lacks, and I want the criticism directed at both the spine and the body. Define the *fidelity* of A's instantiation in B as B's counterfactual predictive accuracy regarding A: how well B's internal model predicts A's choices, evaluations and reactions in novel situations, relative both to chance and to a base-rate model of an average person matching A's description. Then say that A survives A's death to a degree proportional to the fidelity-weighted sum over living hosts, with a threshold below which it is simply false that A survives in any interesting sense. Call this the fidelity-weighted version. Its attraction, as I see it, is that it converts a piece of consoling metaphor into something in principle checkable against the psychology of person perception and of memory-for-others. My supervisor's response was that I have either made it trivially true — of course intimates carry reasonably accurate models of the people they love, and no one disputes that — or made it something other than a thesis about survival, since predictive accuracy in B is a fact about B's representations and not about A's continuing to exist. I want to know whether that lands, and what else there is to worry about.

So: treat the position as a candidate metaphysical thesis and map its exposure. I want the empirical findings that would undercut it — from grief research on continuing bonds and on the bereaved person's sense of the deceased's presence; from whatever is known about how accurate intimates actually are in judging another person's traits and anticipating their behavior, and where that accuracy degrades (idealization, hostility, saintification, motivated reconstruction, drift over decades); from cases in which a host's model of the deceased is destroyed by dementia, amnesia or focal injury, or in which the surviving image diverges sharply from the person, as with public figures. I want the conceptual problems too: whether a multiply instantiated pattern yields parts of one person or poor copies of several, and what mereology of selves the view would need before "distributed" is anything other than "badly copied"; whether pattern-instantiation in someone else's head ever gets you a subject of experience, let alone a partially surviving one, and what it would take to find out; whether the view can be distinguished from reductionism about identity plus the ordinary facts of memory and causal influence, or from relational and socially constitutive accounts of personhood, without collapsing into one of them. And I want the questions it seems unable to answer — what happens to A's degree of survival when a host dies, or when two hosts carry irreconcilable models of A, or when a person is survived almost entirely by institutions and recordings rather than by anyone who knew them.
