I spend my working life in provenance research for a mid-sized auction house, and in my spare time I have been drafting an essay about when an object's *history of making* is allowed to bear on what we owe it aesthetically, as opposed to what we owe it commercially. I have got stuck at the point where my own verdicts no longer look like a view. What follows is my running list, in the order I accumulated it. I would like a principle — or a small set of rival principles — that generates these verdicts rather than merely listing them.

1. A "Vermeer" bought in good faith in the 1960s and adored for decades is shown by a scholar to be a 1930s forgery of the van Meegeren kind. My verdict: the paint has not changed, but my standing to admire it has. I now find it actively worse-looking, which I suspect is irrational, yet I do not want to call it a mere mood.

2. The very same canvas, sold openly from the start as a twentieth-century pastiche after Vermeer, competently done. My verdict: entirely legitimate; I would hang it and admire the draughtsmanship. So a change in the label moves the object from tainted to innocent, and I want that explained without collapsing into the claim that my aesthetic response is only ever a report of my beliefs.

3. A Tiepolo ceiling water-damaged in a corner; the restorer paints the loss in the master's manner, indistinguishably, and records the patch on the reverse with her own initials and the date. My verdict: praiseworthy, and the ceiling is now *more* the work it was. Note that this is structurally the same act as the forgery in (1) — painting in another artist's style so well that the join cannot be found.

4. I prompt an image model on the master's corpus, have the result printed at scale on canvas, sign it with the model's name and the date, and show it under a label that says exactly what it is. No deception whatsoever. My verdict: not a forgery, and yet worse than (3) and worse even than (2) — hollow, a rival claimant to a credit that nobody earned. This is the pair I most need help with: (3) and (4) are equally transparent style-mimicry, and I think one is a service and the other a small fraud without a victim.

5. A large studio canvas, mostly assistants' hands, retouched in the faces by the master and signed by him, sold as his during his lifetime and catalogued as his now. My verdict: genuine, and I would resist calling it mostly a forgery — yet in (1) a single hand, the forger's, is fatal.

6. A beloved wooden chapel burns and the town rebuilds it from photographs with new timber, the same joints and tools. My verdict: a different object, and a proper object of civic pride, but not the original. If instead they had replaced it plank by plank over fifty years with the identical timber, I would say it is still the chapel. I cannot say why the rate of replacement should matter to identity.

7. A modern maker's copy that wins a blind listening test against a famous eighteenth-century instrument; the soloist says she would still rather play the old one, and I side with her without being able to justify siding with her.

8. A forged Haydn symphony turns out to be by a talented contemporary: orchestras keep playing it, and I think that is fine, the work survives the correction of its attribution. A forged Shakespeare play, once exposed, would be ruined for me and would be staged only as a curiosity. I want to be told whether this asymmetry is principled or snobbery, since both look like notated, reproducible arts on Goodman's autographic/allographic line — and if it is principled, what feature the line is actually tracking.

9. A living celebrity artist has assistants produce entire works, which he signs, with a certificate reading "hand-signed original." My verdict: not forgery, but inflation, and the buyers are deceived about something that matters even though nothing they are deceived about concerns his hand. This looks like (5) with the history removed, and my verdicts flip.

10. A scholar shows that a group of drawings cannot be reliably attributed: some are certainly the master's, some certainly not, and no one can say which. All of them fall in value and, I confess, in the intensity of my looking at them. My verdict: the innocent ones lose nothing they had — though if what they had was never in the paper, perhaps they do.

My own tentative rule, which I have been calling the delegation clause, is that mimicry in another artist's style is legitimate when it serves the continued existence of that artist's work and illegitimate when it manufactures a rival candidate for the credit of having made it. That seems to separate (3) from (4), but I cannot make it touch (5) and (9), and it says nothing at all about (6).

Please answer in the following sections, in this order, and please do not send me a bibliography — I cannot look anything up, so name views in prose (Goodman's distinction, the achievement account of why forgery wrongs an audience) but cite nothing. First, "Bottom line" (about 100 words): your best single principle, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case you have not seen. Second, "Clauses and their motives" (about 350 words): the principle broken into clauses, each clause tagged to the numbered case that forces it, and each clause stated so that its failure condition is visible. Third, "Rival principles" (about 350 words): two or three competing accounts, each given a name of your own devising, with the verdicts each delivers and the specific cost each pays — I want to see at least one rival that saves (1)–(4) cleanly and say exactly which of my later verdicts it wrecks. Fourth, "The restorer and the generator" (about 300 words): cases (3) and (4) head to head. Either exhibit the shared feature that lets a principle separate them without special pleading, or concede that no honest principle separates them and tell me which of my two verdicts I must reverse, and what the reversed verdict would then predict about restoration practice generally. Fifth, "Three cases you choose" (about 300 words): three situations not on my list — at least one from outside the fine arts — on which your principle delivers a definite verdict that I think I would find surprising or unwelcome. State the verdict flatly. Sixth, "What I have to give up" (about 250 words): the numbered verdicts your principle cannot save, ranked by how much it costs me to abandon them, and an account of what residual tension survives if I stubbornly keep the two I am least willing to lose.
