I chair the weekly architecture review for a team that builds an agent platform on top of a fairly conventional distributed systems stack, and there is an argument that has recurred in those reviews for about two years, always in the same shape. One camp — call them the substrate people — insists that any high-level claim in a design document can be rewritten, in principle, as a statement about states, transitions, packets and activation patterns, and that once you have done so the high-level sentence has no work left to do: it is decoration, or at best a compression format for humans. The other camp insists that the high-level description is precisely what explains the behaviour, and that designing against it is not a convenience but the only tractable option; they will happily write that the scheduler is fair, that the type system prevents a class of bug, that the agent has a persistent goal, and treat those sentences as facts about the world rather than as prose. I have read Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach a couple of times over the years and it gave me the vocabulary I keep reaching for here: in one of the dialogues the brain is treated as a seething machine of interacting parts — the careenium — in which symbols, the simmballs, somehow get to shove the machinery around, even though the machinery is doing nothing but physics. That is exactly the shape of my reviewers' dispute, with design documents in place of skulls. I have no formal philosophy training, so what I want from you is not a survey of the debate but something I can hand to a room of engineers.

Please write a decision procedure that a reviewer can run on a single high-level claim in a design document and come out with a defensible verdict. It should have a triage step at the front, because I suspect the camps are partly talking past each other: some claims are causal (this is what prevented the split-brain), some are constitutive or definitional (this invariant is what makes the module a queue), and some are normative or design-level (this allocation is fair), and the procedure needs to say which of these a claim is before it starts testing it. Then give me the ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and where the procedure terminates in one of three verdicts: the claim is load-bearing and belongs in the document; the claim is a redescription of the substrate and should be marked as commentary; or the case cannot be settled at review time and must be handed to a named human because the remaining question is about design values rather than facts. Ground the steps in whatever the philosophy of mind and the causation literature actually offers that survives contact with practice — the exclusion worry, epiphenomenalism, multiple realisation, screening-off, counterfactual and intervention-style tests, the question of whether something acts in virtue of the property you named or merely in virtue of a property that happens to travel with it — but name a piece of machinery only where naming it changes how a step gets executed. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations; if you mention a philosopher, mention them in passing at most. Each step should be runnable by a competent engineer in about five minutes against a paragraph of prose, and I want you to be explicit about which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedure's clothes, about the failure modes (a genuinely load-bearing claim being marked down as decoration, or the procedure being used as a rhetorical weapon by whichever camp is currently winning), and about the classes of claim it will systematically get wrong.

Finally, run the procedure on three cases and show your working, including the intermediate answers rather than just the verdicts. The first is a claim from an agent design document: the model has a persistent concept of plurality, and that concept is why it produces the plural form, as against a colleague's counter-claim that all there is is a pattern of activations and the concept talk is inert. The second is a claim from a post-incident review: leader election is what prevented a split-brain during the network partition, as against the substrate people's counter-claim that the whole event is fully described by which messages arrived at which node when, and that mentioning the protocol adds nothing. The third is my own pet claim, which I want you to be willing to demolish: that the module boundary is what makes this codebase maintainable, and that maintainability is a property of the boundary rather than of the many small decisions on either side of it. If the procedure says something I will not like about that third case, say it plainly and explain which step did the damage. Close with a short section on what the procedure cannot do — the questions it looks as though it answers but does not — and please write the whole thing in British English.
