I work adjacent to interpretability research and there's a habit in the field I can't quite pin down philosophically. When people want to say a model genuinely has a representation of something — some concept, some property of the task — they run one of two tests and treat either as sufficient. One is decoding: you train a probe on a layer's activations, or pull a direction out of a sparse autoencoder, and if you can reliably read the thing out, you say the model represents it. The other is steering: you add the direction, or patch those activations into a different run, and if behavior shifts, that also counts as proof the representation was there. Papers report one or the other interchangably and conclude the feature is real.

Everyone also knows the two come apart. You can decode information the model demonstrably never uses downstream, and you get steering effects from directions nobody would have identified by decoding alone.

So what I want is the implicit theory, not a survey. For each test, spell out what has to be true of the model for it to be evidence at all — I vaguely sense one rests on an informational notion of representation and the other on a causal or functional one, but make that precise. What rival accounts does each rule out without argument? Does the field's willingness to accept either one commit it to a single view of what representations are, or is the practice actually underdetermined — consistent with several accounts, including fairly deflationary ones where "representation" is just a way of talking about dispositions? And if you enforced the causal criterion strictly, which claims people currently make would you have to retract, and what would be left that anyone would still care about?
