I am a third-year PhD student in political theory, and my dissertation will concern Marx's account of machinery and automation — specifically, how far his analysis of large-scale industry can be reconstructed as a coherent theory rather than a set of suggestive but incompatible claims. I have roughly three years left, my training is textual and conceptual (I read German; I do no formal modelling and no econometrics), and I have drafted five candidate questions as possible cores of the project. Here they are, in no particular order:

(i) The status of the general intellect in the 1857–58 notebooks. In the so-called fragment on machines, Marx suggests that as large-scale industry develops, the creation of real wealth comes to depend less on direct labour time than on the general state of science and its application, and that the conditions of social life come under the control of the general intellect. Is this a value-theoretic claim — that a mode of production whose measure of wealth is labour time collapses once science becomes the main productive force — or a historical and strategic claim about the possibility of emancipation? And what should one make of the fact that the vocabulary of the general intellect essentially disappears in Capital, where some readers argue it is displaced by the concept of the collective worker?

(ii) The tool-machine analysis in the machinery chapter of Capital, volume I. Marx holds that fully developed machinery comprises a motor mechanism, a transmitting mechanism, and a tool or working machine, and that the working machine is the starting point of the industrial revolution because it detaches the implement from the worker's hand and makes it perform the same operations mechanically. Can this analysis accommodate means of production that have no working machine in his sense — software and machine intelligence, which act on symbols and decisions rather than seizing and modifying a material subject of labour — or does his concept of the machine essentially presuppose physical transformation, so that his theory of automation covers only a subclass of contemporary automation?

(iii) The law of the tendency of the rate of profit to fall in Capital, volume III. Automation raises the organic composition of capital, but Marx also catalogues counteracting influences. Is there a distinctively philosophical question here on which a political theorist could make progress — about the explanatory status of the law, the way the counter-tendencies underdetermine its predictions, or what would even count as confirming it — or is the question wholly economic and empirical, such that a dissertation should set it aside?

(iv) An apparent internal tension in the same chapter. Marx argues both that machinery deskills the worker and devalues labour-power, and that it cheapens the commodities entering the worker's consumption, thereby reducing necessary labour time and raising the rate of surplus value. On which of these two mechanisms does any Marxian immiseration claim actually rest, and is the deskilling thesis even compatible with the general-intellect material in (i), which makes general knowledge rather than fragmented manual skill the principal productive force?

(v) The politics of application. Marx repeatedly distinguishes machinery as such from its capitalist application, and in his treatment of the workers' struggle against machinery he insists that workers must learn to distinguish the material means of production from their social form and direct their attacks at the latter rather than the former. Does this distinction carry genuine explanatory content — that the same machinery could have been applied otherwise, with different effects on skill, hours, and control — or is it a moral complaint bolted onto a determinist analysis? And could it ever yield guidance about choices of technique, or only retrospective criticism?

Please rank these five questions for my purposes, from most to least worth building a dissertation around. I want the ordering defended by reference to the following criteria, in roughly this order of weight: how many of the other four questions depend on the answer to a given one; how tractable it is within three years for someone with my textual and conceptual toolkit, in the sense that I could produce a result specialists would have to engage with rather than dismiss as rehashing an old dispute; and whether the current disagreement about it is genuinely open or already so deadlocked that a dissertation could not move it. Please do not rank a question higher merely because it resonates with contemporary debates about AI and work, though you may note where that resonance has made a question overattended or oddly neglected.

Format: reply with exactly five numbered items, ordered from highest to lowest priority, and with no introduction, no conclusion, and no other text. Each item should be one or two sentences that identify the question by number and give the decisive reason for its position relative to its neighbours; in the item ranked first, state additionally what consideration would demote it to third.
