I am assembling a table of Augustine's rulings on lying — his two treatises on the subject plus his scattered remarks elsewhere — and one of the cases I need to classify does not obviously follow from the others. The verdicts I have so far: the Egyptian midwives and Rahab were rewarded for their mercy, not for their lies, and their lies remained sins; a lie told to save someone's life is a sin, though of the lightest grade, and is not to be told, since one may not do evil that good may come; a lie told purely for the pleasure of telling it is culpable even when it harms no one and gains the teller nothing; lies touching religious teaching are the gravest of all; concealing the truth by silence is a different act from lying, and is sometimes permitted; prophetic and figurative speech is not lying, because the sense intended is not withheld from those it is meant to reach; a Catholic may not feign agreement with the Priscillianists in order to expose them, however good the aim; and an utterance is assessed by the speaker's mind, so that a man who asserts what he believes false lies even when it turns out true.

The case: a man is sheltering a fugitive, and soldiers at the door ask where he is. He answers that he saw a man of that description heading for the mill road half an hour ago — true, though the fugitive doubled back into the house afterwards. Nothing he says is false; the soldiers go to the mill road, which is exactly what he intended.

Would Augustine call this a lie, a permitted concealment, or a third thing that is not a lie and yet culpable? Set out the principles his recorded verdicts admit of, what each predicts here, which you would bet on and why, and what further case would decide between the two closest candidates. Please mark how confident you are in each principle you attribute to him, and single out the one you are least sure of.
