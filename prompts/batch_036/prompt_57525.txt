A graduate student in my social choice seminar has defended the following argument. I find its conclusion unacceptable but cannot yet locate the mistake, and I want a diagnosis rather than a summary of the theorem.

1. Arrow's theorem: no procedure that maps profiles of individual preference orderings over three or more alternatives onto a social ordering can satisfy unrestricted domain, the Pareto condition, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship while always yielding a complete and transitive social ordering.
2. Independence of irrelevant alternatives is not a technical convenience but a condition of minimal rationality: the social ranking of x against y must depend only on how individuals rank x against y, so that introducing an option nobody ranks above either of them cannot change the result. A procedure that fails this is moved by considerations that are, by hypothesis, irrelevant.
3. Pareto is minimal in the same way: if everyone ranks x above y, society must rank x above y.
4. Therefore every non-dictatorial procedure is either moved by irrelevant alternatives or productive of intransitive social rankings. There is no third possibility.
5. Majority rule is moved by irrelevant alternatives; the spoiler effect in any three-candidate contest is precisely that.
6. So majority rule is irrational in the sense given at (2), not merely clumsy.
7. The reply that majority rule becomes transitive when preferences are single-peaked cannot rescue it: that reply assumes preferences have the shape the desired conclusion needs, and a free society cannot guarantee the shape.
8. Price-mediated aggregation, by contrast, ranks options by what people are actually willing to give up for them, so each person's influence depends only on their own preferences, and it generates no cycles. Markets are therefore the only known aggregator meeting (2) and (3) without a dictator, and elections are not a rational means of collective decision at all but a crude stand-in for exit.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would rather abandon (6) or (8) than (1), but tell me whether (2) can be kept as a claim about rationality rather than as a stipulation, and whether anything recognisably democratic survives if independence goes. Where more than one repair is available, rank them by what they cost. And if you judge the argument sound and think my distaste for the conclusion is not itself an objection to it, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a defect to spare my sensibilities.
