ok so I'm a third-year PPE student and my political theory seminar has me writing a paper on humanitarian intervention and regime change, and I've gotten stuck in kind of the worst way: I've read maybe six different accounts and I can't tell whether they're even answering the same question. Some are trying to tell a minister what to do next month, some are trying to render a verdict on whether NATO's Kosovo campaign was right all things considered now that we know how it went, and some are really trying to say when sovereignty stops shielding a regime from outsiders. So before I commit to arguing with anyone I want the spec first — the list of things any theory in this area has to be able to do before it's allowed to count as an answer, whatever it concludes.

Fix the purpose like this, because I think a lot of the disagreement dissolves once you do: I want a theory good enough to be turned into a standing ex ante decision rule that a regional body could actually apply beforehand, by people who don't know how things will turn out. That means no criterion that can only be evaluated in hindsight, and no criterion that requires reading anyone's mind. With that constraint in place, give me the full requirements list: the phenomena a theory has to explain, the cases it has to get right (and say which case is doing the work for each requirement — Kosovo, Iraq 2003, Libya 2011, Rwanda 1994 as the non-intervention, Syria 2013 as the other non-intervention, anything else you think is load-bearing), the distinctions it can't collapse (I already suspect civilian-rescue vs. regime change is one, and intention vs. motive vs. publicly stated rationale is another), and the empirical findings it can't contradict if it wants to be taken seriously by IR people and not just philosophers — stuff like what the record actually shows about foreign-imposed regime change, the moral-hazard worry that credible promises of intervention encourage rebellion, and the selectivity problem of interventions happening somewhere other than where the killing is worst. Also tell me which of the standard requirements are genuine and which are just one camp's house prejudices wearing a lab coat: is the right-intention criterion a real requirement or a demand no institutional rule could ever operationalize, is reasonable prospect of success a requirement or a dressed-up status-quo bias, can last resort ever be satisfied by anything that isn't indefinite delay, and does saying an intervention can be unlawful yet morally justified name a coherent category or a contradiction. Flag any pair or trio of requirements you think can't be jointly satisfied — that's the part I most want, e.g. if authorization-by-international-body and effectiveness-under-a-veto simply can't both hold, say so, because then the list is itself the result. And then: which single requirement knocks out the most candidates?

Format-wise, put the requirements in a markdown table with exactly these columns: id; requirement (one sentence, phrased as something a theory has to do, not as a claim about the world); motivating case or consideration; verdict, one of genuine / camp-prejudice / negotiable; status, one of settled / contested; and conflicts (which other ids it clashes with, or none). After the table, prose for the unsatisfiable sets and for the knockout-requirement answer. Keep the scorecard minimal — a few sentences at the very end on how the main contenders do against the list is plenty, I'm not asking you to referee the whole debate. One thing I do need: seperate clearly, in the status column and in the prose, what basically everyone who works on this agrees on from what is genuinely contested and you're taking a side on. Don't hand me a contested position as if it were the consensus — half my paper is going to be that the field doesn't agree, so if you flatten that out you've actively misled me.
