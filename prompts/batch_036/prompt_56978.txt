I retired four years ago after thirty-seven years in chemical ecology, nearly all of it spent on recruitment and trail-laying in ants — work in which one watches a stable, population-wide regularity in behaviour get established, maintained, and repaired with nothing remotely resembling an articulated expectation in any participant's head. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy with more persistence than I ever applied to the history of my own discipline, and I have worked through David Lewis's *Convention* (1969) closely, along with enough of the evolutionary signalling-game literature — Skyrms's work especially — to follow the mathematics without help. I have come to accept the Lewisian analysis, and to accept it in a fairly strong form: that a convention is a regularity in the behaviour of members of a population in a recurrent situation, sustained because conformity is each participant's best response conditional on the others' conformity, and that the mutual expectations and preferences which keep the regularity in place are not merely true of the population but common knowledge within it. Two further commitments go with this, and they are the ones I want tested. First, that the common-knowledge clause is load-bearing rather than a decorative formality: it is what separates a convention from a mere habit or from a regularity that happens to be adaptive, and it is what supposedly allows newcomers to pick up an existing convention without being told anything about it. Second, that this is an analysis and not a description of a family resemblance — that is, a population which reproduces the entire behavioural profile while failing the epistemic condition has regularities but no conventions, and my ants are therefore outside the extension.

What I want from you is a map of this position's exposure. Which arguments, cases, formal results, or empirical findings would count against it, sorted by how decisive they are, and with an explicit division between what is fatal and what is merely awkward? I am especially interested in the following lines of attack, though you should add any I have missed: whether the account can handle partial conflict of interest at all, given that Lewisian signalling games are games of near-coincident interest while the honesty of real signals in my own field is often maintained by cost rather than by shared preferences; whether common knowledge is attainable in any actual population, and what happens to the analysis if it is not; whether the appeal to salience, precedent, or prominence as alternative routes to the required expectations silently evacuates the epistemic condition; whether my ant colonies are genuine counterexamples or simply outside the subject matter, and how one tells the difference between those two verdicts rather than choosing whichever is convenient; whether the position collapses into a rival — a bare equilibrium-selection story of the focal-point sort, a reproductive or copying-based account of conventions that needs no beliefs at all, or a joint-commitment account on which the obligations come from the participants' own avowals; and what the Lewisian view predicts that its rivals do not predict, so that one could in principle catch it out. Please also say which of these a committed Lewisian would concede as counting against them and which they would dismiss, and then answer the harder question directly: has the position been qualified into immunity — common knowledge softened into something like sufficiently wide mutual awareness, scope narrowed whenever a recalcitrant case appears — and if it has, whether that is a defect in a view of this kind or exactly what one should expect of a conceptual analysis, which unlike my old empirical hypotheses was never supposed to be refutable by a field observation.

For what it is worth, the argument that would move me most is one showing that the iterated-belief machinery does no explanatory work whatsoever — that every phenomenon the analysis claims to explain, including the re-establishment of a convention among people who never encountered its origin, can be accounted for by mechanisms that require nothing beyond local responsiveness to what others have done. Having spent a career documenting such mechanisms, I find that possibility more disturbing than any technical objection about equilibrium refinement, and I would rather know now whether my position is defensible against it or whether I have simply adopted an intellectualist picture because it is elegant. One practical constraint: I am working from my own shelves and have no appetite for a reading list or for citations I cannot check, so please argue the matters out rather than pointing me at sources, and where a defender's response turns on a technical result, sketch the result in words. I would like, at the end, your own verdict stated plainly — whether this view is well insulated and that is no problem, well insulated and that is a serious problem, or genuinely at risk, and if at risk, where it would break first.
