In three weeks I present a chapter of my dissertation at the joint colloquium our psychology department runs with the philosophy group two campuses over. The claim I am defending is narrow, but I think it is the right shape: that a newly learned color lexicon can alter early visual discrimination, rather than only the judgment a subject makes about what she saw. Concretely, we trained adults across nine sessions on a four-way naming scheme covering a stretch of the blue–green region of a Munsell-type array — two of the names straddle a boundary we selected, two fall entirely on one side of it — and then tested same-different sensitivity at the trained boundary against a control pair matched for physical distance in CIELAB space. Between-category d′ exceeded within-category d′ at the trained boundary but not at the control pair. Three design features are supposed to do the real work: continuous articulatory suppression across the retention interval, to block covert naming; a six-point confidence rating on every trial, so that sensitivity and response criterion are fitted separately rather than reporting accuracy alone; and eye tracking with a drift-corrected fixation requirement, to rule out differential scanning. My argument for why this is penetration and not merely post-perceptual re-description is that if the effect lived in the decision rule or in the report, suppression and criterion separation should have eliminated it, and they did not; and that an advantage which is category-specific at matched physical distance is awkward to express as a shift along the response axis.

The room will not be hostile in a general way but in a very particular, technical one. About two-thirds are vision scientists and psychophysicists who run their own two-alternative forced-choice or threshold labs; several of them signed onto a widely circulated review that went through the top-down literature case by case and concluded that essentially none of the claimed effects survives scrutiny, and they think within the encapsulation lineage running back to Pylyshyn's notion of cognitive penetrability and Fodor's informational encapsulation. One senior faculty member is a methods hawk who will ask about our power analysis, whether the boundary and control locations were fixed before or after we saw data, and how many analysis choices we tried before settling on the reported one. A postdoc who records from early and mid-level visual cortex will insist that whatever I have shown is recurrent feedback inside the visual hierarchy, which on his view is perception doing what perception always does, not cognition intruding on it. Two philosophers of mind care about a different question entirely: whether, granting the effect, perception can still serve as an independent check on belief, or whether an experience shaped by a lexicon simply cannot play the justificatory role we want from it.

Build me a question-and-answer prep sheet rather than a defense of the view. I want the eight or so objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do if I handled it badly, each phrased the way the objector would phrase it — the psychophysicist's version, not a philosopher's generic paraphrase of it — with a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is softest. Tell me what to concede in the first five minutes to buy credibility, which single answer has to land for the talk to survive, which objection I should prepare to lose gracefully, and how much of my remaining time each reply costs me. One thing you should know, because it determines the question I most dread: a European group ran a close variant of our training paradigm last year with roughly twice the sample and reported no between-category advantage in d′, only an effect in their confidence ratings; we have been unable to reconcile the two datasets, and an internal reanalysis suggests our suppression load was probably less demanding than the preprint implied. If someone in the room has read that paper, I need to answer without either overclaiming or collapsing. Finally, please mark clearly which background assumptions in your answers are settled among people who work on this and which remain genuinely contested — as far as I can tell, whether separating sensitivity from criterion is even the right test for a perceptual versus a post-perceptual effect is not a closed question — and do not write as though either the encapsulationist or the penetrability reading of this literature were the consensus. No reading list, no citations, no bibliography: I will not have time to read anything before I walk in, and I want only what to say.
