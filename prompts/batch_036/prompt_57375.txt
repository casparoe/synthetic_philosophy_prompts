Last Thursday our department's weekly business meeting took an unpleasant turn. A senior colleague interrupted a graduate student's report — the student had circulated a draft the night before, two extensions late, full of quotations with no attribution — and told him, in front of roughly twenty people, that sloppy sourcing is not a venial fault but a breach of trust with one's readers. The content of the remark was, as far as I could judge, accurate, and the student said afterwards that he had found it useful. What I could not decide was whether the colleague had any business saying it, because he is known in the department for something very like two decades of precisely the habit he was denouncing: his own drafts come back from referees with the same complaint, and he has twice been asked to supply missing citations at copy-editing. When a junior faculty member across the table said so — in the form of "that's the pot calling the kettle black" — the room split into factions that never actually stated their positions, and we moved on to the budget. I keep going back to it because I suspect the two sides were not disagreeing about the same claim.

What I want is a menu rather than a verdict on the colleague. Take that proverb and separate the distinct, precise things a person could be asserting by using it in a room like that one. I am particularly interested in the parameters the surface grammar conceals. Must the accuser's fault be literally the same one, or does adjacency suffice — here both faults concern sourcing, but the colleague's is chronic and the student's a single instance? Does the tense of the accuser's own fault matter: a person who did this for twenty years and genuinely stopped five years ago, versus one who did it in last month's draft, versus one who was, as it happens, twenty minutes late to the meeting at which he rebuked a student for missing deadlines? Is the claim that the blame is untrue, that it is fitting but the accuser has forfeited the right to voice it, that his own record disqualifies him as a witness to the standard, or merely that he is being gratuitously harsh and should choose a different setting and register? And there is a reading I have been turning over that seems to run in the opposite direction — that sharing the fault is exactly what earns someone the credibility to deliver the rebuke, the way the warning lands hardest from someone who has been through it — and I want to know whether that reading can honestly be attached to the proverb at all, or whether I am using the proverb as a hook to hang something else on.

For each reading, say in a sentence whether it amounts to a platitude nobody would deny, a substantive and defensible principle, or something false; then tell me which of them would actually have done the work the junior colleague needed, that is, which would have shown the senior colleague's remark to be objectionable rather than merely uncomfortable. I would also like to know whether his charge and the retort that he had committed a tu quoque are two descriptions of a single objection or genuinely different objections, and whether the Gospel line about noticing the speck in another's eye while ignoring the plank in one's own is making the same claim as the proverb or a different one. Please give me the reasoning rather than references, a reading list, or names of papers. Be explicit about which readings are ones you would expect to be defended in the literature and which you are constructing on the spot for this particular mess, and do not stretch a reading to make the proverb come out true, or to make either colleague right, if it will not stretch. Finally: if you think the framing of my question is itself off — if the proverb is too indeterminate for precisification to tell us anything, or if what went wrong in that meeting had nothing to do with hypocrisy and everything to do with, say, the public setting or the colleague's position as the student's own supervisor — say so plainly instead of producing a tidy list to please me.
