You are a philosopher of social science who also knows how birth-cohort categories get used in market research, polling, and journalism. Think carefully, and sketch a list of candidate concepts to yourself before committing to an answer. Please write in American English.

I work in audience research and keep running into a pattern I have no name for. It goes like this. A birth-year bracket is in wide use — say, people born in a given sixteen-year stretch — with a bundle of traits attached to it (student debt, delayed homebuying, phone habits). Someone objects that the bracket is arbitrary and that the traits really belong only to its later members. Rather than drop the bracket, the discussion produces a narrower bracket straddling the boundary, presented as more accurate, which leaves the original category untouched. The same thing then happens at the far end of the preceding bracket, and again with the newest cohort, so that every criticized category sprouts one or two flanking sub-categories.

Three things strike me about it. First, the sub-categories are built with exactly the machinery the critic objected to — another round-numbered year cutoff, another trait bundle — so the objection is answered by more of the thing it was an objection to. Second, the coarse categories are never retired; they simply acquire an outer layer of narrower ones, and the whole scheme looks more empirically articulated after each round of criticism than before. Third, outside the argument, the new finer labels get picked up by advertisers, HR decks, and news desks, so the scheme gains institutional footing precisely as it is being attacked.

I know the folk idea that a label can shape the people it is applied to, and I know roughly what an ad hoc rescue looks like, but neither seems to be this. Is there an established concept — in philosophy of social science, sociology of knowledge, or the methodology of demography — that names this specific defensive pattern? I am not after the bare label: I want the carving.
