I keep going in circles on whether my phone is literally part of my mind, and I think the only way to sort it out is to lay out my gut calls case by case and see if there's a real principle hiding underneath them.

Background: years ago I read that paper with the guy who has Alzheimer's and carries a notebook around — Otto, and Inga who just remembers the museum's address normally — and it felt obviously right at the time. But now I'm not sure it's earning its keep, because when I actually go through my own cases the verdicts don't fall out the way that paper wants them to.

Here they are:

1. The paper notebook where I write down every number, every grocery list, every half-formed arguement I want to come back to. Counts. Losing it would feel like losing part of my memory, not like losing a possession.

2. My phone's photo library. Also counts. I genuinely don't remember what my niece looked like at two and the phone does, but consulting it doesn't feel like checking the world, it feels like remembering.

3. Googling something. Doesn't count. As far as I can tell this is structurally the same as 1 and 2, but it feels like being told rather than thinking. Still no.

4. My wife's memory for names and dates. Twenty years together, and I ask her when the Hendersons' wedding was in exactly the same automatic way I open the notebook. But her memory is not part of my mind and never will be. She'd be appalled and I'd agree with her.

5. Our shared family calendar. Counts, for both of us. Which is embarrassing given 4, because the calendar is basically just her memory written down where I can reach it.

6. A calculator. Counts when I sit down and use it. But there's a version where it's running in the background all day auto-categorizing my spending, and there I'd say no, I didn't compute anything at all.

7. The car GPS. Doesn't count, and here's the part I can't explain to myself: after ten years of driving to the same office with it I still don't know the route. Whereas after ten years of the notebook I can often recite a number without looking. So maybe what matters is whether the thing leaves a residue in my head. But then 2 should fail too, since I can't recall a single one of those photos.

8. The chatbot I've spent a year messing with — saved instructions, our whole history, it knows how I argue. This is the one I flip flop on. Some days it's the best scratchpad and second memory I have; other days it's obviously just a very fluent stranger.

So: is there a principle that delivers 1 through 7 correctly? My suspicion is that any honest one has to be pretty baroque, and that either the 4/5 pair or the 1/3 pair is going to blow up the simple versions. What I want is the principle stated clause by clause, with each clause tied to the case that forces it; what it says about 8; and its verdict on at least one case I haven't listed here but should have. And if one of my verdicts has to be given up, tell me which one and argue for it. If it comes down to it I'd rather be argued out of the GPS one than the wife one.
