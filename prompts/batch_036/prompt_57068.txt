the pitch for moral trade is that when people weigh the same values differently, letting them swap makes everyone better off — nobody's forced, everybody signs, so its Pareto. I want a thought experiment that breaks that, built to order.

Design a realistic case — siblings splitting an estate, three program officers at a small foundation, whatever, no sci-fi — with three people, three things that matter morally to them, and a sequence of voluntary swaps (four is fine) such that: every swap is signed by everyone it affects and is a strict improvement for both signatories by their own lights; nobody lies, nobody's ignorant, nobody changes their mind, nobody's coerced, and nobody's values shift; the damage does not come from spillover onto some fourth party. And at the end all three are worse off than they started, judged by their own standards. Make the pairwise exchange rates explicit — what each person will give up of one thing for how much of another — so I can check the ledger myself. Keep the scenario to two short paragraphs at most.

Then: walk the ledger trade by trade; name the single feature of the case that does the work, and explain how three people can each have perfectly defensible pairwise rates and still get caught in a loop like this without being dumb or confused; say what happens if you strip that feature out (e.g. force everyone onto one consistent scale); and say how a defender of moral trade would attack the case and whether the attack lands. Start with the scenario itself, no preamble and no restating the question. And commit: is this a genuine counterexample to the Pareto defense of moral trade, or does the defender escape? Give me your verdict and your reasons, not a menu of options.
