I work on benefit design at a state policy office. We are drafting a proposal for a publicly funded endowment paid to every resident at majority age — a "baby bond," in the usual formulation — and, to write the short memo on the idea's ancestry that the legislative staff have asked for, my colleagues and I have been reading the eighteenth-century texts that get cited as precursors. I have hit a paragraph I cannot confidently parse, and I would rather get the reading right than guess, since the memo ends up turning on it.

The text is Paine's Agrarian Justice, written in the mid-1790s. The passage below comes from the address he prefixed to the pamphlet when he dedicated it to the French legislature and executive directory, and it runs continuously:

> France has had the honor of adding to the word Liberty that of Equality; and this word signifies essentially a principle that admits of no gradation in the things to which it applies. But equality is often misunderstood, often misapplied, and often violated. Liberty and Property are words expressing all those of our possessions which are not of an intellectual nature. There are two kinds of property. Firstly, natural property, or that which comes to us from the Creator of the universe — such as the earth, air, water. Secondly, artificial or acquired property, — the invention of men. In the latter, equality is impossible; for to distribute it equally it would be necessary that all should have contributed in the same proportion, which can never be the case; and this being the case, every individual would hold on to his own property, as his right share. Equality of natural property is the subject of this little essay. Every individual in the world is born therein with legitimate claims on a certain kind of property, or its equivalent.

Some surrounding material, which I give as paraphrase rather than quotation so that you work from what is above rather than from recall of the whole pamphlet: later in the text Paine proposes a lump sum paid to every person on reaching twenty-one and an annual payment to everyone past fifty, financed by a levy on inherited landed property, and he insists that the payment is owed as a right and is emphatically not charity. He also argues that the earth in its natural state was the common property of the human race, and that cultivation raised its productivity so dramatically that those shut out of the original common inheritance are owed a share of the improved value. In the same address he turns to the franchise and objects that the French constitution conditions the right of voting on a share in a certain direct tax, arguing that the vote belongs to everyone equally precisely because everyone has a legitimate birthright in a certain species of property.

What I want is help understanding the paragraph itself. First, what is "natural property" in his first sense — the earth, air and water as things, or a claim on them — and does the reason he gives for calling the second kind "the invention of men" (that nobody made the first kind) carry the weight of the distinction? Second, the sentence about equality being impossible in acquired property because contributions could never be proportional reads like a desert or entitlement argument: unequal holdings are legitimate because inputs are unequal. Is that the right reading, and if so how much has he conceded? One of my colleagues argues that this makes Paine much closer to an entitlement theorist who simply appends a one-off severance payment for the original appropriation of land — a proviso-style remedy rather than a theory of distributive equality — and I cannot tell whether the paragraph supports that or whether it is a bad fit. Third, what is the object of "equality of natural property": equal shares, equal claims, or equal standing? How much work does "or its equivalent" do — if the equivalent is money, has he already converted a division claim into a compensation claim, and does compensation logic cap the payment at the value of what was taken, which would make it a ground-rent dividend that moves with land values rather than a fixed grant? Fourth, does the "no gradation" remark do real argumentative work here or is it rhetorical, given that he deploys it for the franchise as well: is it a reason the payment must be universal and uniform, or does it constrain only political rights? Finally, briefly: which of the questions I actually have to answer in the memo — eligibility, incidence, financing, adequacy, whether the payment is clawed back against other transfers — are addressed at all by this vocabulary, and which simply have no place in it?

Please open with a two-sentence plain-English gloss of what the paragraph argues, then take my questions in order, and wherever the paragraph underdetermines the reading say so rather than settling it by appeal to parts of the pamphlet I have not quoted. Keep the whole thing to 600 words or fewer.
