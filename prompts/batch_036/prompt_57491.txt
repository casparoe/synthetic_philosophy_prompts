I run a monthly reading group at a city public-health agency and our argument about nudging hasn't moved in three years, which its driving me nuts. Everyone agrees on the numbers: defaults and reminders shift uptake, the effects are real but modest, disclosure doesn't seem to kill them. Where we're stuck is the permissibility question, and I want to know what kind of disagreement that actually is.

The setup we keep circling: a pilot that auto-schedules people for a screening appointment unless they opt out, and also reorders the options in the benefits portal, with the ordering personalized by a model nobody on the team can explain. Three positions in the room. A says it's fine — non-coercive, disclosed on the front page, and when you ask users afterwards most endorse it. B says endorsement is irrelevant: the thing works by exploiting a bias the users have no reason to want exploited, and the fact that they wave it through afterwards is just more evidence the bias is doing the work. C says A and B are having a verbal fight about the word autonomy and there's nothing further to say.

My hypothesis, which may well be wrong: the stalemate isn't ethical at all but turns on a hidden counterfactual — what the person would have chosen if the bias weren't operating — and A and B are quietly disagreeing about whether that counterfactual is even well-defined for a real human being. Right? Or is this bedrock stuff about whether influencing someone ever counts as wronging them, or two separate questions (is it permissible? is it legitimate for an agency to do it?) being fought under one heading?

Give me the diagnosis with reasons, what would count as progress on that diagnosis — a distinction, an empirical test, a change of question — and if your answer is that no progress should be expected, say why. Bottom line in the first two sentences, under 500 words total, and no tour of the nudge-versus-boost literature, I've read it.
