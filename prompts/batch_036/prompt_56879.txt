The setup: second-year section, mostly non-majors, 700 words max, worth 20 points. The question I set was:

"Popper proposes falsifiability rather than verifiability as the criterion for demarcating science from non-science. Explain the criterion, explain what Popper takes to be wrong with the verifiability criterion favoured by the logical positivists, and assess whether the Duhem–Quine thesis is fatal to his account."

Here's the answer I need marked:

Popper's proposal is that the line between science and non-science is to be drawn by falsifiability. A theory belongs to science only if it is logically possible for some observation to conflict with it — only if, that is, it rules something out. A theory compatible with every possible course of experience is not an empirical theory at all but an unfalsifiable picture of the world.

He came at the problem through three cases. Marxist history had made predictions that failed, yet its partisans reinterpreted the failures so that the theory emerged strengthened. Freud and Adler were worse: the same framework could explain a man who dives in to save a drowning child and a man who pushes a child in, one as sublimation and the other as repression, so no conceivable human behaviour counted against it. Einstein's theory of gravitation was the contrast case, since it predicted the bending of starlight at the 1919 eclipse and would have been refuted had the measurements come out the other way.

The advantage over verifiability is that falsifiability exploits an asymmetry in logic. A universal law like all swans are white can never be established by any finite run of white swans, but a single genuine black swan contradicts it deductively. Refutation is therefore a matter of logic rather than of accumulated support, and Popper took this to dissolve the problem of induction: science does not reason upward from instances to laws, it conjectures laws and then attacks them.

The positivists had wanted a criterion of meaning and not merely of science — a sentence is cognitively meaningful just in case it is analytic or empirically verifiable, and anything else is nonsense. Popper's criterion does the same work more honestly. On his view an unfalsifiable statement is not merely unscientific but meaningless, which is why he could write off Marx, Freud and the metaphysical tradition together as cognitively empty.

This is where Duhem and Quine bite. As Duhem argued for physical theory and Quine generalised in Two Dogmas of Empiricism (1951), no hypothesis meets experience on its own: hypotheses meet it collectively, along with the auxiliary assumptions linking them to the instruments and the initial conditions, so a failed prediction shows only that something in that whole bundle is false, never which piece. Popper knew this, and it is why he required that a falsifying observation be a reproducible effect rather than a one-off anomaly. But having accepted the holism he could no longer maintain that any individual theoretical statement is refutable, and so he fell back on corroboration — how severely a theory has been tested and survived — as his real ground for preferring one theory to another. Corroboration cannot carry that weight, though, because a pseudoscience gets corroborated too, by practitioners who simply agree among themselves about which tests counted as severe. The upshot is that falsifiability fails as a criterion in principle and not just in practice, since any theory whatever can be saved by the right adjustment, and Popper cannot have demarcation and holism both ways.
