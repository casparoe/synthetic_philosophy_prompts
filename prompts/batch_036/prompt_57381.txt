I spent last summer volunteering at a legal aid clinic attached to a detention facility, and I am now auditing a graduate seminar in migration ethics. The seminar keeps returning to the question of whether states have a right to exclude, but it conducts that debate almost entirely at the level of principles — self-determination, freedom of association, the value of political community — while the actual machinery of exclusion, which I watched operate up close every day, seems to me to encode a set of commitments nobody in the room wants to articulate. So I would like to work through a description of that machinery with you. I am not asking for a survey of the positions on open borders, and please do not give me references, a bibliography, or a reading list; what I want is a reconstruction of what the practice itself commits us to. If any part of my description strikes you as inaccurate or overgeneralized, correct it before reasoning from it rather than building on something false.

Here is the package of features I have in mind, all of them ordinary and widely shared across wealthy states rather than exotic. First: admission is treated as a matter of discretion at the threshold. A visa refusal typically requires no reasons, is often unreviewable, and carries no obligation to justify itself to the person refused; yet once a person is inside the territory, even irregularly, constitutional protections thicken considerably — hearings, proportionality review, constraints on indefinite detention. The same legal system is markedly less deferential to the executive about presence than about entry. Second: membership, once acquired, is very hard to lose and is transmitted automatically to children born abroad however many generations removed, while in some states it is acquired automatically by being born on the soil; naturalization, by contrast, must be earned through years of residence, language and civics testing, a clean record, and frequently the renunciation of any prior citizenship. The upshot is that a newborn with no relationship whatsoever to the country stands ahead of a person who has lived, worked, and paid taxes there for twenty years. Third: the recognized human right of movement is a right to leave any country and to return to one's own, not a right to be admitted anywhere in particular; states resist accepting a renunciation of citizenship unless another citizenship is already in hand, and treat statelessness as an anomaly to be cured by assigning the person to some state rather than by admitting them. Fourth: removal is not a unilateral act. To expel someone, the expelling state needs a second state to issue travel documents and receive the person; where that state refuses, delays, or disputes the nationality, removal simply stalls, and the person is frequently released on a renewable toleration status that can persist for years. Much of the exclusionary machinery therefore runs on bilateral readmission arrangements rather than on any single state's authority.

With that in view, I want to know what theory of political membership and of territory this package presupposes. Is it a property model, on which a people collectively owns a tract of land and exercises an owner's control over who crosses it? A contractual or club model, on which the state is a voluntary association entitled to select its members? A household or kinship model, under which membership is a matter of descent and cannot be applied for at all? A model on which the point is not property but the integrity of a self-governing collective? For each, tell me whether the practice strictly requires it or merely sits comfortably with it, and identify the places where the package is philosophically underdetermined — where two rival theories would license exactly the same institutions. Then press on three tensions in particular. Does the fourth feature show that the much-discussed right to exclude is not a unilateral right at all but a system of mutual recognition, in which every state's power to turn people away depends on other states' willingness to take them back — and if so, what happens to justifications framed as a state's authority over its own territory, when the authority in fact has to be negotiated and can be defeated by another government's inaction? Is the third feature coherent when paired with the first: if the recognized liberty is only a liberty to exit, and exit is into a world where no one is obliged to admit you, does the practice presuppose that the habitable surface of the earth is exhaustively and legitimately apportioned, and that freedom of movement is a claim against one's own government rather than a claim to a place to be? And do the first and second features presuppose two incompatible accounts of who counts as a member — presence and contribution on one side, blood and birthright on the other — or is there a single theory that can hold both without embarrassment? Finish by telling me what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced: under a view on which persons have an equal original claim to the places of the earth, or on which movement is a general liberty that any state may restrict only on articulated justification, which of the four features would disappear, which would survive with a different rationale, and what institution would have to take the place of the bilateral readmission system on which removal currently depends?
