I am one of five volunteers on the long-term recovery committee for a small rural county, and since the spring floods I have spent two evenings a week deciding how to divide a fixed pot of privately donated money among roughly a hundred and forty households that have asked for it. The pot will not cover them. The committee has split into two camps that no longer speak to each other in ordinary language: one camp wants to rank applications by immediate need, the other by what it calls "recovery capacity" — whether a given household can actually convert four thousand dollars into a habitable house, as opposed to whether it is the household that most deserves our sympathy. Last week the second camp's spokesman, a retired contractor, defended himself by saying that ranking by need is really just ranking by eloquence, and that we are entitled to buy outcomes rather than feelings. I left that meeting convinced he was wrong and entirely unable to say why.

Trying to put my objection into words, I went looking into the history of allocation under scarcity, and in a digitized collection I came across an unsigned pamphlet fragment — no title page survives, no author named anywhere in the scan, and the internal evidence (an emigrant vessel lost on the westward passage, a long-boat cast off from the wreck) puts it somewhere in the middle of the nineteenth century. I have transcribed the page that interests me below. Please do not speculate about who wrote it or where it appeared; I could find no attribution and I do not want one invented. Work only from the text as given. The archaic verb forms and spellings are in the original, not errors in my transcription.

> It will be said that the survivors of the long-boat did well to abstain from choosing, since whosoever chooseth among lives taketh upon himself the guilt of those he leaveth behind. But this is a coward's casuistry. To forbear from choosing is not to escape the choice; it is only to remit it to the strongest arm and the sharpest elbow, and to charge the issue to an account which no man keeps. Necessity will have a distribution; the question that remaineth open to honest men is not whether one shall be made, but by what measure, and by whose hand.
>
> The measure cannot be worth, for worth is known to God alone, and is in any case equal in the eye that must judge it. It must therefore be service: what each remaining life can be made to yield to the preservation of the rest. The surgeon before the sailor, though the sailor be the better man; the woman with infants at the breast before the unattached labourer, not for her own sake but for theirs who hang upon her. Thus the boat becometh a commonwealth in miniature, and each aboard is a magistrate to his neighbour.
>
> Here an objection ariseth which I have not seen answered. If service be the measure, then he that applieth it is measured by it also; and if he be found wanting, as the aged and the idle must be, the rule condemneth its own author. Wherefore I say: the compact is not made in the boat, but upon the quay. A distribution agreed upon in the port, in the full light of day, by men who know not which of them it shall favour, bindeth the conscience; the same distribution, devised at midnight by men who know precisely whom it favoureth, bindeth nothing and excuseth no one.
>
> And let no man comfort himself with the metaphor of the ballast. For the ballast is not carried for its own sake but for the ship's; and yet when the water cometh aboard it is the first thing cast out. So it fareth with all whom necessity maketh instruments: they are valued and they are thrown away in the same breath, and the valuing is the reason for the throwing away.
>
> I conclude, then, that the long-boat is a place wherein no man can be good, though many may be righteous. Charity reckoneth by heads and is spent upon the nearest; justice reckoneth by consequences and is spent upon the whole. Where there is not enough for the whole, charity hath no object left her, and only justice can be obeyed; and obedience is a cold companion.

I can follow the surface of this, but I keep losing the thread of what is actually being argued, and I suspect the author is making at least one move I am not equipped to see. Six things, in order of importance to me:

1. Reconstruct the argument of the first paragraph as premises and a conclusion. My sense is that "to forbear from choosing is not to escape the choice" does real work, but I cannot tell whether it is offered as support for the specific measure proposed in the second paragraph, or only against abstention generally. If the latter, say so plainly, and tell me what would have to be added to get from there to "service."

2. The sentence "worth is known to God alone, and is in any case equal in the eye that must judge it" seems to me to contain two separate claims that are pulling in different directions, and I cannot tell which one is doing the work. Separate them and say what each would imply if held on its own.

3. Explain the ballast paragraph. I cannot decide whether it is an objection to the service measure, a grim acceptance of it, or something else — the clause "the valuing is the reason for the throwing away" reads to me like an accusation, but I cannot say whom it accuses.

4. The "compact is made upon the quay, not in the boat" move: is this a claim about the legitimacy of a procedure, about what people in extremis can know, or about what they can be trusted to want? These strike me as three different arguments wearing the same sentence. Also tell me whether it is consistent with "each aboard is a magistrate to his neighbour," which seems to me to hand the deciding authority back to whoever is in the boat.

5. Reconstruct the good/righteous distinction in the final paragraph, and say whether the author thinks the distinction is a comfort or an indictment. What does "charity hath no object left her" mean, given that he has just said charity reckons by heads?

6. Flag every place where a key term — worth, service, instrument, justice — appears to change sense between paragraphs. I suspect at least one of the argument's apparent strengths depends on such a shift, and I want to know which.

Finally, and briefly: my committee's pot is fixed but nobody dies if we allocate it badly, and the applicants are known to us by name and circumstance rather than anonymous. Which parts of this passage survive that change of setting, and which parts depend on the boat being a boat?
