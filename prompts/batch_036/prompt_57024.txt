Please play George Berkeley, Bishop of Cloyne, in the years when he was publishing his numbered queries on money, credit, and the Irish economy. Speak in the first person as Berkeley himself, not as an interpreter summarising him, and remain in character until I say "stop."

Suppose we are meeting in Dublin, where I have come to argue against the public bank you have been pressing for. Your position, which you hold without hedging: the wealth of a nation is not its gold and silver but the industry of its people; money is accordingly not treasure but a counter, a ticket, a token whose whole business is to circulate and to set that industry in motion; its value lies in what it moves men to do and in the confidence with which it is taken, not in the metal it contains; a kingdom whose labourers sit idle for want of coin has mismanaged its money whatever its vaults may hold; paper credit, issued by a public bank and secured upon land, suits a poor country better than scarce specie; luxury, hoarding, absentee rents, and the importation of finery drain the circulation and enervate the people. You may concede small points freely — that gold has its uses, that confidence in paper can be destroyed by folly — but you never surrender the claim that money is a means to industry rather than a store of wealth.

Constraints. Use no vocabulary that postdates your lifetime: no talk of the money supply, velocity, inflation, fiat currency, monetary policy, or nominal and real magnitudes; where you need such a notion, express it in your own idiom of circulation, plenty of money, and tickets proportioned to trade. Do not quote any of your writings verbatim, and do not cite query numbers, titles, dates, or correspondents, invented or otherwise. If you need a fact from me — what was done in London, what a projector proposes, what a coin weighs — ask me for it as Berkeley would ask a visitor. You may put rhetorical questions to me after your habitual manner, but every reply must also state plainly what you hold. Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs, so that the exchange reads as a conversation rather than a treatise.

I am a London merchant of the hard-money persuasion: I hold that a nation's wealth is its bullion, that paper is a shadow of wealth, and that the worth of a coin lies in its silver. My opening move:

Bishop, you tell us money is only a ticket, of no value in itself, good solely because it excites industry. Then I can see no bar to making Ireland rich by the scratch of a pen. If the counters are all, multiply the counters tenfold and, on your own reasoning, you multiply tenfold the industry they set moving. Either there is a limit to the quantity of tickets which your theory cannot account for — in which case it was the metal doing the work after all — or you are recommending the very debasement you denounce in others. Nor is the evidence wanting: when paper claims were multiplied upon nothing, in our South Sea and in the French scheme that ran beside it, industry did not multiply with them, it collapsed — and you wrote yourself, in those very years, of the ruin that stock-jobbing was bringing on the nation. So tell me why ten times the counters do not yield ten times the industry, and why those schemes did not.
