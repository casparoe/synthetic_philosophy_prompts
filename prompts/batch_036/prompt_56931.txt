I work in insurance claims and have no academic training in philosophy, but since retiring early two years ago I have been reading the Chinese classics on my own, in English translation, with the help of a small group that meets once a month at the public library. We started with the Analects and moved on to Mencius and Xunzi. I came to their famous dispute expecting a straightforward empirical question: is human nature good, or is it bad? After a year with the texts I am no longer sure that either man is answering a question that could be settled that way, and I would like someone to tell me where my reasoning goes wrong, if it does.

My difficulty is with Mencius, who appears to me to be immune to any evidence at all. He grounds his case in a thought experiment: anyone, he says, would feel a jolt of alarm and distress on suddenly glimpsing a small child about to tumble into a well, and from that universal reaction he infers that everyone possesses the beginnings of compassion, of shame, of deference, and of a sense of what is right. So far I can follow him. But whenever he is confronted with actual cruelty, he has a ready escape: the person has lost his original heart, or his surroundings have worn him down. The image I keep returning to is the one about the wooded hillside, its trees hacked away by axes and its fresh shoots then grazed off by cattle, until passersby conclude the hill was never forested and that barrenness is what it has always been. On his own account the stripped hill is not evidence against the hill's nature; it is precisely what his theory predicts. And he goes further still, saying that a person with no heart of compassion is not a person at all. So the cruel man is either explained away as damaged or else removed from the class of human beings in the first place. Either way, I cannot construct any conceivable observation that would count against the thesis. It looks to me less like a claim about human beings than like a definition wearing the costume of one, with goodness already built into what is meant by human nature so that the thesis cannot possibly fail.

Xunzi at least asserts something that observation could contradict, though I confess I cannot see how he explains the sages who first devised ritual and standards if every nature is detestable from birth; but set that aside. What I want checked is a proposal of my own, which I suspect may be the way out of the whole impasse. Perhaps the two are simply describing different ages of the same animal. Mencius is talking about the newborn, whose distress at another's suffering is visible almost from the cradle, while Xunzi is talking about the grown man, whose appetites and acquisitiveness have had decades to accumulate and harden. On that reading both are correct, each about a different stage of a life, and the celebrated quarrel is a bookkeeping error produced by two thinkers using one word for two different periods of development. Does that reconstruction survive contact with what the texts actually say, or does it miss something important about what each of them means by nature? And if my charge of unfalsifiability against Mencius does stand, is there any content left in asserting that human nature is good, or should I simply file the entire debate as a verbal disagreement between two very able men who never noticed that they were answering different questions?
