For the last two years I have been the person who signs off on whether our hospital's readmission-risk model is fit for clinical use, and a few months ago I noticed something about our validation procedure that has made the epistemology of bootstrapping uncomfortably personal for me. We check the model's predictions against a gold-standard outcome record, but that record is assembled by a coding pipeline whose rules were themselves settled years ago by looking at what the model's predecessors flagged. The instrument that is supposed to certify the model is therefore calibrated in part by the model's own outputs. When I described this to a philosopher friend she said it was a textbook case of bootstrapping, and I went to that literature expecting a settled answer. Instead I found a dispute that has run for three decades with a small number of very well known moves on each side and no sign of convergence.

Here is the shape of it as I understand it. Everyone accepts the core case: a subject forms beliefs about the fuel level in her car by reading a gauge that is in fact reliable, with no independent evidence about the gauge; each reading gives her two things, what the gauge says and what is so, from which she can note that the gauge was right that time; accumulating these, she reasons her way to the conclusion that the gauge is reliable. Each step looks unimpeachable, the accumulation looks obviously worthless, and the tension between those appearances is the problem. One camp holds that the reasoning is genuinely defective and that any theory permitting it is thereby refuted: allow knowledge or justification to be basic in the relevant way and knowledge of this easy kind follows, which is a reductio of the allowance. A second camp accepts basic knowledge and locates the defect in the reasoning rather than in the theory, because the pattern is rule-circular, or because it would have led the reasoner to the same conclusion whether or not the source was reliable, and so cannot be a way of coming to know. A third camp bites the bullet: nothing is wrong with the reasoning as a matter of epistemic status, and its bad reputation comes from its uselessness to the reasoner or from pragmatic features of the context in which we imagine it. A fourth camp says the whole problem is a symptom of imposing a reflective requirement, that one must be able to justify one's sources, which no theory can meet short of skepticism, and that the circularity is benign once the requirement is dropped. Underneath all of this runs the older argument about whether perceptual warrant transmits: whether an argument can have warranted premises and a warranted conclusion and still fail to be a good route to that conclusion, because the warrant for the premises depends on something only the conclusion could supply.

My working hypothesis, which I want tested rather than flattered, is that the first two camps are not disagreeing about the same thing. One is evaluating tokens of reasoning as ways of forming beliefs for the reasoner who undertakes them; the other is evaluating the resulting doxastic states; and much of the apparent deadlock is what you get when a verdict about the second is offered as an answer to a question about the first. On that reading the dispute is not substantive in the way the literature assumes, and progress would come from stating the two questions separately and showing that each camp's favorite cases are counterexamples only to the other's question.

Tell me whether that is right, or whether the disagreement is genuinely substantive, or verbal in some narrower sense turning on the word defective, or a case of two sides answering different questions in some way other than the one I have described, for instance by individuating the source differently, or by covertly disagreeing about whether the intuitions here are data to be respected or verdicts to be explained away. I want reasons drawn from what each side is actually committed to, not a map of the terrain. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a formal result about closure, a new case one side cannot absorb, or nothing at all, in which case explain why the impasse is the correct permanent state.

Finally, as a check on your diagnosis rather than as a request for advice, say what it predicts about my own case. Note the variant I find harder. Suppose we replace the gold standard with a genuinely independent audit of outcomes, so that the checking instrument shares no history with the model, but the definition of the audited outcome, which readmissions count, was originally chosen because it is what the model is good at predicting. Say which of my two cases your diagnosis classifies as defective and why, and whether it treats them differently; if your diagnosis cannot tell them apart, count that as evidence against it and say so plainly.

Please write to me in continuous prose, the way one writes a letter or a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, no tables. Just paragraphs, one after another.
