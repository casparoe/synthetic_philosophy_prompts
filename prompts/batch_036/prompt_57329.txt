For the better part of two decades I have read the same loose cluster of rationalist blogs and their comment threads, and the arguments I find most instructive are the ones in which both participants have plainly done the reading and neither is performing for an audience. In November two people from a meetup group I belong to had exactly that kind of argument in my kitchen. I took notes out of habit, and I have since failed to referee the thing to my own satisfaction. Neither is a pushover: Marcus builds optimality models for a living, and Elena works on the development of vertebrate craniofacial morphology.

The subject was the human chin. We are the only primates with a projecting bony one; it appears late in the hominin record and is absent or rudimentary in Neanderthals and earlier forms. The question they got stuck on was what kind of explanation a fact like that calls for. Here is the exchange as I reconstructed it, with the moves numbered:

1. Marcus opened by arguing that the chin is an adaptation. It is novel, species-specific, costly to build, and unlike anything else in the primate mandible, and the most economical account of a structure like that is that selection made it. He offered two candidate functions: resisting the bending and wishboning loads that chewing imposes at the mandibular symphysis, and serving as a visual signal of maturity and robustness in mate choice and status contests. He said that "it is there for no reason in particular" should be the last resort, not the first.

2. Elena said this was precisely the reasoning Gould and Lewontin attacked in their 1979 paper: treating the plausibility of a functional story as though it were evidence for that story, and failing to enumerate the alternatives before picking one. Her account was developmental. Over the later Pleistocene the human face reduced and retracted; the tooth-bearing alveolar portion of the lower jaw is resorptive while the region beneath it is depositional, a pattern tied to the forward position of the hyoid and the cervical column crowding the oral cavity. A protruding knob of bone at the base of the symphysis is what that pattern leaves behind — a consequence of two regions changing in different directions, not a target of selection. She noted that recent work links chin prominence to overall facial reduction rather than to chewing loads.

3. Marcus made two replies. First, a byproduct account is a positive empirical claim, not a null: it says the chin should covary with degree of facial reduction across living populations and across the fossil record, and should not track dietary toughness. That is testable, so it ought to be tested, and if the covariation fails, the byproduct story sits in exactly the same evidential boat as his — with the difference that his at least explains why the structure exists rather than merely why it might. Second, even if the covariation holds, the account relocates the question instead of answering it: facial reduction had causes, and if those causes are dietary and mechanical — softer, processed food, reduced masticatory loading — then the adaptive explanation has simply moved one joint up the chain, and adaptationism has lost nothing.

4. Elena conceded the first reply with a caveat: she thinks the association is real, but she admitted the fossil sample is thin and the correlation nowhere near clean. She rejected the second outright, saying Marcus had changed the explanandum. The trait under dispute is the chin; if the chin is what a shrinking face leaves behind, then the chin is not an adaptation, however adaptive the shrinking was. Someone who retreats from "the chin was selected for" to "the cause of the chin was selected for" has conceded the original claim while sounding as though he is still defending it.

5. Marcus said that this concession is what makes the position unfalsifiable. Every structure is a consequence of something else and every trait has developmental causes; if locating a trait downstream of some other change suffices to deny it adaptation status, then no morphological feature could ever be shown to be an adaptation, and the label just records where you decided to stop the regression. He said the honest version of Elena's claim is not that the chin is not an adaptation but that no one has produced evidence that it is one, and that those are not symmetric: natural selection is the only process anyone has identified that builds intricate, function-specific form, so its absence is what needs a special argument. He added a sharper point — if facial reduction was itself favored by selection, then the chin persisted through a period in which the rest of the face was being dismantled. Bone is not free. Either it was maintained because it did something, or because there was no heritable variation capable of decoupling it from the surrounding pattern, and the second of those is an empirical claim about development, not a default.

6. Elena rejected the allocation of the burden. She said the presumption that selection is the null is the very defect Gould and Lewontin identified: a program that starts from one favored hypothesis and requires the others to displace it, rather than holding several architectures live at once — no change at all, drift, developmental necessity, correlated response to selection on a neighboring trait. She also said the burden claim begs the question, since whether selection deserves default status depends on how much heritable variation in chin form existed and how strong selection could plausibly have been, which is exactly what they disagree about. And she said "bone is not free" was rhetorical inflation: the mental region is a few cubic centimeters of cortical bone in an animal that was simultaneously reducing its entire face, with no reason to think the protrusion carried a detectable cost.

7. Marcus said he agreed that pluralism about architectures is fine in principle, but that Elena's particular null never loses. When chin size tracks facial reduction, the byproduct account is confirmed; when it does not, the sample is too small; when a performance test comes out against the load-bearing story, that shows only that a bad functional story was bad, not that the byproduct story was good. He added that Elena's own account is committed to functional claims about the face, the hyoid, the cervical column, and the diet, so the two of them disagree about considerably less than the rhetoric suggests — hers is adaptationism with one additional step, and that step is doing all the work.

8. Elena said the disagreement was never about whether selection matters but about where the explanation of a given trait is permitted to terminate, and that "one additional step" gets the direction backwards: the step is the explanation of the trait, not a distraction from it. She said she would readily call the chin an exaptation if independent evidence emerged that it was later co-opted as a signal, but that this would be a different claim from the one Marcus began with, and it would require evidence about perception and preference rather than about the geometry of the bone.

Please adjudicate this move by move rather than in the aggregate. For each of the eight numbered moves, assign a rating on a five-point scale running from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justify it in two or three sentences; where a move is compound, say explicitly which part earns the rating and which part drags it down. Then take a position on the five places I could not resolve:

(a) Can a byproduct account serve as a default, or must it be earned on the same terms as a functional one?

(b) Does Marcus's second reply in move 3 rescue his original claim or concede it?

(c) Is the unfalsifiability charge in moves 5 and 7 fair, given the concession Elena makes in move 4 and the covariation test she accepts in principle?

(d) Is the burden-of-proof asymmetry Marcus asserts in move 5 defensible, and what would it take to state it non-question-beggingly?

(e) Does the exaptation distinction Elena draws in move 8 genuinely separate two claims, or is it relabeling?

Finally, tell me what neither of them said that they should have. Specifically: does the architectural analogy from which the word spandrel comes really license the notion of a necessary byproduct that Elena's argument depends on? Are the categories in play — adaptation, spandrel, exaptation, nonadaptation, constraint — being used consistently by both, or is one of those slippages doing quiet work in the exchange? And is there anything in the chin case that generalizes into a lesson about how adaptationist and structuralist research programs ought to divide the space, or is the moral local to this trait?

I am not looking for citations, a bibliography, or a reading list. I want the adjudication and the reasoning behind it.
