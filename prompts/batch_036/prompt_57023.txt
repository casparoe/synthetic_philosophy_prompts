For a seminar on federalism I have been working through an argument a classmate of mine constructed, intended to show that joint decision-making is indefensible in principle rather than merely inefficient. I can hardly deny the premises and cannot accept the conclusion, and I would like a diagnosis rather than a rebuttal.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) Subsidiarity is a principle of allocation: authority over a policy domain belongs to the lowest level of government that can effectively pursue the objectives of that domain.

(2) A level can effectively pursue a domain's objectives only if it can internalize the effects its decisions have on those who bear them, and it can internalize those effects only if it can decide the matter on its own. Under a concurrence requirement each level is exposed to outcomes it could neither have produced nor prevented, while remaining responsible for them.

(3) Where two levels must concur, the status quo can be altered only with the agreement of every veto player, so the result gravitates toward the position acceptable to the party least willing to move.

(4) Whenever the levels' preferences differ at all, that position is worse, for each of them, than what either level could have achieved by deciding alone within its own competence.

(5) Therefore any domain governed by concurrence is a domain in which subsidiarity has been violated: the allocation is not the one subsidiarity recommends, and the output is inferior to the available alternative.

(6) In a modern state every policy domain generates effects that cross internal boundaries — fiscal, environmental, migratory, commercial — so in every domain more than one level has a claim to decide.

(7) Where two levels each have a claim to decide a domain, the only constitutional way of honoring both claims is to require their concurrence.

(8) Hence every domain is governed by concurrence (from 6 and 7), and every domain thereby violates subsidiarity and yields lowest-common-denominator outcomes (from 5).

(9) Effects that cross internal boundaries do not stop at the state's external border. Applying (2) consistently, the only level capable of internalizing the effects of any modern policy is the global level, and there is no global government. So no existing polity, federal or unitary, holds subsidiarity-compliant authority over anything.

My classmate's conclusion: Germany's consent legislation, the European Union's ordinary legislative procedure, and the equal power of the two Swiss chambers are all objectionable in the same way, and a legitimate constitution would assign each domain, entire, to exactly one level. He adds that adopting such a constitution would itself require an amendment procedure in which two levels concur, and regards this as confirming his view rather than embarrassing it.

I would prefer to keep (1) and to keep (3) and (4), since I think the drift toward the position of the most reluctant player is real and worth worrying about; I do not want to keep (9). Which step should I give up? For each serious candidate, tell me why it looked unobjectionable when it was proposed and what dropping it costs. In particular: if I deny (2), on what ground can I continue to object to shared competences and joint tasks in a cooperative federation? If I deny (7), what distinguishes a claim to decide that can be honored without concurrence from one that cannot, and does the distinction survive contact with framework legislation, preemption, and differentiated integration? If I deny (6), what is left of the empirical part of the case? And is the amendment-procedure remark a genuine self-defeat or one further flaw among others?
