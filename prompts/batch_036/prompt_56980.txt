You are a political philosopher who works at the intersection of democratic theory and measurement — someone who has thought hard about what it takes to turn a contested concept like democracy into a number that can survive being argued with. Please think this through carefully and step by step before committing to a diagnosis. I am not looking for a literature survey, a reading list, or references; I am looking for your own reconstruction and judgment.

**My situation.** I work in the research unit of an organization that publishes an annual country score. Two governments and one lending institution use the score to decide whether to invoke a rule-of-law conditionality clause. The instrument is small enough that I can describe it exactly. There are eleven indicators; three of them concern what we call the rules of political competition — who draws constituency boundaries, who administers and adjudicates elections, and who appoints the judges with jurisdiction over those first two questions. Each indicator runs 0 to 4. The aggregate is not a mean but a minimum: a country's overall score is the score of its weakest indicator, on the stated theory that strengths in one dimension cannot compensate for collapse in another. There is also an internal drafting rule, in our style guide from before I joined: a downgrade must be justified in language that does not evaluate the policy preferences of any party. We are permitted to describe what a rule does to the conditions of competition. We are not permitted to say whether a party's program is good or bad.

In two weeks our review committee meets on the Central European cases, and I have to defend or retract a proposed downgrade. My difficulty is that a senior colleague — whom I respect, and who is no cynic — produced an argument that seems to show the whole exercise is impossible, and worse, that if it is possible it is not what we claim to be doing. I have read it perhaps thirty times and I cannot find the exit. I am also a citizen of one of the countries under review, which is either a conflict of interest or the reason I was hired, depending on whom in the office you ask.

**The argument.**

(1) A government backslides when it uses its temporary hold on office to change the rules of political competition in ways that make it harder for that government to be turned out.
(2) Every government does exactly this. It draws boundaries, sets the electoral calendar, defines eligibility, writes the finance and media rules, appoints judges, regulators and central bankers to terms that outlast its own, and places whole policy domains behind supermajority requirements — always knowing that these choices will shape the odds under which it next faces the electorate. Entrenchment is not an abuse of governing; it is governing.
(3) So on (1), every government backslides.
(4) Yet some of these entrenchments are praised as democracy-strengthening: locking minority protections beyond ordinary repeal, insulating electoral administration from the government of the day, making the central bank independent. Nobody in this office would score those as backsliding.
(5) Therefore the difference between backsliding and ordinary politics cannot lie in the act of entrenching, nor in the intention to bind one's successors, since both are present in the praised cases. It must lie entirely in the value of what is entrenched.
(6) But the value of what is entrenched is precisely what the parties dispute. The government calls a bill a safeguard; its opponents call the same bill a capture. There is no further fact to consult: not the text, which says safeguard; not the procedure, which was followed to the letter; not the intention, which was in any case to bind.
(7) So backsliding does not describe a change in a country's institutions. It describes a verdict on a government the speaker already dislikes, and observers on opposite sides will classify the same reforms in opposite directions — which is exactly what we find in our own review meetings.
(8) Therefore our downgrade is not a finding of fact but a political act wearing the costume of one, and the minimum rule guarantees it: whichever single indicator we judge worst becomes the entire country's score, so one contested evaluation silently determines everything.

What I cannot give up is the negation of (8). I need to be able to tell the committee either that our downgrade is a finding, or that it is not and we should stop publishing. Everything else is negotiable, including (1), which I wrote.

**Two cases the committee will put to me.** First: a government won a two-thirds parliamentary majority under the old rules, followed every procedure those rules laid down for constitutional amendment, and used that majority to adopt a new constitution together with a body of statutes that only a future two-thirds majority can amend — among them statutes governing the electoral commission and the tenure of judges. Our draft treats this as a downgrade. Second: a long-established democracy that elects its legislature in single-member districts under first-past-the-post rules, where a modest plurality of votes routinely converts into a large majority of seats, where those districts are drawn by bodies the governing party appoints or controls, and where the seat distribution in the chamber departs substantially from the distribution of votes. We score that country 4 on all three rules-of-competition indicators and have done for years. I cannot explain to the committee why case one is a downgrade and case two is not without appealing to which outcome I happen to prefer.

**What I want from you.**

- Begin with brainstorming. Before arguing for anything, list the candidate exits — every step you could reject, and every distinction that could be inserted between steps — and note in a sentence or two what each rejection would cost me.
- Then commit. Which single step, if you had to name one, is the load-bearing failure? Is it simply false, or only false under a reading the earlier steps do not establish? If there is an equivocation, a scope slide, or a suppressed premise, name it precisely, and explain why the step looked unobjectionable when I first read it.
- Say whether the argument is better read as valid with a false premise or as invalid with a hidden assumption, and what turns on that difference. I ask because the two readings seem to imply different repairs, and I can only attempt one before the meeting.
- Test your diagnosis against my two cases. Does the distinction you propose actually separate them, or does it dissolve on contact? If it separates them, tell me what I should say to the committee in the language our style guide permits — describing what rules do to the conditions of competition, without evaluating any party's program. If it cannot separate them, say so plainly; I would rather learn that now than in the room.
- Address the final clause of (8) on its own terms. Does taking a minimum across indicators make the situation worse than the argument claims, better, or is it a red herring? What does minimum aggregation presuppose about the structure of democracy — about compensability, thresholds, or the ordering of dimensions — and is that presupposition defensible independently of this argument?
- Be explicit about where the argument is strongest and where I should concede ground. I have no interest in a defense of my employer that survives only by refusing to engage (6).

Aim for roughly fifteen hundred words, in plain prose under numbered headings. Do not cite works or recommend readings — I cannot chase them before the meeting and would rather have your reasoning than your references. If you conclude that the exercise is genuinely impossible, say so; but then tell me what an institution in our position ought to publish instead, and what it would have to stop claiming about itself.
