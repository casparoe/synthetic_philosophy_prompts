I'm an infra engineer at a mid-size open source foundation and somehow got roped onto the panel that's deciding how to spend a $600k grant a corporate donor just handed us. The donor's restriction: the whole amount has to go to two specific member projects, call them A and B, split however we decide, and it has to be comitted by the end of the quarter or the offer lapses and both projects get nothing. I have zero formal philosophy or econ training, I'm just the numbers person on the panel, so bear with me.

Our rule-nerd (ex-econ) says we should use the Kalai-Smorodinsky bargaining solution, which as I understand it means you draw a line from the walk-away point to each side's maximum possible ask and take the point on the frontier along that line, so both sides end up with the same fraction of what they were hoping for. Another member says use the Nash solution instead, maximize the product of the gains. He also said the two give the same answer when the problem is symmetric so it supposedly hardly matters, but ours isn't symmetric at all:

- A is asking for $520k (two full-time engineers). B is asking for $240k (one plus a docs sprint).
- I ran both in a spreadsheet: KS comes out around $410k for A and $190k for B, Nash comes out 300/300 flat. So A wants KS and B wants Nash, obviously.

Then it gets messy, and this is the part I actually need help with.

1. A's $520k includes salary for a maintainer whose employer already pays them to work on the project full time. B's numbers are all invoices we can check against the repo. If we feed A's stated ask into the formula as-is, an unverifiable and possibly padded claim moves the answer, and under KS a bigger ask literally gets you more money — so modesty gets punished. Is that a bug in the rule or in how we're using it?
2. We're plugging in dollars like they were utilities. A dollar to a project with 3 maintainers and a dollar to one with 40 aren't the same thing, and neither formula notices.
3. A's lead maintainer told me off-list that if they don't get close to their full ask they'll fork out of the foundation and start a trademark fight, which would tank the ecosystem including B, since B's value depends on A staying alive. Does a credible threat like that get to redefine the walk-away point, or is a fair rule supposed to refuse to let it? Both formulas just seem to take the disagreement point as handed to them and I can't find anywhere they say where its supposed to come from.
4. We do this every year. If threatening to leave works this time, next year's walk-away point is even better for whoever does it.

Draft the memo I'll put in front of the panel. I need: an actual recommended split with dollar figures and a date, the rule we should write into the charter going forward (or an explicit "no formula, here's the judgment call we make each year and here's how"), the reasoning in language both maintainer teams can follow without a game theory background, and a real section making the strongest honest case for the position I'm *not* recommending — including the punishing-modesty complaint, which I think is the best argument in the whole thread and I don't have an answer for it. If your conclusion is that neither formula should be used here, say so and say what to use instead, but don't just punt to "it depends on your values."
