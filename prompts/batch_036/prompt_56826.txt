"The Judy Benjamin problem, invented by Bas van Fraassen, is supposed to show that Bayesianism breaks down when evidence arrives in conditional form. Judy is dropped into a region divided into a Red zone and a Blue zone, each containing two sub-areas, and she begins with equal credence in all four quadrants. Radio headquarters then tells her, 'If you are in the Red zone, you are three times likelier to be in the Second Company area than in the Headquarters area.' The difficulty is that Bayes's theorem requires a proposition to conditionalize on, and a conditional is not a proposition: Adams's thesis, that the probability of 'if A, B' equals P(B|A), was refuted by David Lewis, who proved that no proposition can satisfy it except in trivial cases. So Judy cannot represent what she has learned at all. The textbook repairs people reach for do not help either. Jeffrey conditionalization requires you to have settled new probabilities for every cell of a partition, which Judy cannot do, so her posterior is not merely hard to compute but undefined. Van Fraassen's own answer minimizes a distance between prior and posterior probability assignments, but the distance is chosen by fiat, and an arbitrary measure yields an arbitrary posterior. Its one uncontroversial feature is that it leaves Judy's credence in the antecedent — that she is in the Red zone — exactly where it started, which is as it should be. The moral is that conditional information must be reduced to categorical information before it can be used: replace 'if A, B is three times likelier than C' with 'the radio said thus-and-so', assign the radio a reliability, and conditionalize on that.
