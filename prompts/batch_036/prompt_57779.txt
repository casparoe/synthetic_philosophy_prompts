I retired three years ago after four decades directing a conservation science laboratory, and I have spent the retirement reading philosophy of art with something approaching the diligence I once gave to method validation. One line of argument has left me genuinely puzzled, and I should be grateful for a careful answer rather than a survey of the field.

The argument concerns forgery. As I understand it, philosophers ask us to imagine a fake so accomplished that no viewer, however attentive, could distinguish it from the original; they then maintain that even in that case the original remains the better work of art, on the ground that our knowledge that such a pair is possible changes what we owe to the picture in front of us. Nelson Goodman pressed something like this point, if I recall him correctly, by considering two paintings that cannot be told apart by looking, one of them an imitation of a celebrated master, and insisting that the mere possibility of an undetectable difference is enough to defeat the claim that the two are aesthetically equivalent.

My difficulty is empirical. In forty years I never once encountered such a pair. Every forgery that reached my bench betrayed itself: radiographs in which the crack pattern visible on the paint surface had no counterpart in the cracks running through the support; anachronistic pigments or binding media; lead isotope ratios inconsistent with the claimed ore; an old canvas scraped clean, restretched, and painted over. The van Meegeren forgeries after Vermeer, which my profession never tires of recounting, were undone by precisely this sort of instrumental examination at the hands of a court-appointed committee led by a chemist, quite as much as by any connoisseur's eye. It seems to me, therefore, that the premise of the thought experiment is simply false, and that once the premise is removed the puzzle goes with it. Our preference for the original is not a preference for some invisible relational property; it is a preference for a physical signature that instruments can read and that a trained eye can be taught to read. If a picture matters more because Vermeer made it in Delft than because someone made it in 1937, that is because the making left marks, and the marks are there to be found.

So: has the laboratory refuted these philosophers, and if it has not, what survives of their argument once the hypothetical is stripped away? Is the point merely a logical one about what would follow in a case that cannot arise, and if so, what work is it doing? And if my reasoning has gone wrong somewhere, I should like to be told precisely where, since I cannot see how a difference in what a painting is worth could fail to rest upon a difference in what a painting is.
