I write a science-and-ideas column and I've been sitting on a piece about animal tool use for way too long, mostly because my own category keeps sliding around on me. Here are my gut verdicts.

Chimp stripping leaves off a twig to fish for termites: tool, the paradigm. Chimp using the twig exactly as it fell, unmodified: still a tool. Otter cracking a clam on a rock balanced on its chest: tool, and I don't care that it's an anvil and not a hammer. Octopus hauling coconut halves across the seafloor and stacking them into a shelter: tool, strongly. Dolphin clamping a marine sponge over its beak while it forages: tool. Vulture dropping a rock on an ostrich egg, or dropping a bone from a height to crack it: tool. Finch hooking grubs out with a cactus spine: tool. Captive chimp piling crates up under a banana hung out of reach: tool, but it feels thinner.

And the noses. Spider web: not a tool, even though it's built, external, and does all the food-catching work. Beaver dam: not a tool. Bird lining its nest with pungent green vegetation that suppresses parasites: not a tool, that's nest material, though this one makes me squirm. Archerfish jetting water at an insect: not a tool. Anglerfish's lure: definitely not, it's part of the fish. A coat I'm wearing: not a tool. The same coat bunched up to lift a hot pan off the stove: tool. Ladder to reach a high shelf: tool, though I'd never say the ladder was the star of the story.

The pair I genuinely can't reconcile is the octopus shelter and the beaver dam: both assembled, both external, both doing the sheltering work — one gets the label and one doesn't. My tentative principle, invented out of frustration: something is a tool if it's a detachable object the animal positions between its own body and the goal, and that someone else could pick up and use afterwards. That handles the web, the dam, the anglerfish, the water jet, and the coat worn versus the coat as pad. But the nest plants walk right through it — detached, transferable, sitting between the chicks and the fleas.

So what principle actually captures these, or comes closest? I want it clause by clause, with the case each clause is earning its keep on; what it says about ones I didn't list (hermit crab's borrowed shell, prosthetic leg, elephant's fly-swatter branch, a person using a chatbot to draft an email); whether I have to give up the web verdict or the sponge verdict; and be straight with me about which verdicts it just can't save. Bottom line in your first sentence, then the reasoning — no preamble, and don't restate my question back at me.
