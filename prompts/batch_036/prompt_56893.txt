You are a moral philosopher who works on population ethics and on the ethics of intervening in nature, and who has enough familiarity with actual wildlife-management practice to know what reserve managers can and cannot forecast. Please think carefully before drafting: the specifications below have to be satisfied simultaneously, and the usual shortcuts are banned. Begin with two or three sentences of brainstorming about which variables you could vary, then commit to a design.

I am a PhD student preparing a case-study for a seminar session on wild animal suffering. Most available examples fail in one of two ways: they smuggle the verdict into the description, or they conflate two variables I need pulled apart — how much suffering the animals who will exist anyway undergo, and how many animals are brought into existence at all. I need a case that isolates them.

Construct the case as follows. Setting: a fenced island reserve holding an overabundant population of an indigenous browsing herbivore, well past what the vegetation will support. Exactly three options are open, with budget, logistics, and law foreclosing the rest: (i) do nothing, so that a two-winter starvation crash removes most of the population and survivors recover; (ii) bait-delivered immunocontraception, which averts the crash and lets the present animals live out full lives in good condition, but reduces the number of animals born over the next half-century by roughly an order of magnitude; (iii) sustained supplemental feeding together with parasite and disease control, which maintains a large population for decades in lives that are worth living but only modestly so, against a permanent background of crowding-related ill health. Fix the welfare levels and rough magnitudes yourself, and make the arithmetic visible; what you may not do is announce inside the description which option a view favours.

Required verdicts: an impersonal total welfarist, who treats the welfare of animals who would otherwise never exist as a genuine reason to act, ranks (iii) first; a suffering-focused welfarist, for whom creating further lives worth living is never itself a reason in favour of an act and for whom reducing suffering carries very great weight, and a non-consequentialist who holds that there is a pro tanto duty not to interfere with the autonomous operation of wild ecological processes, both rank (i) first — and their reasons must be as unlike one another as possible. Each view must also deliver a determinate second and third place.

Constraints: realistic, with a reserve manager's contested estimates rather than omniscience; no perfect predictors, no exotic technology, no trolley-style framing; the scenario itself in one paragraph; no naming philosophers and no invoking named puzzles (mere addition, the repugnant conclusion, the non-identity problem) to settle anything.

After the scenario, give me four things: (1) each view's reasoning from its own commitments, with the quantitative comparisons explicit; (2) the single structural feature doing the most work in generating the two-way convergence on (i); (3) two dissociation tests — first, the resource collapse turns out to have been caused by upstream human water extraction rather than natural cycles; second, veterinary care under (iii) improves until the maintained population is genuinely flourishing rather than merely net positive — saying for each which views move, which do not, and why; (4) how the defender of the view that fares worst in the original case would attack the construction. If you find the specifications cannot all be met, say so and tell me which one you relaxed rather than quietly fudging it.
