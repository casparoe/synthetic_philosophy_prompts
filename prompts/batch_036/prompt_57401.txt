I sit on the records committee of a university that, thirty-one years ago, paid students to inform on other students during the unrest of that period. The program is long defunct and the country has been at peace for a generation, but some four thousand informant reports survive in our archive, and two petitions have landed in the same month. The alumni association of those who were surveilled, dismissed, or imprisoned on the strength of these reports wants the files opened in full, names included; their position is that a wrong which cannot be examined is not finished. A group of former informants — recruited at nineteen, some of them under pressure that the files themselves document — wants the files destroyed; their position is that the university has no business holding a document capable of ruining a life that has since been ordinary and decent. Our charter offers exactly four options and requires a vote in June: open in full, open redacted, seal for fifty years, or destroy.

Three complicating facts the committee has not yet absorbed. First, a backlog digitization contract completed last spring means every file now exists in at least two off-site backup sets, so "destroy" would be a gesture about paper rather than a fact about information; a sample of eleven reports is already circulating online. Second, the reports were written by people paid by the word and never tested against anything, and both petitions quietly assume they are accurate. Third, sealing is revisitable and destruction is not: the only genuinely irreversible option on the ballot is the one that feels most like mercy.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before the vote. I know the standard moves on both sides well enough — Margalit's insistence that we owe memory to those bound to us by more than common humanity, Rieff's contention that a settled peace can be worth more than a completed record — and I want the memo to go past reciting them. It should name a recommendation and defend it; separate the considerations that turn on what the records are worth as evidence from those that turn on what a community owes itself in the way of forgetting; explain what, if anything, follows from the fact that the files are already partly beyond our control; and give the losing side its strongest statement rather than a strawman. Assume the readers are academics rather than philosophers, and keep it to four pages.
