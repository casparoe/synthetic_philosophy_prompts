Suppose the object of theorizing is this: what a finite agent believes at a time, and how that state is transformed when the agent reasons. I want the requirements fixed before any formalism is chosen, because the purpose I have in mind is a demanding one. The theory must be able to underwrite an account of logical discovery: a mathematician who believes the axioms spends a year coming to believe a theorem, and what she thereby acquires is something she did not already have, yet at no point was she irrational, confused, or failing to believe what she believed. An adequate theory has to make room for that pattern, and to make room for it as more than an ad hoc exemption.

Write the desiderata for such a theory, numbered. For each, give a statement precise enough that one could tell whether a candidate theory satisfies it, together with the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a preference. Cover at least the following: the phenomena to be explained — the competent reasoner who fails to believe a transparent consequence of what she believes; the agent who believes p and believes q but would reject p and q conjoined; undetected inconsistency of the preface variety; the fact that reasoning takes time, consumes resources, and can be interrupted; the distinctions the theory must not collapse; the constraints imposed by how belief is attributed in ordinary and scientific practice; the constraint that what an agent believes at a time must be settled by her condition at that time rather than by what she would eventually derive from it; and the constraint that whatever norms the theory invokes must not smuggle omniscience back in as an ideal, since the standard coherence-based arguments for rational belief are not obviously available to a bounded agent and the theory should say what survives of them.

Then say which of these are non-negotiable and which are substantive, contestable theses of particular camps presenting themselves as requirements. I expect that some of what is routinely demanded in this area — closure of belief under known implication, or the assumption that an agent's doxastic state is exhausted by a single set — belongs in the second category, and I want both the reason each looks mandatory and the reason it might not be.

The part I most want is this. Identify any subset of the desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which member must be weakened or abandoned. My working suspicion is that the following three are incompatible: that the agent not believe every consequence of her beliefs; that her beliefs be constrained tightly enough to support predictions about what further reasoning would yield; and that she be able in principle to determine, from her own standpoint, what she believes. Tell me whether that is correct, and if it is not, show me where the argument fails. Relatedly, name the single requirement that does the most exclusionary work — the one which, if weakened, would let the largest number of candidate theories through.

One further question the list should settle. In my notes I have been using "believes" and "is committed to believing" interchangeably, and I want to know whether a theory can keep the two apart and still call one of them belief, or whether some requirement above forces their collapse.

Close with at most a paragraph on each of the main families of response — awareness-style treatments, impossible-worlds and other non-normal semantics, neighborhood semantics, fragmentation or compartmentalization accounts, and approaches that dispense with full belief in favor of graded attitude — saying for each which requirement it buys its escape from omniscience by giving up or reinterpreting. No survey and no history; the specification is the deliverable.
