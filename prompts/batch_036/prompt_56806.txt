I am a senior at a public high school, and my capstone this year is an attempt to make two of my courses speak to each other, which my adviser has permitted with visible skepticism. In AP U.S. Government we spent an unusual amount of time on statutory interpretation, and the case we kept returning to was *Nix v. Hedden*. Separately, on my own, I have been reading the Warring States disputers, above all the short text attributed to Gongsun Long, and I have become convinced — perhaps wrongly — that the tomato case and the white horse dialogue are structurally the same argument wearing different clothes. My government teacher thinks the case is simply about legislative intent and that I am reaching; my Chinese teacher thinks I am not reaching far enough. What I want from you is a disciplined case analysis that runs in both directions rather than a verdict on which of my teachers is right, and I would genuinely rather be told the analogy fails than be told it succeeds.

The facts as I have them: the importers John Nix and his sons paid under protest a duty on tomatoes brought in from the West Indies in the spring of 1886 and sued the collector of the port of New York to recover it. The Tariff Act of March 3, 1883 imposed on "vegetables in their natural state, or in salt or brine, not specially enumerated or provided for in this act, ten per centum ad valorem," while its free list covered "Fruits, green, ripe, or dried, not specially enumerated or provided for in this act." The trial record consisted of little else than language: dictionary definitions of fruit, vegetables, and tomato from Webster's, Worcester's, and the Imperial Dictionary, competing definitions of pea, egg plant, cucumber, squash, pepper, potato, turnip, parsnip, cauliflower, cabbage, carrot, and bean, and two witnesses with thirty years in the produce business who testified that neither "fruit" nor "vegetable" carried any special meaning in trade different from the dictionaries'. Writing for a unanimous Court in 1893, Justice Gray held that absent evidence of a special commercial meaning the words "must receive their ordinary meaning," that dictionaries are admissible "not as evidence, but only as aids to the memory and understanding of the court," and that although "Botanically speaking, tomatoes are the fruit of a vine, just as are cucumbers, squashes, beans, and peas," in the common language of the people, sellers and consumers alike, these are vegetables grown in kitchen gardens and "usually served at dinner in, with, or after the soup, fish, or meats which constitute the principal part of the repast, and not, like fruits generally, as dessert." The Court also leaned on its own recent holding that beans are seeds in natural history but not in commerce or common parlance.

The theory I want to hold this against is the white horse dialogue's thesis that a white horse is not a horse. As I read it, the text supports that thesis with two distinguishable moves, and it matters to me that they be kept apart. The first, which I will call the designation move, holds that "horse" is what names a shape and "white" is what names a color, and that what names the color is not what names the shape, so the compound cannot be the simple name. The second, which I will call the substitution move, holds that if you seek a horse a yellow or black horse may be delivered, whereas if you seek a white horse neither may, and that since what is sought differs, the names differ. The dialogue's objector replies that from there being a white horse one cannot say there is no horse, so having a white horse is having a horse. I am already familiar with the standard diagnosis — that the dispute turns on an ambiguity in the copula between non-identity of sense and exclusion from a class — so please do not spend your effort restating it. Treat it instead as something to be tested against the case: does that diagnosis fully absorb *Nix v. Hedden*, or does the tariff dispute exhibit something the diagnosis was never built to handle, in particular the fact that the extension of a name was fixed not by the thing named but by a legislature's purpose and a market's habits?

Specifically, I would like you to work through the following, and to say plainly wherever the theory has nothing to say rather than forcing it to speak:

1. Seat assignment. Who occupies the Advocate's position and who the Objector's — the importers, the collector, or the Court? Note the wrinkle that both litigants reached for dictionaries and that the botanically correct sense was available to both sides; if the Court ends up in a position the dialogue never constructed, describe that position rather than squeezing it into one of the two existing seats.
2. The substitution move applied. Does "vegetable" in the 1883 schedule behave like "horse" or like "white horse"? Take seriously the statutory modifiers — "in their natural state" and, more interestingly, the negative qualifier "not specially enumerated," whose extension is fixed by what else the schedule lists. Ask whether a term whose content is parasitic on a list is a name in the disputers' sense at all, and whether the Court's bean precedent is the same move as the white horse argument or a different one.
3. The designation move applied. The Court weighs an intrinsic respect (the tomato is the fruit of a vine) against a relational one (it is served with the soup rather than as dessert). Is choosing between respects the same operation as choosing between shape and color, or is the case quietly introducing a distinction the dialogue lacks? If you think the hard-and-white argument about separable attributes bears on this at all, say so in a few sentences only; I would rather you leave it out than strain it.
4. Silence and authority. The dialogue assumes that names either match actualities or do not, and never asks who gets to fix the match. The Court instead takes judicial notice of ordinary usage. Is *Nix v. Hedden* a case the School of Names never anticipated — one in which a name's extension is settled by institutional purpose — and what does that gap cost the theory?
5. A verdict in both directions, with the conditions attached: does the episode confirm the white horse thesis as a real insight about qualified names that law must actively manage, reduce it to the familiar ambiguity, or expose a limit in it? And what would the record have had to show — what evidence at trial, what statutory language — for the case to count as a counterexample rather than an illustration?

I am writing this without library access and I am not looking for further reading, so please reason from the material above and from what you know of the disputers rather than pointing me toward scholarship; where your own confidence in a detail is low, mark it as such instead of filling the gap. And if you conclude that the analogy is mostly decorative — that a revenue case and a court disputation share a vocabulary but not an argument — I want that said early and argued, not softened at the end.
