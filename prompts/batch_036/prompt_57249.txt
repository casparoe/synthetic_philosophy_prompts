Write a short story of 1,500 to 2,000 words that dramatizes the symbol grounding problem — the worry that a system can deploy signs flawlessly while those signs are hooked up to nothing — using only human characters and institutional procedure. There is no artificial intelligence in this story, no robot, and no device that so much as hints at machine understanding. Every speaker is a person.

The setting is fixed; build on it rather than replace it.

A walled archive-city maintains the Standard Lexicon, the reference work that settles disputes over contracts, dosages, water shares, and boundaries. Its compilers worked under a rule the city now calls the Termination Rule: every entry must, by way of other entries, bottom out in an entry stamped OSTENSIVE, signifying that whoever compiled it once pointed at the thing itself and the pointing was witnessed. Those stamps were applied three generations ago and have never been rechecked. The city has now ordered an audit. Its stated pretext is frugality — a new edition is to be set in type and there is only so much paper — but its effect is that entries which cannot be re-ostended become candidates for deletion.

Your protagonist is one of two auditors. Her batch includes the entry for "astringent," the drying, puckering effect of an unripe persimmon on the mouth; the Lexicon's own definition routes through "pucker," "harsh," "dry," and "bite," and from there the chain comes back around. The last persimmon tree on the terraces died in her grandmother's time. Her colleague is a nineteen-year-old apprentice who was left at the archive gate as an infant and has never been outside the walls, and who uses every one of these words impeccably — in argument, in a joke, in a draft definition she herself wrote — and has never tasted one.

The institutional apparatus here (the Termination Rule, the OSTENSIVE stamp, the Standard Lexicon) is entirely invented and need not correspond to any real lexicographic practice; likewise all entries quoted in the story are yours to write.

Requirements:

1. Show the loop. Compose at least five Lexicon entries as they would actually appear on the page, with their cross-references and their stamps or absence of stamps, and place them in the narrative so that a reader can trace the chain and watch it close. Make them look like real lexicography: dry, compressed, occasionally and unintentionally touching.

2. Distinguish two kinds of failure. One entry in the batch fails because its referent is simply gone from the world; another fails for a different and worse reason, because nothing of that sort could ever have been pointed at to begin with. Let the reader feel the difference between these without either auditor stating it as a thesis.

3. Include the scene the story exists for: someone who has eaten the fruit tries to convey it to someone who has not. The attempt must fail, and the failure must have a diagnosable shape rather than amounting to a shortage of words. Show what the explainer does once explanation runs out, and let the apprentice's reply be competent, warm, and entirely uninformative as to whether anything has landed.

4. No philosophical vocabulary. Neither narrator nor character may use "reference," "aboutness," "intentionality," "grounding," "signifier," "the map and the territory," or any near-synonym pressed into service as a term of art. "Mean" may appear only in its ordinary social senses. When a character is tempted toward the abstract formulation, interrupt them, or let them find the sentence too embarrassing to finish.

5. Make the auditor a suspect instrument. She ate a persimmon once, at about six, in the years before the last tree died. That memory is the only ostension she has to offer, and by the end she must have some reason to distrust it — and, separately, some reason to notice that her distrust is not the same kind of thing as the apprentice's ignorance.

6. Refuse the easy endings. The fruit may not be found, restored, smuggled, or synthesized; and if anyone tastes anything at all, the tasting must not settle the question the audit raised. Nor may the story close by declaring the whole matter inconsequential. End instead on something the protagonist does to the entry — a stamp, a pen stroke, a page retained or removed — that the reader can read two ways, with both readings earned by what precedes it.

Above all this must work as a story: the audit has to matter to these two people for reasons that are not conceptual, and the reader should be able to say what the younger one wants and what the older one is afraid of. Where any requirement above competes with that, keep the human stakes and satisfy the requirement obliquely.
