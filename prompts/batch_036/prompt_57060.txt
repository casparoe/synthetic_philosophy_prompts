I am preparing a seminar exercise for a public-policy course and need a dialogue between two economists who disagree about how higher education should be financed.

The setting is stipulated and fictional, though it sits close to the Nordic/Anglophone contrast: Middeland has tuition-free public universities, a means-tested maintenance grant for students, roughly a third of school-leavers entering higher education, and a progressive income tax that already recoups a substantial share of the graduate earnings premium. A commission is considering replacing the grant with a larger, universal maintenance loan, repayment contingent on earnings, with a generous write-off point, the savings to be spent on expanding places in oversubscribed fields.

Please write a dialogue of about 800 words between the two commission members who lead the opposing cases — one defending the existing tax-funded model, the other defending the loan proposal. Requirements:

- Neither may be a strawman. Each should open with the strongest version of their case, and each should state plainly the distributional consequence of their own preferred policy that they find least palatable: for the loan defender, something about who bears the risk when repayment is contingent; for the grant defender, something about who is being subsidised at whose expense.
- The exchange must genuinely engage three pivots rather than gesture at them: whether the charge that free tuition is a regressive subsidy to graduates survives the fact that graduates repay through progressive taxation over their working lives; whether an income-contingent loan with a high write-off threshold is anything other than a graduate tax by another name, and what the difference could turn on; and whether the evidence on debt aversion really bears on participation at all, or rather on field choice, duration and completion, and how one might tell these apart.
- Each character should say what would move them, and the dialogue should end without conversion but with a narrowed disagreement: an explicit statement of the parameter or assumption on which the dispute actually turns.
- No citations, no named real economists, no reading list.

After the dialogue, add a short note, clearly marked as outside the fiction, which separates the claims about tuition finance that are broadly settled among those who study it from those that are genuinely contested; flags anywhere a character asserts as fact something that is really a live dispute; and says where the piece takes liberties with the economics for the sake of the drama.
