I convene a monthly reading group in the philosophy of physics. The members can follow talk of probability distributions and have heard the Born rule stated, but none of them has seen a Hilbert space, and none has met the ψ-ontic/ψ-epistemic literature. Next month I have seventy minutes and one goal: that they leave able to classify an interpretation for themselves rather than recite my taxonomy. Don't explain the distinction to me — plan how I should explain it.

Give me the order of introduction and what each step depends on, in particular what must already be in place before the thought that two distinct quantum states could correspond to overlapping ranges of underlying physical situation can mean anything to them, and at which point they, rather than I, should be in a position to produce that criterion themselves.

I want one carrying example, used three times: first to make the question feel substantive rather than verbal, second to state the criterion, and third as the case the Pusey–Barrett–Rudolph argument bites on. It must be a case on which a ψ-ontic and a ψ-epistemic account make identical predictions, and it must work without double slits, cats, or complex numbers. Say what such an example has to have and what it has to lack.

Then map the misconceptions. I expect "epistemic just means it's in the observer's head," "Bohmian mechanics must be epistemic since the particle has a definite position," and "PBR settles it outright." Tell me where in the sequence to head off each and with what. One member is a committed Everettian who will insist the issue is confused because nothing collapses; give me something to do with him at the moment he says it. Close with a single question that would separate someone who has the criterion from someone who has memorized the labels, and with what I should explicitly decline to cover at this level.
