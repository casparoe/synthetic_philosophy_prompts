So, slightly awkward situation and I could use a hand. My county ran one of those civic lotteries this spring — forty of us pulled at random off the voter file to spend three months on a recommendation about where to site a new shelter, with a facilitator and a small stipend — and my name came up. First meeting's in a couple weeks. Meanwhile my book club is doing Shirley Jackson's "The Lottery," and I can already hear two people in the group spend the whole night making the joke that I've been chosen by lot and should watch my back. I'd rather walk in having actually thought about whether the joke lands, because my instinct is that it doesn't land the way they think it does, but that it isn't nothing either.

Here's my problem. I've read enough of the sortition material to know what the story obviously isn't. The case for the lot — in Athens and in the modern proposals — is mostly about power: it breaks the grip of the people who can fund campaigns or stand for office over and over, it makes the body look like the population rather than like the people who wanted to rule, and it takes the choosing out of anybody's hands so nobody can be bribed, threatened, or blamed for the result. Jackson's village lottery has none of that going for it, because what's being distributed isn't an office, it's a killing, and nobody up for it wants it. But when I actually sit with the story, a lot of the details that creep me out are details about procedure, and I can't tell whether I'm projecting my own situation onto it. There's the way Mr. Summers has to make up lists first — heads of families, then heads of households within each family, then members of each household — so the draw happens in stages and the question of who counts as a unit gets settled before anyone pulls a slip. There's the fact that they've quietly swapped the old wood chips for paper slips because the village outgrew them, while nobody will let him replace the shabby black box: reforming the mechanism is fine, reforming what it's for is unthinkable. There's Tessie, who turns up late and jokey and completely at ease with the whole arrangement until it lands on her family, at which point her complaint is that Bill wasn't given time enough to take the slip he wanted — she objects to how the draw was run, not to there being a draw. There's the moment where she tries to drag her grown married daughter into the pool with them even though everyone knows daughters draw with their husband's family, which is the only time anyone in the story questions who's in the pool at all. And then at the end everybody throws a stone, including her small son, and the oldest man in the village says the towns that gave up the lottery are a pack of fools, and no one ever explains what the thing is actually for beyond a proverb about corn and the fact that there's always been one.

So read the story against that debate and be blunt about where the comparison does real work and where its a cheap gotcha I should just refuse. Does Jackson have anything to say about the things people actually worry about with sortition — that nobody voted for the people deciding, so nobody can hold them to account; that the fairness of a draw is something people cheerfully believe in beforehand and instantly repudiate after; that a procedure can be scrupulous in its mechanics and monstrous in its purpose? And what's the force of the fact that everyone in the village is both a possible victim and an actual executioner — is that a point about random selection, or about the diffusion of responsibility that comes from a decision nobody chose? I half suspect the honest answer is that the story isn't about sortition at all, that it's about scapegoating and conformity and the comfort of inherited ritual, and that dragging it into my situation is a category error. If that's what you think, say so plainly and tell me what it is a thought experiment about instead. But I'd also like to know which features of my own panel — the staged draw, the eligibility rules, the fact that we're paid and can't back out without making a scene, the fact that we produce a recommendation that someone else has to act on — the story actually puts pressure on, and which ones it doesn't touch at all. Basically I want something I can bring to the meeting that doesn't make me sound like I'm either cheerleading for my little assembly or trashing it.
