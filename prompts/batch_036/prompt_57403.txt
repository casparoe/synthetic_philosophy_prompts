Two weeks out I'm presenting at our department's ethics workshop and I need a Q&A prep sheet, because I'm going to be the only person in the room on my side of this and I fully expect a mauling.

My thesis: genuine moral dilemmas are real — but the interesting claim isn't the usual one about tragic choices or incommensurable values, and I want to be careful not to sound like I'm just re-running that debate. My claim is narrower. What makes a conflict a *real* dilemma rather than a hard-but-tractable choice is that it leaves behind an inextinguishable residue: duties of apology, compensation, and something like agent-regret that survive doing the best available thing, and that the person you failed has standing to demand from you. Dilemmas are real because the residue is real and owed, not because the normative landscape is incoherent.

Sketch of the argument I'll give, so you can see where the seams are:

- If there's no genuine conflict, then the residual duties have to be either (a) prudential or sentimental add-ons — nice, praiseworthy, but not owed — or (b) rational hedges against the possibility that you miscalculated. (a) collapses, because the wronged party can actually *complain* if you skip the apology, and you can't have standing to demand a merely nice gesture. (b) collapses because the residue is proportioned to the harm you actually caused, not to your antecedent uncertainty about which act was best.
- So both oughts stay live, and agglomeration simply fails in these cases. I'm fine with that, because agglomeration presupposes a single scale to weigh across, and I'll argue the cases I care about are exactly those where the reasons aren't jointly satisfiable *and* there's no common measure — and I'll insist that's a substantive normative fact about the situation, not a report on my ignorance.

The room, about fifteen people. Two Kantians, the senior one of whom thinks ought-implies-can plus the consistency of the moral law settles the whole thing before you've finished your title slide, and who will want to know why my residue isn't just a maxim-conflict wearing a disguise. One committed consequentialist/expected-value person who thinks incommensurability is a measurement artifact and that there's always a determinately best act, so "dilemma" is just the folk word for "hard." Three or four grad students deep in metaethics who will ask whether my view is substantive or terminological — am I actually disagreeing with the people who deny dilemmas, or am I just using the word differently than they are? A legal theorist who works on criminal law doctrine and will point out that necessity and duress already handle these cases without anyone ever having to posit conflicting obligations, and will ask what my residue buys that the existing doctrinal machinery doesn't. Plus one or two people who think the whole tragic framing is a culturally specific hangover and will want to know if my account generalizes.

So: give me the objections roughly in the order they'll land, phrased the way each of those people would actually phrase it, not the way I'd phrase it for them.
