I teach a graduate seminar on the ethics of the far future and am considering setting the following essay question; before I do, I want to know whether it admits of a decisive answer, so please write the essay itself, as a strong model answer would be written.

Sidgwick, and after him Parfit, hold that pure time preference is indefensible: the mere fact that a harm or benefit falls later rather than sooner cannot itself count for or against it, so an impartial evaluator should apply no discount on account of date alone. Against this stands a classical result in intertemporal choice, due to Koopmans: over an unbounded future, any preference ordering that is stationary, continuous, and sensitive to what happens in particular periods must be representable as a discounted sum with a discount factor strictly below one. An undiscounted ordering, on the usual gloss, can be stationary and continuous only at the cost of going blind to finite differences — precisely the finite catastrophes, and the finite centuries of suffering, that the refusal to discount was meant to take seriously.

Does this rehabilitate pure time preference, or is there a way out? Examine the available exits — accepting a positive pure discount; surrendering stationarity in favour of, say, weights that decline with distance without ever becoming stationary; refusing to require a complete ordering over infinite streams at all — and argue which, if any, escapes Parfit's objection. Note that a declining schedule keyed to temporal distance looks like pure time preference under another name; say whether you agree, and whether keying the schedule to something other than date (expected future wellbeing, for instance) changes the situation rather than merely disguising it.

I want a committed answer: a definite verdict on whether the undiscounting position can be saved, and on which assumption must be given up if it cannot, with reasons for that choice. Do not end by recapping the options and leaving me to pick.
