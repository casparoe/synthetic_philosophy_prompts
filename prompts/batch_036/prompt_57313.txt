**Bottlenecked knowledge: why the knowing-how debate is about access, not content**

Ryle's case against the "intellectualist legend" is usually read as a claim about the *kind of thing* knowing-how is: it can't be knowing-that, because if every intelligent act required a prior contemplation of a rule, no one could ever get started. The intellectualist reply — knowing-how is knowing, under a practical mode of presentation, a proposition that answers the relevant how-question — is also a claim about kind of thing: same content, different guise. Anti-intellectualists then produce the chess master, the gymnast, the jazz player, and say: no such proposition is available, so there it isn't.

My claim is that both sides are arguing over the wrong variable. Suppose an agent (i) possesses propositional knowledge which, if deployed, would suffice for a successful F-ing; (ii) can avow that knowledge accurately and without prompting in unhurried conditions; but (iii) systematically fails to deploy it under the actual conditions of performance — clock pressure, load, noise, stress, divided attention. Call this a **bottlenecked** case. Three examples:

- A club chess player who, ten minutes after the game, calmly explains that the position called for cutting off the enemy king, who knows the relevant rook-endgame principles and can state them correctly on a worksheet, and who with 30 seconds left on the clock played the losing move anyway.
- A medical student who recites the sepsis bundle perfectly in tutorial and, in a loud overcrowded ED at 3 a.m., doesn't order a lactate.
- An L2 speaker who scores full marks on a grammar test of article usage and drops articles constantly in fluent conversation.

In bottlenecked cases the content is there and avowable. What's missing is availability at the moment of action. That matters for the dialectic in two directions. First, if the Rylean showcase cases are really bottleneck cases, the regress argument loses its target: the intellectualist never needed the agent to *contemplate* a proposition mid-action, only to possess it — the failure is in the delivery mechanism, not in the content type. Second, the intellectualist can't just fold availability into "practical mode of presentation" and move on, because modes of presentation are supposed to individuate what is known, not how fast it can be retrieved. Two agents can be related to the same proposition under the same practical guise and differ in whether it fires under time pressure; the guise isn't the bottleneck.

If that's right, knowing-how isn't knowledge-that plus a special content type. It's knowledge-that plus reliable availability — which makes it a graded, context-sensitive status rather than a binary one. The same agent can know how to F in the study and not know how to F in the emergency, and that isn't a sloppy way of talking: it's what the state consists in. A prediction both camps should welcome: knowledge-how attributions ought to track reliability under performance conditions, not merely dispositional success rate, and not merely avowal.
