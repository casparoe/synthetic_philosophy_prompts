I sit on a five-person review panel for a small foundation's "foundations of learning" program — three of us do ML, two are philosophers. One proposal this cycle wants three years to build a usable approximation of Solomonoff induction and sell it as a normative benchmark for model selection. The pitch is that once you take the universal prior seriously, Occam's razor stops being a folk heuristic and becomes basically a theorem, and every other simplicity measure (BIC, MDL, whatever) is just a crude knockoff of it.

The panel split. Our statistician says the whole thing is untestable and worse, arbitrary: Kolmogorov complexity is uncomputable and only defined relative to a choice of machine, so the PI gets to decide what counts as simple by picking a reference machine, and anything they actually ship will be some ad hoc heuristic wearing the name. Our philosopher says thats missing the point — it's an idealization like a frictionless plane, and the invariance result means switching machines only shifts description lengths by an additive constant. The PI's rebuttal leaned hard on that: the constant washes out, and anyway they'd only ever use it as a benchmark, never as an algorithm.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before we vote: fund, reject, or fund with conditions, with reasons the PI could actually push back on rather than a score. I want the losing side's best consideration stated at full strength instead of summarized into nothing, and I'd like the memo to say explicitly whether the additive-constant reply does the work the PI thinks it does for a finite dataset.
