I'm prepping a session for a lit-and-ethics seminar and I want to run Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day through Jaspers's fourfold split of guilt — criminal, political, moral, metaphysical. Stevens is the test case that seems to break it: he commits no crime, he makes no political choices that he'd call his own (he sacks the two Jewish maids because Darlington asks him to, and insists throughout he had no opinion on the matter), and still the novel wants you to feel that something unforgivable has happened. So which of Jaspers's boxes does he go in? Does the framework have a slot for him at all, or does he fall thru the gap between the political guilt Jaspers says a whole citizenry bears and the moral guilt he says only attaches to choices you actually own?

I'm most interested in two things: whether Jaspers's idea that moral guilt is the self judging itself does any work on a man who has spent decades refusing to exist as a judge, and the pier scene at the end — is Stevens resolving to practice bantering the start of owning political guilt by bearing its consequences, or a final evasion of the moral kind?

Don't reduce the novel to an illustration. If the reading strains somewhere — the book is about dignity and English class as much as it's about Germany — say so, and tell me what point Ishiguro is making that Jaspers can't make. Film version's welcome too if it sharpens things, tho I think it lets Stevens off easier.
