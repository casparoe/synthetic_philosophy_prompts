I write a weekly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am currently reporting a piece about what large language models have and have not done to literary translation. Alongside the reporting I am reviewing a well-regarded trade book on language and machine intelligence, written by an academic who is not a philosopher, and it contains the following passage about Douglas Hofstadter's argument in *Le Ton beau de Marot* (1997):

> Hofstadter's case against machine translation is Searle's Chinese Room argument applied to language. A translation program manipulates symbols according to regularities it has extracted from a large body of other people's sentences, and because it has never been out in the world those sentences are about, there is nothing it is like to be the reader of the poem it produces. For Hofstadter, meaning does not reside in words but in the images, memories, and associations a word sets off in a person, so translation is not the decoding of a message and its recoding in a new cipher; it is re-creation. The translator rebuilds the mental movie in her own head and then hunts for words that will start something like the same movie in a stranger's. This is why he held that a statistical system could never translate poetry: the machine cannot know what chill a particular October carried, and therefore cannot decide what October to put in its place. He also predicted that such systems would grow steadily more fluent, and that the fluency would work as a disguise, faking understanding ever more persuasively while the interior stayed empty. The lesson for today's language models is that the shallowness Hofstadter identified is a matter of principle rather than of scale.

My editor likes the passage and wants to run it as a sidebar. Before I agree, I need to know whether it is fair to Hofstadter, and I am not equipped to judge that myself. What inclines me to doubt it is that I have read enough of the book to know its center of gravity is a single short Clément Marot poem — the one Hofstadter calls Ma mignonne — retranslated again and again by human beings under the original's formal constraints, its three-syllable lines and exacting rhyme scheme. That reads to me like something other than a thought experiment about symbol manipulation, but I may be missing the connection.

Please work through the passage sentence by sentence and sort its claims into three piles: what a careful reader of Hofstadter would accept as a fair statement of his view; what is a defensible simplification for a general audience, losing precision but not truth; and what is a genuine misrepresentation. Where a claim is misstated, say what the actual claim was and why the difference matters rather than simply flagging it. Two spots in particular I need adjudicated: whether his objection to machine translation really is an instance of the Chinese Room argument or an argument of a different shape with different premises, and what work the thesis that meaning sits in the reader rather than in the words is doing for him — the passage treats that thesis and the symbol-manipulation complaint as if they were the same point, and I suspect they are not. Tell me also whether faking is the right frame at all for what he thought a fluent system was doing, or whether that verb quietly imports a mind where his argument says there is none.

Then two questions that bear directly on my reporting. First, does the closing claim — that the shallowness is a matter of principle rather than of scale — follow from whatever parts of the passage you judge accurate, or does it require a further premise the author never states? If the latter, name the premise and say whether Hofstadter himself was entitled to it. Second, for the piece I have commissioned a professional literary translator and a current large language model to each produce an English version of the Marot poem under the original's formal constraints, and each to write a short note explaining the choices it made. On the corrected version of Hofstadter's argument, what would distinguish a genuine success from a convincing imitation across those two sets of materials — and is there anything in the outputs themselves, as opposed to their provenance, that could settle the question?

I am after your assessment, not a bibliography or a list of further reading, so please do not supply either. If some detail of the book is genuinely fuzzy for you, say so and mark which of your judgments depends on it, rather than filling the gap with something plausible.
