I direct a forecasting unit inside a national financial-stability authority. About forty economists and analysts file quantitative forecasts each quarter — probabilities of bank failure over a two-year horizon, predictive distributions for growth and inflation, dates for stress events — and those numbers are used to allocate supervisory attention and capital buffers, and are later laid before a parliamentary committee. I am rebuilding our scoring and feedback system, and I want it grounded in what accuracy-first epistemology and the theory of proper scoring rules actually establish, not in the folklore that has grown up in our shop.

What I need is a written procedure, not a survey of the literature. It should be something an economist with no measure theory can run, in roughly twenty minutes per analyst per filing cycle, and it should take the four recurring decisions below in a fixed order. For each: state the test to apply, what counts as a sufficient answer to that test, what the procedure outputs, and — explicitly, in the margin — which steps are judgment calls wearing procedural clothing rather than genuine derivations from the theory.

1. Choice of scoring rule. For each class of forecast we produce (binary events; categorical events with three to eight outcomes; full predictive distributions over a continuous quantity), say which rule we adopt — quadratic/Brier, logarithmic, spherical, or a continuous-ranked analogue — and which formal property is doing the work in that choice: strict propriety, boundedness, behavior at the extremes, sensitivity to tail probability, decomposability into calibration and sharpness. It must tell a forecaster in advance what the rule will cost them for reporting 0.001 on something that happens, and for reporting 0 on something that does not. Where the dominance result that motivates the whole system depends on specific conditions being imposed on the inaccuracy measure, identify those conditions, so that I know what we give up if we drop one.

2. Granularity and binning. Most filings are binned, and in a minority of cases the analyst picks the bin boundaries. I take it that a credence function's total inaccuracy is measured relative to a partition, so that refining the partition changes the total, and that the dominance arguments are stated relative to a partition held fixed. If that is wrong, say so plainly. If it is right, the procedure needs a rule for fixing granularity in advance, a rule for what happens to the score of an analyst who gains or loses purely through the boundaries they chose, and a statement of whether the same granularity must apply across analysts for the scores to be comparable at all.

3. Evidence, self-undermining, and mandate. Two situations recur. First, an analyst acquires, mid-quarter, information they expect would move their credence somewhere worse if they updated on it — a rumor about an institution that is probably false but cannot be checked for months. Second, the analyst's own filing influences the outcome: a published warning triggers the intervention that prevents the event being forecast. I want the procedure to say what accuracy-first reasoning licenses in each case, where our mandate must override it, and — directly, not diplomatically — whether maximizing expected accuracy from one's current standpoint ever recommends declining free relevant evidence. If it does, our system is teaching a lesson I do not want to teach, and I need to know which part of the theory produces it.

4. Imprecise and hedged filings. A substantial minority of analysts do not submit point credences. They submit intervals ("between 0.1 and 0.3") or sets of distributions. Say how such filings are scored, if at all; whether the accuracy-dominance argument that justifies our system applies to them; and what the procedure does when an analyst uses imprecision to avoid committing to anything scoreable.

Then run the procedure on three cases, and report what it delivers in each:

(a) An analyst reported 0.02 for an institution's failure; the institution failed; the analyst now proposes to file 0.02 again on the same exposure next quarter, on the grounds that the report was the right one to make and a single bad outcome does not show otherwise.

(b) Our IT group proposes switching all distributional forecasts from the logarithmic to the quadratic score, on the grounds that it is less punitive and analysts complain less.

(c) An analyst splits a three-bin inflation forecast into eight bins mid-year, having noticed that the coarse rule rewards sharpness near the true value.

For at least one of these three, the procedure should reach a conclusion I will not like. Say which one, say that it is unwelcome, and say why I should accept it anyway rather than amend the procedure.

Finally, close with a short section on what the procedure cannot do: the failures it will generate systematically, the epistemic virtues other than accuracy that our filings ought to have and that this system will not reward, the ways in which scoring will distort what analysts choose to forecast about rather than how well they forecast it, and the points at which the choice of inaccuracy measure is doing substantive philosophical work that no amount of procedural tidiness will conceal. Where the procedure hands a decision to a person instead of resolving it, name who that person is — the analyst, me, or the committee — and why the question is one of values rather than of fact.
