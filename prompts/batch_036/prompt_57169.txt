My father died in October. For as long as I can remember he made the household watch It's a Wonderful Life on Christmas Eve, and our local historical society has asked me to write a short piece on the film for their winter journal. The sequence I can no longer watch without difficulty is also the one I trust least: Clarence's world, where George was never born and Bedford Falls has become Pottersville. I would like a reading of the film through the debate about counterfactual history and inevitability, treating the sequence as a real counterfactual argument rather than a sentimental device.

The question I keep circling is whether the sequence is entitled to its conclusion that one man was load-bearing for an entire town. It holds Potter's appetites, the run on the bank, and Harry's war service fixed while deleting a single person from a densely connected system, and I suspect that is exactly the ceteris paribus assumption that makes historical counterfactuals suspect. Please bring in Lewis's account of counterfactuals, on which the nearest world where the antecedent holds either requires a small miracle with the laws kept fixed or a wholesale change in the laws, and tell me which of these Clarence's world is, and whether a "never born" antecedent can count as a minimal rewrite at all. Set that against the two familiar poles of the inevitability debate: Pascal's remark about Cleopatra's nose, and Tolstoy's argument in War and Peace that great men are labels we attach to events whose causes lie in the swarm of individual wills. Is Capra a Pascalian or a Tolstoyan? And is the town's ruin in the counterfactual over-determined — would Potter have swallowed Bedford Falls regardless, so that the sequence proves nothing about George?

Where the reading strains, say so plainly; I do not want the film flattened into an illustration of a position. I would like to end with something the film contributes to the argument about inevitability that argument alone cannot supply. You will not be able to screen it, so describe the scenes you rely on rather than quoting dialogue, and please write in continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write a letter.
