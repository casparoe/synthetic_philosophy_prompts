I've spent about two years volunteering at a member-run community woodshop that meets in the basement of a church near where I live, and there is a practice there that has been quietly driving me out of my mind. The shop owns exactly one lathe — a heavy donated machine from the seventies that everyone wants — and the way you claim it is by setting one of your own projects on the shelf right beside it: a bowl blank, a table leg, whatever. As long as something of yours is up there, the lathe is understood to be yours for the day. Nobody ever voted on this. I have asked several long-time members how it started and not one of them knows; the man who founded the shop moved to another state years ago. As best anyone can reconstruct it, somebody once left a project on that shelf overnight so he wouldn't lose his place, other people copied him, and it hardened into a rule that no one now thinks of as a rule.

My difficulty is that it seems to me like a bad rule, and privately everyone agrees. Because the shelf holds one object, whoever walks in first in the morning can hold the lathe until closing time, and there is a small group of us — I work rotating shifts at a warehouse, so I can only get to the shop on weekday evenings — who have essentially never used it. When I suggested at the monthly meeting that we replace the shelf with a sign-up sheet for ninety-minute slots, the reaction was strange. Two of the older members said some version of "that's just how we do things here," and one asked who had appointed me to change anything. Several people who had complained to me about the shelf the week before said nothing at all in the room. What I cannot get a handle on is the tone: they spoke as though my proposal were not merely impractical but somehow improper, as though the shelf had a claim on us that a better arrangement would not. I cannot see what that claim could be. The shelf is arbitrary — you could have picked any marker you liked, a chalk line on the bench, a hat on a hook — and it would have done exactly the same job. And being first in the door is not a virtue; it is just a fact about traffic.

Things got worse last month. I ignored the shelf once and used the lathe while a project sat up there; the owner was not even in the building. I came back to a note on my bench and several weeks of cold shoulders, and I have been trying ever since to work out whether I did anything actually wrong or was only punished for it. Since then I have put up a sign-up sheet of my own — currently on the back of a flyer for the church pancake breakfast — and I write my name on it every time I come in. Almost nobody else does. A friend of mine who also wants the lathe says that is precisely what she expected, and she gave me an argument I have not been able to answer: everyone would prefer the sheet if everyone else used it, but no single person gains anything by switching while even one other member still respects the shelf, so the sheet stays blank no matter how many people tell me privately that they would rather use it. She thinks the only way anything changes is if enough people stop caring about being sanctioned all at once, and she says that will never happen here. Is she right about that, and if so what should I actually be doing — keep using my sheet and hope, go back to the shelf and argue from the inside, or concede that I was in the wrong and drop the whole thing? I would also genuinely like to understand the older members better, because "tradition" does not feel to me like a reason, and yet they clearly think there is one, and I want to know whether they are confused or whether I am.
