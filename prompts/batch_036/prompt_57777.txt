I'm a software engineer, and eight months ago I was asked to join the five-person grants panel of a small private fund that gives to global health and poverty programs. I'm the only member with no background in economics or public health; I was added because a growing share of the proposals are about data systems. The chair is a former colleague of mine. For the coming round the panel has asked me to draft the recommendation memo, and the instruction I was given is that it should be consistent with what we have already done. I have gone back through six years of minutes. Here is the record, with the reasons recorded at the time and the vote.

1. Funded, 5–0: a bed-net distribution program in an East African country, backed by two randomized trials, with a cost per case averted the panel could check independently. The minutes note that other donors were not covering the demonstrated need.

2. Rejected, 5–0: an unconditional cash transfer program in the same country, also trial-backed, with a cost-per-outcome figure the minutes describe as at least as good as item 1. Three larger donors had committed to scaling it, and the recorded reason is that our money would have gone in regardless.

3. Funded, 3–2: a grant covering living and training costs for someone moving into a high-paying trading job, against a written pledge to give away half of all income above a fixed threshold, directed to the charities the panel already rated highest. Two members objected that this was one more step removed from the people we are trying to reach. The chair's recorded answer was that the expected donations were large and computable and the destinations were already vetted.

4. Rejected, 5–0: a school-based treatment program whose original trial had been followed by two replications finding much smaller effects. The minutes are terse: we no longer have the number we thought we had.

5. Rejected, 5–0: a campaign for a national ballot measure that would have funded programs of the kind in items 1 and 2 at roughly a hundred times our annual budget. The applicant supplied a political scientist's model of passage probability and a cost-effectiveness estimate that beat everything the panel had ever funded. One member wrote that the numbers were borrowed from a model of a different country's elections. Another wrote, separately, that the panel should not be in the business of funding politics. Both reasons appear in the file, and the minutes never say which one carried the vote.

6. Funded, 4–1: a two-year grant to an established evaluation shop to build a shared, open-source reporting tool for clinics the panel already funds. There is no cost-effectiveness estimate in the file, only a budget. The dissenting member's note is that nothing about it touches a patient.

The proposal now in front of us: a small organization in a South Asian country, not rated by any of the third-party evaluators whose rankings the panel usually consults, proposes to build and run the routine health information system for one province — facility reporting, supply-chain tracking, and an independent audit layer. The technical design is unambitious and I could assess it myself. A completed randomized evaluation of the same design in two other provinces is published, and its cost per life-year is better than item 1. No other donor has offered to fund it, and the applicant states that the province has asked for it repeatedly. But the system produces nothing unless the provincial ministry adopts it and keeps staffing it, and adoption in the third province stalled for two years after a change of minister.

What I need from you:

- How do you expect the chair to vote, and with what confidence? The chair voted with the majority in all six cases.
- Lay out the candidate rules that would account for all six outcomes at once. I can think of: fund only if we can put a defensible number on it; fund only if our money is what makes the difference; fund only if success doesn't depend on politicians or powerful institutions choosing to cooperate; fund only if we ourselves are competent to evaluate it; fund only if a trusted outside evaluator already vouches for the organization. I suspect some of these are refuted by the record, that others can't be told apart by it, and that at least one pair I haven't thought of does the real work.
- For each rule that survives all six, say what it implies about the new proposal, and identify which rule you think is most likely driving the chair and why.
- Which of the six is the closest analog to the new proposal, and at what point does the analogy break down?
- If we fund it and if we decline it, what general commitment would we be taking on that we have not taken on before?
- Describe one further hypothetical proposal that would most cleanly separate the two rules you think are genuinely in play.

I should say what is worrying me, so you can judge whether it is a fact about the record or a bias of mine. I have read that funders like ours drift toward whatever is easy to count, and that this quietly starves the slow, political, hard-to-measure work that changes the most. I don't know whether our six cases show that pattern, or whether some other rule explains them just as well, and I would rather not write the memo out of a suspicion I can't defend.
