I'm a backend engineer at a company that is rolling out an internal prediction market for product decisions — release dates, feature adoption, quarterly incident counts. I have no formal training in philosophy or economics; I've picked this up by reading around the subject while arguing with colleagues. I've drafted a blog post explaining why I'm sceptical that the market will deliver what its champions promise, and I'd like a critical read before I circulate it internally. Paragraph numbers are for reference.

[1] The standard defence of markets is epistemic. On this view a price is not a valuation but a computation: it aggregates information dispersed across people who will never meet and who have no particular incentive to tell one another what they know. Prediction markets are supposed to inherit this property, which is why they are said to beat committees and expert forecasts.

[2] There is a result that seems to me to cut directly against this. Milgrom and Stokey showed in 1982 that under common priors and common knowledge of rationality, and absent noise traders, speculative trade cannot occur. The reasoning is short. Suppose you offer to buy from me at the standing price. I know you are rational and I know you share my prior, so your willingness to buy tells me you must know something I don't. But then I know that too — I know that you know it — and the price ought already to reflect it. I decline. You, anticipating my decline, never make the offer. Information that could have been transmitted is suppressed by the very act of proposing to transmit it.

[3] The same structure appears in Aumann's 1976 result that agents with a common prior cannot agree to disagree once their posteriors are common knowledge. If disagreement is impossible in the limit, and if the market's job is to reconcile disagreement into a single number, then the market is solving a problem rational agents don't have. Or, what comes to the same thing: observed disagreement is evidence that the agents are not rational or do not share priors, in which case the price is not a Bayesian computation at all.

[4] Grossman and Stiglitz press from the other direction with their 1980 argument that markets cannot be perfectly informationally efficient. If prices fully reflected all available information there would be no return to spending resources acquiring it, so nobody would acquire any, so prices would not reflect it. A market that functions must therefore be one in which prices are partly wrong, with the degree of wrongness pinned down by the cost of information. Efficiency is a limit the system cannot reach without destroying its own input.

[5] My own contribution comes from distributed systems. In a consensus protocol a message is worth attending to only if the sender paid something to send it, because a costless message from an agent you already believe to be rational and aligned with you carries no signal; proof of work exists to make speaking expensive. I want to say the bid-ask spread plays exactly that role in a market: it is the fee a trader pays for the right to speak, and the fee is what makes the utterance credible. Where the spread is zero the market is running an unauthenticated protocol and should be expected to flood with noise. Where it is wide, listening is expensive, but at least the market knows somebody is talking.

[6] This yields a design claim for our internal market. If trading is free, then either nobody trades, or those who do trade are telling us something we did not want to hear — namely that they disagree with the rest of us about fundamentals, or about how to read public information. A free internal prediction market is not a truth-aggregator but a disagreement detector, and should be read as an alarm rather than an oracle. To make it an oracle you would have to price participation at roughly the value of the information the participant holds, which is a quantity we cannot know in advance. That is, I think, Grossman and Stiglitz restated as a systems design problem.
