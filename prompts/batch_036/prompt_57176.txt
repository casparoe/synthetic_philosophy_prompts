I TA a second-year early modern survey — 22 students, one 100-minute block on Thursdays, a mix of philosophy majors and people filling a humanities requirement, so maybe a third of them only skimmed the reading. We hit Leibniz in week 6 and every single year the same thing happens: they read that this is the best of all possible worlds, somebody brings up Voltaire and the Lisbon earthquake, and the room settles into either "Leibniz was a monster" or "Leibniz was just doing a bit." Neither is a useful place to spend two hours. What I want is for them to feel the actual constraint — that if you have to pick *one* world out of options that are each internally consistent, you cannot buy a local improvement without paying for it somewhere else, and that at some point the thesis stops sounding complacent and starts sounding forced. So: design me a card game they play in groups, where each group is a world-builder and the game is rigged so the highest-scoring worlds are the ones containing the most collateral damage.

Here's the half-sketch, please make it actually work and repair what's broken. Each card is a substance: a one-line law plus two or three downstream consequences it generates, some of those consequences tagged as evils (a famine, a species that has to kill to survive, a talent that goes unused, a war that teaches a people something). Cards can only go in the same world if they're compossable — I want a conflict graph printed right on the cards as little symbols, so certain combinations are impossible rather than merely undesirable. Scoring should reward exactly the two things Leibniz says God weighs, richness of effects and simplicity of the underlying rules, so the winning worlds are the ones with very few law-families doing an enormous amount of work. Give me the full card set (or at least ~40 cards' worth of actual content, I'm printing them at home), the conflict pairs, the scoring sheet, group size, turn structure, and a minute-by-minute for the 100 minutes with a debrief of about 25 at the end. I've got a printer and a photocopier allowance and that's it, so no apps, no props, no real money.

Two things I care about most. First, it must not turn into a politics seminar. Last year I tried an open "design your utopia" brainstorm and within ten minutes 22 people were arguing about universal basic income and inheritance tax and Leibniz had completely left the building. The mechanics have to bite: there need to be moments where a group desperately wants to remove an evil and physically can't, or can only do it by throwing out a whole law-family and tanking its richness score, and they should notice that they've started *defending* the evils they drew. Second, the debrief should map moments onto the doctrine explicitly — which moment is compossibility, which is the claim that a creature is by definition limited so a world with no metaphysical evil would be a world with no creatures in it, which is the gap between willing an evil and permitting it, and which is the worry that there may be no maximum at all, just an endless series of better worlds, which is roughly where Voltaire lands. Be straight with me about what the game cannot reproduce: our worlds are finite card sets and Leibniz's God chooses among infinitely many with full knowledge of every consequence, which is doing most of the work for the optimism. Tell me what I have to supply by hand there instead of pretending the simulation covers it. And tell me what predictably breaks the first time I run it — I'm guessing scorekeeping, and groups trading cards they aren't supposed to be able to trade — plus how to head it off.
