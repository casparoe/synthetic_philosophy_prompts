I cover science and ideas for a monthly magazine and am currently reporting a piece on the foundations of machine learning: roughly 2,200 words, filed in ten days, with the usual sidebar on how predictions get made in practice. One of my sources, a theorist who works on learning algorithms and whose work I have cited approvingly in earlier pieces, took me through an argument after we stopped recording, then agreed to go on the record with it. He claims it shows that the epistemic confidence of evidence-based reporting rests on nothing. I have reconstructed the chain as faithfully as I can from my notes:

1. There is a family of results, originating with David Wolpert and extended with William Macready, known as the no-free-lunch theorems. In their machine-learning form they show that if you average over all possible target functions, the expected error of any learning algorithm on inputs outside its training set is the same as that of any other algorithm, blind guessing included. No algorithm is a priori better than any other.
2. For any finite body of data, the class of target functions compatible with that data is exactly as large as the class of all target functions; the data therefore excludes nothing beyond itself. Goodman's grue is the folkloric version of the point.
3. Consequently, under the only averaging procedure that does not already presuppose a favored class of hypotheses — the uniform average over all functions — every hypothesis that fits the evidence stands on precisely the same footing as every other.
4. Any preference for one such hypothesis over its rivals must therefore come from an inductive bias: an antecedent weighting declaring smooth, simple, or computable functions more likely.
5. That weighting cannot be justified empirically without circularity, because every empirical justification of it proceeds by the very inference it licenses. This is Hume's point, and I do not see a way around it.
6. Nor can it be justified a priori: any particular prior is arbitrary relative to the uniform average, and the uniform average is the unique assumption-free starting point, so every alternative smuggles in a favorite.
7. It follows that no finite evidence confers more support on one generalization than on an incompatible rival that matches it everywhere the evidence reaches. Support is stipulation wearing a lab coat.
8. Therefore, where an established theory and an arbitrary rival agree on everything anyone has measured, there are no evidential grounds for preferring the established one; and, as a matter of practice, the settled consensus of a field carries no more epistemic weight than a dissenting minority that accommodates the same data, however that minority arrived at it.

He then used (8) to argue that journals should treat pre-registered replications and speculative but unconstrained claims symmetrically, which is the news hook my editor wants. My difficulty is that the chain appears to prove far too much — it seems to license equal column inches for anything — yet I cannot locate the defective step, and I am unwilling to publish a diagnosis I could not defend against a letter from a philosopher.

Please begin with two or three sentences stating plainly which step or steps the argument turns on and where it breaks, if it breaks, and whether what survives is a genuine open problem or a piece of misdirection. Two constraints on your diagnosis: I am most attached to (5), because I think Hume's circularity point is honest and I would rather keep it than buy my way out of it, and I am least willing to deny (1), since it is a theorem I have reported before and would have to correct earlier copy if I called it mistaken. If either has to go anyway, say so explicitly and tell me what I would be conceding.

Then give me a table, one row per numbered step, with exactly these columns: step (the number); claim (the premise or inference restated in one plain sentence, no jargon); hidden_assumption (what the step requires to be true that the argument never states — write none only if you genuinely mean it); verdict (one of accept, accept-with-qualification, reject); cost_of_rejecting (what the argument loses, or what its proponent must then explain, if this step is given up — and for steps you accept, what accepting them commits us to); best_position (which standing response to the problem of induction — Hume's own conclusion, Carnap-style prior-based Bayesianism, Solomonoff-style universal priors over computable hypotheses, Popperian falsificationism, a pragmatist or Wittgensteinian dissolution of the question, or the NFL authors' own account of what their theorem does and does not show — is best placed to absorb or reject this step, plus one clause saying why).

Finish with two or three sentences I could realistically paraphrase in print: what a careful scientist is entitled to claim about why their models beat blind guessing, stated without overclaiming.
