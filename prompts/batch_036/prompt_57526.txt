I have to give the main talk at a memorial service in three weeks for a colleague who died in August at forty-one — a bicycle accident, no warning, nothing to prepare anyone. He was an atheist in the unapologetic sense: he had no wish to be survived by anything, and his partner has asked me specifically not to smuggle in an afterlife, not even as a metaphor. What I cannot settle is which thing to say about whether his death was bad for him. I have been a philosopher long enough to know the literature and not long enough to have ever had to use it on someone I loved, and the arguments have stopped behaving for me: they were built as objections to one another, not as consolations, and I now need to know which of them survives being said out loud to a room of people who miss him, including his daughter, who is nine and will be sitting in the second row.

Here is what I would like you to do. Take the following positions on whether death harms the person who dies and rank them, in a single ordering from first to last, by how much of the grief in that room each one could honestly carry, on the assumption that there is no afterlife and no consolation I do not believe. By honestly I mean that the position must acknowledge the loss rather than explain it away; by carry I mean that a person hearing it would feel less bereaved afterward, and not merely corrected. The positions are: the Epicurean claim that nothing can be good or bad for someone who cannot experience it, so that death is nothing to the one who dies; Lucretius's mirror-image addition, that the eternity before our birth was nothing to us and so the eternity after our death should be nothing to us either; Nagel's deprivation reply, that death is an evil because it removes goods the person would otherwise have had, together with his answer to the mirror-image — that the time before birth is a time in which he could not have existed, whereas the time after death is one in which he might have; the timing problem, that if death harms, it is unclear at what time it does so, and with it the response that it harms at no particular time, the harm lying in a comparison between whole lives rather than between moments; Williams's account of categorical desires, that what makes going on living worth anything are projects reaching beyond any particular satisfaction, so that death is a misfortune insofar as it severs them, while Williams himself refuses the further step of thinking endless life desirable; the narrative view, that a life's value is not the sum of its moments, so that a life ended at forty-one is not forty-one years of good with the remainder missing but a differently shaped story, and the badness is in the shape; Parfit's discussion of our bias toward the future, the fact that we care enormously about a pain still to come and very little about an equal pain already past, together with the suggestion that this bias is not something reason demands of us; and finally a position that is not in any book, put to me by his brother at a dinner last month — that what death takes is the possibility of revision, that afterward a person can no longer answer back, correct the record, or reinterpret himself by what he does next, so that every misreading of him becomes permanent.

Tell me where the mirror-image argument lands and why it lands there. I have been reading Lucretius at night for the past week and sleeping better for it, and I want to know whether that comfort is something I should distrust. Then commit: say which position you would build the talk around, state what the talk's central claim would be, and if you think two of them should be combined, say which two and show me that the combination does not collapse into contradiction. Be willing to place a view you find philosophically strongest in a low position if it cannot be said aloud to grieving people, and say so plainly. I do not want a survey with the decision left to me; I would rather have a definite recommendation I can argue with. I also do not want citations, a bibliography, or a reading list — I want your judgment, not the apparatus behind it.

Please write to me the way one writes a letter: continuous prose only, with no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables. Give the ranking in the order you propose, one paragraph per position, with the reason for its place stated inside the paragraph rather than beside it.
