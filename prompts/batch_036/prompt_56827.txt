Was home over the break and read a back issue of a history magazine at my parents' place — there was a piece on why dueling and footbinding disappeared, and it summarized the main book it was riffing on like this:

> Appiah's claim is that moral revolutions happen when honor changes hands. People had been writing pamphlets against dueling for the better part of two centuries, and against the Atlantic slave trade for decades, and none of it worked; what actually ended these practices was that they stopped being honorable. The lesson is that moral argument is basically powerless in politics — you can win every debate and change nothing, which is why Appiah thinks philosophers overrate it. His word "honor" just means reputation: the estimate your peers have of you, plus the shame you feel when it drops. That's also how the book settles an old fight. The slave trade didn't end because it stopped being profitable, it ended because holding slaves stopped being something a British gentleman could do in front of his equals, so the economists who explain abolition by changing interests are simply wrong. The upshot, which Appiah draws himself, is that reformers should give up on persuading anyone and start engineering status instead: attach shame to the practice you dislike and it will go. He does admit this only works in aristocratic societies like Georgian England or Qing China, since modern people care about dignity, which everyone has equally and so can't be weaponized the way honor can.

I half-liked this because I'd always found the "it was really just economics" version of abolition kind of smug, so I was happy to see it knocked down. But the whole thing reads like a book-length argument arguing that arguments don't work, which struck me as odd, and I also remember the review mentioning that one of his four cases is honor killings in Pakistan today, which doesn't sit well with the last sentence.

Can you go through it claim by claim and tell me what to trust? Specifically: which parts would Appiah happily sign, which are defensible simplifications of something more careful, and which are just wrong. I'm most interested in (a) what he actually thinks the role of moral argument was, if not the engine, (b) whether "honor = reputation plus shame" is right or whether he's distinguishing honor from something nearby, and (c) whether either of the two big conclusions — that the economic-interest account of abolition is refuted, and that reformers should switch from persuasion to status-engineering — survives once the summary is fixed. And then tell me what I'm left believing about why slavery and dueling ended once the article is corrected, since that was the thing I actually wanted to know going in.
