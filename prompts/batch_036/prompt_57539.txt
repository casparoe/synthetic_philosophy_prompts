I am preparing a submission in response to a consultation on whether commercial surrogacy should remain a criminal offence in my jurisdiction, and I would like help auditing an analogy that is doing a great deal of work in the debate here. I think it is a descendant of Devlin's side of the Hart–Devlin exchange, though it has been recast in structural-mechanical terms that Devlin himself never used.

The setting, briefly: the proposal has the same shape as the mid-century British debate that followed the Wolfenden Report of 1957. The conduct is adult and consensual, the immediate participants report no injury, and the case for prohibition rests largely on the claim that the practice expresses and entrenchs an attitude — toward children, gestation, and money — that the community has reason not to endorse, whatever the balance of harms in any individual transaction.

The analogy. A colleague on the other side of the consultation (a practising barrister, not an academic) puts it roughly like this, and I have made it more explicit and more mechanical than he did:

> A society's shared morality is not the bricks of the building but the mortar between them. Bricks can be replaced one at a time — practices change, individual prohibitions are repealed — and the structure stands. The mortar is a single continuous substance: you cannot replace it while the wall is up, and the builder who rakes out a course to discover whether it was doing any work learns the answer only once the wall has already moved. There is no control building next door, no way to run the experiment safely, and a wall that has come down cannot be rebuilt to the same specification. So however archaic a surviving prohibition looks, the presumption lies with leaving it in place.

He also gestures at the thought, familiar from Devlin, that the person who flouts the sexual morality of the community is a subversive rather than a mere sinner; and I have been answering him with what I take to be Hart's standard line — that Devlin runs together the existence of a society with its identity, since a society can change its constitutive morality and still be the society it was.

What I want from you:

1. **The correspondences.** For the mortar image, say what in the target plays each role: mortar, bricks, the building, the builder, the load-bearing question, the absent control building, the impossibility of rebuilding to the same specification. Where the image has no honest candidate in the target, say so rather than supplying a strained one — a table is fine.

2. **Disanalogies that matter versus disanalogies that are merely untidy.** A disanalogy counts for my purposes only if it bears on the inference from the comparison to the conclusion, which is a presumption in favour of retention. I expect some failures to be decorative (mortar is homogeneous and continuous, a moral code is neither, so what?) and others to do real damage. Please sort them.

3. **What the source quietly imports.** In particular: the unity of the moral code — one continuous substance rather than many independent joints with redundant load paths and known substitutes; the globality and suddenness of failure, as against change that is local, gradual and reversible; the impossibility of repair, when societies demonstrably do rebuild altered norms; and the total ignorance of the builder, when in fact a good deal about how norms respond to decriminalization is learnable in advance from other jurisdictions. Are these imported by the analogy, or argued for independently of it?

4. **Whether the conclusion survives.** Once the disanalogies are on the table, does anything remain that supports a presumption for retention — or only something weaker, such as a requirement of gradualism, of pilot jurisdictions, of monitoring with reversal mechanisms, or of compensating institutions? State what the analogy can bear at its maximum and what it cannot, and mark which of your claims depend on contested empirical matters about how norms actually change rather than on the logic of the comparison.

5. **The Hart point.** Does the existence-versus-identity objection actually land on the mortar version, or is the mortar image built precisely to evade it — the claim being not that society would cease to exist but that it would cease to be the society whose stability the law was protecting? If it evades the objection, identify the further premise the evasion requires and say whether that premise is defensible.

6. **The treason comparison.** Is the subversive-or-sinner thought doing the same work as the mortar image or a different one? My sense is that one concerns the agent's standing in relation to the community and the other the fragility of a structure, and that the two get run together illegitimately — the structural claim supplying the urgency that the standing claim cannot. Test that hunch rather than accepting it.

7. **A better analogy.** If either side can be given a comparison that carries its intended weight with less smuggling, offer it, say what it licenses, and say where it too gives out. If you think no analogy in this neighbourhood can do the work, say so and explain what kind of argument would have to replace it.

8. **My own image, audited on the same terms.** I have been countering that a morals law of this kind is like a splint left on a bone that has already healed: it did real work once, and left in place it stiffens the limb and wastes the muscle. I suspect this imports a determinate endpoint of healing that moral change does not have, and that it flatters the reformer by casting him as the physician. Give it a short paragraph of the same treatment.

On register: I want a committed assessment, not a survey. Where you think the analogy fails, say it fails, and say how confident you are and what the confidence rests on; where you think my colleague has a point that survives the audit, say that too and mark it clearly as surviving. Distinguish what you regard as settled from what is genuinely arguable, and flag any place where you suspect you are working from a popular simplification of Devlin or Hart rather than from their actual positions — I would rather be told that than have the simplification silently load the analysis. No references or reading list, please; I want the reasoning.

Finally, if you think the request itself is misconceived, say so at the top instead of complying politely. Three specific worries I have about it: I have reconstructed a loose remark into an explicit mechanical argument my colleague never made, and the reconstruction may be a strawman; the analogy may be doing no inferential work at all in his case, so that auditing it as an argument is a category mistake — he may simply be expressing a disposition, and the image is decoration; and the Hart–Devlin frame may be the wrong lens for commercial surrogacy, which is at least as much about the commodification of women's reproductive labour and the position of the child as about a shared morality whose loss would be structural. If any of these is right, say which, say why, and tell me what I ought to be asking instead.
