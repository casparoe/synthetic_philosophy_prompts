Last month I sat in on the open session of a reading group devoted to the methodology of evolutionary explanation, and two of its regulars — I'll call them H and W — got into a sustained argument about the human philtrum that I was unable to referee. The philtrum, for context, is the vertical groove between the base of the nose and the upper lip, flanked by its two ridges. Here is the exchange as nearly move by move as I can reconstruct it.

(1) H opened by claiming the philtrum is an adaptation. His evidence: it is universal and species-typical; it is a precisely patterned structure rather than a blur — two ridges, a consistent depth, and measurable variation that tracks developmental insult, since a smoothed-out philtrum is one of the recognized facial signs of fetal alcohol exposure; and it is homologous to a structure that plainly does work elsewhere, because in most other mammals the groove carries dissolved odorants from the moist nose pad toward the vomeronasal organ. Universality, complexity, functional fit, homology — four of the standard marks, he said.

(2) W replied that the first two marks prove nothing, because any unavoidable byproduct of a necessary developmental process will also be universal and also be precisely patterned. The philtrum sits exactly where the medial nasal and maxillary processes of the embryonic face meet and fuse, and when that fusion fails you get a cleft lip. So the groove is a seam — the visible trace of how a mammalian face gets assembled — and seams are not designed. She said everything on H's list is equally true of the navel.

(3) H answered that W had described a proximate mechanism and then announced that she had settled a historical question. Every trait, including every uncontested adaptation, is produced by some developmental process; showing that the groove arises at a fusion line says nothing about whether natural selection shaped that fusion pattern, its timing, or the surface geometry it produces. The live question, he said, is whether selection molded the architecture, and W had not touched it.

(4) W said that move makes adaptationism unfalsifiable, which is just the charge the spandrels paper presses. If any byproduct can be immunized by asserting that selection must have shaped the developmental architecture responsible for it, then no possible observation counts against an adaptationist story. She offered what she took to be a testable claim instead: the geometry of the groove should be predictable from the mechanics of facial fusion alone, with no reference to odor transport; and the human face lacks the equipment the groove would need in any case, since there is no moist rhinarium and no working vomeronasal duct in adults. Nothing to do it with, and nothing for it to do.

(5) H said the missing equipment is a claim about present-day human anatomy, whereas he was making a claim about descent. The ancestral therian condition was a functioning rhinarium-and-groove system, and strepsirrhines still have it. Parsimony, he argued, favors reading the human philtrum as a retained component of that system rather than as an independent construction byproduct, and he accused W of ignoring phylogeny altogether.

(6) W said homology establishes common ancestry of a body region, not common function, and that letting function travel down a pedigree by default is precisely the Panglossian habit: because a structure is present and could be useful, assume it was built for that use.

(7) H then shifted ground, and this is the move I found hardest to evaluate. He said W had been calling the philtrum a spandrel all evening and that the label was doing unearned work. On the architectural analogy, a spandrel is the tapering triangular space that the geometry of adjacent arches and the dome above them forces into existence; the whole point of the image is that something you might mistake for a designed panel is a byproduct of the design of the structures around it. The philtrum, he said, is nothing of the kind — it is a shrunken remnant of an organ system humans lost, which is a different category with different evidential standards — and by collapsing the two W was using "spandrel" as an all-purpose synonym for "not an adaptation." W called this a lawyer's trick: a seam is a seam, and whether the groove is a leftover of a lost smelling apparatus or a leftover of a facial fusion, it remains a trait whose presence is explained by something other than its current contribution to fitness.

The group ran out of time before either conceded anything. What I would like is a patient adjudication. Please work through moves (1) to (7) in order and say for each which side is right, partly right, or wrong, and why — and only then give an overall verdict on who was closer to the truth. I am particularly interested in three things: whether H's objection in (3) genuinely rescues adaptationist reasoning from the falsifiability charge or merely restates it; whether W's appeal to missing anatomy in (4) is legitimate evidence about the trait's status or a change of subject, given what H said in (5); and whether H's terminological point in (7) carries real methodological force or is a way of giving up the trait while winning the argument. Please also tell me what neither of them said that should have been said — including whether the philtrum belongs in a category that neither "adaptation" nor "spandrel" captures, and what evidence, if any, could actually discriminate between their two stories rather than merely being consistent with both.

Finally, I would like you to be explicit about your own confidence: state how sure you are of each verdict you render, and single out the one point in your answer where you think you are most likely to be wrong, explaining what would have to be true for you to be mistaken there.
