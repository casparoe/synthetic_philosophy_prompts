Write a dialogue between two philosophers serving on a hospital committee that must rule on a draft patient decision aid. One holds that manipulation is a genuine kind of wrong: influence that operates by bypassing or exploiting the addressee's capacities for rational evaluation, and that can be manipulative even when every claim the influencer makes is true. The other denies that this picks out a kind at all, on the grounds that ordinary rational persuasion is never free of framing, salience, tone, and emotional engagement, so that a bright line either sweeps in nearly all communication or fails to track anything of normative significance.

The aid reports surgical outcomes as a 90 percent survival rate rather than a 10 percent mortality rate, and closes with a two-minute video of a recovered patient saying how glad she is that she had the operation.

Neither philosopher may caricature the other, and each must answer the other's actual moves: the first has to say how bypassing is to be cashed out without making every non-propositional feature of a message a bypass, and the second has to say what her position can still offer by way of explaining why some influence seems worse than the rest. Do not let them settle for agreeing to disagree — end somewhere definite, whether a split verdict on the two components of the aid or a shared verdict reached for incompatible reasons.
