In reading the arguments for and against the moral equality of combatants, I keep meeting a particular way that the arguments go, and I have no term for it. I would like to know whether one exists.

Here is the pattern. Someone advances a claim about the first-order moral truth of war — say, that soldiers fighting for the state that began the war do nothing permissible when they kill, because the people they kill are not threats in the sense that would make killing them defensive. The response they receive is not about whether the claim is true. It is about what would happen if people believed it: that soldiers on the just side, once told their opponents have no right to fight, will conclude those opponents may be killed on any terms; or that the whole practice of surrender, of taking prisoners, and of accepting defeat depends on treating both sides' fighters as equally permitted to fight. And then the author of the original claim replies — not by objecting that these are questions about the effects of belief rather than about the truth, but by arguing the empirical point the other way: a soldier who believed he had no right to kill except in immediate defense would be more restrained, not less. The exchange continues on that footing, and everyone treats it as a substantive disagreement about the original question.

A second instance, which I take to be the same thing in different dress. These disputants are, at the first order, non-consequentialists: they hold that the permissibility of killing turns on liability rather than on outcomes. Yet when the question shifts to whether a principle should be taught to soldiers or written into the law of armed conflict, considerations of institutional stability are allowed to settle what they elsewhere insist morality settles. The asymmetry is stable across the debate: consequences-of-acceptance count against a view when the view is unwelcome, and are met with better consequences-of-acceptance when the view is one's own, rather than being ruled inadmissible on both occasions.

What I want to know is the following.

First, does this have an established name? I am aware of several near neighbours and I would like to be told precisely how what I have described differs from each: self-effacing theories, and the thought that a principle can be true yet harmful to act on directly; esoteric morality and the publicity condition on moral principles; the distinction between the deep morality of war and the law of war, which at least announces that two levels are in play; indirect and motive-oriented varieties of consequentialism; and the ordinary fallacy of appealing to consequences, which I take to be a different thing, since the consequences cited here are consequences of believing a claim rather than of its being true.

Second, if nothing fits exactly, propose a name and a definition that separates the thing from those neighbours. I want the boundaries, not merely a label. In particular: does the phenomenon still occur when the person making the move is an explicit consequentialist? Does it occur when the change of level is announced rather than silent? Does it occur when the two levels are proposed as a division of labour, with philosophers handling truth and lawyers handling institutions? Which of these are the same thing, and which are different things that merely resemble it?

I am not after a name for its own sake. I am trying to determine whether the move I keep encountering is a legitimate argument I have failed to appreciate or a persistent confusion, and I cannot settle that until I know exactly what it is and where it stops.

Finally, please tell me how confident you are in the identification you settle on, and single out the part of your answer you are least sure of — especially any claim that no established term exists, since I have no way of checking that for myself.
