He's deep in AI alignment stuff. The topic was "low impact" agents — the claim that you can get an AI to avoid trashing the world *without* handing it a full specification of what humans value. His exhibit A was attainable utility preservation: you penalize an action by how much it reduces the agent's ability to achieve some auxiliary goal, and the supposedly wild part is that the auxiliary goal can be randomly generated — carrying zero information about human values — and you still get cautious behavior that goes around the vase instead of through it. He also went on about relative reachability (count how much the set of states the agent can still get to shrinks) and about how a side-effect penalty is really two separate design choices bolted together: a baseline, usually the state you'd end up in if the agent did nothing, and a deviation measure, how far the actual state is from that baseline. Plus corrigibility — never do anything irreversible, because you can't repair an irreversible mistake later even once you've found out what the right goal was. He mentioned they test some of this in Game of Life style environments where the agent has to build one target pattern without wrecking the rest of the board.
