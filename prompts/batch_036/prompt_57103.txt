Write a short story of 1,000–1,500 words that dramatizes the central inversion in von Neumann's *The Computer and the Brain* (1958) without any character ever stating it. The inversion is this: the nervous system is not a crude digital computer but a different kind of machine altogether — its components are far less precise and its pulse rate orders of magnitude slower, yet it is reliable because it is redundant, with many cheap, fallible units effectively voting on every result, and it is quick because it computes in very few serial steps spread over an enormous parallel width, where a computer of von Neumann's day needed long, exacting chains of exact operations. I want a reader to feel the difference between a system that is right because each step is precise and one that is right because each step is numerous, sloppy, and disposable, and to be given a stake in which kind they are.

Invent the setting freely — I would rather not see von Neumann on his deathbed, a robot waking up, or a machine passing for human. But include one scene in which redundancy fails in the particular way his analysis predicts: a majority scheme does not break cleanly at the first bad component, and it does not fail loudly. It degrades. There is a threshold fraction of unreliable units below which error is suppressed and above which the very same machinery begins to amplify it, and a system crossing that line keeps delivering answers just as confidently as before, only quietly less true. Nobody announces the crossing; it has to be legible from what the characters do and from what the prose stops being able to trust.

Close on the cost — or the surprising absence of a cost — of trading exactness for width, and let the ending belong to the story rather than to the thesis.
