So I TA a philosophy of language seminar and the prof asked each of us to stick a short where-I-stand blurb at the top of the shared doc, so the students attack that instead of the readings. Mine came out pretty Davidsonian and I've been staring at it too long to trust myself. Here it is:

1. Radical interpretation isn't a special case for exotic fieldwork, it's the general model of what meaning is. What makes it the case that my words mean what they mean is what an interpreter with enough evidence about my behaviour and its causes would, in principle, arrive at as the best charitable theory of me. There's no leftover fact sitting behind that.
2. Charity is constituitive, not a heuristic. Not: I provisionally assume you're mostly right and may have to retract the assumption. Rather: being mostly right, by the interpreter's lights, is part of what makes you a believer and a sayer at all.
3. So a creature that diverged from me on most things wouldn't come out as interpretable, and massive systematic error isn't a coherent possibility. Local error, sure. Total error, no.
4. But what I find most attractive here is that it's a story about how I can be corrected. Making content depend on public third-personal evidence means my own take isn't the measure of what I mean or believe, and the interpreter's position can show me I'm wrong about things I'd have sworn I was right about. Humility toward the other is supposed to be the payoff, and it's why I'm not a sceptic.
5. Also, no conceptual schemes. I take the scheme/content dualism to be a dogma: no unconceptualised given, and no framework so alien we couldn't get inside it. Not a relativist.
6. And I take seriously the line that there's no such thing as a language — prior theory versus passing theory, and it's the passing theory that does the work. Shared conventions and rules learned in common in advance aren't what meaning consists in; convergence on the occasion is, and that's closer to wit and luck than to running a common code.
7. That said, communication plainly works most of the time, and I don't want to end up saying we never really understand each other.
8. Two more. (a) A competent speaker understands indefinitely many brand-new sentences, and this is explained by her having learned a language as a kid in an ordinary way — that datum has to survive whatever else I say. (b) Content needs triangulation: a creature on its own, with nobody else reacting to the same things and nobody to check it, can't have thoughts with determinate content. Which is why I also hold that the last speaker of a dead language still means exactly what she meant, and nothing whatsoever changed about her words when the last person who could have interpreted her died.
9. Oh, and unlike Quine I don't think there's any deep indeterminacy of translation worth losing sleep over. If two charitable theories both fit, they're both fine, and nothing turns on which one we write down.

Can you audit this? Tell me which of these actually pull against each other and which only look like they do, and rank the real ones by severity, worst first — not a flat list where everything is equally bad. For the worst one I want you to commit: name the single thing I have to give up and say what I loose by giving it up. No menu of options, no both-sides. If you think the whole set hangs together, say that plainly and argue for it instead. Under 450 words for the whole thing.
