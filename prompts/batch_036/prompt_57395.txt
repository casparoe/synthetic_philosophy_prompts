I have no academic background in philosophy — no degree, no courses — but for several years I have been working my way through puzzles about completing infinitely many actions in a finite time, using popular books and my own notes. I have arrived at a small diagnostic of my own, and I would like you to test it by ranking a set of cases against it. I am not asking for references, a bibliography, or a reading list; I could not chase them down, and what I want is your judgment rather than your sources.

Here is the diagnostic. I call it the leftover test. Take any such puzzle and write out, as completely as you can, what happens at every moment strictly before the final instant — every stage that actually gets finished. Then ask whether anything about the final instant is left without a determinate answer. Three verdicts are possible:

(A) Nothing is left over. The stage-by-stage description already fixes the state at the final instant, and the puzzle only feels paradoxical because we smuggle in an expectation — a last step, a last act — that finite tasks happen to have.

(B) Something is left over, but harmlessly. The description simply does not say what happens at the final instant. The puzzle is therefore not a proof of impossibility; it is an unfinished story, and the missing line could be filled in more than one way without contradiction.

(C) Something is left over, and it cannot be filled in. Any completion of the story contradicts something the earlier stages guarantee, or requires a quantity the stages force to be unbounded. Here, and I suspect only here, is there a genuine impossibility.

My hunch, which I would like you to attack, is that almost everything people find terrifying in this area turns out to be (B), and that (C) may not be occupied at all.

Please sort the following nine cases into tiers by verdict, and give each a number between 0.00 and 1.00 for how much of its original sting survives the test — 1.00 meaning it still looks like a real impossibility once the test is applied, 0.00 meaning the test dissolves it entirely. Then give me one ordered ranking, most threatening to least. A sentence or two of justification per case is plenty; I care about the ordering and the numbers more than the prose.

1. The runner who covers half of the remaining distance to the finish line at each stage, so there is always another stage but never a last one.
2. The lamp whose switch is thrown at one half-minute, three quarters, seven eighths, and so on, with the question of whether it is on or off at the full minute.
3. A needle on a pivot, which I invented. In the first half-minute it turns through 90 degrees, in the next quarter-minute through 45, in the next eighth through 22.5, and so on. Note the speeds: every stage turns at exactly 180 degrees per minute. The total turn is 180 degrees, so at the end of the minute the needle points opposite to where it started, and nothing about the endpoint is undetermined.
4. The same needle run the other way, also mine. Each stage turns it through 90 degrees, so the required speed doubles every stage and grows without bound, and the angle it ends up at does not exist at all.
5. An urn: at each stage you add ten new consecutively numbered balls and remove the lowest-numbered ball in the urn. The number of balls inside grows without bound at every stage, yet at the end the urn is empty, since every ball was removed at some stage.
6. The same urn under a different removal rule: at each stage you add ten balls and remove the highest-numbered one. Now, at the end, infinitely many balls remain.
7. Twin lamps, which I invented to put pressure on my own test. Two identical lamps in two identical rooms start off and are toggled on exactly the same schedule, at one half-minute, three quarters, seven eighths. By stipulation, one is on at the minute and the other is off. They have never differed at any earlier moment: at every time before the end their states coincide, their histories coincide, their wiring coincides. If verdict (B) is right that the endpoint is simply not fixed by the earlier stages, then the two rooms differ at the final instant while differing at no instant before it, and nothing in either history explains the difference. That strikes me as worse than an unfinished story, and it is why I doubt (B) is a stable place to stop.
8. The mechanical arrangement of infinitely many identical particles spread along a line, all at rest, in which a disturbance arriving from arbitrarily far away sets them all in motion, so the whole infinite system begins moving spontaneously with no external cause. There are several versions of this; take the simplest one you know.
9. A machine whose stages take half as long as the one before, arranged so that within a single minute it has examined every natural number in turn and printed the answer to a question no ordinary computer can settle.

Three further things, briefly:

- Tell me plainly whether tier (C) is occupied by anything on this list, and if so by which case. If your answer is that it is empty, say what you think people are actually reacting to when they insist these puzzles prove something impossible.
- Tell me whether my test is a distinction that is already standard here. I gather that an objection along these lines — that the description of the lamp simply fails to fix its final state, so no contradiction follows — was published decades ago, and I have no interest in being told that I am not original. What I want is a judgment about whether my three-way split adds anything to that objection, or whether (A), (B), and (C) collapse back into it.
- Cases 3, 4, and 7 are mine. If one of them is trivial, or if one of them quietly assumes something I have not noticed, say which and what.

If you think a case is misdescribed, or that the test does not apply to it at all, give it a tier of its own and say why — but still score it, so the ranking stays complete.
