I attend a small evening seminar in my department that mixes philosophers with people from the theoretical physics group, and last Thursday two of its regulars argued for the better part of three hours over a toy cosmology that had been circulated beforehand. I was the one keeping time, so I ended up with the fullest notes; the rest of the room split down the middle and nobody could referee, and since I was persuaded at different moments by each of them, I would rather hand the exchange over intact than summarise it into a victory for either side. The model in question is one in which the universe eventually settles into an empty, equilibrium vacuum that persists forever, and in which everything that ever appears to happen — galaxies, fossils, telescopes, and people reasoning about cosmology at a seminar table — arises as a random thermal fluctuation within that vacuum. The authors' own calculation, as reported to us, is that observers produced this way outnumber observers with genuine, causally connected histories by an enormous factor, while the model reproduces every observation we actually make, since a fluctuated observer can be fluctuated into having exactly our apparent evidence. What follows is the argument as it developed, in order, with each speaker's moves numbered so that you can take them one at a time. (1) Fiona, who works in philosophy of science, said such a model can be set aside without further evidence: if we were fluctuated, our apparent memories and records — including the data used to construct the model in the first place — would be accidents with nothing behind them, and a theory that renders its own proponents' evidence worthless has undermined itself. She insisted this is not the same as saying the model is improbable; it is self-defeating, and no amount of internal consistency rescues it. (2) Rahul, the physicist, said this is special pleading dressed as epistemology: theories are assessed by what they predict, and this one predicts with certainty that observers will seem to have precisely the evidence we seem to have. If you reject a model because you dislike the kind of observer it says you are, you owe a rule, and any rule strong enough to do that work will equally allow people to discard theories whose implications merely strike them as unpalatable. (3) Fiona replied that the prediction is empty rather than impressive: on the model our evidence appears whether or not the model is true, and whether or not anything at all caused it, so a prediction that would be satisfied just as well in a world where the theory is false is not confirmation. Her conclusion was therefore weaker and stronger than Rahul assumed — the model is not refuted by the data, it is simply not supported by it, and it is unstable besides, because the reasoning that led anyone to propose it was, on the model's own account, a fluctuation. (4) Rahul said: then state the confirmation rule you are using. If a theory cannot be supported by the fact that it entails our evidence, what replaces that, and does the replacement also condemn the ordinary multiverse models he would happily keep, in which fluctuation-produced observers outnumber ordinary ones by a large but finite margin? If it does, he said, it is not a principle about fluctuations at all but an assumption that we are typical observers, smuggled in as a lesson about evidence. (5) Fiona said the difference is one of kind rather than degree: in an ordinary multiverse our evidence is generated by processes that track how things are, so having it is a mark of the world being a certain way, whereas in a fluctuation-dominated model nothing tracks anything and having the evidence is a mark of nothing. Rahul answered that this distinction is covertly a claim about our own location in a distribution of observers, and that it therefore requires a reference class and a prior over that class — neither of which anyone in the room had offered, despite the phrase 'self-sampling assumption' being used repeatedly as though it named a single rule rather than a family of choices. (6) Finally Rahul added that the debate may rest on a false premise: he had seen it argued that a vacuum with only finitely many distinguishable states cannot go on minting new observers indefinitely, but merely revisits states it has already occupied, so the counting that generates the whole problem may simply be wrong. Fiona's response was that if that is right her own conclusion does not follow either, since one cannot dismiss a model whose central prediction has not yet been shown to be well defined, and she conceded that nobody had specified how to count observers across an infinite spacetime at all.

What I want is an adjudication of the exchange as it was actually conducted, not a fresh essay on Boltzmann brains. Please go through the six moves one by one and rate each — clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken — with a brief justification in each case, and say explicitly where a move is right but for reasons its author did not give. Then tell me who is more right overall, and whether the winner wins for the reason they think they do. I would also like you to say what both of them missed: whether the disagreement survives once the different verdicts in play are pulled apart — refuted, self-defeating, unconfirmed, and not yet well defined are not the same claim, and the two of them slid between them constantly — and whether there is a version of either position that survives the other's best objection. If you think the exchange could be settled by some further argument or by some observation in principle available to us, say what it would be; if you think it cannot be settled and why, say that too.

Please be candid about the epistemic standing of your own verdicts rather than presenting them as settled: give me a rough confidence for each of the six ratings, say how confident you are in your overall conclusion, and single out the point in your answer where you are least sure you are right and what would make you change your mind about it.
