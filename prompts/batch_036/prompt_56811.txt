I cover epistemology-adjacent science and policy stories for a general-interest magazine, and I have been shortlisted for a year-long residency at an institute that funds writers working at the intersection of philosophy and public life. The application requires a proposal of roughly 1,500 words describing a project I would complete during the residency. I would like you to draft it from the following half-formed thesis.

For a decade and a half I have watched the debate over the norm of assertion — whether one ought to assert only what one knows, only what is true, or only what one justifiedly believes — proceed almost entirely on the basis of individual speakers uttering single, unqualified sentences. My working life involves an institution that publishes thousands of qualified, hedged, revisable claims a year, and I have become convinced that this institutional setting is not merely a field in which to apply that debate but a source of pressure on the way the debate is framed. Three observations, which I can supply from reporting but which you should treat here as premises:

First, newsrooms maintain a graded modal vocabulary — reportedly, apparently, it is understood that, we have learned that, appears to have — that publicly marks where a claim sits on an evidential scale. If assertion is a single speech act governed by one norm, hedged publication is either not assertion at all, in which case the debate's central cases describe a practice almost nobody actually engages in, or it is assertion whose norm can be satisfied by considerably less than knowledge, in which case the knowledge norm fails on its own home turf.

Second, blame comes apart in a revealing way. A story that was impeccably sourced but false attracts correction and often residual criticism; a story that was sloppily sourced but true attracts a different criticism, aimed at process, which is not the criticism appropriate to a bad assertion. On the truth norm the well-sourced falsehood is blameless; on the knowledge norm the true-but-lucky story is blameless. Professional practice seems to fault both, in two distinct registers, which suggests it tracks a two-factor standard the debate has no vocabulary for: one factor governing content, one governing the procedure by which content was acquired.

Third, an institution cannot know things the way an individual can, having no first-hand acquaintance and depending entirely on chains of testimony, yet it asserts continuously; and retraction functions as the institutional analogue of taking back an assertion without, unlike an individual's retraction, appearing to dissolve the criticism that attached to it.

Turn this into a proposal containing: a precise statement of the question, phrased so that it has a determinate answer rather than inviting a description of press practice; a section explaining why the existing positions — the knowledge, truth, and justification norms, together with the view that practical stakes shift the epistemic threshold — leave that question open; the method, laid out month by month across the twelve months, saying what conceptual work I would do, what reporting I would gather to discipline it (structured interviews with standards editors, a systematic reading of correction and retraction practice), how the two would inform each other rather than sit side by side, and why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives of a purely armchair argument or a purely quantitative study of error rates; an account of what a successful result looks like — what claim I would be in a position to defend at the end of the residency that I cannot defend now; and an honest statement of what would count as the project failing, including where the twelve-month scope is likely to prove too ambitious. Please also add a short paragraph anticipating the two obvious deflations — that the project collapses into applied media ethics, and that it rests on the trivial observation that institutions are not individuals — and identify the argumentative work that would have to be done to avoid each.

No bibliography and no literature review by citation; I will supply references myself, and it is fine to refer to positions and arguments by their standard names. Keep the register formal and unapologetic: this goes to a panel of philosophers, several of whom work on assertion and will not be impressed by a journalist announcing that their cases are toy cases.
