Archaeologists often date sites by ordering assemblages according to the relative frequency of decorative styles — the proportion of sherds carrying one rim motif rather than another, say. The method (frequency seriation) assumes that a style's popularity swells from rarity to a peak and then tapers away to nothing; that this curve looks broadly the same from site to site within a region; and that the lifespans of different styles overlap enough that assemblages with similar profiles must be close in time. The same reports, and the same discipline, then read those very motifs as social signals: a particular decoration or fastener form is taken to mark a household's standing, a marriage tie, or a boundary between groups.

What theory of cultural transmission does the dating practice presuppose? What must be true of the people who made and chose these objects for their preferences to work as a clock — faithful copying, continuous small-scale innovation, indifference to what the decoration says about its owner? Which rival accounts of style does the method quietly rule out, and which would be equally compatible with the same bell-shaped curves?

Here is what puzzles me. When a cemetery seriation yields a sequence that correlates strikingly well with the sex or the wealth of the buried, reports typically treat that as contamination to be filtered out before the real chronology appears, rather than as a finding. Yet the identity reading says such correlation is exactly what style is for. Are the two readings genuinely inconsistent, or does one depend on the other? Is the tension conceptual or merely statistical?

Finally: separate what the method strictly presupposes from what it merely assumes for convenience, say where the practice is underdetermined — where several rival theories of transmission would license the same results — and explain what would have to change in excavation, in typology, or in the conclusions a report is permitted to draw if styles turned out to be chosen strategically, to say who one is, rather than drifting unconsciously.
