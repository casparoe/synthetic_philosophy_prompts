My term paper for a philosophy of science seminar has to take a position on whether an argument run entirely in the imagination can justify a claim about a region no experiment can reach — the interior of a black hole, or the first instants of the universe. Rather than start with the rival accounts, I want first the list of demands any account of the epistemic power of thought experiments has to satisfy: the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the constraints it inherits from the rest of epistemology, each with the case or consideration that motivates it.

Build in the following, and tell me what else belongs on the list. It must handle the contrast between impossibility-driven and conceivability-driven cases: Stevin's wreath of spheres settles the law of the inclined plane by arguing that an unbalanced chain would turn forever and that this is absurd, whereas Galileo's tied bodies forces a contradiction out of an opponent's own claims about weight and speed, and these look like different kinds of achievement. It must say where the modal force comes from in each, and whether the impossibility invoked is knowable independently of experience or an empirical regularity dressed up as a principle. It must satisfy what I'll call the transfer requirement — saying what makes a conclusion about an imagined setup bear on the actual world rather than only on the setup. It must explain the reliability differential across disciplines, why we trust such arguments in mechanics and distrust structurally similar ones about personal identity or meaning, or else own up to a uniform skepticism. And it must diagnose failure prospectively rather than by hindsight: a seminar partner offered a Stevin-style case of his own, a rigid ladder on a perfectly smooth floor against a perfectly smooth wall, arguing that since nothing in the setup privileges one direction of sliding over the other the ladder cannot begin to move — which is false, since it collapses. I want a list on which an adequate account can say why that argument misfires and Stevin's does not, without appealing to which intuitions we happen to share.

Mark each desideratum as non-negotiable or as a partisan demand; I suspect that requiring every thought experiment to be reconstructible as an explicit argument with nothing left over, and requiring it to deliver a direct intellectual grasp of a law, each beg the question for one camp. Then say which single desideratum excludes the most accounts, and whether some subset is jointly unsatisfiable — if it is, that is the result I most want from the paper, so state it plainly. End with no more than two or three sentences on how the reconstructivist and the intuition-based families fare against the list; the scoring is not what I'm after.
