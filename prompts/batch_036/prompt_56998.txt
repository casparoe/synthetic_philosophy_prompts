My older brother is a CS major and he lent me this book about AI and learning from humans, not a textbook, more of a popular one, and there's a passage on inverse reinforcement learning that I've now read maybe four times. My robotics teacher keeps calling IRL "the smart version of imitation learning" but won't say more than that, so this passage is basically all I've got. It goes:

> "The trouble with teaching a robot by demonstration is that it only ever copies what you did. Watch someone drive for an hour and the naive approach just memorizes their steering wheel movements. That fails for an obvious reason: the moment the learner drifts a little off the path the human showed, it's in a situation that never came up in training and has no idea what to do, and the small error snowballs. Inverse reinforcement learning gets around this by not learning the behavior at all — it learns the reward behind the behavior. You watch the expert and work backwards from their choices to recover what they were actually trying to maximize, and then your robot can pursue that goal in situations the expert never showed it, which is why it generalizes. This is also why IRL matters for the alignment problem: if a system can infer human values from watching us go about our lives, we don't have to write those values down explicitly, which was never going to work anyway. There's a classic result that the reward can only be recovered up to multiplying by a positive number and adding a constant, so strictly speaking the algorithm can never pin down your exact values — but that's harmless, because those transformations don't change which actions come out best. The robot ends up with the right goals either way."

I mostly buy this, and the "small error snowballs" bit matches what I saw when I tried to clone my own driving in a simulator for a project, so I think the book is at least competent. But two things bug me and I cant tell whether they're real problems or just me not getting it. One: the whole inference seems to lean on an assumption about the human that the passage never states out loud — something has to be true about the driver before "they did X" can license "they want X," and I don't see where the book says what that is. Two: the ending feels like a shrug. It concedes the answer isn't unique and then says uniqueness doesn't matter, but three sentences earlier the entire selling point was acting correctly in situations nobody demonstrated.
