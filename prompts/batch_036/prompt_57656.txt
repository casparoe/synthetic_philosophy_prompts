I am a junior at a high school with a health-sciences program, and I spend my weekends volunteering with an ambulance corps that trains us in mass-casualty triage. The sorting rule we were taught is straightforward: with limited hands and supplies, you treat in whatever order saves the greatest number, and anyone whose injuries are unsurvivable given what we have on hand is tagged expectant and passed over for treatment altogether. But the same training told us to give those expectant patients comfort measures — pain relief, positioning, staying with them rather than leaving them alone — whenever resources permit.

That instruction puzzles me. Under the rule that assigns the tag, every minute of staff time and every dose of analgesic spent on someone we have already decided we cannot save is a minute and a dose taken from someone we can. So what theory of value licenses the comfort-care step? Does it presuppose that suffering is bad in a way that does not depend on whether the sufferer survives — and if so, why does that claim never outweigh the goal of saving lives, given that the two rules are applied in strict order rather than weighed against each other? Or is the step not expressing a value at all, but a professional norm about how the dying are to be treated, or a concession to the consciences of the responders and the families watching?

Please separate what the practice strictly requires from what merely happens to fit it: could a view on which only lives saved matter still generate the instruction? Then tell me what a protocol built honestly on the premise that relieving suffering matters most would have to change — would the tags themselves be assigned differently?
