I'm TAing an interdisciplinary first-year seminar where one week pairs Frankenstein with material on the governance of risky research — the unilateralist's curse, the principle of conformity, that general line of thought where the idea is roughly that if an act has big consequences for people who aren't party to the decision, one person shouldn't get to do it alone just because they happen to be the one who can. My section is thursday and I'm nervous it's going to collapse into "Victor should have filed an IRB application."

Here's my worry, and also what I think is the more interesting question. Victor doesn't really ignore advice — he makes sure there is no advice to ignore. He gets to Ingolstadt, hears Krempe tell him the alchemists were a waste of time, gets encouragement from Waldman instead, and then spends two years completely seperated from Clerval and his family, working at night in a room of his own. As far as I can remember there's no scene where a competent peer tells him not to do it and he overrides them. Which would mean the principle of conformity — defer to your peers' objections — has no input to operate on. So does the novel actually illustrate the unilateralist's curse, or does it expose something that principle is silent about: that you first have to construct a situation in which peers are positioned to object, and that the real vice is engineering your way out of having any?

Then there's the scene I can't stop thinking about, which cuts the other way. When Victor tears up the half-finished female on the island — creature's face at the window, the remains going into the sea — he looks like he's doing the right thing on exactly this reasoning. A second being who never agreed to anything, the possibility of offspring, a species-level risk he can't estimate. He's making something like a precautionary judgment. And the novel punishes him catastrophically for it: Clerval, then Elizabeth. If the moral is "don't act alone," why is the one time he makes a well-reasoned unilateral refusal the worst decision in the book? I want that taken seriously, not explained away as Shelley just needing more deaths.

Walton is the third thread — he's the guy who wants to do the same thing and ends up turning the ship around when the crew ask him to. And the whole novel is letters wrapping a spoken narrative wrapping letters, ending up in the hands of his sister Margaret. Tell me whether the form is doing something the concept can't.

What I want is a reading I can defend in a room of nineteen-year-olds that treats both the concept and the novel seriously, and that's honest where the lens distorts.
