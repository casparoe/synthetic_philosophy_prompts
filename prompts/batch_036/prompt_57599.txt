I am drafting a short essay and want substantive comments on the argument, not copy-editing. Working title: "Doable, Checkable, Sayable: Complexity Theory as Modal Epistemology." The draft runs as follows.

Modal metaphysics asks what is possible; epistemology asks what is knowable. I want to argue that complexity theory contributes categories philosophy has lacked the vocabulary for — what is *doable* and what is *sayable* — and that its central asymmetry, between finding and checking, is part of what makes knowledge social in the first place. Three claims.

(1) Classes like P and NP are not natural kinds in the world but indexical classifications relative to an agent's resource budget; they behave like modal operators rather than like descriptions of physical structure. On this reading, P versus NP is not primarily a question about the fabric of reality but about the shape of our epistemic access to it. The obvious objection is that if P = NP the world would be a dramatically different place — cryptography would collapse, discovery would be cheap — which suggests the question is doing empirical work after all. I plan to concede this and reply that the empirical content lies not in the classes themselves but in which natural problems happen to sit where.

(2) The verification asymmetry — that a certificate can be checked vastly more cheaply than the corresponding solution can be found — is not merely a technical curiosity. It is the condition under which mathematical testimony works at all. We can accept a proof we could never have produced because checking is cheap and public, so the authority of a proof is transferable across agents in a way that the ability to produce one is not. Complexity theory on this view underwrites the division of epistemic labor: knowability outruns ability.

(3) The neglected category is sayability: what a group can establish by exchanging a bounded number of bits when no member individually has the answer. Communication complexity shows that some functions demand nearly maximal exchange, so a group's knowledge is not the union of its members' knowledge but is bottlenecked by the channel. Testimony epistemology treats testimony as the transfer of already-formed belief; what happens in a research community is often that beliefs are computed by the exchange. I want to extend this to Fitch's knowability paradox: if ◇K is read as "knowable within a budget," the derivation seems blocked, since it appears to require an agent able to know everything. I do not yet know how to state the budgeted operator cleanly — it plainly fails the usual closure conditions — and I suspect this is the weak point of the essay.

Comments I want: (a) does claim (1) amount to more than relabeling indexical possibility with a resource subscript, or is the modal framing idle; (b) does (2) slide from "checking is cheap" to "checking confers authority," and is any inference from complexity results to the sociology of proof licensed; (c) does the budgeted reading in (3) survive re-running Fitch's diagonal construction at an arbitrary fixed budget; (d) where does the draft conflate worst-case with typical-case hardness, or treat polynomial time as a stand-in for feasibility when it plainly is not; (e) which of the three claims should I cut in order to save the other two.

Begin with a single sentence naming the most serious problem in the draft, then give your comments. Do not suggest literature or sources; I want your own reasoning. Maximum 450 words.
