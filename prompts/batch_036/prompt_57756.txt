I spent thirty-seven years in a physical chemistry laboratory, most of it measuring things nobody can see — binding energies, transition rates, chemical potentials — and I have been reading philosophy and the philosophy of economics since I retired. I am auditing a course in the foundations of social science this term, and in the second week the instructor defended the utility concept with an analogy I keep hearing, from economists and from textbook authors alike, and which I find both appealing and slippery.

The analogy runs as follows. A choosing agent is like a body moving in a potential. Utility is the landscape; the agent's choices are the local gradient of that landscape; utility itself is like potential energy, which is never observed in itself, is defined only up to an additive constant, and is objectionable to no one. On this picture, only differences matter, the zero point is a convention, an agent need not calculate anything in order to be described as descending the gradient, and departures from the prediction are friction and noise rather than failures of the law. The conclusion drawn from it in my class was that the complaints about utility being an unobservable mental fiction are confused in exactly the way a complaint about potential energy being unobservable would be confused, and that the revealed preference programme was simply doing what physics had already done: discarding an occult quality in favour of what the behaviour shows.

I would like a careful audit of this analogy, written for someone fluent in the physics but new to the economics and to the philosophy of it. Specifically:

1. A correspondence table. State what plays the role of the coordinates, the landscape, the gradient, the equation of motion, the zero point, the friction, and the conservation law — and mark any role for which the analogy has no answer.

2. Where the mapping breaks, sorted into the disanalogies that matter for the use my instructor made of it and those that are merely picturesque. I care above all about whether the analogy survives as a defence of utility's unobservability and as a claim about the empirical content of the theory.

3. What features of the source domain the analogy quietly imports that the target lacks. I have three suspicions and would like them tested rather than echoed: that a potential requires a conservative field, so that path-independence is a substantive assumption and not a convenience; that a gradient requires a metric and a dynamics, neither of which the theory supplies; and that the claim that only differences matter presupposes a scale type that ordinal theories do not license.

4. Whether the integrability problem in revealed preference — the question of when local choice data can be reconstructed into a single global ordering — is literally the physicist's question of whether a one-form is exact. If it is, tell me what that does to the analogy: does it vindicate the comparison by showing where the analogy must be cashed out in a theorem, or does it expose the analogy as concealing the one place where the economics has to do work the physics does for free?

5. Whether a better analogy exists. If some other physical or biological image — a temperature with an absolute zero, a free-energy landscape with kinetics, something else — carries the reasoning more honestly, say what conclusions it would license and which of my instructor's it would withdraw.

Please do not supply a bibliography; I will chase the literature myself. Do avoid hedging into generalities about models being useful fictions: I want the specific joints of the comparison examined, and I want to be told plainly where the analogy is load-bearing and where it is decoration. If any step of your argument depends on a technical result you are not certain of, flag it as something I should verify rather than asserting it.
