I TA a second-year course that's nominally "games, decisions and rationality" but is really half philosophy, half econ, and Thursday I get them for eighty minutes with a whiteboard and no slides to do backward induction and the centipede game. It's a 9am slot so a third of them will be running purely on coffee. They've already had simultaneous-move games, Nash equilibrium, iterated elimination of dominated strategies, the prisoner's dilemma and finitely repeated versions of it, and they can read a game tree, but nobody has met perfect-information games or subgame perfection before. Don't explain the centipede game to me — I know it, I know Rosenthal's version with the doubling pot, I know the unique subgame perfect equlibrium has player one taking immediately, and I know the lab results where hardly anyone does and people end up better off than the equilibrium predicts. What I want is the plan for how to sequence the explanation. Specifically: which components have to be in place before which others, with the dependency claims spelled out rather than just a list in a plausible order; and what the single example that carries the whole session should be, including what that example needs to have and needs to lack. I'm torn between drawing the 100-node doubling game as a truncated tree and doing something much shorter — four or six nodes, played live with two students at the front for real money (well, sweets) — and I suspect there's a third option I haven't thought of.

Here's my working hypothesis about the shape of the thing, and I want you either to build the plan around it or tell me plainly that it's wrong: the real content isn't the recursion at the far end of the tree, which they'll pick up in two minutes, it's what a player is entitled to believe once they witness a move that the recursion says can't happen. On that view the centipede is fundamentally a story about belief revision after a surprise, and the tree is only the vehicle. Related subtlety I want handled properly, because I don't think most intro treatments are careful about it: taking is not locally dominant at each node in the plain one-shot sense — it's the right move only given a conjecture about how play continues, and the induction itself is what manufactures that conjecture. If they walk out thinking "passing is dominated so passing is just an error," I've failed the session.

Map the misconceptions for me in the order they'll actually surface. My guesses: (i) that this is basically an iterated prisoner's dilemma in a longer costume; (ii) that the player who passes is being generous rather than betting on getting paid back later; (iii) that backward induction is an instruction about how you should play rather than a prediction about what players who are rational and known to be will do; (iv) that "common knowledge of rationality" just means everyone is rational and everyone knows it, which is exactly what makes the off-path nodes so strange; (v) my own favourite — that the argument has the same structure as the surprise exam, so if the surprise exam reasoning is a fallacy the centipede reasoning must be too; and (vi) that the experiments straightforwardly prove people are irrational. Also: one student plays a lot of chess and will insist the whole thing is trivial recursion a computer could do — tell me what to say to him and at which point in the sequence to say it, not at the end.

Answer as someone who has both taught this material many times and thought about it as a philosopher: I care about the conceptual dependency structure more than classroom choreography, and I want you to think carefully before drafting. If it helps, sketch two or three candidate arcs first and then commit to one. Format it as a numbered sequence with a one-line "why here and not earlier/later" attached to each step; then a table of misconceptions with columns for what a student will actually say, roughly where in the sequence it surfaces, and the specific move that heads it off; then two short lists — things I should explicitly refuse to get into at this level (I'm thinking reputation and incomplete-information types, trembling hand, forward induction, the whole surprise-exam literature) with a sentence each on what I say out loud to justify skipping them, and one closing question that separates students who got the belief-revision point from students who just memorised "player one takes immediately."
