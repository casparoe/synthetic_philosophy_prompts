Retired three years back after a career in a diffraction lab, and now I audit a philosophy seminar two mornings a week. I've been chewing on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem for about two months, and last week I wrote out an argument to check whether I'd finally got it. It comes out proving something I'm sure is wrong, so I figure I've botched it somewhere and I can't find the joint.

(1) Harsanyi's theorem says: given a group of agents and a set of prospects — mixtures, lotteries, over outcomes — if each agent's preferences over those prospects satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, and the group's preferences over the same prospects satisfy them too, and the group respects Pareto (whatever every agent weakly prefers, the group weakly prefers), then the group's utility function is a positive weighted sum of the agents' utilities. There's no moral premise in the proof. It's a representation theorem.

(2) Nothing in the proof mentions persons, welfare, or ethics. The "agents" are just whatever satisfies the axioms over a common mixture space.

(3) So it applies to any such family: me now and me in ten years; the competing objective terms inside a planner program; a wolf pack, if you can read vNM preferences off their behaviour.

(4) So in every one of those cases the only coherent aggregation is a weighted sum. No exceptions, no lexical priorities.

(5) But each individual utility is itself only fixed up to its own positive affine transformation — that's part of the vNM setup. Multiply one agent's utility by a thousand and the theorem still holds, the weights just come out a thousand times smaller. So for any positive weight vector I care to write down, there's a rescaling of the individual utilities that produces it.

(6) Which means the theorem fixes no weights at all. With the right choice of scales it delivers anything from pure egoism (my weight 1, everyone else's effectively zero) to strict equal-weight averaging. It is compatible with any distribution of concern whatsoever.

(7) Except it also has teeth. It rules out giving absolute priority to the worst off, because that ordering isn't linear in the individual utilities, and it rules out any group ordering that violates the group's own continuity. Rawls and Sen both think something's wrong with the utilitarian argument built on this theorem, and they can't be objecting to a tautology.

(8) So (6) and (7) can't both stand, and (1)–(5) are supposed to be nothing but the theorem plus bookkeeping.

Where did I go wrong? I don't want to give up (1), it's about the only thing in this literature that's actualy proved, and I don't want to give up (7), because if the theorem is compatible with everything I can't see why anyone argues about it. So: which step do you reject, and why did it look so innocent when I wrote it down — I keep having the feeling that two things I'm treating as one thing are seperate. Then tell me what rejecting it costs: what the theorem is still allowed to claim afterwards, and what has to be added on top before it can say anything at all about equal weights or about utilitarianism. And if there are two or three standard exits here, I'd like to know what each one pays for.
