I'm reading a pop-philosophy book about identity, and chapter 4 is on Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles. Here's basically how the author sets it up:

"Leibniz said no two things can be exactly alike — if x and y have all their properties in common, they're just one thing. Max Black finished that off in a couple pages. He asks you to imagine a universe containing nothing but two spheres that are exactly alike: same size, same iron, same everything. And since the world is otherwise empty and perfectly symmetric, every property one sphere has the other has too, including the relational ones, like being the same distance from the other sphere. So there you are with two indiscernable things, which is precisely what Leibniz said couldn't happen. What Black is really showing is that the principle was never a truth about the world at all, just a confused demand that objects carry labels around with them; if two things can't be told apart even in principle, then 'two' was never buying you anything. Adams tried to plug the hole by handing each sphere a haecceity, the property of being that very sphere, but that's circular, since you have to already know which sphere you mean before you can have such a property. So the principle is dead, and most metaphysicians these days treat it that way."

I went in already suspicious of the haecceity move, which is exactly why I don't want to just take the book's word for it. Can you go through that passage and sort it into what's accurate, what's a defensible simplification, and what's actually wrong? In particular: does the claim that the spheres really share every property survive the standard replies about relational and demonstrative properties? Is "circular" the right charge against Adams if he's offering thisness as primitive rather than as an analysis? Does the bit about labels and 'two' not buying you anything slide between two different jobs the principle could be doing? And does "the principle is dead and most metaphysicians agree" follow from anything in the rest of it, or is that just rhetoric?
