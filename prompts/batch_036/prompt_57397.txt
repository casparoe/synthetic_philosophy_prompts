I direct the Office of Performance and Innovation for a city of roughly 340,000. My staff is five people, none of them economists; our one analyst is a part-time data person who mostly maintains dashboards. In the spring our city manager attended a conference session on governance by prediction markets and came back convinced we should pilot something he is calling futarchy on a handful of decisions this fiscal year. The version of the idea that persuaded him was roughly the one its originator popularized: let elected officials settle what we are trying to achieve, then let speculators bet on which of the candidate policies will achieve more of it, and adopt whichever branch the market prices higher. He has asked me to produce a written procedure that any program manager in any department can run on an incoming proposal before we commit public money to opening a market. He does not want another essay on whether prediction markets are wise in general; he wants steps, in order, with a stopping condition at each one.

The constraints are these. The pilot has $60,000 in total, most of which is earmarked for seeding liquidity, because the platform vendor assures us the contracts themselves are trivial to write. Proposals arrive continuously — we field fifteen to twenty serious ones a year and expect four or five to reach the pilot — and each one gets a single afternoon of analysis before our monthly review board, at which the city attorney and one council member must sign off before anything goes live. The decisions at issue range from about $1 million to $15 million and affect anywhere from a few hundred households to most of the city; a couple of them are effectively irreversible for a decade. One department head insists any market must be written on a single dollar figure, and another insists that a dollar figure is exactly the wrong thing to write it on and wants a composite index instead. Please write the procedure so that this disagreement is resolved by the procedure rather than by whoever talks longest.

Specifically, I need it to tell a non-specialist, step by step: what makes a decision eligible at all, and whether repeatability matters; how to tell a measurable outcome apart from a value, and who is permitted to choose the outcome the contract pays on; how to condition a contract on the policy actually being adopted, and who gets to declare that the condition was met; how thick a market has to be before its price is evidence rather than noise, and what to do when the honest answer is that we cannot get a number; how we would ever know the price was right, given that we only observe the branch we took and the losing branch is never resolved; how we keep a trader from buying the outcome he wants, in the market, in the world, or both, especially when the best-informed potential traders are also interested parties; what the losing side of the bet is entitled to do afterwards; and at which step the procedure must stop and hand the question back to elected officials because it is about what we should want rather than what will happen. Mark clearly which steps are genuinely mechanical and which are judgment calls wearing a procedural costume.

Then run the procedure on three proposals we actually have on the table. First, whether to convert four low-ridership bus routes into a higher-frequency network, where the transit department wants the contract written on total boardings and the equity office wants it written on total rider-minutes, and where implementation would be partial and phased. Second, whether to sign a ten-year exclusive contract with one waste hauler or keep the collection zones out to competitive bid, where the parties with the best information about the answer are the bidders themselves. Third — and this is the city manager's flagship, the one he has already mentioned to the council — whether to open an overnight shelter at a specific vacant site, with the market written on assessed property values within a half mile over eighteen months. If the procedure kills the flagship, say so plainly and explain what specifically fails; if it lets all three through, tell me it is not a procedure but a rubber stamp. Finally, give me the limits: what classes of decision this will systematically get wrong, what a bad outcome would look like, and what we should be watching in month six that would tell us the pilot is failing before the failure is expensive.
