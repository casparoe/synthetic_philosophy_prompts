I spent thirty-one years on the calibration side of satellite radiometry, which is to say my professional life consisted of insisting that a number coming out of an instrument was a number about a physical quantity rather than a convenient fiction maintained by the people who built it. Since retiring I have been auditing a philosophy of mind seminar at the university here — Thursday afternoons, and I am comfortably the oldest person in the room — where the topic this term is the distinction between original and derived intentionality: Searle's insistence that the Chinese Room's symbol-manipulation can never amount to genuine aboutness, and Dennett's counter that the very idea of intrinsic syntax is unstable, illustrated by his two-bitser, a device with two bits, one of which is set whenever a switch is up and the other whenever it is down, whose "being a one" is no more a fact about the hardware than its "being about the switch" is. The distinction appeals to me for reasons I half suspect are sentimental: it looks like the philosopher's version of the difference between a raw reading and a calibrated measurement. What has fascinated me, though, is not the argument but our seminar leader's track record. She has been handing down verdicts all term, and I have been writing them on cards, because I want to know what rule she is actually running.

Her verdicts so far. A mercury-in-glass thermometer and a bimetallic-strip thermostat: no genuine aboutness, only our bookkeeping. A modern thermostat that infers a household's schedule and preheats the boiler: still no, and dismissively so — cleverness, she said, is beside the point. A chess program of the 1980s with a hand-written evaluation function: no. A self-play program of the AlphaZero type, containing no human chess knowledge whatever, trained from nothing but the rules: also no, which struck several of us as the hardest of her calls. A numerical weather-prediction model whose state variables were defined by the equations its authors wrote down: no, because the pressure gradient it carries is ours and not its own. A large language model: no, and not a close case for her, on the ground that everything such a model produces is downstream of human sentences. An inscription in a language whose last speaker died many centuries ago: no — she held that the aboutness left when the last reader left, and that the marks were thereafter no more about anything than a frost pattern on a window. Against all of that: the waggle dance of a honeybee, yes, genuinely about the bearing and distance of a food source; a bacterium running up a glucose gradient, yes, though grudgingly; a Venus flytrap, yes; a border collie, emphatically yes. And one verdict that does not sit neatly in either column: the notebook kept by an amnesiac patient who can no longer retain new memories. The entries themselves, she said, are only derivatively about anything; but the patient-plus-notebook is genuinely about his daughter's birthday, and she was firm that in that case the aboutness belongs to the system rather than to any particular stuff in it.

Next week I would like to put a case to her that I think breaks the pattern, and I want your prediction before I do. A group a few years back grew on the order of a million living human neurons, derived from stem cells, on a multi-electrode array, and coupled them to a simulated game of Pong: the array fed in patterned electrical stimulation encoding the position of ball and paddle, and the culture's discharges moved the paddle. Missing the ball was followed by unpredictable, random stimulation; returning it was followed by an ordered, predictable pattern, and within minutes the cultures were rallying. So here is my case. The stuff is biological — these are living human neurons, with the plasticity mechanisms that natural selection built. The milieu is entirely ours: we chose the game, we chose the electrodes, we chose which pattern should count as reward and which as noise. Nothing in the arrangement is anything to the culture; it does not need to return the ball, though it plainly registers the difference between the two stimulation regimes, and it will die if the nutrients stop regardless of how it plays. And yet after training there are states in that dish whose dynamics reliably and non-arbitrarily track the ball's position. Does that dish have genuine, original aboutness with respect to the ball, or only the borrowed kind?

What I want from you is not a list of positions but an analysis of her cards. Set out the candidate rules that are jointly consistent with every verdict above — I can think of at least a substrate-or-causal-powers rule, a pedigree rule about whether the content was borrowed from an interpreter who already had it, a rule keyed to whether the system has ends of its own and something to lose, a rule keyed to selection history fixing the content of a mechanism, and a rule keyed to whether the mapping from physical state to content is constrained rather than arbitrary — and say plainly which of my twelve data points each one leans on hardest. Then tell me what each predicts about the dish, and where they genuinely diverge rather than merely sounding different. Give me your best single guess at her verdict, with some sense of how confident you are in it, and say what she would have to abandon or reinterpret on each possible answer: in particular, whether a "no" costs her the notebook case, and whether a "yes" costs her the self-play chess program, which is the card I suspect she most wants to keep. I would also like you to say whether the two-bitser consideration is fatal to her whole way of carving things up — if a state's syntactic properties are themselves interpreter-relative, is there any coherent notion of "derived" left for her to appeal to, or is she a Searlean who has simply stopped advertising it? Finally, propose the one further case, sharper than mine if you can find one, that I could put to her the week after to separate the surviving rules. Please do not send me to the literature; I am not after a reading list, and I would rather have your reasoning laid bare, including the places where you think her verdicts are simply incoherent.
