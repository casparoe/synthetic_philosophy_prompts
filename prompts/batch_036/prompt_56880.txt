A few weeks ago our son sat a routine follow-up assessment at the hospital where he was operated on, and afterwards the neuropsychologist, explaining one of the tasks to us in plain language, referred to what had been going on inside each of the two halves. She meant nothing by it; it is how people in that clinic talk. But I have not been able to put the phrase down since. Our son had a complete corpus callosotomy at the age of nine for epilepsy that had resisted everything else. He is fifteen now, the seizures are largely controlled, and by any ordinary measure he is one intact, opinionated, entirely recognisable boy. I took up reading philosophy of mind in the evenings to settle myself, which has had the opposite effect, and I have now written out an argument that I can neither live with nor dismantle. My brother-in-law, who teaches the subject, says it proves too much and will not tell me which step is bad, on the grounds that I ought to find it myself.

So that you need not look anything up, here is what I take myself to know. The operation severs the corpus callosum, but leaves the older, subcortical connections between the hemispheres in place, and the surgeons regard that as part of the reason the everyday picture is so much less dramatic than the laboratory one. In the laboratory, with a stimulus confined to one half of the visual field, he can name what falls on the right and not what falls on the left; yet he can reach out and pick the left-field object with his left hand, and sort and match it correctly while sincerely denying that anything was shown. The right hemisphere in patients like him can spell simple words, choose among pictures, and respond affectively, including with fear, to something the speaking hemisphere insists it never saw. And the speaking hemisphere will explain behaviour it did not initiate: in the well-known demonstration, a patient shown a chicken claw to one hemisphere and a snowy landscape to the other selects a chicken and a shovel, and then, asked about the shovel, offers a reason tying it to the chicken — something about clearing out the coop — in perfect good faith. Outside the laboratory these patients finish school, hold jobs, drive, quarrel with their parents, and show almost none of the disconnection the tests predict; the gap between the clinic and the lab is a standing puzzle, and I am aware that the classic results have recently been re-examined by people who think they overstate the disunity.

This is the argument I have written.

1. If a single body houses two centres of control, each able to perceive, to form intentions and to initiate action, and such that what one of them knows is in principle closed to the report of the other, then that body houses two subjects of experience rather than one.
2. In a complete commissurotomy patient, the left hemisphere reports only what is presented to it, while the right hemisphere perceives, sorts, spells and responds affectively to items the left hemisphere sincerely denies having seen; and the left hemisphere will confabulate reasons for acts it did not initiate.
3. Therefore my son houses two subjects of experience.
4. Nothing in (1) mentions the corpus callosum. What (1) asks is only whether information guiding behaviour in one system is closed, as a matter of principle, to the system that speaks for the person.
5. In ordinary, intact brains, information guides behaviour that is closed to the reporting system in just that way: we fail to see large changes we are looking straight at; we act on meanings we cannot report having extracted; people state the reasons for their choices confidently when the reasons they give are demonstrably not the ones that moved them; and the corpus callosum, severed or not, permits only partial and slow transfer between the hemispheres, so that functional disconnection is a matter of degree in everyone.
6. Therefore an ordinary person too houses many subjects of experience.
7. If everyone houses many subjects, then the split-brain findings showed nothing distinctive about my son, and the notion of a subject has ceased to be a way of counting persons at all.
8. So either (1) is false, or my son is not one person.

I cannot accept the second disjunct, and I would rather surrender (1) than say that there are two of him in that house. But surrendering (1) appears to leave me with nothing whatever to say about whether there is anything it is like to be him, which was the only question I started out with; and (1) still looks to me like the least disputable premise in the list, which is presumably the sign of a bad argument rather than a good one.

What I want is a diagnosis. Which step do you reject, and why did it look unimpeachable when I wrote it down? Is (1) simply false, or is (5) false, or does (4) quietly reinterpret (1) between the two — and if the trouble is that I am using one word for more than one kind of disunity, tell me how many kinds there are and which ones my premises actually invoke. Then price the alternatives rather than merely listing them: the suggestion that a patient of this kind is both one person and two, depending on what is being asked of him; the reductionist line on which the split-brain case shows that identity was never what mattered; the view that consciousness is unified simply in virtue of belonging to a single subject, and that one brain supports only one subject at a time; and the view that whatever else the laboratory dissociations show, they do not show two fields of consciousness at a single moment. For each, say what it has to give up, and what it can say to me about the difference between my son and myself. Finally, tell me whether there is a principled line between disunity that would make two subjects and the disunity that is present in everyone, or whether the counting question is simply malformed; and tell me without flattery whether this argument is bad, or whether I am only unwilling to accept a conclusion that happens to be true.

Please write to me in continuous prose, as one writes a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, nothing that resembles a handout or a lecture slide. British English, please. I am not asking for references or a reading list — I have more books than I have evenings — only your reasoning. Take as much room as the problem genuinely needs, but no more.
