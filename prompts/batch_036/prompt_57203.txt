I host a small philosophy-of-mind podcast out of a university library studio, roughly four thousand regular listeners, and next month's guest is a philosopher who has built a reputation on the claim that the Gödelian material in Hofstadter's *Gödel, Escher, Bach* is scaffolding rather than substance: nothing about diagonalization, consistency, or incompleteness does any real work, on her account, in explaining mindedness, and what survives is a claim about levels of description. Her version of the residue is specific enough to be argued with. She holds that a system acquires what she calls a closed coarse self-model when it represents its own information-processing at a resolution too low to expose the mechanisms doing the representing, and when that representation feeds back as an input into the very processes it depicts. Both conditions matter to her. An inert read-out of one's own states is not a self, and neither is a self-representation fine-grained enough that the system could audit its own machinery; the low resolution is not a limitation of the theory but the point of it. She is emphatic that this is not a descendant of the Lucas–Penrose argument, which she thinks fails, and she does not claim that human mathematicians can see the truth of their own Gödel sentences.

I want ten questions that make her argue for the residue rather than for the demolition, since the demolition is well trodden and my listeners have heard it before. Please include at least one each on: what content her strange-loop talk carries beyond plain self-representation once diagonalization is set aside; whether the level-crossing idiom is an ontological claim or an explanatory one, and what turns on the difference; the relation between the low-resolution condition and the explanatory gap, given that opacity is a fact about access and access facts do not obviously entail phenomenal facts; how her view differs from Metzinger's self-model theory of subjectivity, where transparency does recognizably similar work; whether the feedback condition rescues her isomorphisms from the Putnam-style objection that a correspondence between one thing and another is a representation only relative to an interpreter — the ant whose track in the sand happens to curve into a caricature of Churchill — and what separates a self-model that is causally used from a thermostat's bimetallic strip, which is also causally used; where the cutoff falls among thermostats, macaques in the uncertainty-monitoring paradigm, split-brain patients, and large language models; and what observation would count against the view. For each question give me the follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give; I would rather have the second move prepared than a longer list of first ones.

Structure the reply in these sections, in this order. **The position, restated neutrally**: about 120 words, flagging any place where my summary above already smuggles in an objection of my own. **The question ladder**: ten numbered rungs, each with a one-line label of what the rung tests, the question itself in words I could plausibly say aloud in a single breath, and a follow-up introduced by the reply you expect it to meet. **Escape routes**: about 150 words naming the two moves she will most plausibly use to leave a question — relocating the dispute to another level of description, or treating a demand for a cutoff as a demand for spurious precision — and saying at which rungs each is likely to appear. **The one I cannot cut**: about 80 words choosing a single rung and explaining why the rest of the interview could be sacrificed for it. Keep the whole thing under fourteen hundred words, and please do not pad the ladder with warm-ups about her intellectual influences.
