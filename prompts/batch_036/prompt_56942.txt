I have been reading a chapter in a recent trade-press book on the mind whose central exhibit is the experimental work on spontaneous movement that Benjamin Libet and his collaborators published in the early 1980s. The passage runs roughly as follows:

"Libet asked people to sit in front of a special clock whose marker swept rapidly around a dial, and to flex a finger whenever they felt like it, noting the position of the marker at the moment they became aware of the urge to move. At the same time he recorded the readiness potential, a slow build-up of negative electrical activity over the motor cortex that precedes self-initiated movement. What he found was striking: the brain begins preparing the movement about half a second before the subject reports having decided to make it. The decision, in other words, has already been made unconsciously, and consciousness arrives on the scene too late to have caused anything. The only room Libet could find for the will was a brief window of a couple of hundred milliseconds in which the conscious mind can still call the action off — but that is not free will in any meaningful sense, since what gets cancelled was never chosen in the first place. And the finding generalizes: every voluntary act is initiated by unconscious neural processes, so the experience of deciding is a story the brain tells itself after the fact. This is why Libet's experiment is usually described as the empirical refutation of free will, and why philosophers who want to keep free will have been driven to the weak position of insisting that the timing measurements must be unreliable."

I would like an accuracy audit rather than a summary of the debate. Please work through the passage sentence by sentence and tell me which claims a researcher who works on this literature would accept without qualification, which are defensible popular simplifications, and which are wrong or simply unsupported by the experiment described.
