I am seventeen and in my final year of secondary school. Over the summer I have been working through the metaphysics of persistence on my own, and I am now trying to write a competition essay on the dispute between endurantism and perdurantism. Before I draft my own version, I would like to study a strong model essay, so that I can see how an argument of this kind ought to be organised. Please write that essay.

I should say at the outset that I do not want a survey. I have already read enough to reconstruct the familiar lines of attack: temporary intrinsics, fission and branching, the argument from vagueness, the charge that relativity theory makes a wholly-present object impossible. A summary of these would teach me nothing, and I would rather have one argument developed properly than five recited. So please build the essay around a pair of cases I have constructed myself, described below. Roughly three quarters of the essay should be spent on these cases and on what follows from them; the remainder may be used to state the two positions precisely enough that the discussion is intelligible.

Case One, the Vault. A small animal of the sort that can dry itself out and curl into a dormant husk is placed in a sealed vault at constant temperature. For forty years, by stipulation, nothing happens inside it. There is no metabolism, no chemical turnover, no repair, no internal causal traffic among its parts beyond whatever is required for those parts simply to stay where they are. Nothing touches it, no light falls on it, no part of it is replaced. Then the vault is opened, water is added, and the animal resumes its life: it feeds, moults, and carries on with the dispositions and the old injuries it had before. I take it that nearly everyone's first reaction is that the animal which resumes is the very animal that dried out, and that it was there, existing, all through the forty years.

Case Two, the Copy. Just before the animal dries out, its complete molecular arrangement is recorded at atomic resolution. During the forty years, a duplicate body is assembled from that record in a second vault, out of freshly prepared matter; meanwhile the original husk is destroyed without ever being wetted. At the end of the forty years water is added to the duplicate, and it resumes the same kind of life, with the same dispositions and the same old injuries. Almost everyone, I think, says that the resumer here is not the original animal.

Here is the worry that motivates the essay. In Case One, what actually obtains across the forty years, over and above the fact that a certain arrangement of matter was preserved and then reanimated, seems on reflection to be nothing at all, since by stipulation nothing occurs in the interval. The arrangement is preserved in Case One by the matter's sitting still, and in Case Two by a record plus an assembler; but if a stretch of time in which literally nothing happens contributes nothing, then the two cases differ only in how much causal machinery sits outside the animal, and it is not obvious that machinery of that kind is the sort of thing that could ground identity. So I suspect that the confident intuition about Case One may be a hangover from ordinary cases, where an animal that appears to sit still is in fact busily maintaining itself, and where that maintenance is genuine causal work.

With that set up, the essay should address the following, in whatever order makes the argument flow best:

1. Which view handles Case One and Case Two better? I am aware that both views can adopt the same criterion of diachronic identity — immanent causation, continuity of biological organisation, a closest-continuer rule — so please confront directly the objection that the cases therefore do not discriminate at all, and that the whole dispute is orthogonal to them.
2. Consider the third option: denying the datum, and holding that the animal simply does not exist during the forty years. Is that open to both views, or is it cheaper for one than for the other?
3. Does the following asymmetry matter? For the perdurantist, an object seems to exist at a time only by having a temporal part then, so a period of total stasis is a hole in the worm. For the endurantist there is no such automatic link, and the object may be wholly present at every moment of the interval without any of those moments involving change. If that is right, endurantism is better placed to say the animal exists throughout the gap while doing nothing. Is that a merit, or is a thing that exists for forty years without any activity an idle posit, detectable in principle by nobody?
4. Reach a verdict. Do these cases decide the debate, tip it, or leave it untouched? And separately, which view should the essay endorse overall? A conditional verdict is acceptable — for instance, that the cases settle things only given a particular criterion of identity — but I do not want a shrug, and I do not want a closing paragraph saying the matter is deep and further research is needed.
5. Give one paragraph near the end to the strongest objection to your own conclusion, and either answer it or explain honestly why you cannot.
6. Finish by saying what kind of further case or consideration would change your mind: if the Vault and the Copy do not decide the question, what would?

Two further notes. If you think a third position — one on which only momentary stages exist and talk of persistence is really talk of counterpart relations among them — handles my cases better than either of the two I named, please argue for it, but then be explicit about what it costs. And please write for a reader like me: explain each technical term the first time it appears, and do not attribute views to philosophers by name unless the attribution is doing argumentative work, since I have read very little of the primary literature and could not check it. I am not asking for a bibliography, footnotes, or a list of sources; I want the argument. Between 1,500 and 2,000 words, with headings if that helps.
