so i'm one of nine people on the provenance and ethics committee at a university museum. we meet a few times a year, we vote, and the votes are usually 6-3 or 5-4. here's our record — i'll give you the facts we had in front of us rather than the reasoning, because reconstructing the reasoning is part of what i want:

1. eleven brass plaques, West African, almost certainly taken in the 1897 sacking of Benin City. Came to us in 1961 from a London dealer, and we had literally no paper at all between the dealer and 1897. Claim from a national museums commission. → we transferred title and sent them.

2. a set of Akan goldweights and a carved linguist's staff, acquired 1911 from a missionary's estate, with a receipt, a stated price, and two family letters describing the purchase from a goldsmith's household as a friendly transaction at what the letters call a good price. Claim from the same sort of national body. → retained.

3. ancestral human remains collected in 1904 by a government survey party, no transaction record of any kind, no names, no consent. → returned unanimously, barely argued.

4. illuminated manuscripts removed from a highland monastery by British troops in 1868, bought by our founder at a London auction in 1912. Two competing claims: the national government, and a private individual claiming descent from the monastery's patron. → we transferred to the government and declined the individual, on the grounds (it's in the minutes) that he had no standing to speak for the monastery.

5. chieftaincy regalia acquired in 1936 from the widow of a colonial district officer, with a typed note saying the officer "was presented with" the set as a farewell gift. Claim from the current holder of that office, who told us the regalia belong to the office rather than to any individual chief, and that they're still used in an installation ceremony. → returned.

6. about forty carved ivory tusks and a large bundle of trade textiles, bought 1898–1905 through documented purchases from coastal traders at prices consistent with everything else we know about the period market, in a region with no military action in those years. Claim from a national body. → retained.

the new one lands on our agenda next month and it's a mess. a carved stool, a brass bell, and a bundle of divination objects, all acquired together in 1928 by the wife of a colonial agricultural officer. we have a receipt for £4, which by our own comparators from case 6 was roughly right for the period. we have the officer's diary, which says the seller "seemed glad to be rid of it" and that the money went on seed. and we have district records we only found last year showing the village was under a collective fine at the time for non-payment of a tax, and that the officer in question was one of the men collecting it.

claimants: the head of a lineage, who says the stool is a seat of authority that no individual was ever entitled to sell, that the bell is still rung once a year, and that the divination bundle hasnt been used in decades; the national museums commission, which says it will hold the group in trust and display it; and a diaspora association that funded the research that turned up the district records and wants a say in where everything goes.

one more wrinkle: the stool has serious insect damage. our conservator says it cannot travel without months of stabilization, and the lineage head says he would rather it decayed at home than stayed here.

so what should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? specifically: which of the six is the closest analog, and at what point does it stop being one. what rule we've actually been running — i can name four that seem to fit our whole record and i cannot choose between them (was the acquisition valid under the law and property norms of the time; can the museum discharge the burden of proof about how it got the thing; was the seller's consent real given the colonial context; is the object still in use by a practice that survived) and i suspect there's a fifth i'm missing, possibly about who gets to claim at all. a recommendation for this case, including whether we should seperate the stool from the bell and the bundle and send them to different places, and to whom. and if the record genuinely doesn't decide some part of it, say which part and tell me what principle we'd be committing ourselves to under each option — i want the version where you admit we're making new law rather than applying old law. plus, if there's some kind of future claim that would force us to choose between the candidate rules, name it so we know what we're deferring.

and if you think i've set this up wrong — like, six close votes can't pin down a rule at all, or "consistency with ourselves" is the wrong standard for a body like ours, or one of my four candidate rules isn't really a candidate — say that straight instead of playing along. i'd much rather hear it.
