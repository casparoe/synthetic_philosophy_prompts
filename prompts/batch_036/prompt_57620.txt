Here is something I have been turning over on my own for months, and I would like a number from you rather than a survey of what philosophers have said about it. The claim: there are cases in which a person's own guilt removes his standing to blame someone else for the very same failing, so that identical words from someone without that history would have been entirely proper blame. My working example is a friend who has lied to me repeatedly telling me, correctly, that I lied to him. My credence that the defect in such a case lies in the blaming itself, rather than merely in its tact, timing, or persuasiveness, is about 0.75. Give me yours, but decomposed. Roughly what probability do you put on "standing" picking out a genuine normative status rather than a loose conversational convention? On blame being essentially a claim to a standard that the blamer himself holds, such that his own violation undermines the claim? On the person being blamed having an entitlement to object that survives the criticism's being true? On there being any real difference between the attitude of blaming and the act of expressing it, once the expression is sincere, private, and gentle? Assign a figure and one sentence of justification to each of those terms, say how you combine them into your headline number, and then tell me which term that number is most sensitive to and what would move it. Please write in continuous prose, the way you would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
