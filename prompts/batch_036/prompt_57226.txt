I left school at sixteen and have spent twenty-three years running a small building firm, and for some years now I have been reading Keynes's 1930 essay "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren" on my own. One thing in it refuses to come apart for me, and I want to turn it into a research plan. My intuition: the prediction of a fifteen-hour working week depends entirely on his division of human needs into absolute ones, which can be met once and for all, and relative ones, which we feel only because meeting them sets us above our neighbours. But the good life he sketches for the hours we are supposed to win back — cultivation, art, the enjoyment of beauty, living wisely and agreeably — looks comparative all the way down, and if it is, the pressure to keep working never stops where he says it does. Perhaps what is missing is a third category: a need that is neither subsistence nor status, and that grows as it is satisfied. I have been shortlisted for a six-week independent-scholar residency, open to people outside universities, and I need the proposal that goes with it.

Please write as a philosopher of economics who knows this literature from the inside. First think carefully about whether a sharper and more tractable version of my question exists; if you land on a different one, put that one in the first item. Then give the proposal as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion: the question stated precisely enough to have a determinate answer; why the two usual responses — treating the essay as a falsified forecast about hours worked, or as a sermon on greed — leave it untouched; the method I would actually use over six weeks, and why that rather than simply testing his predictions against the record; what a successful result looks like and what would count as the project failing; and an honest note on what might make it too large for six weeks. No bibliography or citations; I will add any references myself.
