A graduate workshop on observation selection effects closes with a joint statement from two participants who have argued for three days. One defends the self-sampling assumption — roughly, that you should reason as though you were a random sample from the reference class of observers in your epistemic situation — and accepts the Doomsday-style conclusion it delivers. The other defends the self-indication assumption — roughly, that the prior probability of a hypothesis should be scaled by the number of observers it implies — and holds that this scaling exactly cancels the Doomsday shift. Draft the document their collaboration should produce.

Both parties will work from the same four stipulated cases, and the statement must record each side's answer to every case together with the rule that generated it, so that a reader can see precisely where the divergence enters:

Case A (sealed lab). A physically fair coin is flipped behind a screen. Heads: exactly three people are woken in identical, mutually non-communicating cubicles. Tails: exactly three thousand are. Everyone knows the whole arrangement. You wake in a cubicle. (i) Before learning your cubicle number, what is your credence in Heads? (ii) After being told you occupy cubicle 2, what is it?

Case B (rank). You are the five-billionth member of your species ever born. The total number who will ever be born is either eight billion or eight trillion, and your non-indexical evidence is neutral between the two. What credence do you now assign the smaller total?

Case C (cosmology). Two theories agree on every physical fact an inside observer could ever check, but one implies about 10^12 observers in the universe and the other about 10^30. What credence does each side assign the larger theory before any observation, and is that number an admissible output of a normative theory of evidence or a reductio of the assumption that produced it?

Case D (reference class). Repeat Case A, except that on Tails the additional 2,997 are exact functional duplicates of you realized in hardware, sharing your memories of the arrangement and cut off from you causally. Does the reference class include them? Is your answer dictated by your assumption, or is it a further choice your assumption leaves open — and if it is left open, is the choice principled or arbitrary? Does the same question arise for the other side's assumption?

Structure the statement in four parts.

1. A numbered list of claims both can sign: what they agree on about the formal setup (that both are conditionalizing on self-locating evidence, that the coin's physical chance is one half, that the cases are stipulated to contain no other discriminating evidence), about observation selection effects generally, and about the state of the reference-class problem. Where one side will sign only with a rider, print the rider rather than dropping the claim.

2. A disagreements table. For each row: the sub-question in dispute; the self-sampling side's statement of its own position in wording its defender accepts; the self-indication side's statement of its own position in wording its defender accepts; the numerical answers each side gives in Cases A through C; why the two statements are not merely notational variants of one another; and what both sides concede would bear on the question.

3. The cruxes proper: the two or three sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one party, the case from A through D (or a new one they may propose) that both accept as bearing on each, and — recorded before the workshop adjourns — what each side predicts about it and what each would count as evidence against its own assumption. Say explicitly whether any conceivable observation could discriminate the two, and if none could, say what keeps the disagreement substantive rather than verbal.

4. A short section isolating any part of the dispute that turns out to be terminological — in particular, whether anything rests on what each party means by conditioning on one's own existence versus on being a random sample from a class — and stating what remains after that part is stripped away.

Constraints: no citations, no bibliography, no author names, no quoted passages, and no adjudication — the deliverable is a text both parties would put their names to, not a verdict and not a dramatized exchange. It must be intelligible to a first-year graduate student who has never encountered this literature, and each party's own preferred terminology must stay inside that party's column.
