A team working on eliciting latent knowledge from a large language model reports the following. They train a linear probe on residual-stream activations, recorded while the model answers multiple-choice general-knowledge questions, to predict whether a sampled answer will be correct; the probe generalizes to held-out questions and to rephrasings of familiar ones. They then add a multiple of the probe's weight vector to the activations at each layer during inference. Two effects emerge. On questions that unsteered sampling gets right perhaps forty percent of the time, steering markedly raises accuracy, as though the model were being pushed toward information it already had. On questions the unsteered model never answers correctly across many samples, steering does not elicit correct answers; it yields fluent, wrong ones, and the model's stated confidence rises by roughly as much as it does on the partially known questions.

Please describe the most important implications of this pattern for the claim that such models know considerably more than they say. In particular: does the result favor treating latent knowledge as a single recoverable internal state that can be amplified, or as a distribution of partially formed candidate answers that steering merely reweights? What does the confidence gain on the never-correct questions imply about the relationship between the property the probe detects and the model's own disposition to assert? And what follows for the proposal to deploy probes of this kind as confabulation or deception monitors, given that a monitor reading off the same direction would register knowledge precisely where the model is producing fluent error? Finally, say whether any of your conclusions would change if the probe had instead been trained on contrastive pairs of true and false statements rather than on correct and incorrect answers.
