my partner defends a welfare-econ dissertation in a few weeks and the whole thing is basically 200 pages circling Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, so I want to make them something physical to tape above the desk: a poem. Not a poem about utilitarianism in the fuzzy inspirational sense — specifically about the theorem, and specific enough that they'd catch me if I got it wrong (they will, in red pen). So the machinery should be visible: individual von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities over lotteries, a social or moral observer whose preferences over the very same lotteries are also vNM and satisfy Pareto, and out falls the result that her utility is affine in theirs — a positive weighted sum, weights and all, that's the 1955 theorem. Then the second, seperate step that everybody collapses into the first: the "as if" impartial observer with equal subjective probability of being each person, which is what actually buys you *equal* weights and hence average utility, and which is where all the philosophy lives.

Form: a pantoum, five quatrains, lines 2 and 4 of each stanza returning as lines 1 and 3 of the next, with the second and fourth lines of the final stanza being the first and third lines of the opening one. I picked that form on purpose and I want it to carry the argument rather than decorate it — the recycling *is* the linearity, the same line reappearing in a new slot the way one person's utility keeps entering the sum with its weight unchanged no matter which lottery you feed in or how you permute who gets what. Two of the recycled lines should do the two-step distinction: one line for Pareto, which hands you the weighted sum without ever needing interpersonal comparison at all (the weights just soak up whatever affine rescaling each u_i gets), and one line for the equal-chance / observer step. I want the Pareto line to open sounding like a discovery and end sounding like bookkeeping, and the veil line to open sounding like a harmless technical assumption and end up holding the whole poem up, because that's the honest distribution of the load. If my partner can point at the last stanza and say "that's where it stops being a theorem," it worked. Bonus points if one of the repeated lines turns on "positive" doing double duty — positive weights, and the observer's cheerfulness about adding people together.

A few things to keep it from going wrong: don't let the poem claim the theorem proves utilitarianism or settles interpersonal comparability, since the scales have to be pinned down before the weights mean anything; don't slide Harsanyi's observer into Rawls's original position, his is a claim about someone's subjective probabilities over being each person, not a device for deriving principles, and conflating them is exactly the error I'm trying to avoid; and don't name-drop Arrow or Bergson–Samuelson as if they were the same result. Otherwise keep it human — an actual kitchen table, an actual coin, two or three named-ish people in the lottery, whatever makes the abstraction cost something. Length is set by the five quatrains; rhyme is optional, and pantoums don't need it, so don't force it and wreck the lines that have to repeat verbatim. Technical words are allowed to just sit there unexplained, since the one person this is for knows precisely what they mean.
