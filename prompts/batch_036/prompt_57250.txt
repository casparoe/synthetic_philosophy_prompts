I write a weekly column about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I also host a half-hour podcast version of it with my editor — small show, a few thousand listeners, but the people who do listen tend to be the ones working on the stuff. Next week's guest is an ML researcher who leads a team building what the field calls mechanistic anomaly detectors: instead of grading a deployed model on its outputs, you look at its internals and try to flag when it's computing in a way that departs from how a reference model computed on inputs you already trust. I've read enough of the literature to know the shape of the terrain — the detectors get trained on a trusted set, they score activations, they're pitched as a way to supervise models whose answers you can't check yourself, and the honest summary of the empirical results so far is "sometimes works, doesn't generalize." What I want to talk about is the concept hiding underneath all of that, which is that you can't even state what an anomaly is without an answer to when two computations count as the *same mechanism*. That's the thread I want to pull.

My guest's position, roughly: mechanisms should be individuated at the level of what the computation does, not at the level of the particular circuit or the particular activation geometry that realizes it — two models that reach their answers by functionally equivalent routes are running the same mechanism even if every neuron involved is different, and raw distance-in-activation-space is a crude stand-in for that. They're also very good at shutting down the metaphysical line of questioning with something like "we don't have to settle what a mechanism is, we have to ship a detector with a decent ROC curve," and then moving on to benchmark numbers. I want to make that move progressively harder to pull off.

So: draft me eight interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer they're most likely to give — not a hypothetical answer, the one this kind of researcher actually gives, so write the follow-up as a response to it. Please include at least one question each on (a) the reference problem: the baseline model is itself unverified and only partly understood, so anomalies are being measured against something nobody can fully characterize; (b) the multiple-realizability worry: if functional equivalence is the criterion, then anything that preserves behavior counts as the same mechanism, so what's left for the detector to catch beyond ordinary out-of-distribution detection; (c) where the right answer arrives by a mechanism you've never certified, and where a certified mechanism produces something wrong — which failure matters more and whether the framework has a principled answer or just says "depends on deployment"; (d) self-masking, i.e. a mechanism that has been shaped to produce reference-looking internals; and (e) what an operator is supposed to do with a flag that says "different mechanism" but doesn't say what mechanism. Then close with the question about what result would make them abandon algorithmic-level individuation altogether — that's the one that breaks the deflection, so save it for last. Number them, bold the question, put the follow-up on the line under it starting with "If they say:". No preamble, no glossary of what MAD is, no notes to me about tone. Hard cap: 650 words for the whole thing, and no question longer than three sentences — I'm reading these off a page on air and my producer cuts me at 28 minutes, so anything I have to paraphrase on the fly is worse than nothing.
