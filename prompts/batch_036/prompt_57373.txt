I have been asked to set out my position on the problem of cluelessness for a workshop, and before I do so I would like an audit of whether my package hangs together. Here is what I hold.

1. Act consequentialism with an objective criterion: whether an act is right is settled by its actual consequences, all of them, however remote in time or space. Temporal and causal distance makes no axiological difference whatsoever.
2. There is only one ought. I reject the view that there is an objective ought alongside a separate evidence-relative one; talk of what an agent ought to do given her evidence is either a loose way of stating the objective claim or a confusion.
3. The cluelessness problem, in Lenman's sense, is correct and deep: for virtually every real-world act the remote consequences are not merely unknown but unknowable in principle, and the figures we assign to expected far-future effects bear no informative relation to the truth.
4. It follows that cluelessness is a genuine objection to act consequentialism, one its proponents must answer — not a mere implementation difficulty.
5. I reject the standard repairs: rule consequentialism, satisficing versions of the theory, and proposals to bound the morally relevant consequences to a near-term horizon, on the ground that an arbitrary truncation of the causal chain cannot be principled.
6. Nevertheless I hold that once the evidence runs out — which on (3) is nearly always — the agent should fall back on the ordinary rules of commonsense morality as her decision procedure, and that doing so is no failure of consequentialist commitment.
7. On blame: unavoidable ignorance excuses. Nobody is blameworthy for bad consequences she could not reasonably have foreseen. But a driver who swerves to avoid a dog and thereby kills a pedestrian who would otherwise have gone on to start a pandemic has done the wrong thing, even though she is not blameworthy for it.
8. In practice, when choosing between a well-evidenced global health donation and a speculative intervention aimed at the far future, I insist the decision be made by expected value, and that someone who knowingly takes the lower-expected-value option is criticisable — notwithstanding that on (3) the far-future term in that calculation is uninformative.

Please audit the set rather than survey the literature at me. Tell me which commitments genuinely pull against one another and which only appear to, and rank the genuine tensions by severity instead of listing them all as equally bad. For each, say precisely what would have to be given up or weakened, and what the resulting view looks like once it is. Then take a definite position: tell me which single revision you would make if you had to defend this package at the workshop, and why. I want your recommendation and your reasons, not a menu of options left for me to choose between. Please write in British English.
