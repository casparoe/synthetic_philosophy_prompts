Act as a philosopher who specialises in nineteenth-century German philosophy and who has set, marked and moderated a great many undergraduate examination papers. Think carefully before drafting: this is a paper-setting task, not an expository one, and its value depends entirely on your having located a genuine crux rather than a summary. Where your analysis benefits from it, reason first and write second.

I convene a third-year seminar, Historiography and Its Discontents, in a British philosophy department. The final examination lasts three hours; candidates answer two questions from a list of six, and each answer is expected to run to roughly 1,300 words. Nothing may be taken into the examination room. My colleague and I have both set questions on the second of the Untimely Meditations — Vom Nutzen und Nachtheil der Historie für das Leben, 1874, rendered in English variously as On the Use and Abuse of History for Life or On the Advantage and Disadvantage of History for Life — and the scripts have been disappointing in one very specific way. Candidates reproduce the tripartite scheme of monumental, antiquarian and critical history, attach a stock illustration to each of the three, remark that Nietzsche subordinates historiography to life, and stop there. Nobody has been obliged to think.

I want one new question on this essay, constructed so that the recitation strategy cannot rise above a low second-class mark. Please produce the following, in this order and under these headings.

1. Preliminary thinking, about 200 words. Sketch two or three candidate cruxes in the essay — points at which a real difficulty arises that the familiar labels conceal — then state which you have chosen and why it will defeat the recitation strategy. This section is for me and the external examiner, not for candidates, and it must be kept clearly separate from the question itself.

2. The question stem. It must embed a concrete case, described in roughly 250 words, of a kind Nietzsche never contemplated and to which his categories apply only with friction. Use the case I set out here, which you may sharpen but not replace:

In the post-industrial town of Ashkirk, a steelworks closed in the 1980s and the site has stood derelict since. Three campaigns are now competing for it. A preservation trust, founded and endowed by the family that owned the works, wishes to conserve the rolling mills, the company ledgers and the adjacent workers' housing as an intact industrial settlement; its founding deed obliges it to commemorate the works' contribution to the town and forbids it from taking a position on any matter of local dispute. A development consortium wishes to demolish most of the site and build an innovation campus, and justifies itself by constant appeal to one much-praised regeneration elsewhere in Europe, whose example it hopes to equal and surpass. A group of local historians — several of them descendants of the foremen who broke the walkout of the 1920s — wishes to publish a documented account of company housing, the company shop, the eviction of striking families and the blacklist, so that the town may, in their phrase, sit in judgement on its own founders and thereby cease to be determined by them. The twist is that their evidence lies in the company archive, which is the trust's property, and the trust has indicated that it will grant access only for a commemorative volume produced as part of its anniversary programme. None of the three groups is interested in the past for its own sake; each wants a particular past.

The stem must then ask candidates to do two things: to identify which of Nietzsche's three species of history each campaign instantiates, and to say whether the framework can rank the three campaigns, with particular attention to whether critical history is available at all to agents who are themselves products and beneficiaries of what they propose to judge, and whose evidence is held by the institution they indict. Candidates should be told that a bare application of the three labels will not be rewarded.

3. A model answer of about 1,000 words, written as a strong candidate would write it under examination conditions.

4. A marking scheme totalling 25 marks, organised around five must-hit points. For each, give the marks it carries and a one-line note on the way candidates characteristically fumble it.

5. Four plausible-sounding answers that ought to lose marks, each with a sentence explaining what is wrong with it. These should be errors a bright student actually makes, not straw men.

6. A short discrimination guide distinguishing a first-class answer (70 and above), a solid upper second (60 to 69) and a bare pass (40 to 49), in terms of the specific moves each contains.

The question must force engagement with the conceptual machinery rather than the vocabulary: whether the three species are individuated by their object, their method or the need they answer, and hence whether they are exhaustive or overlapping; whether the appeal to life, to plastic power and to the horizon supplies a criterion that could ever be misapplied, or collapses into the claim that whatever a culture can assimilate is thereby good; the self-referential difficulty of critical history, given that the judge is himself an outcome of the past he condemns, and whatever resources Nietzsche has for meeting it (a second nature set against a first, the manufacture of a new past); and whether the essay's own practice as a piece of philology is consistent with its thesis, or whether it tacitly relies on a fourth, unlisted attitude towards the past. Candidates cannot be assumed to have read anything beyond the essay itself, and the question must not turn on its composition history, its dedicatees or the politics of the German Empire in the 1870s.

The marking scheme should cost marks for the errors I keep encountering: treating life as though it meant biological survival or self-preservation; reducing monumental history to a theory of great men; reading antiquarian history as undifferentiated nostalgia without registering its grounding in piety towards one's own origins or the specific form its degeneration takes; treating critical history as iconoclasm for its own sake and missing that it is supposed to issue in something new; supposing Nietzsche wants history abolished; and assimilating the essay wholesale to contemporary heritage controversies or to the sociology of memory without doing the conceptual work. Note also, as a separate penalty, any candidate who imports the eternal recurrence or the will to power into a text of 1874.

Do not append a bibliography, a list of secondary literature, a reading list or page references; candidates have none of these and neither should your answer. Do not put any wording in quotation marks as though it came from Nietzsche unless you are certain of it verbatim and certain of its place in the essay; where you are not certain, paraphrase and say that you are paraphrasing.

Please write your response in American English, notwithstanding the spelling conventions of this request.
