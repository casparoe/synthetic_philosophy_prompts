In six weeks I present a paper at the annual workshop of a European social choice group: a room of roughly twenty-five people, the majority of them mathematical economists working on aggregation, inequality measurement, and the axiomatics of social welfare functionals; two or three philosophers of economics concerned with what von Neumann–Morgenstern utility functions mean and on what basis they may be compared across persons; one prioritarian working in population ethics; and a scattering of experimentally minded people who care mainly about whether the axioms are descriptively true. Nobody there is a decision theorist in the Savagean-foundations sense, and nobody will want to hear my metaethics. What I need is a preparation sheet for the question period, not a defense of my view.

My thesis, in outline. Harsanyi's social aggregation theorem has been read for decades as an argument for utilitarianism, when in fact its normative load is carried by a premise the theorem neither states nor requires. I call it the transposition premise: the assumption that when the impartial observer contemplates a prospect placing her, with some probability, in person i's position, her extended utility for being-i-in-that-state is numerically equal — not merely ordinally isomorphic, but equal on a scale fixed independently of i — to person i's own von Neumann–Morgenstern utility for that state. Four steps. First, the theorem is a representation result: if individual and social preferences over lotteries satisfy the expected-utility hypothesis and a Pareto indifference condition, social utility is a positive linear combination of individual utilities. Second, since each individual's von Neumann–Morgenstern utility is unique only up to positive affine transformation, the coefficients in that combination are not interpretable until the scales are calibrated across persons, and the framework supplies no such calibration. Third, Harsanyi's own appeal to a postulate of psychological similarity — that an observer with the same information and the same tastes would have the same preferences — is precisely an attempt to supply that calibration, and it is a substantive empirical or philosophical assumption rather than a requirement of rationality. Fourth, and this is my positive proposal: I weaken Pareto indifference to what I call calibrated Pareto indifference, under which two lotteries count as socially indifferent only once a prior calibration lottery has fixed the affine scales. A calibration lottery is a common non-degenerate lottery over two publicly identified reference prospects, a best-case and a worst-case state, which every individual ranks in the same order and evaluates at the same probability. I claim this blocks the derivation of linearity while retaining enough structure to rule out dictatorship, and that the resulting class of representations properly contains both classical utilitarianism and a family of rank-dependent and prioritarian forms.

I already know the family of objections I expect: Sen's charge that the theorem concerns one observer's rational preferences and therefore yields no ethical conclusion at all; Weymark's insistence that the interpersonal addition of von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities requires more than the theorem delivers; the prioritarian's point that a veil of ignorance collapses distinct attitudes to risk over one's own life into a single ranking; and the economist's point that weakening an axiom is a modelling choice with a price, and that my price may be completeness or non-vacuity. What I want is the version of these, and of whatever else this particular room will press, in their vocabulary.

Please give me eight to ten objections, ordered by how much damage they would do, each tagged with the subgroup most likely to raise it and phrased as that subgroup would phrase it — the axiomatizers speaking of hypotheses, representation classes, and whether my conditions are independent; the philosophers of economics speaking of measurement, meaning, and the observability of the calibration; the experimentalists asking whether anyone in a laboratory actually behaves as my reference prospects require. For each, a reply I can deliver standing up in two minutes, and a candid note on where that reply is softest. Separate the merely inconvenient from the fatal: tell me which one or two, if I answer them badly, sink the paper. Tell me what to concede in the first five minutes to buy credibility with the axiomatizers, and identify the single reply that has to land for the project to survive the session. Then tell me the question I am hoping nobody asks — I suspect it concerns whether my calibration lottery is even well defined when individuals hold different subjective beliefs, in which case the condition is vacuous and my representation class collapses into everything. Finally, flag any objection I appear braced for that this audience will in fact never raise, since over-preparing costs me the time I need elsewhere.

Close with a short assessment of your own standing: how confident you are in the ordering and in the verdicts of fatal versus inconvenient, and which single claim in the sheet you are least sure of. In particular, say so plainly if any objection you attribute to Sen, to Weymark, or to the prioritarian literature misstates what those authors actually argued, because I will not have time to check before the talk and would rather know now which parts of the sheet rest on your reconstruction rather than on the record.
