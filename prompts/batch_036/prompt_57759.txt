so my essay is due thursday and I think I've argued myself into something I don't believe. It's for the philosophy club at my school (not a class, we just meet in the library and argue), and the topic was self-deception — like the guy who's certain his girlfriend isn't cheating on him when literally everyone else can see it, or my coach who insists we're the best team in the league after we go 2-9.

I wanted to show that "he's lying to himself" is confused, so I built this:

(1) You can't get yourself to believe something just by deciding to. Try it right now: believe there's a hundred dollars in your left pocket. You can't. Belief isn't switchable.
(2) Deceiving someone means intentionally causing them to believe something you know is false.
(3) Self-deception is one person doing both jobs, so the self-deceiver knows p and intentionally causes himself to believe not-p.
(4) But (1) says he can't. At the moment he'd be causing the belief, the knowledge that p is sitting right there in the same head, so there's nothing for the process to get a grip on. The attempt to believe not-p dies on contact with the knowledge that p.
(5) So either the belief changes without any intention behind it — which isn't deception, it's just bias, the ordinary wishful-thinking / confirmation-bias kind — or it doesn't change at all.
(6) Therefore self-deception doesn't exist. There's only error plus bias.

And here's where it runs away from me. Basically every case I'd call self-deception is a case of someone steering their own evidence. My cousin with his startup quit reading anything critical about his market, unfollowed the one guy who asked hard questions, and now he genuinely thinks he can't fail. But if (4) is right that you can't cause yourself to believe against what you know, then that steering isn't a thing either — the avoiding and the rehearsing are just more bias, stuff that happens to you. And stuff that happens to you isn't something you did.

(7) So nobody is ever responsible for any of their false beliefs. Not my cousin, not the coach, not anybody.

Also, and this is smaller, (6) makes a wierd mess of people saying "I was fooling myself all along," like they're reporting on an event that (6) says can't happen.

(7) is where I want to stop and I can't see how. (1) seems obviously true to me and (2) is basically just what the word means, so I keep getting pushed back to (6) and then to (7). If I have to give something up I'd rather give up (1) than accept (7) — but giving up (1) sounds insane, you'd be committed to people believing at will, and honestly even then I'm not sure (4) stops following.

So: which step is the bad one, why did it look fine when I wrote it, and what do I owe once I drop it? And separately — is there a version of this that's straight-up valid, where the problem is a false premise instead of bad reasoning? I need to be able to say something better in the essay than "but self-deception obviously happens."
