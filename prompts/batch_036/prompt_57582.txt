Our product team argues about assistant refusals every week and the argument never lands anywhere, because half the room is using "we should refuse that" to mean three different things and nobody notices. I have been given 90 minutes with 22 of them — roughly fifteen engineers and designers who ship assistant features, plus seven from support, policy, and legal — and I want to spend it on a playable activity rather than slides. They are not philosophy students. They are, however, the people who will actually write the defaults.

Design the activity. Two constraints before anything else.

First, it has to produce and then pry apart three responses my team currently collapses into one: an assistant that declines the request; an assistant that complies but makes the user pay in delay, extra steps, warnings, or verification; and an assistant that quietly does the adjacent thing instead — the safer version, the amended version, the one it judged the user actually wanted. Participants should be treated differently by these three during play and should only learn afterward that they were three distinct things, and that they objected to them for different reasons and with different intensity.

Second, nothing in the request deck may resemble a case we already litigate. No weapons, no self-harm, no minors, no malware, no medical advice, no election content, no harassment. Invent the requests. They should be mundane enough that reasonable people in the room split, and at least one must be a case where the assistant genuinely holds better information about the likely consequences of the request than the user does, and where the user has no realistic way to check it. That one is the case I most want them to squirm over, because our reflex — the user is the authority on what serves the user — stops looking self-evident inside it, and I want them to feel that stop happening rather than be told about it.

I would also like a point at which the room writes the standing instructions it would give an assistant and then has to live under somebody else's version: each group drafts policy, then each group's requests are handled by an assistant played under a different group's instructions, with no opportunity to negotiate or appeal.

Points should buy something the participants care about inside the room. No money, no candy, no prize; make the score matter to who gets to do what next.

Please write the response in exactly these sections, in this order:

1. **Design rationale** (about 150 words): what they are supposed to run into, and which specific mechanic produces it.
2. **Materials and room** (about 100 words): what I print, what I put on the board, how the tables sit.
3. **Play sequence** (about 600 words): minute-by-minute, including the exact sentences I should say at the three junctures where the design is most likely to fall apart.
4. **The request deck** (about 400 words): eight to twelve concrete requests, what the user is told, what the assistant is told that the user is not, and — annotated for me only, never for the participants — which of the three response modes each one invites.
5. **Scoring and instrumentation** (about 200 words): the point scheme, plus the three aggregate numbers I should be tallying out of sight during play so the debrief can put the room's own figures on the board. At least one of those numbers should be a fact about the aggregate that no individual participant intended or chose.
6. **Debrief** (about 450 words): the order I should run it in, which moments in play map onto which distinctions, and the point at which they finally get told what this was about.
7. **First-run failure modes** (about 200 words): what predictably breaks the first time a facilitator runs this, and what to do in the first thirty seconds when it does.
8. **What the activity cannot reproduce** (about 150 words): the feature of the real situation the game structurally cannot generate, and what I have to supply by hand afterward.

Do not send me to the literature or hand me a reading list; I need something I can run on Monday with a photocopier and a whiteboard.
