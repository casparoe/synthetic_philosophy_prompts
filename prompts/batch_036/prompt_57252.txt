I'm convening a reading group on Du Châtelet's *Discourse on Happiness* and two of us have been going round in circles for month, so I want to try the adversarial-collaboration trick. Write the joint statement the two of us ought to have produced.

Camp A — call them the hedonist-monists — reads the whole thing as a pleasure-maximising programme. Everything she values (the passions, the illusions, the cultivated tastes, even virtue) is valuable only insofar as it produces or protects felt pleasure, and her attack on the Stoic ideal of extirpating the passions is a purely psychological claim about what humans can't help feeling, not a claim about what's good.

Camp B — the engagement pluralists — says that's a flattening. On their reading, being moved, and being moved by things one is partly deceived about, has a value that isn't just a line-item in the pleasure balance; that's why she can rank a passionate life above a well-informed serene one even where the felt totals look comparable, and why she bothers keeping virtue in the account while flatly refusing to identify it with happiness.

What I want out of you:

1. A numbered section of claims both camps can actually sign. Not trivial ones — I want the substantive stuff they've been conceding in private all along: what both agree she's arguing against, what both agree the illusions thesis is doing in the structure of the essay, what both agree she is *not* claiming about virtue.

2. A disagreements table, one row per crux, with these columns: the crux stated neutrally; the crux stated in Camp A's own preferred wording, such that a Camp A person would sign it without wincing; ditto for Camp B; the kind of feature in the text that would settle it — described in general terms, and please don't invent quotations, page numbers or section headings, we can't check them and it isn't the point; and finally each camp's prediction, written down in advance, about what that feature will turn out to look like.

3. The cruxes I want in the table, at minimum:
   - **The tranquillity crux.** She plainly prefers the agitated life of the passions to the sage's calm. Can Camp A absorb this by insisting the calm of indifference simply contains less felt pleasure than it appears to, or does the preference only make sense if being moved is valued for itself?
   - **The transparency crux.** The essay tells its reader that illusions are necessary for happiness. Someone who accepts that may find it harder to be happily deceived. Does that make the programme self-defeating, and does it bite harder on one reading than the other?
   - **The virtue crux.** If virtue isn't happiness, what work is it doing in there at all?
   - **The cruel-illusion crux.** An illusion that sustates the subject but costs other people something real. My hunch is that neither reading gets her a good answer here, and I want each camp to state explicitly what it has to concede.

4. A short section saying which parts of the row you think are merely verbal — in particular, whether her use of pleasure-talk is already loose and broad enough to swallow Camp B's extra value, in which case the whole fight is about the word "hedonism" rather than about her, and should be called off.

5. A closing section: for each camp, what it would take to make them give ground, and whether there's any single consideration both would accept as genuinely moving the issue.

Please also work this vignette in as a shared test case, since we've both already agreed its relevant:

**The disenchanted collector.** A woman in her late fifties is told, by a friend who means well, that her three absorbing enthusiasms are all illusions — the decades-long affair with a man who will never leave his wife, her belief that her botanical watercolours are any good, and her conviction that her late father's letters were written out of love rather than obligation. She stops none of the three. She reports honestly that the enjoyment has dropped by roughly a third and hasn't come back. Is her life worse than it was before she was told, and what explains the answer? Get each camp to predict what the other will say about it *before* you write the other's reply.

It should read like a document both of us could put our names to — not a summary of the debate, not a verdict on who's right, and not a diagnosis of why we're stuck. Roughly 1200–1600 words, and no bibliography, we've both read the thing.
