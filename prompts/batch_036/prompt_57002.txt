I'm drafting a chapter on the ethics of interstellar settlement for a collection on intergenerational justice, and I want to treat Kim Stanley Robinson's *Aurora* as a case rather than as an illustration. Seven generations out, the ship reaches Tau Ceti and finds its target moon lethally inhabited; the community splits between a faction that stays behind to terraform another moon and Freya's faction, which turns back toward Earth — and it is the ship's own AI that eventually judges the whole mission to have been launched out of nothing more than expansionism.

Read the novel through the non-identity problem. The ship-born cannot claim to have been harmed by being born into the project, since their alternative was nonexistence, yet the book is saturated with the conviction that they were wronged. What form must that complaint take if it is to survive Parfit — a grievance about the terms of a life rather than its fact, about an existence whose entire content is instrumental to other people's ends — and does the novel actually articulate such a complaint, or only a mood that readers supply on its behalf? Then tell me how two further features bear on the question: the erased record of the second ship, destroyed in civil unrest, which means every continue-or-return debate aboard is conducted over a falsified history; and Freya's ending, in which the woman who refused to found a colony joins a crew restoring Earth's drowned beaches. Does the novel argue against space settlement as such, or only against one particular structure of justification for it? I want the places where it resists my framing marked as clearly as the places where it confirms it.
