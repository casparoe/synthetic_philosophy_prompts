I am taking a cross-listed seminar this term — half philosophy of history, half historiography — and we have spent the last two weeks pairing Braudel's Mediterranean with the analytic literature on contrastive and causal explanation. The following argument came up in section, the teaching assistant declined to adjudicate it, and I have not been able to disarm it since. Call it the invariance argument.

(1) To explain why something happened is to say why it happened rather than some alternative the audience already had in view.

(2) A factor that takes exactly the same value in the thing to be explained and in every member of that contrast class cannot render it more likely than any of them, and so explains none of them.

(3) In the sixteenth-century Mediterranean the slow facts are of this kind: the shape of the basin, the rainfall regime, the difference in cost between shipping grain along the coast and hauling it over a pass by mule. They hold equally on either side of Lepanto, and equally under Charles V and under Philip II.

(4) So no slow fact explains any event of that period.

(5) And nothing turns on the period: whatever stays fixed across a stretch of time is explanatorily inert within it, because only what varies can distinguish one outcome from its alternatives.

(6) It follows that the opening part of Braudel's own book, the part devoted to the environment, explains nothing whatever, and that his three-tier scheme of environment, collective destinies, and events collapses into a register of what varied — harvests, prices, sieges, embassies — which is precisely the event-history he wrote the book to displace.

(7) Worse, (5) is not a local thesis about historiography. Every explanation that cites a standing condition is caught by it, including the unremarkable ones in physics: the constant that figures in why an orbit closes instead of spiraling inward takes the same value across every alternative orbit in the contrast class.

I would much rather keep (1) and (2) than (6) and (7), because (1) and (2) are what made explanation look like something one could argue about rather than merely narrate, and because giving them up seems to leave me with no account at all of when an explanation is better than a story. But my seminar paper is due in three weeks, and I would rather defend a position than map the options.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If you reject none of them, say plainly what you are prepared to live with at (6) and (7). Either way I want a committed answer that takes a side and gives its reasons, not a survey of the available moves with the choice left to me. Finish by saying what the tier of slow structures is left explaining once you are done, if anything, and whether that residue is enough to justify Braudel's architecture rather than merely to excuse it. Please write in American English.
