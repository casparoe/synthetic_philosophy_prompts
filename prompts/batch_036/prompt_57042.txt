I teach a second-year political philosophy seminar: 28 students, one 80-minute session, no budget, and nothing they earn in the activity counts toward their grade. They have already met the distinction between brute luck and option luck and know the label "luck egalitarianism," but they have not met the standard objections to it, and I do not want to state any of those objections myself. I want them to manufacture one out of their own decisions and then have to sit in it. Please design a runnable in-class activity under the following constraints.

1. No veil of ignorance and no ex ante choice of principles. Everyone knows their own position throughout, and the allocation happens after the outcomes are known, because that is what the principle is actually about. I also do not want a rerun of the island auction.

2. The brute luck / option luck distinction must be produced by the mechanics rather than explained to them. Ideally the very same event counts differently for two participants depending on what each of them did beforehand, and they have to notice the asymmetry on their own.

3. Include a stage in which the group, acting as an allocation committee, writes down in its own words the rule it will apply, before any of the random elements are revealed. Then hold the committee strictly to the text it drafted. I want the unpleasant outcomes to be traceable to language they chose while it still looked costless.

4. Build in an information failure: in at least some cases the committee must be unable to determine whether a participant's disadvantage followed a choice or preceded it. This has to be a consequence of what gets disclosed and what stays sealed in an envelope, not of a case I read aloud from a script.

5. At least one participant's disadvantage must trace to a choice taken from a menu that was itself unequal, so that the committee has to decide whether that counted as choosing at all. Do not resolve this for them in the rules.

6. Someone in the room should finish ineligible for help under the committee's own rule, and it should be one of the students who argued hardest for that rule in stage 3.

Deliver: a materials list made of ordinary classroom objects; the rules as numbered steps with a minute count against each, totalling 80 minutes including the debrief; a payoff scheme expressed in tokens that convert to nothing real; roles, if roles are needed; a debrief that maps particular moments of the play onto the parts of the view they have just reenacted, including the places where what the committee wrote and what the principle says come apart; and two or three questions that move the discussion from the tokens to real claims about treatment, disability, and insurance, since that transfer will not happen on its own.

Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this with 28 people: which stage overruns its minutes, which role collapses or refuses to play, what a sharp student will say to break the game early, and how I should answer without naming the objection for them. They may use the label they already have, but the name of what they have just produced should come from them or from their reading afterwards.

Finally, be frank about what this activity cannot produce. My guess is that it has to do with the difference between being handed a token and being turned away at a clinic counter, but I want you to say precisely what the facilitator must supply that no set of mechanics can.

Open with three sentences stating the single experience the activity is engineered to produce and the one thing it must not be allowed to degenerate into. Then keep the entire response, materials and rules and debrief and failure modes and limits, under 900 words, with the rules as numbered steps carrying their minute counts.
