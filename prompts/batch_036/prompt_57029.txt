Set-up: two people arguing about the same question H for years, on the same evidence base. Say two epidemiologists who have both read every study there is.

(1) Rational agents have a common prior. Before any evidence comes in, everybody assigns the same probability to H, because priors aren't the product of evidence — they're just what you're left with when you have none.
(2) If two agents share a common prior and it's common knowledge between them what each of their posteriors for H is, then those posteriors are equal. That's Aumann's result about agreeing to disagree.
(3) So rational agents can never commonly know that they disagree about H.
(4) Our two epidemiologists have exchanged everything: each knows the other's number, each knows that the other knows, and they've been sitting in that state for years.
(5) That state just is common knowledge of differing posteriors. So by (3) at least one of them is failing to be rational — isn't conditionalizing, or is misreporting, or is fooling herself.
(6) Hence, in any public argument that has run for years with all the evidence on the table, sticking with your view isn't a rational response. It's evidence that you're self-deceived or can't do arithmetic.
(7) So in any long-running dispute at most one side is rational, and if you notice you're in the minority the correct move is to abandon your view rather than keep arguing.
(8) And the same reasoning shows a committee that knows everyone's credences has no need to deliberate at all. Just pool the numbers — average them, take the median, whatever — because all argument can ever be doing is moving evidence around, and once the evidence has moved there's nothing left to say.
