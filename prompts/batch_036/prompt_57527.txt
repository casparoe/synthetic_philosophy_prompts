I am drafting a paper for a value-theory seminar at my department — the deliverable is a six-thousand-word argument due in three weeks, and the seminar is run by a Kantian who is openly hostile to hedonism alongside two graduate students who work on the affective neurosciences, so the paper will be read closely by people who want it to fail. My topic is the heterogeneity problem for hedonism. As I take it, the problem is this: introspection and ordinary usage suggest that the pleasure of a cold drink on a hot day, of finally seeing the solution to a hard problem, of a warm bath, of relief when a threat turns out not to have been there, of music, and of sexual activity share no single felt quality, and Ryle pressed this far enough to deny that pleasure is a feeling at all. Both standard hedonist replies seem to me to fail, and part of my paper will say why. The phenomenalist reply, in the line that goes back to Sidgwick, on which what unifies pleasures is that they are feelings apprehended as desirable when felt, either posits a common quality nobody can isolate, so that the unity is asserted rather than shown, or it collapses into the claim that whatever we want to continue is pleasant, which is no longer a phenomenal account at all. The attitudinal reply, associated with Feldman, on which pleasure is a pro-attitude toward a state of affairs rather than a sensation, buys unity at the cost of ceasing to be about experience, and invites the charge that the real work is being done by desire-satisfaction — that the view has become a preference theory wearing a hedonist label.

The positive thesis I want to defend is one I have not managed to find anyone arguing for, so I need the case built from scratch rather than reconstructed: hedonists should say that pleasure picks out a homeostatic property cluster kind in roughly Richard Boyd's sense — a kind with no shared essence, unified instead by a family of overlapping properties that co-occur and mutually reinforce one another because of underlying causal mechanisms, the way biological species, or social kinds like money, are unified. For pleasure the cluster I have in mind is something like this: a positive affective signal that is wanted to continue while it occurs; that captures attention and makes its source salient; that reinforces the behavior that produced it; that is tracked by a common learning signal; and that supports the trade-off comparisons we make when weighing one good against another. None of these is present in every pleasure, and the absence of any one of them from a given pleasure does not make that state a non-pleasure; the kind is held together by the mechanism, not by a set of necessary and sufficient conditions. The normative payoff I need is that a homeostatic cluster kind can still ground reliable, projectible generalizations about what promotes wellbeing, so that hedonism's explanatory and practical ambitions survive the loss of a single essence. (A friend in computational psychiatry suggested I frame the whole thing in predictive-processing terms; I think that is a distraction and I would rather not go down it, but flag it if you think it genuinely helps.)

Please write out the argument for this thesis: numbered premises with the reasoning that connects them, at the level of detail a referee would demand, and constructed as an argument rather than a survey of positions. Then handle the four objections I expect in that seminar, one at a time, each stated as forcefully as the objector would state it and then answered. The first is the why-this-cluster objection: properties picked out merely by causal-functional co-occurrence carry no evaluative weight as such, so the account explains why we lump these states together but gives no reason to value the cluster rather than any proper subset of it — say, only the reinforcing signal — or any wider union, such as the cluster together with simple relief from pain or with the mere absence of distress; and if the reply is that the cluster is unified by the mechanism, the objector will say that a mechanism is a piece of psychology, and psychology does not entail value. The second is the disjunction objection: on this view the claim that pleasure is good amounts to the claim that a disjunction is good, and either the disjuncts are independently identifiable, in which case hedonism is redundant with something else, or they are identifiable only as the things we call pleasures, in which case the theory says nothing. The third is the extension objection: the cluster, described functionally, seems to admit what the hedonist wants out — the compulsive high of addiction shares every listed property, and it is precisely there that approach motivation, reinforcement, and salience come apart from what is good for the agent — while excluding what the hedonist wants in, such as a calm, motivationally inert appreciation that reinforces no behavior and captures no attention. The fourth is the natural-kind objection: homeostatic cluster kinds are supposed to be discovered through the successful clustering of properties in inquiry, and if the affective sciences do not converge on one such cluster — if what they offer instead is several dissociable systems, one for liking and one for wanting, which come apart in addiction, in depression, and in animal models — then there is no cluster kind to be found, and hedonism has not been rescued but left without a subject matter.

Finish with a short, unsentimental assessment of which of the four the argument actually survives and where it is most exposed, since I would rather find that out now than in the seminar.
