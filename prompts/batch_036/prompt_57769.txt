So I got talked into sitting on a standing franchise-and-elections commission in my city. Seven of us, and I'm the only philosopher, which apparently makes me "the theory person." We get four to six proposals a year and now neighboring towns send us theirs too, so whatever I bring needs to be something the others can run without me in the room. The commission is a city clerk, a retired probate judge, a high-school civics teacher, two neighborhood-association types and a small-business owner. None of them will read political theory and I'm not handing anyone a bibliography, so the reasoning has to live inside the procedure itself.

The current stack of three, which public comment keeps conlfating:
1. votes at 16 for city council and school board;
2. local voting rights for non-citizen residents with three or more years in the city;
3. compulsory voting in city elections, enforced by a $25 fine.

Give me a procedure we can run on any franchise proposal in about half an hour at a monthly meeting: the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where a proposal gets stopped with a flat "no," and where we have to admit the question is about values and hand it to a vote rather than pretending the procedure settled it. It should start by sorting the proposal, because I don't think these three are the same kind of question at all — two are about who's inside the demos and one isn't about the boundary but about whether voting can be demanded of people who are already inside. If you think that sort is wrong, say so and give me a better one. Any term of art you can't avoid, define in one line; this thing gets read aloud at a public meeting.

Then it has to face what I can't get around in my own head: any rule about who belongs in the demos is itself a rule that only some demos can make, so we can't fix the boundary democratically without going in circles. The procedure has to be explicit about what it's treating as settled before politics starts, instead of smuggling that in at step two.

It also has to handle the over-inclusion slide. If the test is "everyone affected gets a say," our zoning decisions entitle people three states over plus people not yet born, and we have no stopping rule. And I want something specifically about age: age is odd because unlike race or sex everybody passes through it if they live, so the excluded aren't a standing caste, they're a rotating one. Does that genuinely make the exclusion less objectionable, or am I comforting myself?

Same treatment for the compulsary one, where the interesting bits to me are whether a $25 fine is a penalty or just a price, and whether the usual case for compulsion (turnout gaps track income, so forcing everyone to vote helps the poor) survives the fact that the fine lands hardest on those same people.

Run it on all three. Don't rig it for me: I've said publicly that I favor votes at 16 and I'm against compulsion, so if the procedure sinks one of my positions or rescues the other, write that down, name the step that did it, and say why. Finish by marking which steps are judgment calls in a procedural costume, and by listing what this procedure simply cannot decide — the points where I'll have to stand up and tell a room full of people that we have no method.
