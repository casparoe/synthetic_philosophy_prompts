You are to act as a specialist in comparative constitutional practice, with particular expertise in the reserve powers of ceremonial monarchies in parliamentary democracies. Work carefully, and begin by generating candidate rules before settling on any conclusion; I am less interested in a quick answer than in the reasoning that gets you there.

**The setting.** I serve as secretary to a standing advisory panel in a mid-sized European constitutional monarchy. The crown's formal powers on paper are broad — the monarch appoints and dismisses ministers, summons, prorogues and dissolves parliament, and gives or withholds assent to bills — but our constitution's only text on government formation is a single sentence to the effect that the monarch appoints a prime minister, and everyone accepts that this has been overtaken by a century and a half of convention. For forty years the crown was advised by one constitutional counsel, whom I will call the Adviser, and his written opinions are treated by our panel as precedent in all but name: not binding, but never departed from without an explanation on the record. He died last year. The panel now has to rule in its own name, in his manner, for the first time.

**The Adviser's recorded rulings.**

1. After an inconclusive general election, the incumbent prime minister, whose party had come second in seats, asked to remain in office and meet the new parliament. The Adviser counselled that he might do so, and that the crown should not send for the leader of the largest party on the strength of the plurality alone.

2. A prime minister defeated on an explicit motion of no confidence sought a dissolution rather than resigning, while the leader of the largest opposition party produced a signed agreement with two smaller parties showing an arithmetic majority for a government she would head. The Adviser counselled that the crown should refuse the dissolution and send for her.

3. A government commanding a comfortable majority requested a six-week prorogation shortly before a scheduled debate on a matter of acute political difficulty, citing the need to reset the legislative programme. The Adviser counselled that there was no constitutional objection and that the crown should grant the request without inquiring into the government's reasons.

4. Parliament passed a bill transferring the appointment of senior judges from the crown-on-advice to the prime minister personally, with no independent commission and no requirement of consultation. The monarch asked whether assent could be withheld. The Adviser counselled that it must be given, that the crown's remedy lay in private warning to ministers, and that a refusal would place the monarch, rather than parliament, at the centre of the dispute.

5. A caretaker ministry, which had resigned after its prime minister's departure and was awaiting a successor, advised the crown to approve forty new political appointments to state boards in a single sitting. The Adviser counselled that the crown should ask the caretakers to defer until a government of full authority was in place — but that if, having been asked, they pressed the advice, the crown should sign.

6. A minister proposed that the monarch deliver a national broadcast endorsing a contested fiscal policy. The Adviser counselled refusal, on the ground that the crown cannot be seen to take a side. He nonetheless approved a factual reference to the same policy in a ceremonial address delivered the following week.

7. Asked by a journalist whether our crown should adopt the practice of a neighbouring realm — in which the speaker of parliament nominates a candidate who takes office unless a majority of members votes against the nomination — the Adviser replied, in writing, that a gap in our own arrangements is not by itself a reason to import another country's machinery. He said nothing further and did not answer the follow-up question.

**The case now before the panel.** The prime minister resigned this morning with immediate effect, without recommending a successor and without any party combination having declared itself. Parliament is fragmented: no pairing of the two largest parties is viable, and two rival leaders each assert publicly that they can command confidence, though neither has produced a signed agreement of the kind that appeared in ruling 2. Forty-eight hours after the resignation, the speaker of parliament has written to the crown offering to conduct a formal investiture ballot of members, to report the result within two days, and requesting an undertaking in advance that the crown will appoint whoever emerges. No statute, no constitutional provision and no past practice of our own authorises such a procedure; it is modelled openly on the neighbouring realm described in ruling 7. The outgoing prime minister remains available to serve in a caretaker capacity indefinitely.

The panel is being asked to choose among: (a) accepting the speaker's offer and giving the undertaking; (b) conducting the traditional private consultations with party leaders and forming the crown's own judgement of who is most likely to hold confidence; (c) appointing the leader of the largest party as head of a caretaker administration with an express mandate to let parliament settle the matter; (d) declining to act at all, leaving the outgoing prime minister in caretaker office until the parties reach an agreement among themselves; and (e) accepting the speaker's mechanism subject to conditions the crown would specify in advance, for instance that it be adopted only if it yields an absolute majority.

**What I want from you.**

1. Before addressing the new case, brainstorm the candidate rules that could generate the Adviser's seven rulings. For each rule, state it crisply as a principle of constitutional conduct — not as a label — and show that it accounts for every one of the seven, or identify precisely which ruling sits uneasily with it and what supplementary distinction the rule would need in order to absorb it. I expect at least four genuinely different rules to survive this test; rules that differ only in phrasing should be merged, and I want to see the merge explained.

2. Flag any pair of rulings that is in tension, and say which hidden distinction the Adviser must have been relying on to hold them together — in particular, how ruling 3's refusal to inquire into a government's reasons can be squared with ruling 2's very close attention to the political arithmetic.

3. For each surviving rule, state what it predicts the panel will decide in the new case among options (a) through (e), and why. Where a rule points to a variant or a combination rather than a single option, describe it.

4. Give your best prediction of the panel's ruling, in the Adviser's manner, and defend it: which rule you take the precedents as a whole to express, why you prefer it over the runners-up, and which precedent is the closest analog to the new case together with the point of disanalogy that does the most work. Ruling 7 is obviously relevant; tell me whether you read it as a soft veto on option (a), as a mere caution about the quality of the justification offered, or as no constraint at all, and what turns on that reading.

5. Recommend what the panel *should* rule if its priority is coherence with its own record rather than the outcome it independently thinks best. State honestly which constitutional principle the panel would be adopting by ruling for (a) and which by ruling against it — that is, what it would be committing itself to the next time a comparable gap opens, and what it would be giving up.

6. Propose one further hypothetical case, described in two or three sentences, that would discriminate between your two best-fitting rules: a situation in which they point in opposite directions and nothing else does. Explain why it separates them.

**Constraints.** Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations, and do not name real statutes, court decisions or scholars; if a real-world analogue genuinely changes your analysis, describe it generically and briefly rather than citing it. Treat the seven rulings exactly as I have stated them — do not supply richer reasons for them than are recorded, since the recorded reasons are all the panel has. Where your conclusion depends on an assumption about our institutions that I have not stated, name the assumption and say how the conclusion would change if it were false. Give a rough confidence level for your prediction in part 4, and organise the whole answer under the six numbered headings above.
