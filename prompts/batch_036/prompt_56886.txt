I am commissioning a short story for an anthology about how well-meaning people produce collective disaster, and I want one that treats the unilateralist's curse as a structure rather than as a mood. The situation to build from is this.

Eleven groups — four university labs, two startups, a national institute, a foundation program, and one person working essentially alone out of a leased greenhouse — have each independently developed a viable agricultural inoculant: a soil microbe, applied as a seed coating, that fixes nitrogen in cereals well enough to eliminate most purchased fertilizer. It works, and it works in the first two seasons better than anyone expected. It also, under a contested minority of field conditions, does something that cannot be walked back, and the eleven disagree sharply about how likely that is. Once any one of them releases at scale, the restraint of the other ten purchases nothing.

The protagonist coordinates a small, underfunded notification body that the eleven agreed to inform and gave no authority whatsoever. Write from inside her competence: she is very good at her job and still cannot solve this.

Requirements:

1. The story should run on arithmetic rather than on suspense. Have her compute, correctly and on the page, the probability that at least one of the eleven goes ahead after having judged the risk acceptable in error, given that she would put each individual group's chance of being confidently wrong at one in six. Then have that number fail her in an instructive way. The eleven are not independent: their risk assessments share a common ancestry — one foundational preprint, one assay everyone adopted, three postdocs who trained under the same two people — and she must work out what that does to the calculation in both directions at once, including what it does to the claim that a group's median judgment is wiser than any single member's.

2. Distinguish, in scene and not in narration, what everyone in the story believes they are in — a race, where being second is fatal — from what they are actually in: closer to a drawing in which all eleven hold tickets and nobody gains by moving first, except in scientific priority, which is precisely the motive that keeps hands near the lever. Let a senior character get this wrong loudly, and let the correction cost the protagonist something she cannot recover.

3. Her remedy is the obvious one: persuade all eleven to bind themselves in advance to act only on the group's median assessment, so that no single confident error can move. Show the remedy being built, and show it failing, and make the failure at least threefold. It must be brought into existence by an act that is itself unilateral. The most cautious seat at the table must turn out to be held by someone whose caution is not epistemic at all. And the pact must be worthless against a twelfth party, whom we meet only in the final third — and the twelfth party must have learned that the hazard existed from her own registry, so that the apparatus built to shrink the number of dangerous actors increased it.

4. Make the case for releasing now, sincerely and concretely, through a person who would be saved by it: not a paragraph of counterargument, but a character with her own figures, her own season, and her own reasons for distrusting the eleven. If a reader finishes believing that caution was obviously correct, the story has failed.

5. Neither the phrase unilateralist's curse nor any synonym of it may appear, and no character may state the moral. The ending must turn on a decision that is hers alone and that the reader cannot afterwards classify as either the responsible act or the arrogant one; the last thing she does should be something that would be unobjectionable if one person did it and catastrophic if eleven did.

Craft constraints: 2,000 to 2,600 words. No more than four named characters; the eleven remain a roster of institutions, initials, and email signatures. No boardroom that functions as a philosophy seminar, no reversal in the final paragraph, no detonation. Sketch the biology only as far as the plot requires, and never let it be explained to a listener who has no reason to need it explained.
