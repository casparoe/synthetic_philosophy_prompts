Last spring our regional electricity grid operator appointed me, as its external mathematics member, to the Software Assurance Board that signs off on the machine-checked proofs behind our real-time dispatch program. In five weeks the board votes on a petition I do not feel equipped to adjudicate, and I would like a draft opinion to circulate and revise beforehand.

The petition: our proofs currently live in a constructive type theory, in which nothing counts as established unless the argument carries, at least in principle, an object one could compute. The verification team wants to admit classical reasoning — excluded middle and the full axiom of choice — into the certified library, because two lemmas needed for the next release are unavailable constructively. One is a separation argument: showing that the set of states the dispatch program can reach lies strictly on the safe side of a hyperplane in a space of a few dozen dimensions. The other is a compactness argument: upgrading a guarantee they have checked for every finite time window into a guarantee about an unbounded run. The team's case is largely practical. The classical mathematical library they wish to draw on is large, actively maintained, and already contains both lemmas; rebuilding them constructively is costed at roughly nine engineer-months, against a certification deadline the regulator will not move.

Two difficulties I cannot resolve on my own. First, I suspect the board is being asked to make a general foundational change on the strength of two very particular needs, and that those two needs may not require the same thing, or anything like the full strength being requested; the opinion should say what each lemma actually consumes and why. Second, the board is split by an exchange nobody can referee. One engineer argues that in type theory the axiom of choice is proved rather than assumed, so the petition merely lets us invoke a theorem we already have; a colleague answers that this is exactly what makes the slogan misleading and conceals where the real difficulty lies. I believe they are talking past each other, but I cannot say which of them is confused, or in what terms.

Please draft the opinion. It should end in a recommendation the board can vote on — admit, refuse, or admit under conditions stated precisely enough to be enforced — and it should distinguish non-constructive content that ends up inside the program we ship from non-constructive content that stays inside the argument that the program is correct, telling us whether that distinction carries weight for a certification body or is only a mathematician's comfort. Resolve the engineers' disagreement in language a board member with no mathematics beyond calculus can follow. Set out what conditions, if any, would let us keep the productive parts of the petition without conceding the principle, and say who would have to verify that those conditions are met, and when. Finally, give the strongest case for admitting classical reasoning outright, and an honest account of what a refusal would cost the team and the public beyond the nine engineer-months. I want the reasoning, not the references.
