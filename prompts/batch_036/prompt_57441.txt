Odd favor, and I know it's a strange thing to ask of a chatbot, but I have run out of Saturday. I live in a river town of about nine thousand whose entire claim to fame is a wooden covered bridge, built 1874, one lane, still on the school bus route. Six years ago the county closed it for repairs and then did something unusual: they rebuilt it in place, swapping out every single timber one at a time, with traffic moving the whole way through, so there was never a week when the bridge wasn't in service. Meanwhile the historical society took possession of every original timber, numbered them in chalk, stacked them in a barn, and has spent two winters reassembling the whole 1874 set into a full-size copy on a gravel bar a quarter mile downsteam. Both are standing. Both look exactly like the bridge. There is one bronze plaque, the county has ruled that it goes on the in-place reconstruction, and roughly half the town considers that a theft. My department chair has volunteered me for the five-minute remarks at Saturday's ribbon-cutting, everyone here knows I'm the metaphysics person, and both sides have told me warmly how much they're looking forward to hearing what I say. The room will be three county commissioners; the historical society, average age about seventy-five, two of whom personally handed a plank up to a crew member in 2021 and have never let anyone forget it; the family that ran the toll house for four generations; two local news crews; and eight students from my spring seminar who will absolutely be checking whether I believe what I teach. My colleague who works on material coincidence is an endurantist and has already said, very sweetly, that she'd love to see me try.

Here's what I need, and the shape of the problem is the whole point. The speech cannot announce a verdict about which is the bridge — not because I'm a coward, but because whichever way I go, half that gravel bank hears a professional metaphysician using their grievance as a demonstration. Nor can it collapse into "well, both are the bridge in their own way," which is mush, and the historical society's complaint is a real one that costs something and should be allowed to cost something out loud. What it has to do instead is actually run on the difference between a thing being wholly present at each moment of its life, so that the two candidates are rivals for one indivisible prize, and a thing being a long smear across spacetime of which any given assembly of timbers is just one cross-section — so that "which is the bridge" turns into a question about which longer thing your cross-section belongs to. That distinction is the engine; if I can't get it across using nothing but wood, water, and school buses, then I don't deserve to say it at a ribbon-cutting. So hard rule: the words endurance, perdurance, temporal part, and four-dimensionalism do not appear anywhere in the speech. My own framing, which you're welcome to improve on, is that these two aren't rivals for the same stuff at the same time at all — the original timbers never stopped existing, they just spent five years in a barn — so there are two honest long things here, one that follows the wood and one that follows the service, and they only look like enemies because we've decided in advance that there has to be exactly one bridge. I want the speech to lean on the bridge's own history of change (the flood year, the winter the north truss sagged two inches and nobody told the state for a month), because change is the actual reason anyone thinks objects have parts spread out along time, and I want that to arrive as local color rather than as a premise. I need one moment where my seminar students laugh because they know precisely which argument I just made, and everyone else simply thinks it was a nice line about planks. That's the trick and I cannot do it myself.

Length about 750 words, six minutes, and written to be read aloud: I'll be outdoors with a river behind me, so if a sentence can't survive wind, cut it. Don't end on unity, don't end on "in a sense we're all the bridge," don't end on a question — end on something I can point at. Then tell me whether to push further, into the view where there isn't one persisting thing at all, just momentary stages, and "the bridge" is a way of sorting them; I suspect that's colder than the occasion can bear, but argue it either way, and if you use it, one sentence only. After the speech give me three or four sentences saying which picture it's leaning on at each turn and whether you think my two-worms framing is genuine consolation or a dodge, because I'd rather find out now than on the gravel bar. No bibliography, no name-dropping, no list of who held which view — I know the literature better than my students do and I don't want the speech sounding like a footnote. One honest caveat: I've been a perdurantist since grad school, but I've come to think the change argument does less work than I used to claim, and I dont want the speech carrying a confidence I no longer have. American English throughout, please.
