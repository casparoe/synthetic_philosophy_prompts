I run a Wednesday-night philosophy group at our town library — mostly retirees, a couple of nurses, one guy who works at the fish hatchery. No professor in the room and nobody's got jstor. Once a month I hand out a little take-home essay for the ones who like writing, grade it myself, and we argue about it the following week.

This month's topic is wether brain size has anything to do with moral standing. I want one essay question, answerable in about an hour by a smart non-specialist, that can't be passed by reciting the obvious stuff — encephalization, neuron counts, birds having tiny but densely packed brains, the 19th century skull-measuring business. Here's the wrinkle I want it built around: suppose we learned that brain size doesn't actually cause any of it. It makes no difference how smart a creature is or how much it suffers. It just tracks those things perfectly in every species anyone has ever measured, and nobody can say why. Does the size itself carry moral weight, or only what it tracks? And I don't want the easy answer — "it's just a proxy for the real thing, obviously" — because I think there's a live intuition that a bigger brain means a richer inner life in a way that isn't the same as any capacity you could test for, and the question should force people to face that instead of waving it off. Bonus if the question also makes them deal with what changes once the tracking stops being perfect in one direction only.

Please give me the question, a model answer around 550 words, and a marking scheme out of 15 that says what separates a 14 from a 10 by naming the actual moves each one makes. Also tell me which things should be worth zero — the history-of-racist-craniometry recital, for one — unless the writer is genuinely doing something with them. Nothing in the question or the answer should require looking anything up or citing anybody.
