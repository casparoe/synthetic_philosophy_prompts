I spent thirty-nine years in protein crystallography, most of it running a laboratory of about a dozen people and, in the last fifteen, sitting on and occasionally chairing the panels that decided what got funded. I retired four years ago and have been reading philosophy since, without any formal training. In April I give a paper in a departmental colloquium that mixes history and philosophy of science, and while I have a draft I can defend, I have no feel whatsoever for what will be thrown at me in the discussion. I would like you to prepare me for that period.

The paper takes the tripartite account of the historical modes in the second Untimely Meditation of 1874 — monumental, antiquarian, critical — and uses it as an instrument for describing how a natural scientific field relates to its own past. My argument runs in four steps.

First: the story a field tells about its own origins is not ornament attached to its method but a working part of it. It tells the apprentice what a contribution looks like, which problems are the field's problems, and what the arc of a career is. In my field that story begins with a single heroic determination, proceeds as a relay of named people and named instruments, and ends at us. Programs that were abandoned appear in it either not at all or as mistakes that were corrected, and they were in fact abandoned for reasons of cost and apparatus rather than of evidence.

Second: that story is monumental in Nietzsche's sense — great moments held up to the striving person as models to be imitated — and the field's practice around its own apparatus is antiquarian: methods, journals, techniques and institutes are preserved with a piety that has detached from what they currently show. What the natural sciences have no institutional home for is the third mode, in which a past is brought to trial, judged and sentenced by someone who suffers under it and needs to be free of it.

Third, and this is where I expect the fight: the obvious reply is that science is precisely the institution of criticism, and that I have simply not noticed it. My distinction is that the criticism science institutionalizes is directed at claims — at results, at statistics, at whether the density supports the model — and not at the field's narrative about itself. Criticism of the narrative is not treated as a scientific act but as an act of disloyalty, and is filed outside the discipline, under history or sociology or philosophy. The person who makes it is asked for data, and data about the field is received as gossip or as careerism. My evidence is a review panel I chaired that renewed a program whose original justification had expired some fifteen years earlier. Everyone in the room knew this. Nobody could say it: the sentence "this program is finished" was available only to someone with nothing to lose, and there was no such person at the table, myself included.

Fourth, the consequence I most want defended: the missing mode does not produce bad data. It produces a narrowing of what can be asked. A field in this condition does not decline by error; it loses the capacity to formulate the question whose answer would end a program, and the signature is high internal productivity — better apparatus, better statistics, more papers — alongside a falling rate of questions that would matter to anyone outside. I know this is the weakest link, and that it is close to unfalsifiable as I have stated it. I would rather you treat it as the target it is than protect it.

The room will be about twelve people: two who publish on the Untimely Meditations and will care intensely whether the distinction I am using is between kinds of history or between attitudes of the person doing the history, and whether I have conflated the two; one who works on Nietzsche's reception of the natural sciences of his own day and will be alert to anachronism in both directions; three working historians of science, archivalists, one of whom will reach immediately for the sociology of normal science and another for presentism; a philosopher of biology who will want to know what my account predicts that a purely institutional or incentive-based account does not; and two or three graduate students. Nobody in the room has ever run a laboratory. None of them has any reason to be kind to an amateur with a borrowable typology, and I have no intention of pretending otherwise.

Please give me the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they would do if they landed, each phrased in the vocabulary of the person likely to raise it rather than in mine. For each, tell me what I should concede at once, what I must hold, and give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes standing up, together with a frank note on where that reply is softest. Two constraints on the replies: I have not read the secondary literature and will not be able to discuss it, so a reply that depends on citing a commentator is a reply I cannot use; and I should not have to invent new arguments on the spot — if the honest response to an objection is that my paper does not survive it, I want to know that before April rather than in the room.

Then tell me which single reply has to land for the paper to survive the afternoon, and identify the question I am most hoping nobody asks. I have a candidate of my own — a personal one, about what I did or failed to do at that table — and I want to know whether that is the worst of it or whether there is something worse I have not thought of, with the most honest answer I could give to it.

Finally, and I would ask this as a matter of principle: say plainly anywhere my sketch misreads the essay, including the possibility that an essay whose burden is the danger of an excess of the historical sense cannot be recruited for a proposal to give institutions a third historical mode. Do not work around a difficulty of that kind; state it. And tell me what would change your mind about your own ranking — which consideration, if I had it wrong, would move an objection from likely to fatal — and where you think your assessment of this room is most likely to fail, whether by overestimating the charity specialists extend to an outsider with a usable idea or by underestimating it.
