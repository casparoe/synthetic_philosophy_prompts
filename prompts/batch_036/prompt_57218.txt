I am part of a small working group drafting an expert-elicitation protocol for estimating the probability that a specified class of ageing civil structure fails within a twenty-year horizon, on the basis of sparse and heterogeneous inspection records. The eventual users are engineers and a licensing authority, but the group includes two philosophers, and one of them repeatedly reaches for the slogan "you should not be more precise than your evidence." He has now deployed it three times in ways I strongly suspect are not the same claim, and I would like the claims separated before our next meeting.

The three uses:

1. An analyst reported a point posterior of 0.043 for a twenty-year failure probability, derived from a parametric degradation model with a deliberately diffuse prior. Our colleague objected that not merely the third decimal place but the point value itself outruns what eleven inspection records can support, and asked for an interval or a set of distributions in its place.

2. When the group proposed to meet that demand by publishing a credal interval — say [0.01, 0.30] — or the set of posteriors generated by a family of priors, he objected again, on the grounds that the endpoints are no better grounded than the point estimate was, so that an interval too is more precise than the evidence.

3. In the discussion of how results are to be communicated to the licensing authority, he invoked the same slogan to argue that a report should never display a single number, even where the analysts themselves hold that the evidence supports one.

Please give me a menu of the distinct precise theses this slogan could be expressing, aiming for something in the range of five to eight readings, ordered from the most platitudinous to the most radical. For each reading: state it in a form specific enough that it could be false; identify which notion of precision it trades on (sharpness of the credal state as against narrowness of an interval, as against the number of digits reported, as against the sensitivity of the assignment to further data); identify which notion of evidence it requires (the total evidence, the data set, the amount of evidence as distinct from what it points toward, its resilience or fragility under further enquiry); classify it as a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, or false, with a sentence of justification; and say whether it functions as a requirement or merely as a permission, and whether it governs what an agent may believe or what she may assert and publish.

Then, on that basis, tell me which readings would license each of his three uses; whether any single reading licenses all three; and if none does, which conjunction comes closest without collapsing into inconsistency. I would also like you to address two objections explicitly. First, that a maximally imprecise credal state is insensitive to evidence, and so appears to violate the slogan under at least one of its own readings. Second, that adopting a determinate set of probability functions is itself a precision commitment that has merely been relocated one level up, so that the imprecise response to use 1 may not escape the charge it was meant to answer.

Finally, indicate how a Keynesian framework that separates the probability of an argument from its weight, and a framework on which rational credal states may be sets of probability functions, come apart with respect to this slogan: whether it is best read as a claim internal to one of those frameworks, to both, or to neither, and what turns on that.

I would rather have the distinctions than the literature map, so please omit any bibliography or list of sources, and feel free to conclude that in one of the three uses the slogan is doing no work at all.
