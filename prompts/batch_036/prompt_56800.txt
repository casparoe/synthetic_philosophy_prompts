For some years I have been working through the arguments about whether an actually infinite collection of things can exist, mostly in connection with the claim that the universe cannot have existed for an infinite time, and Hilbert's hotel keeps being produced as the standard reply to that claim. I believe I have at last located the weakness in that reply, and I would like a candid assessment of whether my reasoning holds up or whether I have gone wrong somewhere.

The story runs as follows. A hotel has infinitely many rooms and every one of them is occupied. A traveller arrives. The manager moves the occupant of room 1 into room 2, the occupant of room 2 into room 3, and so on along the whole line, which leaves room 1 vacant. Defenders of actual infinity say this shows there is nothing contradictory about an infinite collection of guests, since the guests can be matched one to one with the rooms.

But consider what that matching depends on. Room 1 comes free only once the relocations have been carried through, and the relocations have no last member: for any guest you please to name who has already moved, there is another one still sitting in the room he started in. So the arrangement the argument requires is never reached. What the story actually depicts is a hotel perpetually in the middle of admitting people, which is a potentially infinite collection dressed up as an actual one. Hence, on my reading, the hotel does not establish that an actual infinity is possible; it establishes only that a finite hotel can always be extended, which nobody disputes in the first place.

Two questions. First, is this a real difficulty for the hotel argument, or have I misdescribed what the hotel is being used to claim? Second, if my reasoning is sound, does it transfer to the case of an infinite past, since arriving at the present day would likewise mean having worked through an endless succession of days one after another?
