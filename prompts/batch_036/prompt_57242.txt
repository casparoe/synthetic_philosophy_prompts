I lead a small modelling team at a national health technology assessment agency. Roughly once a month we must put a number on something for which there is essentially no data — the probability of a rare but serious adverse event from a newly approved device, the between-site variance of an outcome, the rate at which a treatment effect decays after a trial ends — and our submissions are required to state a prior that "assumes nothing." I know that requirement is incoherent: a distribution uniform on the probability is not uniform on the odds, or the log-odds, or the reciprocal, and the defensible posterior means can differ by an order of magnitude. Our reviewers include two statisticians who will catch every equivocation and a lay panel that reads only the summary.

Please write a decision procedure my analysts can run before any such parameter goes into a submission: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer at each; the criteria a proposed non-informative prior must satisfy, including which invariances it must respect and which parameterization must be declared on the record; the step at which the procedure must stop and log that indifference is unavailable rather than simulate it; and the points where the choice is handed to a person because it reflects institutional risk appetite rather than fact. Every step must be executable in under an hour by someone with one course in Bayesian statistics.

Then apply it to three cases: an adverse event with zero occurrences in 400 patient-years; the between-site variance of a continuous outcome; and an annual decay rate extrapolated ten years beyond the trial. In at least one of these the procedure must deliver an answer my team will not like — for instance forbidding the number they have already drafted — and state what they must submit instead. Close with what the procedure cannot deliver, and a four-sentence plain-language paragraph explaining to the lay panel why "we assumed nothing" is never an available claim.
