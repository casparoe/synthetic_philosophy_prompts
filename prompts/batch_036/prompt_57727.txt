so, slightly weird request. for about a year i've been logging my own snap reactions to other people's feelings in a notes app — mostly on trains, mostly when i'm irritated by someone — and I've realised I have no idea what rule I'm actually applying. I've read Smith and I know the vocabulary (propriety, merit and demerit, the impartial spectator, whatever you want to call the double-sympathy thing), but my own verdicts don't sit still in it. Here's the log, more or less verbatim:

1. My flatmate eats with enormous audible relish, delighted by his own dinner. I feel nothing on his behalf, faint revulsion if anything. But when a friend tells me he's gone stupid over someone and is making a fool of himself, I cant feel it a tenth as hard as he does and yet I go along with it — I find it charming and forgive the excess. Both are passions about something bodily-ish and vivid to the person having them, opposite verdicts.

2. My friend's mother died. What I actually felt was maybe five percent of what he was feeling. I judged his grief entirely proper, and I'd judge anyone who felt only what I felt as cold. So the amount of feeling I have seems to be irrelevant to whether I approve; it's the amount he has that's getting graded, against something.

3. A stranger posts a long thread grieving their cat, arranged for an audience. I disapprove — and I notice the disapproval attaches to the arrangement of the grief, not the grief. But the widow who over-grieves her husband for years I approve of. Same passion, same excess, opposite verdict. I also disapprove of the man who over-grieves his dog more than of the widow, which makes me suspect I'm grading the object, not the passion, and I don't know what licenses that.

4. A man shoves past me on the platform. I resent it and I think a bystander who saw it would go along a bit. The identical shove from a man who's just been told his brother is dead: no resentment at all. So the demerit isn't in the shove or in the harm.

5. Someone does me a large unasked-for favour that I would have hated. I feel real gratitude, and the spectator in me still refuses to credit any merit to him.

6. A surgeon cuts into me and causes agony: zero demerit. A quack who sincerely believes his poultice cures me and makes me worse: some demerit, even though he meant well. Note 5 and 6 together wreck any simple rule about intention — in 5 good intention earns nothing, in 6 good intention earns a little blame.

7. A friend brags. Full disapproval of the vanity, but no punitive machinery whatsoever — I don't want him to suffer or to be made to feel bad. The small lie he told to acquire the bragging material does switch the machinery on. Same person, same afternoon, and something like punishment appears in one case and not the other.

8. The one I can't place anywhere. A stoic type stays perfectly composed when his child dies. I call it admirable, and I'd call a total breakdown less admirable, but I feel no fellow-feeling with the composed man at all — there is no passion there to go along with, no concord, nothing to match. Is my admiration sympathy? If not, what kind of approval is it? And relatedly: I feel properly guilty — sick, actually — about a private thing nobody will ever find out and which hurt nobody. There's no person acted upon there either.

9. I care more about my own finger being caught in a door tomorrow than about the deaths of some thousand people I'll never hear of, and I know that's monstrous, and yet when I actually imagine the thousand deaths what I feel is real but thin, and the finger is thick and immediate. Then if you made me choose, I'd give up the finger instantly, every time, without hesitation. Two scales running in parallel that never talk to each other. I assume the thin one is the morally serious one but it barely registers as a feeling at all.

10. My friend's brother was murdered eleven years ago. The family still wants the killer to suffer and I go along with that completely, even though the brother can't resent anything now and nobody is being protected. But when someone digs up a slander about a person who died a century ago, I feel a much weaker version of the same response, and I can't say why the two cases differ — both victims equally insensible.

my working guess is something like: approval = being able to go along with the agent's passion, blame = being able to go along with the resentment of the person acted on, maybe twice over. That handles 4 and 6-ish and dies immediately on 8 (no passion), 10 (no resenting person), 9 (no person acted on) and 3 (the object of the passion doing the work).

What I want: is there a principle, or a small set of clauses, that generates all of these or nearly all? State it precisely enough that I could hand you a case you've never seen and you'd get my verdict out of it. For each clause tell me which item on my list forced it in. Then tell me bluntly which of my verdicts it has to overturn — I expect at least one goes, and I'd rather it be 3 or 5 than 8. Then run it on four cases I havent listed: someone furiously angry at themselves; resentment aimed at a government department or a corporation that can't resent back and has no feelings to sympathise with; a case where the person acted on is somebody I actively dislike; and a society where the whole community's spectator verdict is, I think, simply wrong — if the impartial spectator is just an average of the actual spectators I've grown up around, where does my confidence that they're wrong come from?

One more thing that seems to decide half the list: does the spectator's verdict track what the person acted upon actually feels, what they would feel if they were reasonable, or what an ideal bystander would feel standing in their shoes? Pick one and defend it, and show me which of 4, 5, 6, 10 it flips.

Aim for maybe 900-1200 words. No need for sources or a reading list or page numbers — I've got the book, I just can't get my own reactions to stay inside it.
