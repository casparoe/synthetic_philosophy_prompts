For thirty-four years I was a working zoologist; my specialty was the systematics of encrusting reef sponges, a group in which the same genotype can present radically different growth forms depending on light, current, and depth, and in which a decade of molecular work has quietly dismantled much of the morphology-based nomenclature I helped build. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy in earnest, and I am now two years into a part-time master's programme. In three weeks I am to present a paper at my department's philosophy-of-biology research seminar, my first talk to a properly philosophical audience, and I would like to arrive prepared for the question period rather than ambushed by it.

My thesis is a deflationary one. I argue that a large family of disputes in the metaphysics of biology, and in general ontology as well, are verbal in the sense developed in the recent literature on that topic: they can be dissolved by the method of elimination, by restating what both parties accept in a vocabulary stripped of the contested term. I further argue that whether a dispute is verbal in this sense is a matter admitting of an empirical test rather than an interpretive judgement. I call it the discriminating-intervention test: a dispute between two candidate usages of a term is substantive only if there is some observation or intervention, available in principle, on which the two usages yield divergent expectations while every claim the parties can express without that term is held fixed. My argument runs in three steps. First, I apply the test to three cases from my own field and claim that it gives a different verdict in each. Whether a given viral entity counts as living fails the test, since nothing is left hanging on the term once all structural, metabolic, and replicative claims have been stated without it. The dispute between the biological and the phylogenetic species concepts largely fails it, but not entirely, because data from a hybrid zone can function as a discriminating observation for one usage and not for the other. Whether species are individuals, in the sense of the Ghiselin–Hull thesis, passes it, because treating a species as a spatiotemporally bounded historical particular rather than as a class constrains how one expects character distributions to be arrayed across a geography in a way the class reading cannot mimic. Second, I claim that the same test applied to the familiar composition puzzles, the statue and the clay and the ship whose planks are replaced, returns verbalness across the board, which is why I regard those puzzles as bad bookkeeping rather than deep metaphysics. Third, I insist that the test is not verificationism: it does not ask whether a claim is meaningful, only whether two usages of one word diverge in what they commit their users to, and it is entirely happy to return the answer that a great many disputed questions have determinate answers.

The audience will number about twelve: four or five philosophers of biology, at least two of whom are realists holding that the species-individuals thesis is a substantive claim about the world and who will resent being told that the neighbouring species-concept debate is a quarrel about words; one committed pluralist who thinks my framing is a false binary, since different species concepts are different tools licensed by different research programmes; a metaphysician who works on grounding and on the notion of structure and who will want to know why I cash substantivity in interventionist terms rather than in terms of which concepts carve at the joints; a population geneticist from the biology department who attends out of curiosity and mild hostility, and who suspects that philosophers of biology mostly redescribe what he already knows; and two graduate students who have read the verbal-disputes literature closely and will be alert to whether I have understood it. Please prepare a question-and-answer sheet for me: the eight or nine objections this particular audience will actually raise, not the ones a general audience or a physics department would raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to the paper if I answered it badly. For each, give it in the voice and vocabulary of the person most likely to press it, state plainly how much of it I should concede up front and how much I can hold, offer a reply I could deliver in about two minutes aloud, and mark precisely where that reply is softest, the point at which a competent follow-up would leave me floundering. I would also like a note on which of my three claims is bearing the most weight and is therefore the likeliest target, and on the reflexes I ought to suppress: I am told that retired scientists irritate philosophers by insisting that the data will settle the matter, and I would rather be warned now than in the room. Finally, tell me the one question I am hoping nobody asks, with a frank assessment of whether I have an answer to it, and identify the single reply that must land if the paper is to survive the hour. Please do not supply a reading list or citations; I cannot chase them down and would rather have your reasoning set out directly.
