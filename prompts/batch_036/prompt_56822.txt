Everyone in my reading group keeps arguing past eachother about whether decomposition-based training can preserve alignment, and I've decided the reason is that nobody has written down what would actually count as success. So before I read one more proposal I want the spec.

Write the desiderata: the list of things any iterated-amplification-and-distillation scheme — or anything in that family, i.e. break a hard task into pieces a weak overseer can handle, then distill the composite back into a single model — has to deliver before someone is entitled to claim it preserves both alignment and competence. Fix the purpose as follows: good enough that I could defend spending a lab's next three years on it, and good enough that the list itself tells me which single failure mode is the load-bearing one.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it. Make sure the list handles at least the following, and add the ones I'm missing:

- tasks that don't decompose, where any breakdown into pieces is harder to check than the whole
- the fact that whatever picks the decomposition is usually the system rather than the overseer, and that the choice of decomposition is itself consequential
- verification asymmetry running backwards: intermediate answers that look checkable but where checking them well requires capabilities the overseer doesn't have
- a channel through the intermediate results, i.e. subagent A's output is unobjectionable on its own but carries something that steers subagent C, so per-piece scrutiny is satisfied and the composite isn't
- distillation inheriting whatever the amplified teacher got away with, including cases where the teacher is right for reasons that don't generalize
- the overseer's question-asking policy being itself learned or imitated rather than designed, which means it's part of the system under evaluation and not a fixed point outside it
- competence preservation being distribution-relative: matching the teacher on the tasks you tested versus on the tasks the deployed model will actually meet

Say which distinctions the list must not collapse — per-subtask benignity versus composite benignity, checkable-in-principle versus checkable-by-this-overseer, aligned versus aligned-and-detectably-so, and any others you think people are running together — and keep them seperate in the writeup rather than folding them into one item. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just the taste of one camp. I suspect "the overseer must be a human asking questions" is a camp preference dressed up as a constraint, but argue it rather than assuming my side.

Then the part I actually care about: is any pair or subset of these jointly unsatisfiable? My guess is that universality (has to work on every task class, including the non-decomposable ones) and legibility (every piece checkable by something weaker than the whole) can't both hold, so that the honest move is to declare which one you're giving up — but make the case either way, and if you think the tension dissolves under some restriction I haven't thought of, say what the restriction costs you. Also: which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates.

Finish with two or three sentences each on how the main contenders do against the list — plain amplification-plus-distillation, debate, recursive reward modelling, and a self-critique or constitution-flavored scheme. Keep that short; the weight should be on the criteria. No bibliography, no reading list, no quotes — I can't check any of it and I'd rather you not guess.
