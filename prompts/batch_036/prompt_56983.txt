I run a monthly meetup on AI and the future, mixed crowd — a couple of people who've actually read the literature and alot who've only seen headlines. Next month I want to open the session with a poem instead of a slide, and I want it to carry the argument rather than name it.

You're someone who knows both the AI-risk arguments and formal verse cold. Think it through before you write anything: first sketch, in a few lines, your six end-words and the sestina permutation you'll run them through, and say in one sentence each what an end-word is standing in for. Then write the poem.

The form is the point. Six end-words for six recurring subgoals — self-preservation, keeping the goal intact, resource acquisition, getting smarter, plus two you pick — and the fact that each of them ends exactly one line in every stanza, in a different order each time but never once absent, *is* the claim: the same drives come back however you shuffle the final goal, because the shuffling isn't up to the agent. The envoi's three lines are where the other thesis should land — that any amount of cleverness goes with any final goal, which is exactly why those six keep returning.

Two constraints. Don't use the words orthogonal, convergence, instrumental, terminal, or paperclip anywhere; if it's a name-drop the room will hear nothing. And let one stanza be the concession — an agent whose goal is already satisfied, or whose goal is only about switching itself off, where the permutation keeps running but the end-words have nothing left to grab. I want the sceptics in the room to hear that the poem knows when the argument stops applying.
