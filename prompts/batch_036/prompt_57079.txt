I'm reading Douglass's 1860 Glasgow address on whether the Constitution is pro-slavery or anti-slavery, and I'm stuck on the stretch where he insists on establishing what the Constitution *is* before he'll say anything about what it means:

> "the American Government and the American Constitution are spoken of in a manner which would naturally lead the hearer to believe that one is identical with the other; when the truth is, they are distinct in character as is a ship and a compass. The one may point right and the other steer wrong. A chart is one thing, the course of the vessel is another. The Constitution may be right, the Government is wrong. … it should be borne in mind that the mere text, and only the text, and not any commentaries or creeds written by those who wished to give the text a meaning apart from its plain reading, was adopted as the Constitution of the United States. It should also be borne in mind that the intentions of those who framed the Constitution, be they good or bad, for slavery or against slavery, are so respected so far, and so far only, as we find those intentions plainly stated in the Constitution. … It was what they said that was adopted by the people, not what they were ashamed or afraid to say, and really omitted to say."

Three things I cant work out.

1. He changes metaphor mid-thought — compass, then chart, then "course of the vessel." Is the switch doing argumentative work, or is it just oratorical looseness? A compass and a chart are not interchangeable navigational instruments and I'm not sure he notices.

2. The "so far, and so far only" clause looks like flat textualism: intentions drop out entirely unless they made it onto the page. But right after it he leans hard on facts about the framers' actual behaviour — that they sat behind closed doors, that the debates weren't published for a quarter century. How can he use their conduct and habits as evidence while claiming their intentions only count when stated? Is there a stable position underneath this, and if so can you state it precisely, in his terms rather than ours?

3. "not what they were ashamed or afraid to say, and really omitted to say" — the word *ashamed* is carrying an enormous load. Silence about slavery gets read as concealment of something discreditable rather than as a bargain struck in the open. Couldn't the very same silence equally be read as the framers refusing to endorse slavery at all? How much of his conclusion depends on which reading of the silence you pick, and does he ever argue for his rather than assume it?

Finally: is he getting close to what would now be called public-meaning originalism as against original-intentions originalism, or is that an anachronistic overlay on an argument that's doing something else entirely?
