I am in the middle of a dissertation chapter in formal epistemology about how perceptual experience should change one's degrees of belief, and I have hit a wall. My supervisor is on sabbatical until Easter and the reading group has migrated to social choice theory, so I have been testing my ideas against a log I keep of my own reactions to concrete cases rather than against a person. What I would like from you is not a survey of the literature, nor a list of sources, nor a reconstruction of any published author's view: I want the principle itself, stated precisely enough that I could go back through my log and check it case by case. Please write in formal British English throughout.

Here are the cases and my verdicts on them. (i) Driving home in the rain at dusk, I see a traffic light too far off to read: before the glance, 0.5 that it is red, 0.5 that it is green; after it, 0.75 and 0.25; my credence that I ought to ease off the accelerator moves correspondingly. Rational, and I take this to be the paradigm case of updating on experience that does not deliver certainty. (ii) The same structure with a card glimpsed from a shuffled deck, hearts against diamonds, moving from even odds to 0.9 and 0.1. Also rational, and I do not think it matters that here the outcome was already fixed while in (i) it was a matter of ambiguous illumination. (iii) I walk into a colleague's flat and get an inarticulate sense that it has been searched — a drawer slightly out of alignment, someone else's cigarette smoke in the hall — and I go from 0.2 to 0.8 that her things have been through. Rational, but note that I could not have written down in advance any partition of possibilities across which my probabilities were redistributed, and I still want to say this is the same species of update as (i) and (ii). (iv) On Sunday evening I resolve that, come what may, I shall end Monday 0.95 confident that my keys are on the kitchen table, because I am tired of retracing my steps. Irrational: this is willing a belief rather than answering to evidence. Yet it has exactly the formal shape of (iii), a shift not pinned down by redistribution over a pre-given partition, and I cannot see what separates the two beyond saying that one was caused by experience and the other by decision, which looks more like a relabelling of my verdicts than an account of them. (v) I have been certain for years, credence 1, that the departmental seminar meets at three o'clock; today someone mentions it again. Nothing should change, and any rule that changes anything here is to that extent wrong. (vi) I have been certain for years that the seminar meets at three; today I see a notice suggesting it has moved to four, and my credence falls to 0.6. Rational. Cases (v) and (vi) are formally identical — antecedent credence of 1, fresh input, output — and I want the principle to explain the difference, not merely register it. (vii) In a gallery lit unlike anywhere I usually see pictures, my probabilities over the colours in front of me shift, and so does my sense of how those colours would look under that lighting; the conditionals linking appearance to colour move during the experience itself. Rational, and I suspect this is precisely where the requirement that such conditionals stay fixed across the update breaks down.

What I want back is the following. First, state one candidate principle, or at most two competing ones, with its constraints made explicit: what qualifies a change in probability as experiential at all; whether an antecedently specifiable partition is required; whether the conditional probabilities must remain rigid across the update; and whether a credence of 1 may be lowered. Second, for each clause of the principle, name the case in my log that motivates it, so that I can see which of my intuitions is doing the work. Third, give the principle's verdict on the case I keep going back and forth on: at dusk I seem to see a deer at the end of the garden, I settle on 0.5 deer and 0.5 the shadow of the trellis, and half an hour later I remember that the trellis was taken down that morning — I flip on whether this memory-driven redistribution is the same kind of update as the perceptual one or requires a rule of its own, and I want to know whether the case is a genuine counterexample to your principle or a redescription of one it already handles. Fourth, tell me what your principle implies for a case I have not listed, namely an experience that seems best modelled by a continuum rather than a finite partition, such as reading an angle off a protractor, where every finite partition I write down afterwards looks like an artefact of how I chose to chop the continuum up. And finally, be honest about the cost: list which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, say which single verdict you would advise me to abandon, and tell me whether a principle that saves all of them does so only by licensing any probability shift whatever, provided the agent is willing to describe it as experiential.
