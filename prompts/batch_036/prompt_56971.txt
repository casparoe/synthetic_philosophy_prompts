I am in my third year of a philosophy of science PhD programme and I am drafting the survey chapter of my prospectus on verisimilitude. I have spent the past three weeks in the technical literature and my notes have become an undifferentiated heap — constituent structures, normalised difference measures over possible worlds, weighted averages of true and false content, and a great many inequalities I can verify but no longer interpret. Before I commit to writing anything, I want to know what I should have been asking of an account of truthlikeness all along. So: write the desiderata. Give me the specification any adequate account of closeness to the truth has to meet, with the purpose fixed as follows. I want an account strong enough to underwrite the scientific realist claim that a successor theory can be nearer the truth than its predecessor even when neither is true, and strong enough to be used comparatively inside an actual dispute about theory choice, rather than one that merely licenses the truism that true theories are better than false ones. One caution about me: I came into philosophy from forecast verification work, so I am instinctively drawn to numerical measures with metric properties, and I would like you to tell me plainly if that instinct is smuggling in a requirement rather than reflecting one.

For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that motivates it — the concrete verdicts that would come out wrong if the requirement were dropped, not just its statement — and mark it non-negotiable, negotiable, or a preference of a particular research programme that has been promoted to a requirement. Candidates I suspect belong on the list, and that I want you either to justify or to reject: that an account must order falsehoods among themselves; that it must allow a precise falsehood to be closer to the whole truth than a vague or trivial truth; that truthlikeness must come apart from logical probability, so that the aim of inquiry is not the most probable statements; that it must not collapse into empirical adequacy, coherence with the evidence, or mere consistency; that it must say something definite about tautologies and contradictions; that it must behave sensibly under conjunction and disjunction, including the question whether adding a true conjunct to a theory can ever make it less truthlike; that the resulting ordering must be stable when the same subject matter is described in a different but intertranslatable vocabulary or carved up more finely; and that it must be usable from inside inquiry, in the sense that we can sometimes have evidence we are getting closer without already knowing the truth. Add anything important I have omitted, and do not spare any of the above if it turns out to be bogus.

Then the part I most want: the joint-satisfiability analysis. Tell me which subsets of these requirements cannot all be met at once, and for each clash what exactly breaks and why — I have in mind the classical result that on Popper's own definition no false statement can have positive verisimilitude, and the complaint that constituent- or possible-worlds-based measures deliver different orderings depending on how the language carves up the space. If the list is inconsistent, that is a result and I want it stated as one: identify the single requirement that does the most work in eliminating candidate accounts, and say which requirement a scientific realist should be prepared to abandon, together with what the abandonment costs the claim about progress across theory change. Finish with a paragraph each, no scorecard, on how the three broad families of proposal — the content approach, the consequence approach, and the likeness or similarity approach — fare against the list. Please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I already have the literature, and what I lack is the standard of assessment.
