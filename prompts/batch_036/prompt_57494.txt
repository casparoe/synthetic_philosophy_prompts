I sit on the research ethics committee of a clinic that has been asked to approve a consent document covering two protocols: mitochondrial donation, in which the nuclear material of a patient's egg is transferred into a donor egg whose own nucleus has been removed, so that the resulting embryo carries the patient's nuclear genome but the donor's mitochondria; and an experimental arm in which sperm and eggs are derived in the laboratory from a patient's own reprogrammed somatic cells. The framing section of the document was drafted by an outside consultant with some philosophy training, and before we adopt it I want to know whether it represents the metaphysics fairly. Here is the passage:

"Kripke's necessity of origin holds that a person could not have come from a different sperm and egg. The argument is a short corollary of the necessity of identity. You are identical to yourself, and identity is necessary: if x = y, then necessarily x = y. You in fact came from the particular gamete pair you came from. It follows that nothing which came from any other gamete pair could be you, in any possible world whatever. So a child produced using a donor's mitochondria, or from gametes grown in a dish from a woman's own skin cells, is not a child who could otherwise have been you, and cannot be treated as a variant of the same person; a world containing someone with your memories, your appearance, and your parents is a world containing a near-duplicate stranger. Origin essentialism tells us the converse as well: whoever comes from your gametes is you, so the question whether a procedure produces 'the same child' is settled simply by looking at the biological starting point. Those who resist these conclusions are usually resisting the intuition rather than the argument; the standing objection that origin essentialism makes each of our existences a matter of breathtaking luck is only the thesis restated in emotional terms."

Two things strike me as shaky — the claim that all of this follows from the necessity of identity alone, and the "converse" claim, which is the one the consent document actually relies on — but I could not say what the correct version looks like, and the rest of the committee will not catch the problems either. Please work through the passage claim by claim and tell me: (i) what a metaphysician working on origin essentialism would accept as it stands; (ii) what they would regard as a fair simplification of a more careful or more contested thesis; and (iii) what they would reject outright, naming in each case the specific confusion at work — a missing premise, a direction of implication that does not hold, two distinctions collapsed into one. In particular, spell out what a real argument for the necessity of origin requires beyond the necessity of identity, and say whether that thesis licenses the converse the memo asserts, given that a single early embryo can be split into two and that one and the same egg seems able to survive the replacement of some of its parts. Then tell me whether the memo's practical conclusion survives the correction: does origin essentialism, properly stated, actually settle whether the children produced by these two protocols are or are not "the same child" the patients would otherwise have had, or does the case turn on questions the memo never raises? If the latter, say what a defensible version of the passage would have to say instead, and how much of the memo's confidence it can keep.
