I am drafting the framework chapter of a book about how protest movements coordinate, and my two readers have asked me to set out my commitments on joint action in advance, on a single page, so that they can be attacked directly. Here is what I am prepared to defend.

1. Some intentions have irreducibly first-person-plural content: intending that we repaint the porch is not my intending to do my part plus beliefs about what you will do.
2. Shared agency, where it occurs, is nothing over and above a pattern of such individual attitudes that interlock, mesh in their subplans, and are mutually responsive. There is no further group-level item.
3. Nothing is joint action unless each participant knows that the others are participating; short of mutual knowledge you have, at best, parallel behaviour.
4. Committees, firms, and movements are nevertheless agents in the sense that matters for explanation: they take up positions, act on them, and can be irrational in ways in which no individual member is.
5. I reject group minds and collective guilt alike: nothing is believed, intended, or done, and nobody is answerable for anything, except in virtue of the states and conduct of individual members.
6. The events my book is about — tens of thousands of mostly anonymous people converging on a square on the strength of a slogan and a date, most of whom never learn who else was there — were cases of acting together, and calling them aggregates misdescribes what happened.
7. What a joint commitment generates is not a moral duty and does not depend on the activity being worth pursuing: participants owe one another their part, each may demand performance and resent defection, and being released requires that the others let you out.
8. Someone who becomes convinced that carrying on would be wrong may walk away, and having walked away is not properly resented by those they left.

I do not know where the cracks are, and I am not going to indicate which of these I would rather surrender. Audit the package: say which claims genuinely pull against one another and which only look as though they do, and for each real conflict state precisely what forces the choice. Rank the conflicts by severity. For the two worst, commit to a repair — name the claim that goes or is weakened, give the weakened version, and say what the account loses as a result — rather than laying out the options and leaving me to choose among them. Close with a straight verdict on whether the set can be held together at all, or whether the diffuse cases the book is actually about require me to abandon part of its machinery.
