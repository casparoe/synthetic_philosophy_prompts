I'm TAing an intro social theory section and I've realized half my students have never actually read Weber — they've just absorbed "Protestant work ethic" from pop usage, and they keep quoting the last line of the book like its a prediction about hustle culture. Here's the closing passage of the final chapter, in the old Parsons translation:

> The Puritan wanted to work in a calling; we are forced to do so. For when asceticism was carried out of monastic cells into everyday life, and began to dominate worldly morality, it did its part in building the tremendous cosmos of the modern economic order. This order is now bound to the technical and economic conditions of machine production which to-day determine the lives of all the individuals who are born into this mechanism, not only those directly concerned with economic acquisition, with irresistible force. In Baxter's view the care for external goods should only lie on the shoulders of the 'saint like a light cloak, which can be thrown aside at any moment'. But fate decreed that the cloak should become an iron cage.

Can you help me get straight on what's actually being claimed, so I don't have to improvise in section?

1. The first sentence turns on "wanted to" vs. "we are forced to." What exactly differs between the two sides — freedom, the motive for working, or the source of the constraint?
2. What is the cloak-to-cage image doing? Baxter recommended treating external goods as something droppable; so what is the thing that got dropped, and what hardened into the cage — the goods themselves, the attitude toward them, or the economic order built out of that attitude?
3. "Fate decreed" — is Weber saying the cage was inevitable, or something weaker about how it happened to turn out? And does the machine-production sentence commit him to determinism, and if so, about what exactly?
4. Parsons' "iron cage" is a loose rendering of a German phrase that's more literally a steel-hard casing or housing. Does the more literal image change how you'd read the passage, or is it cosmetic?
5. The one my students care about: does anything in this paragraph support the claim that ascetic Protestantism is why people work hard today? What would Weber have to be asserting for that to follow, and does he assert it here?

Also, please mark which of your answers you can read off the passage itself and which ones depend on filling in from the rest of the book or from general context — I'd rather know which parts I'm on shaky ground on before someone asks me.
