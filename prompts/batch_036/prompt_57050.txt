I am preparing a two-page handout for a methods workshop whose participants are machine-learning interpretability researchers and cognitive scientists with no training in formal epistemology. The session is on the problem of old evidence for Bayesian confirmation theory, and I want the handout to be a decision tree that sorts participants into positions according to what they actually say about cases, not according to which labels they like. Nothing in it should require having read anything.

Open with a framing paragraph of no more than one hundred words stating the problem in the terms the session will use: a hypothesis that entails a proposition the agent already accepted with probability one cannot raise the probability of that proposition, so on the standard incrementalist criterion it cannot be confirmed by it, even though such confirmations appear to be among the strongest in the history of science.

Then the tree. Participants will be handed the following case bank, and every branch question must be answerable by reference to at least one case in it.

Case A. The anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion had been tabulated for more than half a century before general relativity and was common knowledge among physicists of the day. Einstein derived it from the field equations in 1915, so the evidence was already in hand and already accepted. Four years later an eclipse expedition measured the deflection of starlight, which was new evidence for the same theory.

Case B. The visible lines of hydrogen's emission spectrum were measured before Balmer fitted them with an empirical formula in 1885; Rydberg generalized the formula in 1888. For decades it was established empirical knowledge with no theoretical basis, until Bohr's 1913 atomic model derived it from quantized electron orbits. Note that the same formula had also predicted lines not yet observed, which were subsequently found, so one episode contains both old and new evidence.

Case C (stipulated, contemporary). A lab has two years of evaluation logs recording that a deployed model produces one stereotyped output across a family of prompts. Call that pattern E, and call H a mechanistic hypothesis about an internal circuit, where H entails E. Four variants:
C1. A researcher who has never seen the logs joins the lab, reads them in her first week, and formulates H that same afternoon. E was old for the lab and new for her.
C2. The same researcher wrote the logging harness a year earlier, has been certain of E throughout, and formulates H only now.
C3. As in C2, except that the lab articulated H early on, gave it a middling probability, and set it aside in favor of a rival; she now revives it.
C4. As in C2, except that although she is certain of E and certain of H once she has articulated it, she has never explicitly entertained the proposition that H entails E and says she would have to work through the derivation to see it.

Requirements on the branch questions. Eight to twelve of them, each with two or three answer options, each option a claim about the cases rather than a label. No question may contain the vocabulary of any position in the debate, and no question may be answerable only by someone who already knows which leaf it leads to. At least one question must force the participant to decide whether oldness is a property of an individual agent's doxastic state or of a community's — that is what C1 is there for — and every leaf must then be marked as committed or not committed to confirmation being agent-relative. At least one question must concern whether a fix is allowed to change the algebra of hypotheses and evidence propositions or must operate within a fixed one. At least one must separate the worry that E had probability one from the worry that H had a nonzero prior, using C2 against C3.

Build the instrument from the logical space before you populate it: list the questions with their options, mark which combinations of answers are coherent and which are ruled out, and only then place the positions. For each coherent combination that ends up empty, say in one clause whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it is merely unexplored.

The leaves must include at least the following, which I am describing rather than naming so that you cannot simply copy a standard list: the view that confirmation concerns the firmness of belief rather than its increase, so no probability change is required; the view that the agent learns something not expressible as a redistribution of probabilities over a fixed hypothesis set, namely a logical relation, and that this learning does the confirming work; the view that the correct move is to reconstruct the agent's doxastic state at an earlier time, when the evidence was not yet certain, and to conditionalize on that reconstruction; the view that observation is never certain and that updating on a partial observation dissolves the problem; the view that the phenomenon is real but is a problem for the incrementalist account of confirmation rather than for Bayesianism; and the view that the difficulty is an artifact of assuming logical omniscience and disappears for a realistically limited agent. Add any further leaves you think the space requires. Give each leaf a short descriptive name of your own choosing, one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and a note of which other leaves it cannot be separated from by these questions.

Close with a short list of the pairs of leaves the tree cannot distinguish, and for each pair either a further question that would distinguish them or an explicit statement that no case in the bank can.

Practical constraints: the whole handout should fit on two pages and stay under 1,200 words, prose-light. No citations, no bibliography, no reading list, and no verbatim quotations. Attach a philosopher's name to a leaf only where you are confident of the attribution; otherwise the descriptive name stands alone.
