I am preparing a two-day methods workshop for a national statistics office. Its ministers have asked whether the office should add a life-evaluation item to the household survey — the sort of question that asks respondents to picture a ladder whose top rung is the best possible life for them and whose bottom rung is the worst, and to say where they stand — on the grounds that this is what the best-known international ranking of national happiness is built from. Half my participants are survey methodologists who think about scale construction all day and have never read a page of welfare theory; the other half are policy advisers who use the word happiness casually and have never thought about scale construction. Last year I ran a version of this workshop and discovered that the two camps talk past each other in exactly one way: everyone who doubts the instrument ends up standing next to everyone who doubts that happiness is a proper aim of government, and the two doubts have nothing to do with one another. This year I want to separate them, in public, on the first afternoon.

Please write a decision tree that sorts my participants by what they actually believe about such a number, ending at a named position. Two documents: first, the participant handout, which is nothing but numbered questions; second, the key, which maps answer patterns onto positions.

The requirements:

1. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of views. Begin by stating the axes you take to be in play — at minimum, what happiness is (occurrent pleasant experience; a global judgment that one's life is going well; satisfaction of informed preferences; an objective-list matter of how well a life is going, whatever it feels like from inside), whether a self-report has any special authority over the thing in question, whether one population's ladder scores are comparable to another population's at all, and what a state should do with the measure once it has one. Then say explicitly whether these axes are independent, and drop, split, or add any that you think I have gotten wrong.

2. Treat the last axis as two. A country can want to raise its citizens' happiness, and it can separately want to climb an ordinal table comparing it to other countries; the second aim has properties the first lacks, and I want a node that forces the distinction rather than letting participants slide from one to the other.

3. Phrase every node as a concrete case that a participant can answer without knowing which way it points. Do not ask anyone whether they are a hedonist or a Goodhart skeptic. At least four nodes must be built from material of the following kind: a pilot in which the same sample scored an average of 1.4 points higher when the top of the ladder was worded as the best life they could personally hope for than when it was worded as the best possible life; a district office given an annual happiness target and subsequently measured as having met it; a population that endured a severe recession, recovered economically within a decade, and reported lower ladder scores than a comparable population that never suffered the shock; and a case in which a country gains two places in the ranking by moving its survey from face-to-face interviews to telephone interviews, with no change in anyone's life.

4. The leaves must include, at minimum: a view on which the ladder measures something real but is not happiness, and is nonetheless the best political indicator available; a view on which affect measured close to the moment is the only honest target and retrospective ladder items are confabulation; a view on which the ranking is not even ordinal-comparable across populations, so that no cross-country policy conclusion follows from it; a view on which the measure is a tolerable diagnostic and a corrupting target; a view on which the state should track happiness but not promote it, because promotion violates the separaten of persons' own judgments about what makes a life go well — reconstruct that position carefully rather than borrowing its slogan; and a view on which a country should aim to top the ranking precisely because the ranking is a public, contestable scoreboard and opacity is worse than distortion.

5. After the tree, give the combination grid: for each leaf of the logical space your axes permit, say whether it is occupied, and for at least three unoccupied cells diagnose the absence — incoherent, coherent but unattractive, or simply unexplored, with the reason in each case.

6. Flag at least two pairs of leaves your tree cannot actually separate, say what further question would separate them, and say whether a lay participant could answer that question honestly.

7. Justify the order of your nodes. Where an earlier answer makes a later question moot or changes its meaning, say so, and tell me how to run the sequence live with forty people and one whiteboard.

Please do not include readings, citations, or a bibliography, and do not narrate the history of the debate. Aim for roughly 1,300 words in total.
