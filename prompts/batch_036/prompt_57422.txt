I am at work on a novel whose central scene has a harbour pilot choosing, in the few minutes before a rescue launch goes out, whose child to go after: his own, or a stranger's, who is likelier to survive. A philosopher friend who read the draft says I have the ethics backwards, and offered an image to prove it. Justice wears a blindfold precisely so that she cannot see who stands before her; a moral agent occupies the same position; my pilot's fault is that he looked. On her account, Williams's charge that the man who justifies saving his wife by noting that she is his wife has one thought too many only shows the blindfold chafes, not that it may be removed. Audit the image for me: who plays Justice, who the litigants, what the bench, the procedure, the verdict, and what the blindfold itself amounts to. Then say which features of the courtroom the comparison quietly lends my pilot's situation and which it cannot lend, whether her conclusion survives the subtraction, and, if the figure cannot bear that weight, what figure could.
