I'm presenting at a two-day workshop on formal epistemology in the spring. The room is mostly traditional epistemologists — internalists about justification and a couple of virtue epistemologists, none of whom work formally — plus three decision theorists who use scoring rules, and a research forecaster at a national weather service who designs verification scores and has told me privately that he thinks the philosophy literature on proper scoring rules is naive about practice.

The thesis I am defending: the accuracy-dominance argument for Probabilism should be replaced rather than repaired. My sketch runs in three steps. First, every such theorem is relative to a choice of scoring rule. Second, the accuracy-first framework offers no non-question-begging way to choose among strictly proper rules: they agree that incoherence is dominated, but they disagree about how badly a given incoherent agent has gone wrong and about which coherent credence function counts as the minimal revision of it. Third, the only resources left for making that choice are facts about what the inquirer needs the credences for — so epistemic rationality is interest-relative through and through, and the field's standing assumption that the scoring rule must be neutral among the agent's practical purposes is a prejudice nobody has argued for.

Build me the question-period prep sheet. Give me the eight or nine objections I will actually get in this room, ordered by how much damage each would do, and phrase each one the way the subgroup raising it would phrase it — the forecaster's version should not read like the virtue epistemologist's. For each objection, supply a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, what I should concede immediately, and a candid note on where the reply is softest. Then identify the single objection I will be hoping nobody raises and explain why my argument cannot currently absorb it, and tell me which of the objections I should raise and defuse myself in the body of the talk rather than leave to the floor.
