I'm a third-year graduate student writing the seminar paper for a philosophy-of-art course that this term is devoted to interpretation — what fixes the meaning of a literary work, and whether the maker's psychology has any role in the answer. The seminar is mixed: two students from the law school who think mainly about statutory and constitutional text, one person working on the history of biblical exegesis, and the professor, who is a committed anti-intentionalist. I arrived sympathetic to the opposite camp. I wanted to argue that some form of intentionalism has to be right, on the grounds that otherwise there is no difference between interpreting a novel and reading tea leaves, and so I built the argument below as a reductio of the moderate position — the one that says a work's meaning is settled not by what the author actually intended but by the best hypothesis an appropriately informed audience could form about that intention — on the grounds that this position quietly collapses back into the view it was designed to avoid. A classmate read the draft and told me that the collapse does work, but that the argument doesn't stop where I wanted it to: it runs straight past the moderate position and takes my own with it. I cannot tell whether she is right, and the paper is due in three weeks.

(1) What a work means is determined by the best hypothesis, given all the evidence bearing on the matter, about what its maker intended to accomplish by it. (2) "All the evidence bearing on the matter" means all of it: the work and its genre conventions, the drafts and deletions, the notebooks, the correspondence, the recorded conversations — including material that surfaces long after the work's first audience is dead. (3) As evidence accumulates, the best-supported hypothesis about an intention converges toward the intention itself, and a hypothesis that no further evidence could overturn is not merely well supported but true. (4) So at the limit of complete evidence, (1) assigns a work the meaning its maker actually intended, whether or not the work discloses that intention to anyone. (5) That consequence is intolerable. Consider a poet who, in letters published a century after her death, records that she wrote one ambiguous line as a coded reference to a quarrel with her sister. Nothing in the line's diction, form, or surrounding context gives a reader any route to that reference, and readers for a hundred years have found something else there. It cannot be right to say that the poem meant the quarrel and that a century of readers were all wrong about it. (6) Therefore (1) is false. (7) And (1) was the most intention-friendly account still in good standing: the stricter view, which ties meaning directly to what the maker intended regardless of what the work discloses, is refuted by (5) a fortiori, so no account on which intention determines meaning survives. (8) But if intention determines nothing, there is no principled difference between a work and any interpretable pattern of marks — including a sentence that tide and wind happen to scrape into the sand, which, once we can read it, we cannot help reading as addressed to us by someone. (9) So the alternatives are these: either meaning is fixed by intention, in which case (5) is a bullet we must bite, or meaning is not fixed by intention, in which case interpretation is the projection of intelligibility onto arbitrary marks and nothing distinguishes a dispute over a novel from a dispute over weathering. (10) Therefore interpretive disagreement is not about anything: there is no fact of the matter that a reading of a work could get wrong.

I would obviously rather give up (10), and if forced I would rather keep (1) than (5) — but (5) is the intuition the paper was supposed to be built around, so losing it leaves me with nothing to say. What I want is a diagnosis. Identify the step where the argument goes wrong or, if no step is actually wrong, the premise that is cheapest to abandon and what abandoning it costs; then explain why that step looked innocent to me when I wrote it down, because I still catch myself wanting to assert it. Price the options for each position in the neighborhood: the actual-intentionalist; the hypothetical-intentionalist whose view (1) is meant to state; the anti-intentionalist who welcomes (6) but has to live with (8); and the person who thinks the whole dispute is misconceived because you cannot treat marks as language at all without already crediting someone with meaning them. Tell me in particular whether (3) is doing something illegitimate with limits and with the move from undefeated to true, whether (7) is entitled to assume that these are the only two options on the table, and whether (8) is fair or whether it smuggles in the very thing it is supposed to deny. Finally, if the argument can be salvaged into a narrower conclusion — something like: the moderate view collapses into the strict one and so must be restated or abandoned, with nothing following about intention in general — write out the repaired version, marking exactly which steps change and stating what the repaired conclusion no longer entitles me to claim.
