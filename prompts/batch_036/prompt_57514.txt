Retired clinical chemist here, 35 years runing a hospital lab, now reading philosophy of medicine for fun and getting more confused the further I go.

The debate I want you to diagnose is naturalism versus normativism about disease. As I understand the two camps: the naturalists (Boorse being the one I keep getting pointed at) say disease is a value-free biological fact, namely a statistically subnormal level of functional efficiency in some part of you, measured against a reference class of your species, age and sex, where a function is just what the part typically does that contributes to survival and reproduction. The normativists say that's a magic trick, because you already slipped values in when you picked which functions to count and when you blessed survival and reproduction as the goals, and because the same deviation is a disease in one setting and a mere difference in another. Wakefield tries to split the difference with a two-part account, a factual bit about evolution going wrong plus a value bit about harm. Decades in, nobody has budged anybody.

Here's what I keep coming back to from my old job, and it's why I suspect the two sides are arguing past each other. In the lab we never said disease. We said a result fell outside the reference interval, and the reference interval was literally the central 95 percent of values from whatever reference population we had chosen. Choose healthy blood donors and one in twenty of them is abnormal by construction. Switch assay manufacturer, or reharmonize methods, or stratify by age, or build your reference group out of endurance athletes instead of the general population, and the same number from the same patient lands on the other side of the line with no change in the patient. And for the markers that actually drive a diagnosis we mostly stopped using healthy-population distributions altogether and use decision limits set by committees weighing outcomes, the HbA1c cutoff for diabetes or the eGFR thresholds, where the underlying quantity sits on a smooth risk curve and the committee has to decide how much over-diagnosis and how much missed disease it is willing to live with.

So my hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the values the normativists are hunting for aren't buried inside the notion of function at all, they're sitting out in the open in the choice of reference class and in the setting of thresholds, and the naturalist can cheerfully concede both and still insist the statistical-biological facts themselves are value-free. If that's right there is no single disagreement here, just a division of labor that both sides describe as a war.

Is my hypothesis right, or too tidy? Specifically: is there a substantive disagreement left over once you hand reference classes and thresholds to the normativists, something about what functions are, or about whether the evolutionary goal really is neutral, that could in principle be settled by argument or evidence? I'd like you to say what each side would actually reply to the lab case, where my framing overstates things, and what would count as progress, whether a distinction, a formal result, better descriptive work on how thresholds get set in practice, or nothing at all because the question is malformed. A survey of the positions is not what I'm after; I've read the survey.
