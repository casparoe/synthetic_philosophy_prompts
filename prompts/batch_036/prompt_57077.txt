I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am in the middle of a piece on a large biobank study that reports differential disease risk across ancestry-based groupings. Two of my sources reached for the same line, which my editor now wants as the nut graf: "There is no race, only racism." In context it is being made to do real work — it is offered as a reason to treat the study's groupings as artifacts of discrimination rather than as variables with any standing in the analysis. The difficulty is that one of the same sources also says, without any sense of paradox, that money is a social construction, and would never say there is no money, only greed. So the slogan cannot simply mean that what is constructed is unreal, or it would prove far too much.

Before I let this sentence carry my conclusion, I need it taken apart. Lay out exactly five precise readings it could be expressing, ordered from the one that is little more than a platitude to the most radical. For each reading, do three things in no more than a sentence or two: state it as a single precise claim, making explicit what sort of claim it is — about the reference of a term, about biology, about the direction of explanation between categories and treatment, about what exists; tag it as [platitude], [substantive but defensible], [contested], or [false]; and say whether it actually licenses my editor's move, that is, whether it gets you to the conclusion that the study's groupings can be set aside as mere artifacts. At least one of the five must explain why the parallel slogan about money sounds like a category error while this one sounds like hard-won wisdom; if none of your five does that work, you have drawn the menu wrong.

Give me the five numbered items and nothing else — no preface, no summary, and no recommendation about which reading I should print.
