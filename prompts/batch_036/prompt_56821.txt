I'm in a grad seminar on American pragmatism and for my term paper I've somehow ended up in a rabbit hole about something that barely gets mentioned in the secondary literature we've been assigned: the Dewey Commission. Quick recap so you're working from the same facts I am, since I don't want to mis-describe it — in 1937 the American Committee for the Defense of Leon Trotsky set up a "Commission of Inquiry into the Charges Made against Leon Trotsky in the Moscow Trials" and got John Dewey, then in his late seventies, to chair it. A five-person sub-commission went down to Trotsky's house in Coyoacán and ran thirteen hearings with him over about a week in April 1937, with Trotsky represented by a lawyer and the commission having its own counsel too; one member, Carleton Beals, walked out before the hearings were finished and the report notes the resignation and the fight over a question counsel said was based on private information that couldn't be put on the record. In September the commission published a 422-page report called Not Guilty, whose conclusions include the finding that "the confessions themselves contain such inherent improbabilities as to convince the Commission that they do not represent the truth, irrespective of any means used to obtain them," and the summary line "We therefore find the Moscow Trials to be frame-ups." Dewey apparently worried the whole thing would be written off as a Trotskyist front, and he insisted the commission was judging the charges, not Trotsky's politics.

What I want is an analysis of that episode through Dewey's own experimentalism about inquiry and democracy — and I want it to run in both directions, not just be a summary of either. On the theory side, set up the machinery as Dewey had it by then: inquiry as the controlled transformation of an indeterminate situation into a determinate one, the pattern of problem / problem-determination / suggested solutions / reasoning / operational testing, warranted assertibility in place of correspondence truth, the anti-spectator idea that knowing is a way of acting on things — plus the Public and Its Problems stuff about the eclipse of the public, experts versus laymen, and his point that the shoemaker may be the best judge of the shoe but only the wearer knows where it pinches. Then do the mapping honestly: what exactly was the indeterminate situation here, what counted as the problem once it was stated, what were the candidate hypotheses, and — the part I can't figure out — what could possibly count as an operational test when the object of inquiry is a closed state, the defendants are already dead, the Soviet side refuses to participate, and the main evidence is other people's testimony and documents plus an analysis of internal improbability? Treat the Beals walkout as data: is that the breakdown of a cooperative inquiry, or evidence that the commission had drifted from inquiry into advocacy, and can Dewey's own framework tell the difference from the inside? Then flip it around: does the commission rescue experimentalism from the charge that it only works for lab-scale manipulable objects (what does Dewey owe us about historical and legal knowledge of the past), and does warranted assertibility do real work in the report, or is it just "the best we could do" with a technical label on it? I'm especially interested in the later complication that Trotsky seems to have withheld from the commission that he did have some kind of contact with oppositionists inside the USSR — not what the trials alleged, but not nothing either. Does Dewey's fallibilism absorb that cleanly (the verdict was warranted given the inquiry available at the time, now it's revised), or does that move make warranted assertibility impossible to ever fail, so that it explains everything and predicts nothing? And where does the expert/public material land: is a body of a dozen experts handing a verdict to a public that can't reproduce the reasoning the cure for the eclipse of the public, or a particularly clean specimen of the disease? End by being explicit about where the theory is simply silent rather than forcing it to speak.

Same for the philosophy: if you're reconstructing what Dewey would say rather than reporting something he actually said, flag it as reconstruction.
