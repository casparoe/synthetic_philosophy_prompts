I write a science-and-ideas column and I'm mid-draft on a piece about a transit agency here thats ripping out its old turnstiles and replacing them with open-gate tap readers. The agency's design lead keeps telling me "the gate is the policy," and I want to know whether that's a real idea or just a good line for a slide deck.

To get up to speed I asked a friend who does design writing to send me a paragraph on the classic source — the Langdon Winner essay about whether artifacts have politics. This is what came back, and my editor wants to run it as a sidebar practically verbatim:

"Winner's argument is a clean case of technological determinism: once a thing is built, its physical properties dictate how people have to organize around it, and politics is just what falls out downstream. His famous example is Robert Moses's Long Island parkway bridges, built deliberately too low for buses to pass under, so poor and Black New Yorkers couldn't reach Jones Beach — an engineer's intentions get poured into concrete and then enforce themselves for fifty years. The rest of the essay is about technologies that come with a built-in form of government: nuclear power only works if you have a centralized, hierarchical, expert-run system, so opting for nuclear is opting for something close to authoritarianism, while solar lets you be decentralized and democratic. The upshot is that the neutrality story is a fraud. Everything is political in the sense that every artifact carries somebody's values, and designers ought to be answerable for the politics they embed."

I cant tell which bits are Winner and which bits are my friend free-styling, and I don't want to publish something that makes a specialist wince. Can you go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what someone who actually works on technology and politics would nod at, what's an acceptable shorthand for a real distinction, and what's just not the view? Two things I especially want pinned down: (a) is the determinism framing right, or is Winner actually positioning himself against that label, and (b) I gather he distinguishes two different ways an artifact can be political — does this paragraph collapse them into one, and if so, what does the collapse cost?

What I want to know is whether Winner's thesis survives if that particular case falls apart, or whether the whole arguement is load-bearing on it.
