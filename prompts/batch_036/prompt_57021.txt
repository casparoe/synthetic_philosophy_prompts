I am a second-year PhD student in philosophy, with a background in statistics, and I must lodge a summary of my intended dissertation project on my department's internal funding portal before next week's research-committee meeting. The portal has exactly five short-answer fields; each admits only a sentence or two, which I would ask you to treat as a hard cap of about seventy words per item, and there is no space anywhere for a preamble, a conclusion, or a list of sources. Please draft the contents of those five fields, in British English, as a numbered list of exactly five items and nothing else. I will supply the references myself, so speak of positions and arguments rather than of authors, titles, and dates.

The half-formed idea I want the proposal built around is this. The dispute between those who treat the Kelly criterion as a general account of rational choice under multiplicative risk — holding that what matters is the time-average growth rate of an agent's wealth rather than the expectation taken across states at a single time — and those who reply that this is either a relabelling of logarithmic utility or a confusion about the proper object of evaluation, seems to me to turn on an assumption neither side examines: that the stakes available to the agent are proportionally scalable, so that she can always wager any chosen fraction of her current wealth and reinvest the proceeds. My suspicion is that the much-invoked equivalence between maximising expected log wealth and maximising the long-run growth rate is really a theorem about scalability and reinvestment rather than about ergodicity as such, and that it fails in structured and predictable ways once scalability is restricted — lumpy minimum stakes, a portion of wealth that cannot be pledged or liquidated, payoffs that are additive in wealth rather than multiplicative, or outcomes that are correlated across periods. If so, the geometric criterion carries genuine normative content in some betting environments and none in others, and the debate has been conducted as though the verdict were uniform.

The five fields ask, respectively, for the following:

1. The research question, stated so precisely that one could say what would settle it.
2. Why neither of the two existing positions settles it — what each gets right and what each leaves undetermined.
3. The method: what I would actually do, and why this rather than the obvious alternatives, such as simply simulating the restricted environments, or simply arguing that the whole dispute rests on an equivocation in the word rational.
4. What a successful result would look like, and what would count as the project's having failed.
5. A realistic assessment of what is achievable in eighteen months of part-time work alongside teaching obligations, together with your own candid view of where my framing is most likely to be wrong: which consideration, were I to accept it, would persuade you that the question is not open after all, or not tractable by this route.

On the fifth item I would rather you be blunt than diplomatic. The committee will not be gentle, and I should prefer to learn where the weak point is now, before I commit three years to it.
