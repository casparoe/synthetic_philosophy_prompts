I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine — mostly AI, logistics, and the strange stuff that falls out of the two of them touching. I've got a reported feature coming up on autonomous long-haul freight corridors and my editor has agreed to run a piece of original short fiction alongside it as a companion. The fiction is what I need from you.

Here's the situation. The reporting turned up something I haven't been able to stop thinking about. On one of these standardized corridors there are two interchanges, 40 km apart, built to the same spec by the same contractor — same gantry, same sodium lighting, same cracked overpass lip, same exit sign in the same font. And the truck's route planner runs under a regulator-mandated no-persistent-state rule: its context gets wiped at each interchange for security reasons, so it genuinely cannot tell which of the two it's sitting at. The human in the seat is the fallback decision-maker at interchanges, and after eleven hours in monotone desert the human cant tell either.

The stakes, straight from my reporting:

- Take the first exit and you're routed back to the depot. Load refused. Nothing gained, nothing lost — call it 0.
- Take the second exit and you make the delivery window at the right hub. Full pay — call it 4.
- Take neither and the corridor runs out at a border checkpoint. You arrive, you deliver late, you get a fraction of the fee and a mark on your record — call it 1.

So the trap is the one from that game theory puzzle about the driver who can't remember which of two identical intersections he's at. The planner back at the depot — who knows exactly where the truck will be and what each choice is worth — will happily prescribe a policy that says: randomize, take the exit something like one time in three. That beats both deterministic policies. But the person actually at the interchange, who doesn't know which one they're at, does the arithmetic from inside their own ignorance and concludes they should exit, every single time — which is a different policy, and a worse one, and there is no way for them to see that from where they're sitting. Nobody's preferences changed. Nobody was tempted or tired or corrupt. The plan and the action just come apart.

What I want from you:

Act as a literary science fiction writer who also happens to know decision theory cold — the register of Ted Chiang or Ken Liu but grubbier, someone who has spent real time in truck stops and dispatch rooms. Not a pop-sci explainer with a story glued to the front of it.

First, before any prose: think carefully and do your brainstorming out loud. Five to eight bullets. I want to see the arithmetic actually worked — the depot's optimal policy given the 0/4/1 stakes and what it's worth in expectation, then the separate calculation the driver faces at the interchange, i.e. their conditional odds on which of the two they're standing at and what exiting versus continuing is worth from in there. Show the numbers, don't hand-wave them. Then, in a sentence or two, name the place where my freight corridor and the clean textbook puzzle might come apart in reality — a driver can squint at the odometer, or smell the rendering plant, or radio somebody — pick the leak you think matters most and tell me how the story plugs it.

Then write the story, under these constraints:

- 1,200 to 1,800 words. One protagonist in the cab. Present tense.
- The reader must not be able to tell which interchange they're reading about, at any point. Its a formal requirement, not a mood. Put the reader in the same information set as the driver. If you want to do it with repetition — the same passages coming around with slightly different wear on them — do that. I'd love it if the structure carried the puzzle instead of the dialogue.
- Nobody explains expected utility out loud. No dispatcher monologue, no "you see, it's all about the odds." The randomness has to arrive as a physical object and as a humiliation: the driver has been issued something to randomize with, and being told to sometimes turn the wheel for a reason they cannot feel is the emotional center of the piece.
- What has to land: what it's like to be bound by a plan a better-informed version of you made, when you can no longer remember being that person or reconstruct why they knew more than you do. Loyalty to your past self as a species of faith. If the driver could leave themself a note the whole problem dissolves, so make it vivid why they can't, and don't let that be a technicality you wave at.
- Give it a title. Not one containing the words absent, minded, driver, exit, or paradox.
- No footnotes, no bibliography, no author's note explaining the real puzzle. My editor would cut it anyway.

And after the story, two or three sentences flagging anything in the fiction that's technically wrong about the decision problem, so I can check it before it goes near a fact-checker. I'd much rather you tell me you bent the math for a scene than have me discover it in print.

Deadline's Thursday, so don't ask me clarifying questions — just take a run at it and I'll tell you what to fix.
