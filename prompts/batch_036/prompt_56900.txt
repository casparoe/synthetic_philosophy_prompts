So there's this thing about big museums I keep turning over and I can't quite get it to hang together.

They sink a huge share of their money into objects almost nobody sees. Most of what a major museum owns never gets hung — it sits in climate-controlled storage, sometimes for decades, and the place still pays to keep the humidity right, catalogue it, conserve it, insure it. And selling is basically taboo: the long-standing professional norm was that money raised by selling a work could only be spent acquiring other works, so a museum facing layoffs still can't flog a middling drawing to keep its free schools programme going. Some of the bodies have loosened the wording in recent years, but when a few American museums floated something like that during the pandemic the backlash was instant and at least one planned sale got called off.

Then the same institutions justify themselves to funders entirely in terms of experience: visitor numbers, participation, community engagement, wellbeing surveys.

And two other habits sit awkwardly with that second paragraph. When a picture turns out to be a forgery — the van Meegeren Vermeers are the classic case — it doesn't just lose some lustre, it comes off the wall, even though not one brushstroke changed and people had been sincerely moved by it for years. Provenance runs the other way: a work jumps in value if it once belonged to someone famous, again with zero change to how it looks. Same museums, incidentally, run Friends societies and name galleries after donors, i.e. they deliberately cultivate personal affection and then convert it into money.

What picture of value does all this presuppose? Is the store-it-and-never-sell rule committed to the objects mattering whether or not anyone is looking at them, or is it just rule-of-thumb consequentialism about perverse incentives — once directors are allowed to sell, everything gets sold? (Why can a public library weed its stacks without scandal, but a museum can't?) Does the forgery reaction show the place isn't valuing experiences at all, or can an experience-based account absorb it — I have a vague memory of an argument from around that period that the good lies neither in the beautiful thing nor in the pleasure but in the whole complex of a mind admiring something worth admiring, and that sounds like exactly what's wanted here, except that on that view selling to fund access looks totally fine.

So: reconstruct the implicit theory as best you can, separate what the practice strictly commits you to from what merely fits it comfortably, tell me which rival accounts of beauty and of value the habits quietly rule out, whether the three parts of it presuppose consistent things (including the donor-affection bit), where it's just underdetermined so that several theories would license it, and what a museum would actually have to do differently if it took an experience-based view seriously.
