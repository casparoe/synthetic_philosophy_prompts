I'm in a reading group with two friends from the econ department and we've been trying to get a handle on the imprecise probabilities / "imprecise credences" stuff, mostly because one of them keeps insisting it's the only sane way to think about deep uncertainty and the rest of us suspect its motivated reasoning with extra steps. Someone circulated a passage from a trade-press book on uncertainty to set up the discussion — not by anyone in the literature as far as I can tell, but it reads like the author knows what they're talking about. It's the clearest intro I've seen and that's exactly what worries me: I have this nagging feeling it's smoothed over precisely the parts that are contested. Here it is:

> Your credence in a claim doesn't have to be a single number. It can be a range — say your belief that the new drug works sits somewhere between 0.2 and 0.5 — and a range is just a compact way of describing a set of ordinary probability functions, each one a perfectly Bayesian state of mind. The motivation comes from Ellsberg's urn: you're told a jar holds 90 balls, 30 of them red, and the other 60 are black or yellow in some unknown mix. There simply isn't a number to be had for the chance of drawing black, and anyone who writes down a precise one is manufacturing information they don't have. Two payoffs follow. First, honesty: when you have no evidence at all, the full interval from 0 to 1 is the correct state of mind, which is strictly better than the classical principle of indifference, which pretends to know that a coin you've never seen lands heads half the time. Second, and more importantly, nothing is lost — you can still act, by taking the worst case inside the range, which is what any sensible person does when they don't know the odds, and since your belief state is still built entirely out of perfectly Bayesian credence functions, every tool that works for a single prior works here too. The one genuinely surprising result is dilation: sometimes learning something new widens your interval instead of narrowing it, which shows that the familiar picture of evidence as a machine for sharpening your opinions was wrong all along.
