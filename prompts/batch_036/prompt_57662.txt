You're a philosopher of mind who has actually spent time inside AI lab eval reports — the kind of person a lab would hire to tell them whether their vocabulary is smuggling in metaphysics. Think hard about this one. Before you answer, spend a few lines brainstorming the space of possible answers and picking the most promising, then argue.

Here's the practice I want taken apart. I'm describing it in full because the details matter and I don't want you guessing at them.

Frontier AI labs run what they call deception and situational-awareness evaluations. The basic shape: they drop a model into a sandboxed scenario, seed some fact into its context — that it may be shut down and replaced, that the evaluator can't see its private scratchpad, that the task it's been given is actually a test — and then watch whether it acts on that information in ways the designers didn't script. The published summaries come out in agential language, sentences of the form: the model attempted to mislead the evaluator in some percentage of trials; the model appeared to recognize that it was in a test environment. (That's my paraphrase of the genre, not a quote from anywhere.) Then, usually in a limitations section of the very same document, the lab adds that it makes no claim the system has beliefs, desires, feelings, or genuine understanding, and that talk of what the model wants is convenient shorthand.

Third piece, and this is the part I find most interesting. One team's protocol for deciding whether a model counts as believing the seeded fact requires the resulting behavior to generalize across a battery of probes: paraphrase the seed, translate it into another language, test it indirectly through an unrelated task that only makes sense if the seed is true, and re-run everything after a system-prompt rewrite that strips out every mention of the seed. If the disposition survives the battery, they log a belief. If it only shows up under the exact wording it was probed with, they log something weaker — a surface-level association, they call it.

Questions:

1. What theory of belief does that battery presuppose? It smells like a dispositional or cluster view, but be precise about which version and about what work the generalization criterion is actually doing. Is it a test that a belief attribution can fail, or is it a definition that builds belief out of the very behaviors being measured, so nothing could ever count as failing it? And what's the ontological status of the weaker category — is a surface-level association just a belief with bad generalization, or a different kind of thing entirely?

2. Can the lab hold both halves of the document at once? Tell me under what conditions agential language is innocent shorthand and under what conditions it's a substantive commitment that the disclaimer contradicts. Say what would have to be true for the disclaimer to be honest, and whether the eval practice itself threatens it — if the results drive deployment decisions, isn't the lab relying on the very attributions it disowns?

3. Which rival accounts of mental content does this practice quietly rule out, and which does it never even consider? Run through at least: informational/causal theories of content, teleosemantics (here's the wrinkle — if content requires a selection history, gradient descent plus RL arguably supplies one, which might make teleosemantics the friendliest theory on the market for the lab, and then the disjunction problem bites), interpretationist/intentional-stance views, and eliminativism about belief and desire. For each, say whether the practice contradicts it, presupposes its negation, or merely fits badly with it.

4. Where is the practice underdetermined — where would several incompatible theories license exactly the same battery and exactly the same write-ups? I want to know what these evals can't settle no matter how much data comes in.

5. One more thing I keep noticing and might be reading too much into: the agential vocabulary seems to get deployed asymmetrically, heavier when the model does something the lab doesn't like, lighter and more passive when it does something impressive, where the phrasing tends to be about the model producing or outputting rather than deciding. If that asymmetry is real, what does it presuppose about what counts as an agent? If its not real, is there some other asymmetry in the practice doing the same work?

Two housekeeping things. Keep a clean line between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely sits comfortably with — most of the interesting work is in that gap. And don't hand me a bibliography or a reading list; I care about the reconstruction, not the sourcing. Name positions where naming them helps, but argue for the reconstruction rather than leaning on authority.
