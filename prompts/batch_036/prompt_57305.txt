I'm a graduate student helping to run a one-day workshop in our history department on non-human causes — pathogens, climate forcing, volcanic aerosols — and the awkward opening slot has fallen to me. Rather than deliver a fourth talk about whether a bacillus can be an "actor," I want to circulate a short piece of satire as a handout and let people argue about it over coffee. The conceit I'd like you to write: a large firm that supplies historical causation on contract — a staffing agency for non-human forces, essentially — and the document is a completed annual performance review for one of its employees, with the employee's written rebuttal appended in the space the form provides. Please invent the firm, its unit names, and the employee's identifier; do not model any of it on a real institution.

The employee under review should be a mid-career tropical volcanic eruption of the sort that gets held responsible for harvest failures, grain prices, and unrest on another continent eighteen months later, and the reviewing supervisor should be a human historian in the firm's Causal Attribution unit. Use the full apparatus of a real review form: a rating scale with a legend, competency headings, a strengths section, an "areas for development" section, a development plan with deliverables and target dates, and a boxed employee-comment section. Around 900 words in total. The competencies should be recognizable caricatures of criteria actually used in the literature — evidence base and documentation (the employee's presence on the roster is attested mainly by a sulfur signal in an ice core, which two of the countersigning managers treat as dispositive and one treats as no evidence at all), mechanism specificity, dating precision, counterfactual robustness (would the harvest failure, the migration, the fiscal crisis have occurred on schedule anyway?), scope discipline (the employee has been credited with outcomes well outside its mandate, and once even with an outcome that had already happened), and collaboration with human agents. Please also include a brief forwarded memo from the firm's Compliance office observing that several of the employee's recent placements have had the incidental effect of relieving named human officials of responsibility for outcomes that were, on the paperwork, discretionary — the memo should be bureaucratic, faintly alarmed, and should not moralize.

The target of the satire is a practice, not a person: the way a non-human cause gets invoked grandly and then quietly demoted to "contributory factor" or "context" the moment anyone asks what the mechanism was, and the way a forcing flexible enough in time and space can be retrofitted to explain whatever in fact happened. So the internal consistency matters more than the punchlines. Every criticism the supervisor makes must be one that a genuine methodological skeptic of environmental explanation could make in good faith, and every line of the employee's rebuttal must be one that a defender of climate and disease history could make in good faith — including the complaint that credit is systematically reallocated to "conjunctures," "structural conditions," and individual decision-makers as soon as a client gets nervous, and that the employee is held to a standard of documentary precision never demanded of the human actors named in the same file. Do not name any scholar, living or dead, do not put words in anyone's mouth with quotation marks around them, and do not invent citations, proxy values, or journal titles. If you want famous cases to anchor a joke, use ones you are confident about — the 1815 Tambora eruption and the wretched year that followed, the mid-fourteenth-century plague pandemic, the sixth-century dust veil whose volcanic source is still disputed — and keep the details deliberately loose. The register should stay deadpan from first line to last: the form should do the work, nobody should wink, and a reader should be able to tell what is being ironized without being told. The piece needs to be funny to a first-year undergraduate who has never heard of the debate and sharper still for someone who reads ice-core and dendrochronological literature for a living.
