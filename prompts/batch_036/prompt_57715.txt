So — our linguistics reading group has asked me to give a 25-minute talk in about three weeks, with roughly twenty minutes of discussion afterwards, which is the part I'm dreading. I'm a philosophy PhD student working on mind and language and the talk defends something in the neighbourhood of Hofstadter's "shallowness" objection to machine translation: the line of argument he developed in Le Ton beau de Marot and then pressed again, more pointedly, in that Atlantic piece from a few years back where he put Google Translate on a passage from Yang Jiang's memoir We Three and argued that the shape of the errors shows there's no model of the situation behind the words. But my angle isn't just "he called it." It's that the neural era sharpens the objection instead of burying it, and my actual load-bearing claim — the thing I want the talk to turn on — is that translation evaluation is a two-party act. The system emits a sketch; the human rater, or reader, or whoever is reading the LLM-as-judge's verdict, supplies the situation that the sketch is a sketch of. So when someone says "human eval says near-parity now," that isn't evidence against Hofstadter, it's a live performance of his thesis: the understanding is happening in the room, not in the weights. Fluency and situation-modelling are orthogonal, and fluency is exactly what makes the residual failures predictable and easy to paper over. There's a second strand too, which is that Hofstadter's demos were diagnostic rather than refutational, and the field quietly converted them into a benchmark question — can the system render this sentence — which changed the subject. (I know the Weaver decoding-credo business, the 1947 letter to Wiener about Russian really being English in strange symbols, is where the whole thing starts, and I want to use it, but I don't think that's the crux.) Also, entirely irrelevant to the talk: I've spent a month reading through all the Ma mignonne translations in the back of the book and I have opinions about them that I am not allowed to express at this event.

The audience is the problem. About fifteen people and almost none of them are philosophers: a core of MT and LLM researchers, two of whom work at a lab nearby on evaluation and one of whom does low-resource MT and is acutely aware that her domain is not sixteenth-century French verse; two psycholinguists who do human sentence processing and will happily talk about surprisal and prediction error; one historian of computing; and a single philosophy-of-mind person who's broadly sympathetic but thinks I'm just running the Chinese Room with translation as the case study. Nobody there has read Le Ton beau de Marot. To most of them Hofstadter is the Gödel Escher Bach guy and, if they've seen the Atlantic piece at all, it's as an old man being grumpy about a demo he didn't like. My advisor thinks I'm walking into a firing squad and I dont entirely disagree.

Build me the Q&A prep sheet, not a defence of the view. I want the eight or so objections this particular room will actually raise — not the generic AI-skeptic ones I'd get at a philosophy conference, and not strawmen I can knock down — ordered by how much damage each would do to the talk. Phrase each one the way the person raising it would phrase it, in their vocabulary, with whatever terms are load-bearing for them: adequacy versus faithfulness metrics, hallucination, calibration, probing, distributional semantics, the "scaling plus better data closed the gap" story, and so on. For each: a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, an honest note on where that reply is soft or where I'm quietly changing the subject, and a tag saying whether I should concede it up front in the talk to defuse it or hold it back for Q&A.
