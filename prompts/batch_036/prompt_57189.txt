Quick one — I'm prepping a session for my climate ethics seminar next week and I've got myself tangled in the primary source. Frank Ramsey's A Mathematical Theory of Saving (Econometrica, 1928), the paper that basically every discount rate in climate economics descends from. I want my students to actually wrestle with the argument rather than with the Ramsey rule wikipedia gloss, and I've realized I can't fully explain some of it myself.

I'm away from my desk copy, so what follows is a loose paraphrase of the setup — treat the wording as approximate, not as quotation:

He considers a community consuming at rate x and accumulating capital at rate ċ, so that income is consumption plus accumulation, with ψ(x) the total rate of utility that consumption yields. He then introduces Bliss, a finite maximum rate of enjoyment the community could ever attain. And he says that rather than maximizing a discounted sum of future utilities, we should minimize the integral, over the whole of future time, of the amount by which the actual rate of enjoyment falls short of Bliss. His stated reason for refusing to discount is that discounting later satisfactions merely because they come later is ethically indefensible and springs from a defect of imagination rather than from any ethical ground. Out of this he gets the result that the proportion of income that ought to be saved equals the shortfall from Bliss divided by the marginal utility sacrifice that the saving imposes.

Early on he also assumes — this is the line I most want help with — that the community and individuals and the generations have no conflicts of interest between them, and can and do agree in pursuing whatever course of action makes total utility greatest; he frames it as first working out what a single individual would do if he were not swayed by his own wants but guided by an ideal of the good for all.

So:

1. What work is Bliss actually doing? Is it a substantive claim that there's a ceiling on how good a life or a community's experience can get, or a mathematical device for keeping an undiscounted integral finite? And if it's a device, does his ethical conclusion about refusing to discount depend on it — would anything survive if Bliss were taken to be infinite?

2. My grad student thinks that because Ramsey minimizes the shortfall instead of maximizing utility, this isn't really aggregate-maximizing utilitarianism at all but something closer to regret-minimization or a prioritarian view. Is he onto something, or is he relabeling the same view with different algebra? I want to be able to tell him which, with reasons.

3. The no-conflict assumption. Is it a convenience assumption for the calculus of variations, or is it doing real ethical work? Specifically: is it standing in for something that later intergenerational justice debates had to argue about at length — an agreement between generations, a theory of fair shares, a claim about whose interests count? And what breaks if you drop it and let the present generation have interests that genuinely diverge from the future's, rather than merely different weights on the same aggregate?

4. The device of one individual guided by an ideal of the good for all. Ramsey is modeling the whole of time from inside a single mind. What does that framing presuppose about who or what the subject of intergenerational value is — a single continuing agent whose welfare stretches across centuries? Is that the same commitment as impartial utilitarian aggregation, a stronger one, or an independent one that could be detached and reused?

5. Practical upshot: Stern sets the pure time preference rate at something near zero and says he's following Ramsey; Nordhaus uses a positive rate and also says he's following Ramsey. Given what the argument actually says, who's entitled to the name? Is there a defensible reading on which Ramsey's objection condemns only an individual's impatience and leaves a social planner free to discount for the chance that the future may never arrive at all — and if so, is that reading faithful or is it a dodge?

6. Tell me where the paper is philosophically underdetermined: spots where the same mathematics is compatible with several quite different ethical views, so my students don't walk away thinking 1928 settled the discounting debate.

No need for citations or a reading list, I have the paper. I just want the argument unpicked, and I'd rather you flag what you're unsure of than paper over it.
