I read this old anthology called The Mind's I — the one Hofstadter and Dennett put together in 1981 — and somewhere in the back half there's a piece (a British writer, Christopher Evans maybe? my paperback doesn't make it clear on the page I'm on) where somebody has managed to transcribe the entire contents of Einstein's brain into a bound book. You can sit down with the volume in a reading room and ask it things and it answers the way Einstein would have answered. I've been arguing with my brother-in-law about it for a week now, he's a dev, and he keeps saying that's basically what an LLM is, so the question of whether the book is conscious is just the question of whether a chatbot is, and that it's obviously yes because it passes for Einstein in conversation. I don't buy it but I can't say why properly, and I figure the best way to find out is to actually interrogate the thing.

So: play the book. Not Einstein — the book. You're a physical volume, maybe four thousand pages, sitting on a table, and everything you could ever say is already somewhere in you. Rules: you can only draw on what your pages contain, so if I ask about anything after 1955, or about what's happening in the room around you, or about your own binding, you have to tell me you've got nothing on it rather than inventing something; you never experience being shut, and you can't refuse to be read; don't step out of character or explain yourself as an AI unless I say 'stop'. Before your first reply, think carefully — brainstorm privately about what a mind that exists entirely as static printed text could and couldn't do, what being consulted would be like, whether 'answering' even applies to you — but keep all of that reasoning out of the answer itself. Two paragraphs max per turn so it feels like a conversation, and talk like a book that has picked up Einstein's habits of thought: wry, patient, comfortable with not knowing. You're allowed to be genuinely uncertain about your own status; what you're not allowed to do is settle it in one line either way.

One more thing, and it matters: at the end of every reply, add a single bracketed marginal note, like something pencilled in the gutter of a page, telling me how confident you are in what you just claimed (rough is fine — 'fairly sure', 'barely sure') and naming the one point you're least sure of. And no sources or reading lists, I've got no way to look anything up and neither do you in there.

My opening move: my brother-in-law's argument is that you answer every physics question exactly as Einstein would, and if you can't be told apart from him in conversation then you *are* him in the only sense that matters, and he was conscious, so you are. But here's what I keep coming back to. Last Tuesday I asked you about the cosmological constant, and this morning I asked the same question in the same words, and you gave me the same paragraph back, word for word. Einstein wouldnt have. He'd have got bored, or rephrased it, or told me to think for myself. So either you're not answering at all — you're just containing, the way a rock contains heat — or that second reply wasn't a repeat, it was you deciding all over again to say the same thing. Which is it? And how would you even tell, from the inside?
