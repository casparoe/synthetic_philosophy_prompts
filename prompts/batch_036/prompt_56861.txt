I sit on the animal welfare committee at a mid-sized public aquarium-and-science-center. Besides signing off on husbandry standards we maintain the collection's classification list, which sorts every holding into one of three statuses: **subject** (gets an individual plan — keepers offer choices, refusals get logged as information, and nothing gets split, transferred, or culled without coming back to us), **colony subject** (no per-animal plans, but the group as a whole is one subject, so dividing or transferring it still needs our sign-off), or **husbandry only** (standard protocols, staff handle and propagate freely). The list is supposed to track whether there's actually a somebody in there whose preferences the keepers have to consult, or whether the thing is fully handled by water chemistry, physiology, and species-typical behavior. We've never written that criterion down properly, which is now my problem.

Our precedents, with the reasons recorded in the minutes (paraphrasing, roughly):

1. **Common octopus** — subject. Keepers' predictions of what it would do next only started working once they wrote their notes in terms of what it seemed to want and what it seemed to think was inside each jar; the old protocol built on species-typical foraging had failed.
2. **Cuttlefish** — subject. Switched hunting approach mid-session after a failed attempt; staff couldn't get that out of any repertoire sheet.
3. **Ochre sea star** — husbandry only. The aquarist predicts it perfectly well as "moves up the odor gradient toward the mussel bed," and nothing more was needed.
4. **Reef-building coral colony** — husbandry only. Everything the colony does tracks the water-chemistry log; the spreadsheet was judged sufficient.
5. **Venus flytrap** — husbandry only. Education was told the signage may not say the plant "decides" when to close; approved wording referred to trigger hairs and pressure changes in the cells — even after one member pointed out that a trap only fires if a hair is touched twice in quick succession, which arguably is the plant keeping a short record of what's happened to it.
6. **Honeybee hive, pollinator house** — colony subject. No individual bee gets a plan, because there's nothing a keeper could offer that one bee would take or refuse; but the hive is one subject, so requeening, splitting, or transferring comes to us. The minutes accepted the beekeeper's phrase "it doesn't want to swarm this year" as tracking something real.
7. **The reptile house climate controller** ("the HVAC brain") — instrument, husbandry only; we classified it on the same list for consistency. The minutes explicitly note that the technicians cannot troubleshoot it without saying "it thinks the room is too damp," and that the committee did not treat that as decisive.

New case, and it's on next Tuesday's agenda with two requests that collide. The invertebrate house keeps a *Physarum polycephalum* culture, the yellow slime mold, roughly dinner-plate sized, growing on oat flakes. (a) Education wants to cut it into about forty fragments for school outreach kits — each fragment grows into a full plasmodium within days — and to put it on a display panel billed as an organism that "decides where to go" and "remembers where it's been." (b) A keeper has filed an objection asking that the culture be classified as a subject with its own plan. Her reasons: the exhibit runs a weekly twelve-hour chill cycle, and on the two occasions we skipped the chill the plasmodium still slowed and bunched up around the time the chill would have hit; it routes around a patch of agar where it previously met a bitter repellent even when nothing is there now; and when she offers two food sources at different distances it trades quality off against distance in a way she says she can only predict by writing "it expects the far one to be the better one." The curator's counter: it's a single giant cell with no nervous system, the whole show is oscillating tubules and cytoplasmic streaming, there's a mechanistic model of the chill response she's confident in, the anticipation fades after a few cycles without reinforcement which is exactly what entrainment looks like, and if a keeper can make forty new individuals with a scalpel there is no single somebody left to consult.

Whatever we rule also governs the *Caulerpa* tank — also one enormous cell, also propagated by cutting — and it sets the pattern for the planarian regeneration touch-tank going in this spring. Half the committee wants "husbandry only, and strike 'decides' from the signage"; the other half says the hive precedent commits us to at least colony-subject status for anything we've accepted intentional-sounding descriptions of.
