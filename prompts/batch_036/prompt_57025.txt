I write the science-and-ideas column for a national outlet, and I am reporting a piece about a municipal water-contamination episode that came down to a redundancy that never fired. The facts, as the utility's own incident review lays them out: a turbidity alarm sounded at the treatment plant at about two in the morning; the overnight shift carried two operators, and the system was deliberately built so that either one acknowledging the alarm from his own console would have started a sequence isolating the affected basin before the morning pump schedule pushed it out into the distribution network; neither acknowledged it; the water reached roughly nine thousand homes, and several hundred people reported gastrointestinal illness over the following week. Both operators were separately on duty, separately trained, and separately accountable under the plant's own written procedures.

For the ideas angle I interviewed two academics, and their disagreement — which I recorded and have since transcribed — is the most substantive one I have had to report in years, and I cannot tell who won. I will call them M, a metaphysician who works on causation, and T, a tort scholar. M's opening move was that omissions are not events and causation is a relation between events, so nothing that failed to happen at two in the morning caused anything; the outbreak was caused by the contamination and by the pump schedule, and what the operators did was fail to prevent it, which is a different thing entirely, and any theory that treats the two as the same is buying causal relevance with counterfeit currency. T answered that causation as the law uses it was never a metaphysical substance but counterfactual dependence screened by a policy-laden filter, that but for at least one of the failures there would have been no outbreak, and that if an ontology cannot find room for that then the ontology is answering a question nobody in the building was asking. M replied that T now owes a way to stop the flood: on counterfactual dependence over absences, M's own failure to fly to the plant that morning and M's failure to have been born are equally causes of the outbreak, and the only non-arbitrary repair is a normative filter admitting just the absences that violate a duty or a settled expectation — a filter which, M insisted, is not causal at all but a way of choosing which causal candidates to mention, so T has conceded that the causation lives elsewhere and the cause talk is really about whom to hold to account. T said the filter is not a patch bolted onto the account but the account itself, and that it is simply the doctrine of duty; and that M's alternative cannot draw distinctions anyone needs, since on M's view the physical situation in the control room is identical whether the two men are employees operating under a procedure or strangers who wandered in off the street, and if nothing about that difference makes a causal difference then causation was never going to deliver the discrimination M claims for it. M then turned the case against T directly: with two operators, each independently sufficient and each failing, the but-for test fails for both, so T either gets "neither caused it," which is a reductio, or reaches for a substantial-factor test, or for asking whether each failure was a necessary element of some set of conditions jointly sufficient for the outcome — and every one of those is a further choice about which alternatives to hold fixed, which is to say more of the very normativity T had just declared to be the whole point. T said overdetermination is a solved problem in practice, that two hunters firing at the same instant does not make either of them a non-cause, and that the more damaging asymmetry runs the other way: in a single-operator version of the case, where the but-for test works perfectly and everyone agrees the operator is answerable, M still says there was no causation at all, so M's view is not more discriminating but uniformly negative, and a uniformly negative verdict about a whole category cannot count as a discovery about that category. M's last move was that this is why M suspects one word is being made to do two jobs — a productive job, about what got the contamination into the taps, and an attributive job, about whose conduct makes a difference we are prepared to answer for — and that T wants a single relation to carry both, at the cost of doing neither well. T refused the diagnosis: prevention is causal language all the way through, since M cannot say what the acknowledgment would have done without causal talk, so M's productive story already borrows from what it excludes, and if this were merely two jobs there would be no live disagreement about the single-operator case, where in fact there is one.

One feature of the incident neither of them engaged, and which I noticed only while transcribing: the first operator did not merely fail to acknowledge. According to the review, he had switched off the audible alerts on his console at the start of the shift, after a week of false alarms from a faulty sensor. So there is a positive act in the case as well as an absence, and it has the shape of a prevention of a prevention — his switching off stopped the alarm from reaching him, and the alarm reaching him would have stopped the contaminated water from reaching the taps — while the second operator, who had simply left the control room to help a colleague move equipment, looks like a pure omission. Please approach this as a philosopher of causation who is genuinely literate in tort doctrine, and think it through carefully before pronouncing: begin by sketching the space of ways the dispute could resolve, including outright victory for one side, a clean split, a merely verbal standoff, and any substantive third framework both speakers overlooked, and only then commit. I want a rating for each of the eight turns in the order they were made, on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a sentence of justification apiece; a verdict on each of four sub-questions — whether omissions ever cause anything, whether the normative filter is a concession or the substance of T's position, whether the two-operator structure costs T more than T admits, and whether M's "two jobs" reading is available or evasive; an account of what both of them missed; and an assessment of whether the switched-off-alert wrinkle helps M more than M's own argument did, given that it supplies a real act where M's position seemed to need one. Do not send me references, a reading list, or anything I would have to look up and cannot verify — I want the reasoning exposed, not the apparatus. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which single argument or consideration, made better than either speaker made it, would move your rating on any turn or flip your overall verdict, and where in your own reasoning you think you are most likely to be wrong. Present the ratings compactly, then the four verdicts, then the rest, and end with your overall verdict on a single line in exactly this form: \answer{...}, where the braces contain M, T, or DRAW, a colon, and no more than six words.
