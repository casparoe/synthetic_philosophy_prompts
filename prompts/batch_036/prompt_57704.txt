Right, so I sit on the committee of a small housing co-op in Sheffield — forty-one households, all ages, all sorts of circumstances — and in March we finally settled a five-year row with the firm that used to manage the building. They'd been overcharging us on the communal heating since 2016, and we've just had about £38,000 come back to use as a lump sum. Nobody wants it going into the repair fund and nobody wants it sitting in the account "for later", so the question is what to actually do with it before the AGM in six weeks. Unfortunately I'm the one who has to draft the proposal, and after Tuesday's meeting I've realised I don't have a principled way of choosing between four options that all sound completely reasonable when different people say them out loud. The four: (a) equal split, forty-one cheques of roughly £930, done in an evening, no forms, everybody's claim treated as identical; (b) straight pro-rata refund, each household getting back precisely what it overpaid, which is what the settlement letter implies and which the other side's solicitor called the "only sensible" reading — except that the biggest overpayers were the two top-floor flats (largest, warmest, and owned by the two highest-earning households in the building, one of whom is a barrister couple who've already said they'd probably just donate theirs) and, more interestingly, Nadia on the third floor, who uses a wheelchair, is home most days, and runs her heating far harder than anyone because she can't regulate her own temperature; so this rule happens to hand a big chunk to the one person whose condition genuinely makes her life more expensive to live, while also handing the two very biggest cheques to the two people who need them least; (c) an equal base amount plus a top-up that households can apply for if they can demonstrate hardship, with the committee assessing the applications; or (d) spend the lot communally on things that improve everyone's day to day — the derelict back yard, the laundry, a proper party — on the argument that what we should care about is how good people's lives actually feel to them rather than what lands in their bank accounts.

Two things have me stuck. First, option (c) is the one that sounds fairest and would, I suspect, quietly fail the exact person it's designed for. There's a woman in the building, Maureen, second floor, seventies, chest condition, gets by on a very small income, and in eleven years has never once asked the co-op for anything; she told the meeting, warmly and without any bitterness, that we shouldn't fuss over her, that she's always been happy with very little and can't see the point of a song and dance. Two members then cited her as evidence that the worse-off people here aren't really suffering, and used that to push for the equal split. It made me feel ill but I couldn't say why at the time and I still can't quite. Second, there's Dan and Priya, both comfortably paid, who want a slice under option (c) to convert their spare room into a soundproofed practice space because Dan plays in a working band; their line is that they'd get far more out of £4,000 than anyone else would, and that penalising people for having ambitions seems a bit rich coming from the rest of us. I genuinely can't tell whether that's a serious claim or a joke, or how I'd justify saying either to their faces. I ran the whole thing past a mate who does grant-making at a community foundation and she said I was overthinking it, that its a refund and not a benefit, so just give people their own money back and stop moralising about it — which sounded sensible for about an hour and then stopped.

So: which of the four is actually the fairest, and is there a fifth I'm not seeing — something that gets Nadia meaningfully more without an application form and without rewarding the barristers, and that doesn't collapse the moment someone asks why we're not just refunding what people paid? And separately, can you tell me what was wrong with the way Maureen's cheerfulness got used as an argument, because I know something was wrong with it and I've got six weeks to explain that to forty people, one of whom is a retired accountant who will very much enjoy taking whatever I say apart. I don't want a survey of the theory for its own sake, but do tell me what's driving my own intuitions here, since they seem to be pointing in four directions at once.
