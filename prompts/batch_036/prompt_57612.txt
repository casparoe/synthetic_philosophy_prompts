I'm working through a view about precommitment and I want somebody to argue me into it, not give me the even-handed survey version. Here's the position, in my own rough words:

Ulysses contracts can't be grounded in rational choice at all, because of what I've taken to calling the mate's regress. Ulysses doesn't bind himself — he gets the crew to bind him and to ignore whatever he shouts later. But the crew's willingness to follow the earlier order rather than the live one is itself something that has to be secured, and securing it just pushes you back a level: a promise, a standing instruction, an oath, a second crew watching the first, forever. So whatever does the actual binding at the bottom isn't a decision or a calculation, it's a disposition people happen to have — basically Hart's rule of recognition point turned into a problem for precommitment.

Please make the case for this. Two things I want you to really push on:

1. Get the regress going carefully. Say exactly which premise generates it, and then handle the obvious stopper — that the crew has its own long-run interest in keeping the deal, since a crew that breaks oaths today gets no oaths tomorrow. Explain why that's just one more floor of the same building rather then a foundation.

2. Show that the Homeric version is the easy case and constitutional entrenchment is the hard one. In the story Ulysses solves the crew problem with wax: he takes away their capacity to respond to the song entirely. Judges, soldiers and central bankers can't be waxed, because if they stopped responding to reasons they'd be useless at the job. So argue that Article V-style entrenchment and central bank independence are strictly worse off than the mast, not better.

Use Madison's parchment-barrier worry if it helps and pick whatever real institutional example you think is most damning. No references or reading list needed, I just want the argument. If you think one of the steps is actually bogus, make the case anyway and tell me at the end which step you'd drop.
