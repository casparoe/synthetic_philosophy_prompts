I retired four years ago from a research career in experimental physics, most of it spent on questions of stability, where the interesting issue is never whether some quantity changes but whether a boundary condition is met. Since then I have taken up philosophy with more seriousness than my former colleagues expected, and this year I am helping a friend who convenes a final-year seminar on free will and moral responsibility. She has asked me to draft one of the essay questions for the final examination, and I should like your assistance in bringing it to a standard I can defend.

The topic is Galen Strawson's Basic Argument: the claim that to be truly morally responsible for how one acts one must be truly responsible for how one is, in the relevant mental respects, and that this generates a regress terminating only in the impossible demand for self-creation. My difficulty with the summaries I have read is that they present the argument as a piece of rhetoric whose force one either feels or does not. I want a question that instead makes the student exhibit the mechanism and, as in any sound measurement, renders it sensitive to one distinction and insensitive to everything else.

Here is the wrinkle I want built in, and I would be grateful if you preserved it rather than softened it. The question should stipulate a case: an agent, call her Vesper, who has existed for an infinite past and who, at each of infinitely many earlier stages, deliberately reshaped her own character in ways for which she was, by hypothesis, responsible — so that for every state of her character there is an earlier state she formed responsibly, yet there is no first such act and no moment at which she brought herself into being. The students must then address two things. First, whether the argument's demand is a demand that the chain terminate — in which case Vesper may meet it, or at least the objection to her case is no longer the regress — or a demand for origination, in which case an endless series of self-formings is still not self-creation; they must say which reading the argument requires and what follows on each. Second, whether true moral responsibility, as the argument conceives it, is a quantity admitting of degrees, so that a partially self-formed finite agent such as the rest of us retains some nonzero share, or a threshold property lost the moment the boundary condition fails — and what that choice implies for how we should describe ordinary blame. A first-class answer should also state what a theorist who requires only responsiveness to reasons, rather than origination, must concede about the notion of desert.

Constraints: closed book, 75 minutes, 25 marks on a 100-mark paper; the vignette must be stated in full within the question so that no external text is needed; and nothing should turn on recalling who said what — I would rather the question forbade citation altogether.

Please provide, in this order:

1. The question exactly as it will appear on the paper.
2. A model answer of roughly 700 words, argued rather than listed, with no references and no bibliography.
3. The marking scheme as a single JSON object with these fields: total_marks (integer); components (an array of five objects, each with id, tests — the specific distinction that component isolates — marks, must_do (array of strings), full_marks_requires (string), common_error (string), and error_cost (string)); A_vs_B (an object with two arrays, A_requires and B_typically, each naming the moves that separate the two grades); and hard_caps (an array of objects with condition and maximum_mark, covering answers that, for instance, merely restate the regress or conflate degrees with origination). The marks across components must sum to total_marks, and no component may award marks for naming an author or a work.

After the JSON, add one short paragraph identifying the single sentence of the model answer that most nearly decides whether a script reaches first-class standard, so that I may calibrate against the actual essays.
