I am a third-year doctoral student in philosophy of mathematics, and this autumn I am co-organising a two-day graduate workshop on constructive foundations. The registration list is telling: most attendees are classically trained — analysts, a few set theorists, two or three people from theoretical computer science — and the form asked them to indicate their foundational sympathies, whereupon eleven of them wrote some variant of "intuitionist" without meaning anything determinate by it. I want to hand out a diagnostic decision tree at the start of the first session that sorts each participant into a named position on the basis of what they actually say about concrete mathematical situations, not on the basis of which label they like the sound of. It should work as a self-administered instrument: someone who has never encountered proof theory, and who knows only classical first-order logic plus a standard course in real analysis, should be able to follow every branch and end up somewhere definite.

So please build the tree from the logical space first and from the list of names second. Every node must be phrased as a question about a practice or an inference that a classically trained mathematician can answer without knowing which principle is at stake: whether a proof that an unbounded search cannot always fail hands you the value at which it succeeds; whether a total real-valued function on a closed bounded interval must be uniformly continuous, and what you are prepared to say if someone produces a rule that appears to define one which is not; whether a function on the continuum may be given by a freely proceeding sequence of choices rather than by a rule; whether countable choice is already contained in what the universal quantifier over the naturals means, or is a separate substantive assumption needing its own warrant; whether second-order comprehension may range over totalities defined in terms of itself; whether a real number given by an incomplete expansion has a determinate value now; and whether the logic one works in is fixed by a theory of meaning and of what counts as a proof, or merely by which models one happens to find persuasive. Aim for roughly twelve to fourteen branching questions and about twelve leaves, allowing leaves to be shared where the space genuinely does not separate two views. The leaves I insist on seeing located somewhere: Brouwerian intuitionism with choice sequences; Bishop-style constructive mathematics; the Russian school, which treats every total function on the naturals as computable; recursive realisability understood as a mathematical reading of the connectives rather than as a philosophy; Martin-Löf type theory and the propositions-as-types identification; predicativism; constructive set theory of the CZF type alongside intuitionistic ZF; internalism drawn from topos and smooth-infinitesimal models; semantic anti-realism about proof conditions that deliberately declines to nominate a particular formal system; ultrafinitism; and unrepentant classical practice, which I want on the map as a control rather than as a failure case. For each leaf give a name, the single question whose answer most reliably identifies someone as belonging there, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Two further demands, and then a request about how you should present your own confidence. First, after the tree, walk through the combinations of answers that the space permits but that nobody actually defends — accepting both the computability thesis and Brouwer's continuity principle, say, or accepting free choice sequences whilst also demanding impredicative comprehension — and diagnose each unoccupied cell as incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored, with a sentence on why. Where two of my mandatory leaves cannot be told apart by questions of this kind, flag that explicitly rather than inventing a discriminating question; my own suspicion, which may well be mistaken, is that the Bishop leaf and the semantic anti-realist leaf will prove inseparable by practice alone, and that the realisability leaf may collapse into the Russian one. Second, please do not soften the deliverable into a menu of alternative trees: commit to one structure, mark the nodes you think shaky rather than omitting them, and note that I have the secondary literature already, so I want no bibliography, reading list, or citations of any kind. Finally, close with a short section saying where you think this instrument is most likely to fail: which branch point you are least confident really separates two views; which mandatory leaf you suspect is a philosophical fiction that no working mathematician or logician actually occupies; and, most importantly, what would change your mind about the shape of the tree — which metamathematical result, independence theorem, or separation argument concerning the principles sitting at particular nodes would force you to redraw it. If your verdict on some node rests on a contested reading of what one of the historical figures was committed to, say so and say which reading you have assumed.
