I serve on the collections committee of a regional art museum, and I would like help bringing a much-repeated slogan into focus before our next meeting, where it is on the verge of settling a substantial policy question.

The facts. We hold a small devotional panel from a Netherlandish workshop, dated by dendrochronology to the late 1480s. Conservation has determined that the ground layer is lifting and that the panel cannot tolerate sustained illumination at current gallery levels. A specialist studio has produced a facsimile: the surface was scanned at very high resolution, the image printed and finished by hand, and the whole mounted on a period-appropriate oak support in the original's own frame. At gallery light levels, from behind a railing, our staff cannot reliably pick the facsimile out of a pair. The proposal is to display the facsimile permanently and keep the original in dark storage, available to scholars by appointment. A trustee who supports the proposal has twice closed off discussion with the same sentence:

"If visitors can't tell the difference, there is no difference."

He has used it in two distinct arguments. First, that the swap costs our audience nothing worth caring about. Second — and this is the use that concerns me more — that the objection grounded in the panel's status as a historical document, a physical thing that has survived five centuries and bears the marks of that survival, is not an objection at all but a species of superstition.

What I want is not a verdict on the proposal but a menu. Please lay out, as a numbered list, the distinct precise claims this sentence could be making, running from the platitudinous to the radical. For each reading, give me:

(a) the claim stated exactly, including which parameters it fixes — indistinguishable *to whom*, under what viewing conditions, at what time, in what *respect* (properties, value, identity, or justification for preference), and whether the indistinguishability is actual or merely possible;
(b) a verdict: platitude, substantive but defensible, false, or not well-formed;
(c) whether it is strong enough to carry the first argument, the second, both, or neither;
(d) whether the trustee requires a different reading at the two moments, and if so, which parameter is being shifted between them without notice.

Then three further items:

1. Address the transitivity of indiscriminability. Our docents report that some visitors, when the two panels are placed side by side, express a preference for one they cannot subsequently identify; and our conservator reports that under raking light the facsimile's craquelure is flush with the surface rather than recessed, so the two are discriminable there. Does the existence of conditions under which they differ refute the slogan, or does it merely expose a parameter the slogan leaves unfilled? Say which, and say what follows for an institution whose mandate concerns an audience that will spend, on average, ninety seconds in front of the panel.

2. State whether the slogan presupposes that everything worth caring about in the work is available in a single viewing — that is, that all value-relevant facts supervene on the perceptible surface as presented at a moment — and whether that assumption can be pried apart from the rest of the sentence, or whether it is what the sentence is really asserting.

3. Identify the strongest reading the trustee could have adopted: the one on which his conclusion survives careful argument rather than equivocation. Then state what my side must concede to him if he adopts it, so that I can walk into the meeting knowing what is actually negotiable.

Finally, note where the sentence is doing dialectical work rather than asserting anything — whether it functions to shift the burden of proof onto anyone who claims a difference exists, and whether that shift is legitimate in a decision where the two sides' exposure to being wrong is so asymmetric.

Keep the analysis self-contained: I want reasoning about the readings themselves, not an inventory of which thinkers hold which, and no bibliography.
