I am preparing a session for a graduate reading group in formal epistemology, and there is a dispute about Dutch book arguments that I would like you to help me settle — not by surveying the positions on offer, but by taking a side and defending it.

The group already accepts the mechanical part. A synchronic Dutch book is a finite collection of bets, each priced at the agent's own announced betting quotient, whose payoffs together guarantee her a net loss whatever the world turns out to be; such a collection can be constructed precisely when her quotients violate the probability axioms — a negative value somewhere, a failure to assign one to a tautology, or a failure of additivity across disjoint propositions. The diachronic version runs the same trick across time: an agent whose posterior is not related to her prior by conditionalization on what she has learned can generally be led to accept bets, some offered now and some offered after the learning, that jointly guarantee a loss. Nobody in the room doubts the arithmetic. What the room does not agree about is how much the arithmetic buys.

Consider the case I want to hang the discussion on. Nadia is prepared to sell a bet that pays one dollar if H, for forty cents, and prepared to sell a bet that pays one dollar if not-H, for fifty-five cents. She therefore collects ninety-five cents and owes a dollar in every outcome: a guaranteed loss of five cents. Two features of the case matter for my purposes. First, the construction depends on a step from her willingness to accept each bet separately to her acceptance of the pair, the step usually called the package principle; and second, the loss scales linearly with the stakes, so the very same incoherence, applied to a one-cent payoff, exposes her to a loss of half a cent — less, plausibly, than the attention and time she would have to spend appraising the two offers.

Here is the thesis I want adjudicated. (i) The existence of a Dutch book against an agent is evidence of a specifically epistemic defect in her beliefs rather than merely of imprudent betting habits, because the premise identifying her partial beliefs with her fair betting prices can be argued for rather than simply stipulated at the outset. (ii) The magnitude of the guaranteed loss is irrelevant to the force of the argument: the half-cent book condemns Nadia exactly as firmly as the five-hundred-dollar book, since what does the work is the existence of a loss-guaranteeing structure and not its size. You should tell me whether you accept the thesis as a whole, accept one conjunct and reject the other, or reject both — and if you reject both, say what you take the argument to establish instead. In defending your verdict, take seriously the best version of the opposing case: that the betting-quotient premise makes the conclusion nearly analytic; that an agent who is risk-averse with respect to money rather than utility, or who faces indivisible packages, or for whom transaction costs swamp the stakes, may be coherent and yet unexploitable, or incoherent and yet immune; and that recasting the bets in utility rather than dollars, the standard repair, may quietly concede that the target of the argument is dispositions to bet rather than degrees of belief.

I am asking for a committed answer, not a balanced one. Take a definite position on each conjunct, give your reasons, and weigh the objections by arguing against them rather than cataloguing them; do not end by handing the choice back to me or noting that reasonable people differ. Where your position depends on a contested reading of what the Dutch book argument is supposed to prove, I would like that dependency identified explicitly in a sentence or two at the end, so that I know precisely what your verdict is conceding.
