I teach a community-college intro course called Life in the Universe — 30 non-majors, mostly first-years, one 75-minute block, they sit at six tables of five. About half had a real high school biology class and half didn't. I want an in-class activity that makes them feel how hard it is to say what life *is*, and specifically that the problem isn't that they haven't thought hard enough yet: whatever criterion they write down keeps either swallowing things nobody calls alive or spitting out things everybody does.

The shape I have in mind: each table is a defintion committee for a fictional sample-return mission. Round 1 they write an operational criterion on a card and hand it to me sealed — no taking it back, no peeking at what's coming. Round 2 they get a deck of specimen cards and have to rule each one in or out strictly by their own criterion, and write the verdict down even when they hate it. Round 3 is a challenge round where another table (or me) attacks one of their verdicts and they have to defend it or amend.

Please write the specimen deck for me. I want these in it, at minimum:

- a giant virus — the kind that's big enough to see under a light microscope and carries some of the genes for its own protein-building machinery, so it isn't the easy "just a bag of genes" case
- a prion: a misfolded protein that spreads by converting normal copies of itself, no nucleic acid anywhere in the picture
- a viroid: naked circular RNA, no protein coat, a few hundred bases, makes plants sick
- an obligate bacterial endosymbiont that lives inside insect cells with a drastically stripped-down genome and can't be grown in a dish outside its host — not self-sustaining on any normal reading, and yet nobody wants to call it non-living
- something dormant for years with no detectable metabolism (dried seed, tun-state tardigrade, your pick)
- a mule — obviously alive, can't reproduce
- a crystal that grows and passes its defect pattern on to the crystals it seeds
- a population of self-replicating computer programs on an artificial-life platform where they mutate and get culled, so they clearly change by selection over generations

Add three or four more of your own, and make at least one of those genuinely obscure rather than another chestnut. Order the deck deliberately: start with cards everyone will agree on so their criterion feels comfortable, then land the killers. Every card should be readable with zero biology background in under ~80 words, be factually accurate, and describe what's actually known about the specimen — does it take in and use energy, does it have heredity, does it change across generations, is it bounded off from its surroundings — rather than just naming it, so they're reasoning from evidence and not from whether they've heard the word before.

Also give each table member a role card with a different stake in the outcome: the person designing the detector that has to fire on-site, the quarantine officer deciding what comes back in the capsule, someone deciding what could be treated or destroyed, someone worried about what we'd owe it. The point is that one and the same criterion serves those jobs badly or well in different ways, so by round 3 they should be arguing about which job the definition is for rather than about the facts. That's the moment I'm after, so build the mechanics to produce it instead of hoping.

Include: full rules, a timeline for the 75 minutes, a scoring scheme that uses no real money (some kind of mission budget where bad rulings and amendments both cost something, so patching a definition isn't free), a materials list I can print and cut in under an hour with no budget, the debrief, and an honest note on what'll go wrong the first time I run it — e.g. a table that writes "made of cells and has DNA" and then cruises, or one that amends every single round — plus what to do about each.

In the debrief, map what happened onto the conceptual stuff: the circularity worry in evolution-based formulations (the standard astrobiology-flavored line is roughly that life is a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution — but Darwinian evolution already assumes reproduction, heredity and variation, so did they just define life in terms of life?); list-of-properties versus a loose cluster of overlapping features versus "alive" admitting of degrees; whether a dormant thing is alive *now* or only has the capacity, i.e. current capacities versus being part of a lineage with a history; and the job-relativity point from the role cards. No lecture beforehand and don't say "necessary and sufficient conditions," "natural kind," or "cluster concept" anywhere until the debrief.

Finally, be frank about which feature of the problem the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply by hand. My guess is it can't get them to feel whether there's a fact of the matter independent of our interests — committees will just experience this as an engineering problem they can solve with a cleverer wording — but tell me if you think the design can actually touch that, and if so, how.
