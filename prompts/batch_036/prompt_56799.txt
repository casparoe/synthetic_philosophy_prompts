I'm 71, retired from a national lab after forty years of fitting models to noisy data, so I've developed a reflex about people who tell you a theorem has settled some argument. Since retiring I've been reading philosophy properly, and a chapter in a popular book on "the mathematics of morality" summarises Harsanyi's aggregation theorem like this:

"Take n people choosing among gambles over a fixed set of outcomes, each with a von Neumann–Morgenstern utility function, and suppose the preferences of the society as a whole satisfy the same rationality axioms the individuals do. Add one substantive condition — Pareto indifference: whenever every individual is indifferent between two gambles, society is indifferent too. Then the society's utility function must be a weighted sum of the individuals', and up to the usual rescaling it is the only form it can take. The weights are all positive, and if you further require that people be treated alike they come out equal, which is classical utilitarianism. That disposes of two objections at once. Interpersonal comparisons of utility were supposed to be meaningless, but once rationality is imposed you can add utilities across people on one common scale. And Rawls's maximin principle loses: as Harsanyi argued, behind a veil of ignorance where you have an equal chance of being anyone, expected-utility reasoning picks the sum rather than the worst-off position, so maximin isn't rational at all."
