I'm the student member on my department's climate committee — unpaid, unglamorous, enormous quantities of grocery-store cookies — and I'm also taking the epistemic injustice seminar this term, which has made me realize I have no idea what rule we're actually applying. We hear complaints from students and staff about how they were treated in seminars and labs, and we vote on whether the complaint is "grounded" as an injustice or not, and then recommend something. Here's our record over the last two years, roughly in order:

1. A first-gen senior said her claims in seminar kept getting credited to whoever repeated them five minutes later. Grounded. The writeup said she was being heard as less of a knower because of who she is.
2. A student complained about a TA's conduct and couldn't describe it to us — he kept saying "it wasn't harassment exactly, it was more like…" and there genuinely wasn't a word for the pattern on this campus at the time. Grounded. The writeup said part of the harm was that nobody had the language yet, including him.
3. An international grad student said their teaching evals were dinged because of their accent. Not grounded. We said being hard to follow isn't the same as being prejudged, but we pushed for captions and TA training.
4. A facilities guy told a PI twice that a solvent cabinet was venting wrong and got brushed off until he emailed the safety office directly. Grounded. Our reasoning was that in that building, who you are decides whether your warning counts.
5. A student said nobody believed her account of what happened at a department party. Not grounded. Two people contradicted her on the details and she'd been drinking, and we said a credibility deficit that follows from the evidence isn't the thing we're supposed to be catching.
6. An administrative staffer said faculty talked over her in every meeting. Grounded, but we framed it as her place in the hierarchy rather than anything about her personally.

Now here's what's on the docket for Thursday and I can't predict us. I don't think the chair can either, she's been weirdly quiet about it.

A transfer student from a community college, works full time, is a woman in a mostly-male lab, filed a complaint about their PI. The strange part: nobody disputes a single fact. The notebook records, the email chain, the authorship order — everyone including the PI agrees on what happened. What the student is complaining about is the *description*. They came in with a really precise vocabulary for it, they used terms like coercive authorship and gift authorship, they laid out the norms from their previous institution, they even brought a written policy to point at. The department's response was to redescribe the exact same agreed facts as a "mentorship style mismatch" and "misaligned expectations," very warmly, and to add that they thought the student was extremely articulate. The student says the outcome of the meeting is that they no longer trust their own read of what happened to them, and that they've stopped using those words.

So, four things:

(a) Which of our precedents is the closest analog, and what does it actually share with this one — I keep flip-flopping between 2 and 4 and I can't tell if either is close.

(b) Predict how we rule. Be honest that our record probably fits more than one rule, because I think it does. Give me at least three candidate rules that are consistent with 1 through 6, what each of them says about this complaint, and which one you think we're actually running. I suspect at least two of them say grounded and at least one says not, and I want to know why.

(c) If we want to be consistent with ourselves instead of deciding by vibe, what should we rule — and what principle are we committing to by ruling that way, including what principle we'd be committing to if we went the other direction. I especially want to know whether "everyone agreed on the facts" lets us off the hook, since that's the argument I expect from the two faculty members.

(d) Give me one hypothetical complaint that would split the candidate rules — something where two of them come out opposite, so that next year we'd finally have to say which one we are instead of continuing to have it both ways.

Skip the overview stuff, I've done the reading on testimonial vs hermeneutical injustice and I know the two-type story. I want the casework.
