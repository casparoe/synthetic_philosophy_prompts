So — I'm a fourth-year PhD student in history and philosophy of science and I have somehow ended up giving the ten minutes of "words before dinner" at my department's Alan Turing birthday dinner in June. This year they folded it together with the launch of a new industry-funded AI lab, so the room will be roughly: six historians of computing (two of whom have edited volumes of Turing's papers), about thirty ML grad students and four engineers from the sponsoring lab, the dean, and a handful of donors whose entire knowledge of Turing is the Cumberbatch film. My chair's instruction to me, verbatim, was "make them feel good about the cheque and don't make him sound like a lunatic."

The awkward part is that my dissertation is about the bit of the 1950 paper that everybody skips — the short numbered section near the end on extrasensory perception. In it Turing says, more or less, that he'd expected scientists to dismiss ESP and was surprised to find they didn't; that the statistical evidence looked to him like it established that telepathy, clairvoyance, precognition and psychokinesis are real; that the implications of this are much too disturbing to sit with; and then — this is the bit I care about — he turns around and asks what all this would do to his imitation game. If the interrogator can pick up the human competitor's answers by telepathy, or one of the competitors can overhear by clairvoyance, then the human has an information channel the machine doesn't have and the score means nothing; so the game would have to be run in a shielded room built to whatever standard the telepathy researchers themselves would accept. He treats ESP as a threat to the validity of his own test, not as a threat to his thesis.

I want to build the whole talk around that. Constraints, because I'd much rather have them stated than get something back I can't use:

- I don't believe in telepathy and I'm not going to pretend to in that room. But I also refuse the other available move — "well, he was credulous, it was the era, let's move on." The talk has to hold both: the premise was false, and what he *did* with the premise was right and is mostly ignored. Make that distinction land as a compliment to him, not as a rescue of him.
- Do not call it a footnote. It's a section of the paper, and one of the historians at that table has a long-running public bee in his bonnet about people calling it a footnote.
- Do not say he "anticipated" data contamination, or benchmark leakage, or anything with that retro-fitting flavour. I think that version is cheap. The honest claim is weaker and weirder and I want the weaker one.
- I want exactly one moment that lands for the ML half — something about a system under evaluation getting hold of information the evaluation was never supposed to hand it — that the historians and the donors can follow without knowing what a held-out test set is. No jargon anywhere in the thing.
- Nothing in quotation marks from the paper. I'll add any quotes myself from my own copy; I don't want to be reading aloud something I haven't checked. Paraphrase only. And don't name any parapsychologists, labs, journals or specific experiments from the period — I'm not confident enough about any of them to say them out loud to that room.
- Hard judgment call, and I want you to just get it right rather than ask me: the martyr story is sitting in that room, and the ESP section is the place where Turing says something is too frightening to think about. Do not splice those two things together, that would be grotesque and I'd never forgive myself. But don't write around the fact that the second thing is true of him either. Solve it in the shape of the talk.
- The last minute has to be something the donors can actually applaud that doesn't rest on a claim about whether machines think, because there are two people at that table who'd disagree about it and I don't want a fight before dessert.

Ten minutes, so call it 1300 words, and I'd rather it be short enough that I can deliver it slowly. Warm, a couple of genuinely funny beats but not joke-dense, and absolutely no "in these uncertain times" anything. I'm a grad student giving it, so a little self-deprecation is fine, but no shtick.
