I'm in the third week of a political philosophy sequence that has been working through Rawls, and I also sit on my university's Student Resources Committee, a seven-member body that allocates roughly $1.4 million a year. Our charter commits us, in words that are clearly a paraphrase of the difference principle, to allowing inequalities in the distribution of university resources only where they improve the situation of the students who are worst off. Two further charter provisions matter here: we define the least advantaged group as the roughly 900 of our 8,000 undergraduates who receive the maximum need-based grant and work at least ten paid hours a week during term, and before any vote one member must present the proposal from what we call the presentation chair, that is, as though they did not know which student they would turn out to be.

Nobody on the committee claims to speak for Rawls, and I am not asking what he would have decided. I am asking what our own record commits us to, because we vote next Thursday on the largest proposal in the committee's history and I cannot tell whether our five precedents point the same way.

**P1 — Night shuttle.** We extended the shuttle to 3 a.m., paid for with a flat $15 term fee on every undergraduate. Our group gained about six hours a week previously spent walking or paying for rides, which we recorded as worth roughly $300 a year per student, mostly in fares avoided; the median student lost $15 and gained about $20 in convenience; students with cars lost $15 and gained nothing. Approved 6–1, the lone dissent holding that a flat fee is regressive.

**P2 — Means-tested dining.** We would have replaced the universal dining-hall discount with a targeted cash grant. Our group gained about $420 a year on average and the median student lost $45, but roughly 60 undocumented students, who could not satisfy the aid-eligibility test written into the proposal, lost a discount worth about $260. Rejected 5–2. The minutes record the reason as: the students who would lose were already in the worst position in the community, and their loss was certain rather than a matter of chance.

**P3 — Fellowship lottery.** We funded a competitive paid research fellowship by trimming the emergency hardship fund by a fifth. Our group gained about $150 a year in expectation, since a handful of members would win $3,000 fellowships; nobody's assured resources changed; but a student who hit an unexpected hardship would now receive up to $300 less than before. Approved 4–3. The minutes record that no student's assured position fell and that the downside was speculative rather than built in.

**P4 — First-generation scholarship.** A donor offered $900 a year per recipient for first-generation students on maximum aid; no one else lost anything, and the spread between our group and everyone else narrowed. We approved only on condition that the program abandon its public cohort identity: no published roster, no named certificate, no annual reception at which recipients are photographed. The donor agreed. The minutes record that the money was not the problem, that the arrangement marked its beneficiaries as a separate class, and that standing as an equal among one's peers is part of what we distribute rather than a courtesy we may trade away.

**P5 — Conditional versus unconditional.** Two ways of spending the same pot: $500 per student conditional on completing ten hours of mandated financial-coaching sessions each term, or $450 with no conditions. We chose the $450. The minutes record that a benefit which must be earned with hours a student does not have is worth less than its face value, and that the unconditional grant left the recipient free.

**Thursday's proposal — the Gateway package.** A donor offers $4.2 million over five years, under an agreement that cannot be divided; taking part of it forfeits all of it. It has two components. The first places each of our 900 students in a paid, field-related position for the academic year in place of their current work-study assignment. They keep their existing wages and receive a $200 top-up and a $150 transport and equipment credit, so a net cash gain of about $350 a year each. The placement requires twelve hours a week rather than the ten they work now, but the hours are in their field and come with a supervisor's reference. The top-up and credit are reimbursed in arrears, at the end of the first month of each term. About 70 of the 900 have dependent-care obligations and their providers require payment at the start of term, so each term they must bridge roughly $900 from savings or credit; our staff put the chance of a materially disrupted term at about one in ten per academic year, at an estimated cost of $900 to that student. No student's assured resources fall below their current level. The second component is a $480,000-a-year venture fellowship pool: 40 fellowships of $12,000, open to all undergraduates. On the record of the comparable program run by the entrepreneurship center, about 34 of the 40 go to students from families in the top income quintile, about 3 to the median quintile, and about 3 to our group. Net effect: our group gains about $390 in cash a year per member, the median student's position is unchanged, and the distance between our group and the students at the top of the undergraduate distribution widens by roughly $11,600 a year for those 34. The donor also requires that the Gateway cohort be publicly named each year — published roster, named certificate, and an annual reception at which recipients are photographed with the donor, the images to be used in the campaign — and the agreement states that this condition is not waivable. Finally, the provost has offered a fallback available only if we decline the gift: $450 in cash per student per year for the same 900 students, disbursed in advance at the start of each term, with no roster, no certificate, no reception, and no change to their work-study jobs, financed by trimming the alumni and parent relations budget so that no other group's resources move.

Three members have already told me which precedent they regard as controlling, and they named three different ones. Please work out the candidate decision rules that fit all five precedents taken together, say what each implies for Thursday, tell me which one our record genuinely favors and how we should vote, and state plainly what we would be committing ourselves to either way. Where the record runs out, I want that said rather than papered over.
