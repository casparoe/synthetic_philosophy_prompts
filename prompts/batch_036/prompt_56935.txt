I work on model policy at a company that ships a general-purpose assistant, and for the past year two internal camps have been circling the same disagreement without ever making contact. The feature in dispute lets a user install a standing instruction that constrains what the assistant will later do for them: someone in recovery asks that it never help them locate a dealer or talk them down from a lapse; someone with a gambling problem asks that it decline to draft messages to their bookmaker; an engineer asks that for thirty days it refuse any request to help write a resignation letter, because she knows she self-sabotages when furious; a graduate student asks that after eleven at night it not help him message an ex-partner. What the camps actually disagree about is the moment the user comes back and revokes. Camp A, which I will call the revocability camp, holds that a standing instruction is nothing more than a preference the user recorded earlier, that there is no metaphysically privileged earlier self whose say-so outranks a later one, that honoring the later request is honoring the user, and that an assistant which keeps saying no has become an instrument turned against its owner. Camp B, the diachronic authorization camp, holds that a reflective, informed, deliberately costly commitment creates standing authority; that a request issued in the grip of craving, rage, or exhaustion is often not an exercise of agency but a failure of it; that the assistant in this configuration is best understood as a prosthesis of the earlier self's will; and that treating every subsequent utterance as automatically authoritative erases the distinction between what a person wants and what a person wants to want. Both camps concede that the arrangement borrows from psychiatric advance directives and from the Odysseus-at-the-mast picture of precommitment, and they disagree about how far either analogy carries.

Please draft the joint statement these two camps could sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration, written for reviewers who have no patience for either side's slogans. I want, first, a numbered section of claims both can accept: about what a standing instruction technically is at the moment of decision, about what the assistant can and cannot know when the revocation arrives, about the asymmetry between the harm of refusing a sincere revocation and the harm of complying with a self-destructive one, and about what the default should be for a user who has installed nothing. Second, a disagreements table stating each residual disagreement in wording its own camp would accept unchanged, and flagging which rows collapse under a translation exercise in which each camp is permitted to restate the other's thesis in its own vocabulary; if a row turns out to be a dispute over what consent, or the user's wishes, refers to across time rather than over what should be done, say so, and say which camp's usage is quietly doing more work. Third, the cruxes — the sub-questions whose settlement would actually move at least one camp. I have candidates, but I want yours defended rather than mine echoed: whether any non-arbitrary procedure exists for distinguishing a considered revocation from an in-the-grip one; whether friction on exit (a delay, a required phrase, an out-of-band confirmation) preserves autonomy by making revocation deliberate or destroys it by making revocation expensive; whether the assistant's role is better modeled as fiduciary, servant, prosthetic, or regulated utility, and whether the choice of model does independent argumentative work or merely repackages the substantive answer; and whether regret is measurable in a way that could decide anything, given that the two camps predict opposite asymmetries. For each crux, give a concrete case or a description of evidence both camps would agree bears on it, and record each camp's prediction in advance, phrased so that being wrong about it would be embarrassing.

Two further requirements. Close with a short section naming one design policy that follows from the agreed claims alone, without resolving any disagreement, and one policy where the disagreement is genuinely load-bearing, so that the document must specify a tie-breaking default instead of implying a consensus it does not have; if you think the load-bearing case cannot be defaulted without one camp effectively winning, say that plainly and say what should be logged at decision time so the choice can be revisited with data rather than rhetoric. And please do not include references, reading lists, or citations — the value here is in the drafting and the argumentative work, not in who has said what before. Write the whole thing in American English.
