Write a short story of roughly 1,500 to 2,500 words that dramatizes what the statistic "heritability" does and does not license, for readers who have heard the term but have never been shown where it breaks.

The setting and premise are yours to choose, subject to these constraints:

- Set the story among fictional organisms or a fictional human population — not a real ethnic, national, or diagnostic group, and not a real crop or livestock breed. Do not name any real researcher, textbook, dataset, or study; the ideas must be carried by the plot rather than by appeals to authority.
- At least two characters must want opposite things from a heritability estimate, and at least one of them must be misusing it in a recognisable way — for instance, treating a high within-population estimate as proof that an environmental change could not shift the population mean, or reading an estimate derived under one range of environments as though it held under a very different one, or sliding from a claim about variation across a group to a claim about the fixedness of a particular individual.
- Show the cost of the error through consequences — a failed season, a misallocated programme, a prediction about one person that comes apart — rather than through a character delivering a corrective lecture. If anyone explains the statistics aloud, keep it brief, concrete, and partly wrong in a way an attentive reader can catch.
- Commit to definite authorial choices: a single point of view, a narrator who is not neutral, and an ending that lands on one side. Do not resolve the conflict by having the characters come to agree, and do not leave it to the reader to decide who was right.

Technically, the story should be accurate on at least three of the following and should contradict none of them: heritability as a ratio of variances defined for a particular population in a particular range of environments; its sensitivity to how much environmental variation actually exists, so that equalising environments raises the estimate without making the trait more fixed; the difference between the portion of genetic variance that responds predictably to selection and total genetic variance; and the compatibility of a high estimate with a large environmentally caused shift in the mean.

Begin your response with the story itself — no preface, no framing remarks, no note about your intentions. After the story, append one short paragraph headed "Note on liberties" in which you state, with definite judgements rather than hedges, which of the technical points above you leaned on hardest, where you simplified or distorted them for the sake of the narrative, and which single choice in the plot you would most expect a statistically literate reader to object to.
