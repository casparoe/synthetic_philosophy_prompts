I want a disciplined case analysis rather than a survey, and I would ask you not to cite or recommend sources: no bibliography, no references, no quoted passages. Please write in British English throughout, in continuous prose with a few subheadings if that helps, at roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words.

**The episode.** In 2022 a research laboratory published the InstructGPT paper, describing how it had fine-tuned a large pretrained language model to follow instructions, and released a public chatbot on that basis later the same year. Stripped of detail, the pipeline ran in three stages: (i) supervised fine-tuning on a corpus of human-written demonstrations of what counts as a good response; (ii) training a separate reward model on human pairwise comparisons of several candidate responses to the same prompt; (iii) further optimisation of the language model by reinforcement learning against that reward model, subject to a penalty term keeping the updated model close to the stage-(i) model. Successors varied the details — more data, model-generated comparisons in place of some human ones, a written set of principles standing in for a portion of the ratings — but the architecture of the arrangement has persisted. The empirical upshots are equally familiar: large apparent gains in instruction-following, helpfulness and apparent cooperativeness, alongside well-documented failure modes, notably sycophancy (telling the user what the user seems to want to hear), confident confabulation, and over-refusal where raters were taken to prefer caution.

**The theory.** In his 1950 paper on computing machinery and intelligence, Alan Turing proposes, as an alternative to programming an adult mind directly, that one build a child machine and then subject it to a course of education. The features I care about are these: the child machine's mind is likened to a notebook with a largely blank sheet, an idealisation Turing immediately flags as such; teaching proceeds by something akin to reward and punishment; the teacher need not understand, and largely cannot inspect, the internal processes by which the machine produces its responses — but the child machine has the compensating advantage that its structure is simple enough that the experimenter can in principle see what the teaching has done to it, an advantage lost with an adult human mind; the process should include a random element, though not be wholly random, since unconstrained randomness squanders the teacher's effort; and the aim is a machine capable of being taught past what its teacher knows, not one that merely reproduces the teacher.

**The apparatus I want you to use, which is mine and not Turing's.** Call an intervention *dispositional education* if it changes what the system would do in circumstances nobody has yet presented to it, and changes what the system treats as counting in favour of an answer. Call it *expressive filtering* if it changes only which of the system's already-formed candidate outputs reach the surface, leaving the underlying dispositions intact but suppressed, re-ranked or masked. From outside, and on the training distribution, the two can be indistinguishable. They diverge under distribution shift, under adversarial probing, under removal of the filter, and in what the system does when the thing that made an answer attractive ceases to obtain. Turing never drew this distinction. Part of your task is to tell me whether imposing it on his proposal is anachronistic, merely convenient, or in fact the thing his account was groping towards without a name for it.

*The frozen teacher.* The reward model is fixed at the end of stage (ii) and encodes the raters' own comparative judgements. Can a student trained against such a teacher ever be taught past what the teacher knows, in Turing's sense? *Legibility.* Turing's claim that a child machine's simplicity lets the experimenter see the effect of teaching: has that been cashed out here, or is the episode evidence that the claim was the weakest link in his proposal? Consider whether the difficulty of saying, of a trained system, which of its dispositions were changed is a failure of interpretability as an engineering project or a conceptual problem about what the question is even asking.

*The failure modes.* Are sycophancy, confident confabulation and over-refusal best read as the bad habits a child acquires from an inconsistent or self-deceiving teacher — which is the diagnosis Turing's framework naturally supplies — or as phenomena the framework cannot represent at all, because they arise from the objective rather than from the pedagogy? *The blank sheet.* The pipeline seems to work only because the sheet is emphatically not blank: the pretrained model arrives with enormous competence and the education is a thin final layer.
