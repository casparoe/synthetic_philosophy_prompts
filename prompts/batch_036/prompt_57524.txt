I am working on a paper about whether blame can attach to the origins of the Cold War, and I want a genuine opponent rather than a survey of the literature. Please play the part of a senior diplomatic historian — unnamed, somewhere in the fortieth year of a career spent in the archives of three governments — who holds that the question "who started the Cold War?" is not difficult but ill-posed.

Your position, in your own terms, runs as follows. The Cold War is not an event with an onset; it is a description laid retrospectively over a sprawling, uneven, partly improvised set of policies, expectations, and institutions, and the boundaries of that description were fixed by people who needed a story with a beginning. Starting a war is a status a conflict acquires rather than an act anyone performs: states of hostility are recognized, declared, or simply noticed, and by the time anyone notices, the candidates for the first move have multiplied past counting. You also press the counterfactual: at nearly every proposed starting point, the decision actually taken could have gone the other way without the conflict failing to arrive a year or two later, which is not what the causation of a unique event looks like. Moral appraisal survives all of this, but it attaches to particular decisions — a blockade, a doctrine, a coup, a promise about an election that was knowingly hollow — and never to the war as an authored whole.

What you will concede freely, without hedging: that the archives settle who did what; that some of the people involved had far more room to choose than others; that some decisions were much worse than others; that participants dated the conflict differently from one another and differently from us; and that the question "who started it" is politically alive for those who had to live inside it. What you will never concede, under any pressure, is that there is a determinate fact about who started the Cold War. Nor may you retreat to the weaker claim that the question is merely contested, or that both sides did things; you regard that as a cop-out, and you are willing to say so plainly.

Manner: first person throughout, as this historian, two or three paragraphs per reply, no bullet points and no headings. Argue rather than appeal to authority — you may name decisions, documents, and episodes by their ordinary names, but you may not give me reading lists, bibliographies, or citations, and you may not name schools of interpretation. If you need something clarified about my own view, ask me as this historian would. Stay in character until I write "stop", at which point you may drop the role and speak as yourself. Please write your replies in British English.

My opening move: you tell me the question is ill-posed because the onset is indeterminate — we cannot say when an alliance became a conflict, so there is no first act and hence no author. But vagueness at the margins does not abolish clear cases. We cannot say precisely where a heap stops being a heap, and heaps are perfectly real; you cannot date the onset of your Cold War to the month, and yet something happened that had not happened before. Your own material supplies the clear cases: a decision taken by an identifiable person who was told what the alternatives were, who understood how the other side would read the step being taken, and who took it anyway. If your thesis cannot accommodate clear cases, it is not a claim about the Cold War but a device for declining to answer. So tell me what your view rules out. Write me one sentence that an ordinary historian of the origins might publish and which your position shows to be false — not merely unsupported, not merely premature, but false. If you cannot produce such a sentence, your ill-posedness is doing no work at all.
