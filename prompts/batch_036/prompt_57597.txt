You are a professional philosopher specializing in the philosophy of science, with particular expertise in causation, determinism, and the epistemology of prediction, and you have enough familiarity with software engineering practice to follow its conventions without translation. Please think carefully and reason explicitly rather than arriving at a conclusion first and defending it afterward.

Some context about me: I am a software engineer at a mid-sized company. I have no formal training in philosophy, so I expect that I am describing this practice in the wrong vocabulary and may be missing an obvious name for the problem. Please correct me where my framing is off.

The practice I want analyzed is bug triage, and specifically two rules that my organization treats as self-evidently correct.

First, a defect report is not accepted as describing a real failure until a second engineer, working only from the written report, reproduces the failure on their own machine. If they cannot, the ticket is closed with the resolution cannot reproduce, and it stays closed even if the original reporter is certain it happened repeatedly. The reasoning offered is that a single unrepeatable observation could be a corrupted deployment, a bad disk, a mistaken user, or a fabricated report, and that the cost of chasing it is high.

Second, we maintain a quarantine list. Any automated test that fails on fewer than roughly one run in fifty is presumed to be flaky, is removed from the set of tests that can block a release, and is only restored once someone identifies a deterministic cause for the intermittent failure. The default assumption is that intermittent means unexplained, and unexplained means not yet worth believing.

We do have jargon for the failure modes that violate these rules. A bug that is reliably reproducible is called a bohrbug; a bug that vanishes or changes behavior the moment you attach a debugger, add logging, or slow the program down is called a Heisenbug; a bug whose behavior looks chaotic or non-deterministic because the causal chain is enormously complex is sometimes called a mandelbug; and a bug that is inferred from log entries and anecdotal user reports but that nobody can make happen on demand is sometimes called a higgs-bugson. In practice, the last three terms are used as jokes, and the tracker has no resolution state corresponding to any of them. A Heisenbug, a mandelbug, and a higgs-bugson are all, administratively, cannot reproduce.

Meanwhile, other parts of the same company operate on what looks to me like the opposite assumption. The machine learning team trains on GPUs that use non-deterministic floating-point reductions, samples model outputs at a nonzero temperature, and reports results as averages over several random seeds; nobody there thinks a single run is evidentially worthless because it could not have been predicted in advance, and they treat the distribution over outcomes as the real object of study. Our infrastructure team runs a distributed scheduler in which the same code on the same input can take different paths depending on which message arrives first; that is regarded as a defect to be engineered away rather than a property to be characterized, but it is also acknowledged to be intrinsic to the design.

I am also aware, from popular science rather than study, that there are systems which are fully deterministic yet have a finite prediction horizon because tiny differences in initial conditions grow rapidly, so that determinism does not buy you reproducibility in practice. If any part of what we call flakiness has that structure, I do not know how our rules would handle it.

With that in hand, please address the following.

1. Begin with a short brainstorming section: list the candidate philosophical positions the cannot-reproduce rule might presuppose about causation, evidence, or the relationship between determinism and predictability, one or two sentences each, before you argue for any of them.

2. Then reconstruct the implicit theory in detail. What does the rule take a successful reproduction to establish, and what does it take a failed reproduction to establish? What account of evidence, and of the connection between repeatable observation and causal reality, is doing the work?

3. Separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to fit. Are there rival accounts that would license exactly the same triage behavior? Mark clearly where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that I do not mistake a contingent procedural choice for a metaphysical commitment.

4. Locate deterministic chaos in this picture. If a failure arises in a system with sensitive dependence on initial conditions, is the cannot-reproduce rule still defensible, and is our vocabulary of bohrbug and flaky test able to represent that third case at all, or does it collapse two different things into one?

5. Judge whether the tension between the triage rules and the machine learning team is a genuine inconsistency in our epistemology, or only an apparent one that dissolves once you notice that the two groups are answering different questions.

6. Finally, say concretely what would have to change — the meaning of a closed ticket, the standard of evidence for accepting a report, the quarantine policy, the set of resolution states — if we adopted a view on which irreproducibility is fully compatible with the event having really occurred.

Please do not give me a reading list, citations, or references; I want the analysis, not pointers to it, and I would rather you describe any philosopher's position in your own words than risk a mangled attribution. Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words, and do not hesitate to tell me that part of the practice is simply confused.
