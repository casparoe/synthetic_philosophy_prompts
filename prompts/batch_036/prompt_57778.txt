For a graduate seminar handout on nineteenth-century aesthetics I have compiled the verdicts Schopenhauer actually pronounces on music in the third book of The World as Will and Representation. They are paraphrased below, deliberately without quoted wording, so that the exercise cannot be answered by reciting a passage back to me.

1. Music stands in a different relation to the world from the other arts. Painting, sculpture and poetry copy the Ideas, which are themselves grades of the will's objectification; music bypasses the Ideas and copies the will directly. It is therefore independent of the phenomenal world, would remain even if the world ceased to exist, and strikes the listener more powerfully than any image or verse.
2. Within a piece, the registers correspond to the grades of the will's objectification. The fundamental bass stands for crude, inorganic mass; the inner voices that fill out the harmony stand for the plant and animal kingdoms; the high, singing principal melody stands for the intellectual life and striving of human beings.
3. The bass must move slowly. A bass that runs about with the agility of a melody he judges unnatural, and treats as a compositional fault.
4. Consonance and resolution answer to satisfied willing, dissonance to pain. An unresolved dissonance leaves the listener in a distressing state, and its resolution is required.
5. Arrival at the tonic yields satisfaction, but that satisfaction quickly palls, and the piece must modulate and set out again — for him the image of a will whose every attainment is only the seed of a new want.
6. Music does not express this or that particular joy or sorrow, nor any determinate situation, person or event; it gives joy itself and sorrow itself, stripped of the particularities the other arts must supply.
7. Accordingly, music that sets out to imitate phenomena by resemblance — a battle, a storm, the notes of birds — he dismisses as an abuse of the art, a confusion of its proper object with appearances it has no business copying.
8. Composition, he holds, is the work of genius operating in a language its own reason does not understand: the composer discloses the innermost nature of the world without conceptual knowledge of what he is doing.

The seminar's exercise turns on three cases of music Schopenhauer never considered, each of which I believe the eight verdicts jointly underdetermine.

Case A: a work for a single unaccompanied instrument — one line only, with no bass, no harmony and no chords at any point.
Case B: a work in which the registers are inverted throughout — the florid, fast-moving tune is given to the double basses, while the violins sustain one unchanging pitch from the first bar to the last.
Case C: a work that is dissonant from beginning to end, modulates continuously, and never once closes on the tonic.

What I want from you:

First, predict his verdict on each case — approve, disapprove, or approve with a specific qualification — and derive each prediction from the verdicts above, showing which of them is doing the work and where they conflict.

Second, reconstruct the rule he is running. I expect at least three reconstructions that fit all eight data points equally well: one on which the content of music is a hierarchy of natural strata mirrored in register, one on which it is the formal contour of striving, hindrance, satisfaction and renewed want, and one on which the decisive feature is music's freedom from concepts and determinate content. If you think a fourth fits the data just as well, add it. State for each reconstruction what it predicts about A, B and C, and where the predictions diverge, treat the divergence as evidence bearing on which reconstruction is right rather than smoothing it over.

Third, commit to a best guess about which reconstruction is his, and argue for it from the rest of his system — the will as blind striving, the body as the will's immediate objectivity, resignation and the denialial of the will, the requirement that aesthetic experience issue in a calm, will-less contemplation — not from the aesthetics chapter alone.

Fourth, verdict 3 admits of two quite different explanations, one metaphysical (crude mass cannot dance, and to make it dance is to falsify the order of nature) and one perceptual (tones very low in pitch moving very quickly lose their distinctness, so the ear cannot follow them). Say which explanation better survives Cases A, B and C, and if neither case settles it, design the shortest further case that would.

Fifth, flag the pressure points you find between the doctrine and the system. In particular: whether an art whose professed object is the will itself can deliver the will-less quiet he demands of every aesthetic experience; and whether Case C should be read as the most faithful image of a will that is never satisfied, or as an aesthetic failure for exactly that reason. If a reconstruction forces him into contradiction with something in the eight verdicts, say which.

Finally, name one further piece of music you would put to him, say which of your reconstructions it is designed to break, and what his answer would have to be on each of them. And if any of my eight summaries strikes you as a misstatement of his position rather than a fair paraphrase, say so and correct it before building on it.
