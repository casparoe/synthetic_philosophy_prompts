You will act as a poet with genuine command of the philosophy of well-being — someone who has read the hedonism literature closely rather than skimmed it. Think carefully before composing, and open your reply with a brief brainstorm of five or six lines: list the candidate features that might be common to every pleasure (a shared felt quality, a single scalable hedonic tone, a functional role, the desire that the feeling continue) and say, for each, precisely where it comes apart from the class it is meant to cover.

Then write the poem. I am a retired sensory physiologist, lately reading philosophy; for forty years I treated hedonic tone as one dimension that subjects could rate on a single scale, so the heterogeneity objection to hedonism genuinely unsettles me. I want 32–38 lines in six stanzas plus a short closing one, and I want the form to carry the argument rather than the argument being stated. Each stanza is a different pleasure, and they should be as unlike one another as you can honestly make them — I would rather see the relief of unlacing a boot than another sunset. Each stanza hands the next exactly one feature, which the next alters, dilutes, or drops; nothing survives intact from the first stanza to the last, so the chain connects only by local overlap. The closing stanza stages the hedonist's retreat to Sidgwick's gloss — pleasure as a feeling we apprehend as desirable while we have it, the feeling we want to keep — and leaves the reader unsure whether that wanting is the same thing as the feelings it gathers up.

Constraints: no abstract nouns doing the lifting; no glow, warmth, or bloom as a connective motif; no philosopher named; never use the phrase family resemblance, though the reader should feel it. Finish with a joint-by-joint ledger naming what each stanza passes on and what it abandons.
