You are, for the purposes of this exchange, a philosopher whose specialty is the metaphysics of space, time, and motion, with particular fluency in the literature on infinite task sequences and on Zeno's paradoxes from Aristotle onward. Please think carefully and work through my argument step by step. Do not settle on a verdict before you have canvassed the alternatives; I would rather see the whole field of candidate failures laid out first than be handed a confident answer to the wrong question.

Some background, since it bears on what I can and cannot concede. I spent forty-one years as an experimental physicist, the last twenty of them measuring the time-of-flight of cold neutrons over a fixed baseline — which is to say, timing bodies across known distances with instruments whose systematic errors I understood rather better than most people understand their own habits. I retired two years ago and have taken up philosophy in earnest, which is how I came to write the argument below. I circulated it to a reading group I attend, intending it as a demonstration that Zeno's dichotomy is not the undergraduate curiosity it is treated as, and I now find that I cannot accept its conclusion. I should like to know where it fails, and what failing there costs me.

I have tried to make every step as innocent-looking as possible, and I have deliberately closed off the two replies I was given the first time round.

Version A, the abstract one.

(1) Space and time are continuous: between any two positions along a trajectory there lies a further position, and between any two instants a further instant. This is the assumption under which every equation I ever integrated was written.

(2) To traverse a finite interval L, a body must first traverse L/2, then the L/4 beyond that, then the L/8 beyond that, and so on without end. Traversal of L therefore consists in the performance of infinitely many sub-traversals, ordered one after another.

(3) Each sub-traversal occupies a nonzero duration, and these durations form a convergent geometric series whose sum is the finite total time of the crossing. I grant this explicitly, so that the reply about convergent series is not available to you; my argument nowhere requires the total time to be infinite.

(4) A sequence of tasks performed successively — each begun only once its predecessor is finished — is completed only at some moment by which every member of the sequence has been performed.

(5) In the sequence described in (2), no member is last: for every sub-traversal there is a further one which must be performed after it.

(6) Hence there is no moment at which the sequence described in (2) is completed; the body is never in the position of having performed all of them.

(7) Therefore no body traverses a finite interval. Motion, as I measured it for four decades, does not occur.

A member of the group objected that (2) treats as separate tasks what are in truth mere phases of a single continuous act — that halfway points are abstractions from the motion rather than units of work to be discharged. I found that objection evasive, and so I built a second version in which the sub-tasks are unmistakably separate and each leaves a permanent, inspectable record. I should add that I have come across the famous thought-experiment about a lamp wired to switch itself on and off in a geometric progression, together with the well-known subsequent paper arguing that such examples fail to describe any coherent task at all. That criticism never persuaded me, and Version B is my attempt to answer it.

Version B, the instrumented one.

(8) Replace the successive halfway points with physical apparatus: detectors at L/2, at 3L/4, at 7L/8, and so on, accumulating toward the far end. The crossing body triggers each in turn, and each latches a record of having been triggered.

(9) A trigger mechanism at the far end fires only upon receiving a signal from every one of the detectors.

(10) Since no detector is the last, there is no moment at which every detector has fired.

(11) Therefore the mechanism never fires, and the crossing was never completed — but now with a fact of the matter, in the form of a latched record, about each sub-task, so the phases-not-tasks objection is no longer available to you.

Then the fork, which is where the argument begins to frighten me.

(12) If (10) and (11) fail only because an infinite array of detectors cannot physically be installed in a finite region, the reasoning generalizes anyway: no continuous process can be confirmed to have run to completion, since confirming it would require checking infinitely many intermediate states.

(13) So either continuity as stated in (1) is false and there is a smallest scale of position or duration — a testable claim I should be delighted to entertain — or the completion of any continuous process is unverifiable in principle, or motion does not occur.

My own preferences, for what they are worth. I would keep (1) and (3) at almost any price: (1) because the alternative is a discretism for which I have no evidence, and (3) because I want no part of the crude move to an infinite total time. I would give up (12) if you can tell me precisely what I lose by giving it up. What I cannot do is accept (7).

Please proceed in the following order.

First, as an opening exercise, list every premise and every inference in both versions that you regard as a candidate point of failure, one line each, without yet adjudicating among them. Where you think I have misdescribed a situation rather than mis-inferred, mark it as such.

Second, identify the step you actually reject, and explain it in terms I can check: what plausible principle I was reaching for when I wrote it, why that principle seems compelling in finite cases, and what it would commit me to if it were true in general.

Third, price the alternatives. For each of (2), (4), the inference from (5) to (6), and the move from Version A to Version B, say what it costs to reject that instead, and whether any of those costs is one a working physicist ought to be willing to pay.

Fourth, tell me whether Version B genuinely strengthens the argument or quietly substitutes a different and more vulnerable one, and whether the generalization in (12) survives on its own even if Version B does not.

Finally, compress your diagnosis into no more than five sentences that I could read aloud to the group, and tell me the one question I should put to them afterwards to establish whether they have followed it or merely agreed with me.

Please do not send me a reading list. If a line of reply has a name in the literature, name it in passing and move on; what I want is the diagnosis.
