In three weeks I defend a dissertation chapter at a workshop on the foundations of cost-benefit analysis. The room will be about a dozen people: mostly behavioral and experimental economists who run elicitation studies and who regard the notion of stable, context-independent preferences as a standing joke; two welfare economists who produce regulatory valuations for a living and want to know what they are supposed to do differently on Monday; one philosopher of economics who works on measurement and idealization; and a law professor whose standing question about any view of this kind is who gets counted and who does not. No one in the room is a revealed-preference partisan. The closest thing to a friendly ear is the measurement theorist, and that friendship is conditional.

My thesis is that revealed preference has been misfiled. It is not a psychology of choice that preference-reversal and framing results refute; it is a measurement discipline that fixes the content of utility claims by specifying what may count as evidence for them. Concretely: a utility attribution is a parameter in the best-fitting model of an agent's choices *after* those choices have been passed through an invariance filter — a designated family of elicitation perturbations (frame, ordering, default, decomposition, delay, description of the outcome) — and choices that move under perturbation are not counterexamples to the theory but readings below the instrument's resolution, to be discarded rather than averaged in. On this view, so-called preference construction is not a refutation of revealed preference; it is the calibration step that the theory, properly understood, always required. Utility is therefore neither a felt state nor a bare disposition to choose: it is what survives the filter.

So that the objections have something to bite, here is the argument in outline. First, if utility is to enter public justification at all, its attribution must be the sort of claim that can be contested and settled by evidence rather than by testimony. Second, the only evidence that is publicly checkable, agent-neutral, and non-introspective is choice under cost. Third, choice under cost is interpretable only against a background of elicitation conditions, so the content of a utility claim is exhausted by its role in a measurement procedure. Fourth, the procedure's admissibility conditions are therefore not an external add-on to the concept of utility but constitutive of it.
