Been building an objection to epiphenomenalism for my reading group and it's gone badly wrong — it proves far more than I wanted and I can't find the leak. Our own nickname for the relevant properties is "causally blank": instantiated, maybe, but doing nothing.

(1) For a property to be what a belief is *about*, that property has to play some causal role in producing the belief. Otherwise nothing fixes the content — there'd be no difference between a belief that's about it and one that merely looks like it is.

(2) Epiphenomenalism says phenomenal properties are causally blank.

(3) So on epiphenomenalism no belief is about a phenomenal property. Not even the one I'd put into words as "there's something it's like to see this red".

(4) That's the paradox of phenomenal judgment: the epiphenomenalist can't know anything about her own experience, and the introspective reports she uses as her evidence aren't reports about experience at all. She can't even state her own data.

(5) Up to there I'm happy. The trouble is that (1) never actually mentions experience.

(6) Numbers are causally blank. So are counterfactual facts — what would have happened had the coin landed tails. So, if non-naturalism is right, are moral properties.

(7) So by (1) nobody has ever believed anything about a number, about a counterfactual, or about whether some act is wrong.

(8) I can't assert that while also arguing against epiphenomenalism. If (7) is on the table my objection has no dialectical force whatever, since the very principle that was going to sink the epiphenomenalist has just sunk arithmetic along with it.

(9) Therefore (1) is false. And if (1) is false, (3) goes with it, and the paradox never gets started — which means I've refuted an objection to a theory of consciousness by waving at prime numbers.

My flatmate's suggestion is that (6) is just false, becuase numbers do cause things, via our arithmetical beliefs; but that looks like it concedes (1) rather than escaping it, and I don't think he'd be happy with the version of it he's signing up to.

Where does it break? I'd like you to name the step, explain why it looked so innocent on the way past, and then price the alternatives — what I'm giving up whichever way I turn. For what it's worth I'd rather keep (2) and (4) as live claims than keep (1), but I'll take the other trade if you can show me it's cheaper than I'm assuming.
