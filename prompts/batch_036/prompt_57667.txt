I'm the philosopher on my university's interdisciplinary research fund committee, which mostly means I get asked to referee other people's metaphysics. This cycle there's a proposal on why trust in local institutions collapsed in some regions and not others: agent-based modeling plus regional panel data. The method is fine, what's got us split is the framing. The PI accepts ontological individualism (only people exist) but denies explanatory individualism, and argues that macro variables — network density, institutional redundancy — carry causal weight that no individual-level redescription can absorb. They propose a formal test of this: fit the best individual-disposition model to the same data and show it can't reproduce the observed regional dynamics.

Half the committee thinks thats methodological holism dressed up as a hypothesis, and that the test is rigged from the start — the individual model is only as good as the micro data they bother to collect, and if you never measure people's beliefs about other people's beliefs, of course it loses to a model with more free parameters. The other half says the individualist objection is empty because nobody has ever specified what a successful reduction here would look like, so it can't be failed.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before we vote — fund, reject, or fund with conditions — with reasons the PI could actually respond to rather than a verdict they can only shrug at. I want both sides taken seriously, especially the strongest version of the "your test doesn't establish irreducibility" worry, not a strawman of it. And at the end, tell me what would change your mind: which consideration, if I raised it at the meeting, would flip your recommendation, and where you think your own reasoning is most likely to give out.
