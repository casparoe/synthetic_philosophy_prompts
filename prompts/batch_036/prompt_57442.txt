Retired about three years ago after roughly 32 years doing guidance and navigation on aircraft — mostly estimator design, Kalman filters, that sort of thing. Since then I've been reading philosophy on my own, way more seriously than I expected to, and I've got a half-formed project I want to turn into a real proposal for a nine-month seed grant at a university center that does mind-and-AI work. I have time and a little money but no credentials and no lab, so the document has to sell itself on the idea alone. The panel there is mostly philosophers, and I don't want to sound like a guy who wandered in off the golf course.

Here's the itch. In my old world we said "belief" constantly and nobody blinked. The filter's belief was literally just its probability distribution over possible states, and everyone knew perfectly well that was shorthand for a matrix of numbers updating on a fixed rule. The Dennett-ish people tell me the intentional stance is a stance and not a metaphysical claim, so on their account attributing beliefs to a machine ought to be as unremarkable as it was on my flight deck. But the moment somebody says a language model believes something, half the room reaches for the smelling salts. So: what is the actual difference, if there is one? My hunch is that it isn't flexibility or richness of behavior — animals have that, models have it too, and both of them get argued about in completely different registers. My hunch is that it's about *re-use*: a belief is a state you can put to work in an indefinite number of inferences and actions that have nothing to do with the situation that installed it, whereas a mere disposition-to-say is welded to the circumstances that elicited it. Somebody had a phrase for this a while back, something about beliefs being willing to join almost any argument going, but I can't remember whose it was, so please don't build anything on my recollection of it.

The second half is the part I actually care about, because it's the bit an engineer would contribute. In control work you never validate a model by watching it behave nicely on nominal cases. You validate it by injecting faults and checking that everything you predicted about the response really happens, and that nothing you predicted to stay put moves. So maybe belief attribution should be treated as a species of system identification: poke the thing — alter a premise in the context, perturb an internal activation if you can get at it, quietly change what it was told about some unrelated matter — and then check whether the change propagates exactly where it should if it were a belief and nowhere else.

What I need from you:

- A short title and a precise statement of the question, formulated so that it has a determinate answer rather than being a mood.
- Why the existing camps — call them the interpretivists on one side and the "it's just statistics all the way down" people on the other — both fail to settle it, and what each of them is quietly assuming.
- The method, concretely: what the perturbation battery would actually consist of, what I'd need access to, and what a rough month-by-month plan for nine months looks like for one person plus maybe a grad student willing to be seen with me.
- Why this approach and not the obvious alternatives (armchair analysis of the concept, yet another benchmark, or borrowing the neuroscientists' tests for animals).
- What a result would look like, and — this matters to me — what would count as the project failing. I can think of at least two ways it dies: the re-use criterion turns out to be satisfiable by a sufficiently big lookup table, or it turns out to be unmeasurable in practice. Say whether those are fatal and what else could sink it.
- An honest note on scope: what I should promise to deliver in nine months that a skeptic on the panel would believe.

Please leave the consciousness question out entirely — I'm not asking whether anything is like being the thing, only whether "believes" is literally true of it. And don't give me a bibliography or a literature review with references, I'll fill in my own citations before it goes out, I just don't want you inventing any.

Write it in American English, and keep the prose plain enough that I could read it out loud to my wife without being embarrassed. One more thing: its supposed to run about two pages, so don't pad it.
