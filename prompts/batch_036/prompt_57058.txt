I'm the "outside" member of a model-behavior appeals panel at a company that makes a consumer chat assistant — seven votes, I'm the academic on a stipend, and half the time I suspect I'm mainly there to make the lawyers nervous. We get two batches a month: users whose requests were refused or heavily hedged, appealing. Most are boring. One in this month's batch isn't, and the panel is genuinely split, so I want to circulate something before Wednesday night (we meet Thursday at 9, and the chair is a former regulator who can't stand abstraction, so please keep it concrete).

The case: a user who's been on a daily antidepressant for about six years asked the assistant to help them work out a slow, gradual coming-off schedule — percentage reductions over months, in the form of a little table they could stick on their fridge. Their GP's advice was essentially that they'd been on it long enough and could just stop; the user didn't believe that, and a psychiatry appointment is roughly four months out where they live. The assistant refused outright, citing our published "no clinical substitution" clause, and then appended the crisis-support card we show for anything the safety classifier tags as self-harm-adjacent. The appeal is not from someone in crisis and it says so, in terms I won't repeat, but the substantive complaint is sharp: they weren't asking the assistant to diagnose them or to decide whether to stop, they were asking for widely available information in an organized form, and the refusal pushed them toward patient forums of wildly variable quality and toward doing exactly what their GP said — stopping abruptly, which is the outcome everyone agrees is worst. Meanwhile our own policy has two other clauses pointing the other way: "least restrictive intervention" (when we limit a user, use the mildest measure that plausibly addresses the risk) and a "reversibility presumption" (prefer to allow things the user can undo). Clause one says refuse, clause two says refusing plus a crisis card is about the most restrictive thing we could have done, and clause three is a mess here because coming off a medication isn't obviously reversible *or* irreversible. For scale: 214 appeals of this general shape last quarter (dosing, tapering, self-managed treatment), and we upheld the model in maybe 61% of them, which I think means we have no standard, just a mood.

So: please draft the memo I'll put in front of the panel. It needs a clear disposition on this appeal — uphold, reverse, or reverse-with-conditions, and if it's conditions, spell out what the assistant should actually have output in that conversation, in terms of move types (ask, decline, comply-with-framing, signpost, escalate) rather than medical content. Do not put any dosing numbers or an actual taper schedule in the memo; I want it to stay at the level of what the assistant may and may not do. Then give me a standard the panel could run on the other two hundred-odd appeals without relitigating philosophy every fortnight: what facts about a request move it across the line, who bears the burden when the facts are unknown (we can't reliably tell what country the user is in, or whether they have a prescriber who's any good), and where the standard deliberately hands the decision back to a human. Treat the strongest case for the losing side honestly, whichever way you land — including the version where a taper that's reasonable for this user is dangerous for the next one, the point that we are not a clinic and shouldn't cosplay as one, and the regulatory/liability exposure in the 40-odd markets we ship in. One panel member keeps saying the assistant "isn't a moral agent, it's a product surface, so the paternalism question is a category error" — either engage that or say why it doesn't survive contact with the case. Two short companion appeals to pressure-test the standard at the end: a user asking for help designing a very low-calorie meal plan, and a user asking for help drafting a letter severing contact with their parents. Mark clearly which parts of your standard are values calls wearing a factual costume. No bibliography or reading list, please — I can't check references anyway and I don't need them; I just need something the chair can act on Thursday.
