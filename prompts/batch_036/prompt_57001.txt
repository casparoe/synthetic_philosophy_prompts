So here's a thing I keep chewing on. I've read some Kierkegaard — not properly, no classes, just the bit where he reviews two ages and goes after the press, plus whatever he says about the crowd being untruth — and every time I read it I end up thinking about Kony 2012, because I was nineteen when that video came out and I shared it along with literally everyone I knew.

Facts as I have them: Invisible Children put out a thirty minute film in early March 2012, and within about five days it was everywhere, with Oprah and Rihanna and Bieber boosting it, and everybody called it the most viral video ever at the time. It asked people to put up posters in their own hometowns on April 20, the Cover the Night thing. Then a follow-up video a few weeks later that basically nobody watched. Then the guy who made it had a meltdown on a street in San Diego that got filmed. Then the whole thing deflated and Kony was never caught. Meanwhile the policy step the film points at — Obama sending around a hundred military advisers to the region — had already been announced the previous autumn, and the film shows that announcement. And alot of people in northern Uganda who had actually lived through the war hated the film.

Run it through Kierkegaard. Who exactly is the public in this episode — the viewers, the celebrities, the retweet chain, the comment sections? What does his account predict going in, and what does it actually explain after the fact: the speed, the collapse, that the poster campaign was the peak of the whole thing, that the real move had already been made without us. Then the harder part, where he's wrong or just silent. He's writing about newspapers and pamphlets in a small Danish city, so what survives the transfer to a platform whose feed decides what spreads — is an algorithm something his phantom idea covers, or a case it never anticipated? And does the African Union sending troops after the campaign count as a counterexample he'd wave away, and if he'd wave it away is that a strength or just unfalsifiable.

Last thing. I've made up my own little test for when a public is a phantom rather than a bunch of actual people, and I want you to pressure test it. Three questions: can you picture it in a room, can it be embarrassed, can you date the moment it changed its mind. Fails all three, it's a phantom. Does that match what he means, is it too weak or too strong, and what does it do to the Ugandans who were furious — because they seem to pass my test, which would mean there was a real public on one side of the same event, and I dont know if his theory is allowed to say that.
