So: I want the alignment debate about process-based vs outcome-based supervision translated into quality engineering, as faithfully as you can manage it.

The claims I want carried across, roughly as I understand them: (a) the standard line that the outcome is the only thing genuinely grounded in what we actually want, so process rewards are proxies for a proxy and inherit a double Goodhart problem; (b) the counter-line that step-level supervision is in practice harder to game than endpoint supervision, because faking every intermediate step costs more than faking one terminal readout; (c) the Lightman et al. "Let's Verify Step by Step" result — ~800k human step labels, process supervision beating outcome supervision on MATH; (d) the automatic-label trick that came after it (Math-Shepherd and friends), where you skip the humans and score a step by how often rollouts from that step end in the right answer — i.e. process labels manufactured out of outcome statistics; (e) the scalability argument that for anything genuinely capable the outcome signal is too rare, too delayed and too catastrophic to sample, so you're forced onto the process whether you like it or not; and (f) the IDA/debate-shaped program where a weaker or dumber supervisor is supposed to be made adequate by the structure of the interaction rather than by its own judgment. Map each of those onto whatever my field really has: in-process gauging versus final inspection, leading versus lagging indicators, measurement system variation relative to part-to-part variation, special versus common cause, tampering with a process that was already stable, the fact that you can't run SPC on a batch of one, auditing a management system versus counting hull losses.
