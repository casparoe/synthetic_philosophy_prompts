I am a software engineer at a mid-sized SaaS company. I have no training in philosophy or history; my only exposure to either is a popular book on ancient Greek historiography that I read and annotated last year. My recollection, which may well be garbled, is that Herodotus recorded rival versions of events side by side and sometimes noted which ones he did not accept, while Thucydides claimed to have refused the first account he heard, to have checked participants' reports against one another as far as he could, to have conceded that witnesses contradicted each other in proportion to their loyalties and their memories, and to have admitted that the speeches in his history were partly his own composition, written to fit the occasion while keeping to the general sense of what had been said. If I have any of that wrong, correct me before building on my version.

Here is the job. I own the post-incident timeline. Two to four times a month I have to reconstruct what happened during an outage from fragments: automated telemetry, which is direct observation but is instrumented by us and can itself be lying; a customer's message in a shared channel; a support engineer's paraphrase of a phone call; a third-party status page; a vendor's root-cause summary issued six weeks later; a post from someone claiming to work at a competitor; an on-call engineer's recollection taken the next morning; and our own changelog and runbook entries, which are written to a house style. That timeline is read by executives and by customers, and it assigns cause. Separately, in the first twenty minutes of a suspected incident I make paging decisions, when nearly everything I have is secondhand. Time budget: about twenty minutes per report for triage, a few hours for the full timeline. Audience: engineers who will abandon any document that reads like a humanities seminar.

I want a procedure, not an essay. A numbered sequence of tests I can run on each candidate piece of evidence, in order, where each test states the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, what to do when the answer is unavailable in the time I have, and the test's characteristic failure mode — the kind of evidence it systematically over-trusts or under-trusts. Build in the following six items, which are my own half-formed attempts and should be revised, kept, or discarded on the merits:

1. A notation for evidential status, short enough that engineers will actually type it, distinguishing instrumented observation, first-person human observation, report of a report, my own inference bridging a gap, and boilerplate present only because our template asks for it. Be blunt about the fact that most of what I publish is the fourth and fifth category wearing the costume of the first.

2. A contamination test. My current rule of thumb, which I suspect is wrong, is that two independent reports confirm a fact. In practice the upstream source is often one status page, one thread, or one post, so two reports is one report counted twice. Give me a way to look for a shared ancestor using nothing but the reports in front of me — no tooling, no graph database.

3. Rules for interest and embarrassment. Reports seem more credible when they cost the reporter something: a customer admitting their own misconfiguration, an engineer naming their own service. But people also under-report causes that would embarrass their team, and our template rewards a tidy single cause. Tell me how to weigh the direction of a source's incentives without collapsing into discounting everything anyone says about their own conduct.

4. Access versus inference: whether a source was positioned to observe the thing it reports, or is reasoning about it. Most reports I receive fuse the two inside a single sentence. I want a way to split them apart in under a minute per report, plus a rule for the confident inference offered by a source with excellent access.

5. Genre. Our changelog, a vendor's root-cause analysis, a customer's complaint, and a runbook are different kinds of document with different conventions about what must be said. Tell me which conventions count as evidence and which are furniture.

6. The reconstruction problem. Thucydides's speeches are the analogy I keep returning to: I write fluent connective narrative between the attested facts and readers cannot tell where the attestation stops. Give me a marking convention that keeps the document readable, and tell me whether the convention is worth having if it makes the timeline uglier and executives skim past it.

Then run the whole procedure on three cases, showing the tests firing in order.

(a) 3 a.m. The only signals are one customer report in a shared channel and a flat line on a dashboard that turns out to belong to a service we redeployed an hour earlier. Forty minutes later a second customer reports the same symptom; support tells me they had read the first customer's message before writing theirs. Do I page? If the procedure and my gut disagree, say which one the procedure should override and why.

(b) Six weeks after an incident, a vendor delivers a polished root-cause analysis naming one of our components as the trigger. Our own telemetry from the window partly contradicts it — but that telemetry was sampled at one-minute granularity, and the vendor's engineers were on the call in real time.

(c) The case I expect to dislike. An outage whose cause is attested only by a very senior engineer's recollection, offered five weeks later, with no surviving logs, in a review where everyone else nods along because they trust him. The procedure should be willing to record that the cause is unestablished. Tell me what the document ought to say in place of a cause, in language that survives an executive read.

Finally, close with a short limits section: which of items 1 through 6 are load-bearing and which are academic; which distinction from source criticism genuinely cannot survive translation into a twenty-minute engineering workflow; where the procedure will misfire in a way that yields a confident wrong timeline; and an explicit list of every step that is a judgment call dressed as procedure. No reading list, no references, no titles or authors — I cannot look anything up during an incident and my team would not read it anyway. Assume I know nothing beyond what I have stated here.
