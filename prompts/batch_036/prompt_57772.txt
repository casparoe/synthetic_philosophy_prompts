I am preparing the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on evolutionary ethics. Over the term we have worked through Part I, chapters 3 to 5 of the Descent of Man, together with Darwin's later reflections on religion and on the limits of human conviction in his autobiography and correspondence, and the students have also been assigned a certain amount of secondary commentary. The paper is closed-book, three hours, four questions; I need one compulsory essay question worth 25 marks, to be written in roughly 50 minutes.

I want the question to turn on a crux that secondary summaries almost never expose: the relation between the thought experiment in which Darwin imagines what a community reared under the instincts of a social insect would take to be its duties — including the killing of its own brothers and sisters — and the claims he makes in the same stretch of argument about the moral sense being the most important difference between humans and the lower animals, about conscience arising when reflection compares a transient impulse against a permanent social one, and about the circle of sympathy widening over time. The question must require students to determine what normative work, if any, the insect thought experiment is being made to do, and whether Darwin is left with resources for saying that a wider circle of sympathy is better rather than merely later, more widely shared, and more advantageous to the tribes that happen to possess it.

Requirements:

1. The stem may run four to six lines, must paraphrase the Darwinian material rather than reproduce it, and must locate that material by chapter. Do not place anything in quotation marks anywhere in what you produce: this is a closed-book paper, and I will not accept reconstructed quotations in a model answer.

2. The question must not be answerable by narrating Darwin's account of sympathy, the social instincts, and the enlargement of the moral community. A student who can reproduce that narrative competently should be unable to earn more than a middling second-class mark on it.

3. The model answer should be 700 to 850 words, written in the register of a strong candidate working under time pressure: an argument with an identifiable thesis, not a survey, with no citations and no invocation of contemporary authors by name.

4. The marking scheme should be out of 25, distributed across five or six individually named moves, each with its mark allocation and a one-sentence description of what earning those marks looks like on the page. State explicitly what a solid but unexceptional answer contains and what it lacks; the A/B boundary should be identified by naming a distinction the A answer draws and the B answer collapses. I expect that distinction to involve, at minimum, the difference between descriptive variation in moral codes and any claim about their authority, and the difference between explaining why an impulse feels authoritative to the agent who has it and justifying the code it generates.

5. List four or five specific mistakes that should cost marks, each with its deduction. At least two must be anachronisms: candidates who have read only the secondary literature tend either to recast Darwin as running a debunking argument about moral knowledge, or to assume without argument that he offers a consequentialist criterion of right action alongside a sentimentalist account of moral motivation, failing to notice that these are different explanatory jobs which may or may not be compatible.

6. Conclude with a short paragraph identifying the single sentence a summary-trained student would be most likely to write in the opening minutes, and explaining why it should be penalised rather than rewarded.
