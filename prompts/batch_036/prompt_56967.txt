I spent thirty-eight years in a low-temperature detector lab, retired two years ago, and I've fallen into philosophy of mind the way some people fall into birdwatching — hard, and slightly embarrassingly. Here's my problem. In the lab, before you were allowed to argue about what a signal meant, you had to write down what your instrument model had to deliver: which artifacts it had to rule out, which anomalies it had to preserve, what it had to predict when you changed the shielding or warmed the stage by fifty millikelvin. Nobody let you propose an interpretation until that list existed. The consciousness literature seems to skip straight to the interpretations, so I'd like the list first.

Specifically: the so-called meta-problem — not "how does the brain produce experience" but "why are we so sure there's something left over to explain, why do we talk and argue the way we do about it." I want the desiderata for any account that claims to solve *that*. What does such an account owe us before it gets to count as an answer?

Give me the requirements, each with the case or consideration that motivates it — not a list of slogans. Things I'd guess belong in there, which you're free to reject or reformulate: it has to explain the reports and the arguing behavior, not just the private feeling of mystery; it has to cover the experts too, since I know plenty of people with no dualist bone in their body who still can't shake the intuition that something is missing; it should probably not smuggle in a first-order answer while pretending to be about the meta-question; and it has to dodge the trap where an account says our introspective and conceptual machinery is systematically untrustworthy right here, which raises the obvious worry about why we should trust the machinery that produced the account. Add whatever I've missed — I'm sure there's a requirement about how much variation between people, or cultures, or altered states, a solution has to be able to accommodate, and I don't know how to state it properly.

Then the parts I actually care about:
- Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are really just the preferences of one camp dressed up as criteria.
- Tell me whether any subset of them is jointly unsatisfiable. My hunch is that first-order neutrality plus explaining-the-intuition-away plus not-undermining-itself can't all be had at once, and if that's right then the list is itself a result, not a checklist.
- Say which single requirement knocks out the most of the answers currently on offer, and why.

One more thing. My lab had a discipline for exactly this situation, which we'd have called hunting an artifact: you model the process that generates the report without first deciding whether the phenomenon is real, and your model has to be capable of coming out either way depending on the facts, and it has to tell you in advance what the signal does when you change the setup. Spend a paragraph on whether that discipline transfers to the meta-problem or whether the analogy breaks somewhere interesting — I suspect it breaks, and I want to know where.

Please don't send me names to go read or a bibliography, I have a stack by the chair already and I'm asking for thinking, not homework. Bullets or a table is fine, call it 900 to 1200 words, and write it so a person who last did formal philosophy in 1974 can follow it without being talked down to.
