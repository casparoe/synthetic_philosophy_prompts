I teach an upper-level seminar on probability and legal evidence — sixteen students, one ninety-minute session. They know Bayes, and they assume the reference class problem is a technicality that careful people dissolve by simply choosing the obvious class. Design an in-class game that makes them feel otherwise, built on a stylized version of a real sentencing dispute: a courier arrested at an airport carrying a measured quantity of heroin, whose sentence turns on estimating what he carried on earlier trips for which there is no direct evidence, and where the prosecution offers frequencies for couriers of the same nationality arrested at the same airport during the same years.

Give every team the same case file, but deal each team a different selection principle — one must use the widest class for which reliable statistics exist, one must narrow to the smallest class that reflects everything known about this particular man, one may choose freely but must state and defend a criterion of relevance — and require each team to commit publicly to a probability before anything is revealed. Score by calibration across several rounds, so that hedging and bluffing lose. The collision I want them to run into themselves: all three principles are respectable, they yield incompatible answers to the same question, and any rule for choosing among them must presuppose the relevance judgment it was supposed to supply.

Include rules and materials, a point scheme needing no money, a timeline, a debrief mapping moments of play onto those three collisions, what will predictably go wrong on a first run (I expect teams to average across classes, or to invent classes absent from the deck — tell me whether to permit that), and a frank note on which feature of the problem the game cannot reproduce.
