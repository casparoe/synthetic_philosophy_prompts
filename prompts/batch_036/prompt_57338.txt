I help coordinate a pilot battery in an experimental philosophy lab. Every vignette in the battery is coded by the same senior rater, Marta, and her codes function as our reference standard for what the instrument is measuring: whether an agent brought about a foreseen side effect intentionally. Her codes so far, in order:

1. *Vineyard.* An owner sprays a fungicide knowing the drift will kill her neighbour's rare orchids. Asked about them, she says she doesn't care one way or the other and only wants to save her crop. She sprays; the orchids die. Code: intentional.
2. *Corridor.* A traffic engineer reroutes freight through a residential street, which she knows will raise childhood asthma rates. She has campaigned against the reroute, tells the council it is a grave cost, and signs off only because the alternative route kills more people in crashes. Asthma rates rise. Code: not intentional.
3. *Downdraft.* Same owner, same indifference, but the orchids die because an unseasonal downdraft carries the spray in a direction nobody could have predicted or prevented; she had been told there was some small chance of drift and took the standard precautions. Code: not intentional.
4. *Intake.* A clinic scheduler knows a new intake protocol will lengthen waits for a group of patients she feels nothing about. She adopts it because it improves her department's numbers, describing the waits as neither here nor there to her. Waits lengthen. Code: intentional.
5. *Intake, mirrored.* The same protocol will also, as a byproduct, spare a different group a painful follow-up procedure. She says she is glad about that, though it played no part in her decision. The follow-ups are spared. Code: not intentional.
6. *Boiler.* A building superintendent knows that shutting the boiler off overnight for repairs will kill the tropical plants in the lobby. He is fond of the plants and complains about it for a week, but the boiler is a fire risk, so he does it. The plants die. Code: not intentional.

The seventh vignette is now in front of us: a surgeon knows that a life-saving operation will leave her patient with chronic pain. She is distressed by the prospect and would prevent it if she could; there is an alternative technique that avoids the pain entirely, and she is trained in it. She declines to use it because it takes an extra forty minutes and she wants to leave on time. The patient is left with chronic pain.

Predict Marta's code, and state the rule you believe she is running. Then lay out the rival rules that fit codes 1 through 6 just as well — the familiar candidates in this literature (a control-or-skill condition, a blame condition, a pragmatic or communicative account, an account on which attribution tracks the agent's own evaluative attitude toward the effect) together with any rule you can construct that is not among them — showing for each which of the six it gets right and at what cost. Say which rival you would bet on for vignette 7 and why, and design one further case that would separate the surviving rules; it may vary the wording or the response format rather than the story, if that is what does the separating.
