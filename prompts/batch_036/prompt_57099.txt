Bit of an odd one, this. I've just started a job doing causal inference for a public health team, and in the last couple of months I've become a pretty commited interventionist — causal claims are just claims about what would happen under interventions, and the counterfactual/possible-worlds account is at best a blurry way of gesturing at the same thing. What would actually count against that?

I've got one worry I'd rather you press hard on than list the usual objections at me. There seem to be systems where a clean surgical-style intervention on the variable you care about doesn't exist even in principle — market equilibria, something like ancestry or a geological period, any variable sitting in dense feedback where intervening on it drags half the rest of the system along with it. Is that fatal? Or can an interventionist keep those cases by retreating to hypothetical or modelling relations rather than real manipulations, and if so what does that retreat cost them?

And separately: how exposed is the view to collapsing back into the counterfactual account it was supposed to replace, and what would tip me off that it had? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be clear about which ones a committed interventionist would themselves concede count against the view rather than explain away.
