I've got a philosophy-of-ML essay I want you to write, and I want you to actually land somewhere at the end rather than shrug at both sides.

The setup. Inverse RL: you watch a human's trajectories in some environment, assume they're roughly optimal with respect to their own unknown reward, and back that reward out. The inference leans on the same move as revealed preference in economics — treat observed choice as the only evidence there is about what someone wants, then read the "want" straight off it.

Now, the standard comfort is that underdetermination doesn't matter here, because the recovered reward is only unique up to potential-based shaping, and shaping-equivalent rewards admit the same optimal policies, so who cares which one you pick. I want you to work out whether that comfort survives contact with what people actually use IRL for. My hunch — check it yourself, don't just take it from me — is that shaping leaves within-state action rankings untouched but moves the value of each state by something like a per-state offset, which changes the comparison between the values of two *different* states. And a lot of the interesting uses are exactly cardinal: ranking candidate policies for the human, pricing a risk, trading off A against B, aggregating rewards across several people into one objective. If that's right, the "benign ambiguity" is benign for imitation and load-bearing precisely for the cases where the recovered reward is doing work as a utility function.

Second pressure, which you should weave in rather than tack on: real demonstrators aren't optimal. Present bias, akrasia, procrastination, framing effects. Feed that in raw and your robot recovers the biased reward and happily helps the human procrastinate. Instead posit an idealized preference underneath the noise and you're now inferring something the demonstrations don't exhibit — so where's the evidence coming from, and how is that different from the preference-attribution you were supposedly avoiding?

The controversy to adjudicate: is IRL inferring preferences, or just re-describing behavior with a preference-shaped vocabulary? Say whether the two readings could come apart in principle, what would have to be true for this to be more than a verbal dispute, and then pick a side and defend it. Give me the strongest objection to your verdict and your answer to it. No hedged "it depends on what you mean by preference" ending — if you think it genuinely depends, say which notion of preference you're cashing out and why that's the right one for the job.
