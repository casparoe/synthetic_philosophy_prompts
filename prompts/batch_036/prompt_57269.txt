A long essay in a general-interest magazine on the history of lying devotes a couple of pages to Augustine, and I would like to know how much of its gloss to trust. The passage runs as follows:

"For Augustine, a lie is a statement at odds with the facts that is offered as true. What makes it wrong is the injury it inflicts on the person deceived, and so its wrongness comes in degrees proportionate to that injury: a lie that harms nobody is a peccadillo, one that costs a man his reputation is worse, and one that costs a life is the gravest lie there is. This scalar account is what lets Augustine be an absolutist and still look reasonable — he can forbid the lie to the killer at the door precisely because such a lie risks a life, while leaving us free to mislead that killer with a true statement he is likely to misread, or simply to say nothing at all. Consistently with this, he does not count as lies either the remark made in jest, where nobody expects to be believed, or the assertion you sincerely make that happens to turn out false."

I have never read his two treatises on lying, but two things here strike me as shaky: I had the impression that Augustine located the lie in the gap between what a speaker holds in mind and what he utters rather than in the objective falsity of the utterance, and that the injury he worried about first was the liar's own rather than the victim's. Could you audit the passage claim by claim, separating what a specialist would accept as it stands, what is a defensible simplification, and what reverses or drops something Augustine insists on? In particular: does his ordering of the kinds of lie track harm suffered by the person deceived, and if not, what does it turn on; can he license the deliberately misleading true statement while forbidding the outright false one, given where he puts the lie; and are jest and sincere error set aside for the reason the essay gives? Finally, the essay goes on to conclude that Augustine's absolutism is covertly a harm principle and therefore has nothing distinctive to offer in the case of the murderer at the door. Tell me whether that conclusion follows from Augustine's actual view or only from the summary of it.
