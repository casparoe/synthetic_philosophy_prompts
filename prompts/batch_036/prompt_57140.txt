Twelve to fifteen times a month our hospital's ethics consultation service is asked to settle a treatment question for a patient who cannot settle it themselves, left no directive, and has no one close enough to them to speak reliably for their values: an estranged adult child who knows only that his father "hated hospitals," a group-home staff member reporting secondhand remarks from twenty years ago, a spouse who insists the patient "would never have wanted this" but cannot say what the patient actually said or when. I direct that service. Our current default is a best-interests analysis, which the committee has grown uneasy with, partly because it slides so easily into what the clinicians find reasonable and partly because in a fair number of these cases there is enough real evidence about the patient to do better than a generic welfare calculus. The committee has asked me to draft a substituted-judgment-first procedure that reconstructs what this patient would choose under better epistemic conditions rather than what they happen to have said once, offhand, in worse ones.

I've been reading around coherent extrapolated volition and the idealized-preference literature, and the part I think transfers is the idealization machinery: the move from a raw, actual preference to what the agent would prefer if better informed, more reflective, less distorted by the circumstances in which the preference formed, and more internally consistent. That is exactly the move our consults need, and exactly the move that can go badly wrong. So I want a procedure, runnable in about ninety minutes by a two-person team — one clinician, one social worker, neither with any philosophy training — that produces a documented recommendation for a specific pending decision, to be countersigned by the attending and by risk management before it reaches the chart in plain language.

Please specify, in numbered steps a team could execute in order: what evidence about the patient counts and in what priority order, and what to do when two sources give inconsistent pictures; which idealizing corrections are licensed and which are forbidden, drawing the line between correcting a patient's factual ignorance (a prognosis they never understood, a treatment they imagined wrongly) and overriding their values, including the cases that make everyone uncomfortable such as a preference formed during an untreated depressive episode, under heavy sedation, or under sustained family pressure; how the team should handle a patient's own internally inconsistent reports, and whether it should pool all informants' testimony first and then idealize the pooled picture, or idealize each account separately and look for convergence — take a position on that ordering question and say what turns on it; and a hard stop rule identifying the point at which the reconstruction has drifted so far from the actual person that the resulting preference can no longer be attributed to them, at which the procedure must say so and hand the case back to best-interests reasoning with a written explanation. Mark every step that is really licensing a judgment rather than resolving one, and say whose judgment it is.

Then run the procedure on three cases: a 74-year-old now facing dialysis whose only recorded statement on the matter is a remark at a family dinner a decade ago that he would rather die than be hooked up to a machine; a 29-year-old with a first psychotic break whose pre-illness self documented a refusal of intubation, and whose clinicians believe the documented refusal reflects a fear she no longer holds and would not endorse now; and a patient whose written, witnessed, still-current refusal of a low-burden intervention would, if idealized, dissolve. The third is the one I expect the procedure to answer in a direction our lawyers will not like, and I would rather find that out from you than from them — but if you think the procedure should refuse to reach that conclusion, say why and build that refusal in explicitly.

Format: open with a two-sentence bottom line stating when the procedure should be used and when it should not; then the steps; then the cases in a fixed template (evidence relied on, idealizations applied, resulting preference, confidence, who signs off); then a short closing section on what the procedure cannot do and where it will systematically misfire. Assume no one reading it has read any of the underlying literature, and give me no references or further reading. Keep the whole thing under 1,400 words; if you cannot fit all three cases within that, drop one and say which and why.
