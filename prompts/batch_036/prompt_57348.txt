I got volunteered to write the retrospective on a launch that flopped. Shipped eight months late, overspent by about 40%, and got killed quietly three months after release. Leadership wants a document explaining what went wrong, and I've got about two weeks.

The problem is that when I sit down with the timeline, the thing basically writes it's self. The warning signs are all sitting there in old Slack threads and they read like neon now. One engineer flagged in month two that we were building on a vendor whose API was still in beta, and looking at that message today it's impossible to see it as anything other than the start of the whole disaster. But I was in that meeting. The same engineer flagged two other risks that afternoon, we ranked all three as minor and moved on, and the other two never amounted to anything. It only looks like the important one because of what came after.

Worse, I've caught myself rewriting my own memory. I would have sworn I felt uneasy about the vendor deal when we signed it. Then I found the notes I took that day, and what I actually wrote was that everyone was relieved we'd found somebody who could start immediately.

Second issue: I drafted one honest paragraph — something like, at the time this was close to a coin flip, here were the three signals we had, one of them went bad — and a colleague read it and said it came across as making excuses. The version that sounds like accountability is the one where the failure was there to see and we walked into it anyway. So the accurate account makes us look evasive, and the flattering-to-blame account is the one people call rigorous, and I dont know how to get out of that.

Third: every lesson I can write gets drawn from a failure. We only do these retros when something blows up. Nobody has ever written up the three launches that used the same sloppy vendor process and came out fine.

So two questions really. Is my instinct right that what I'm producing is a story wearing the costume of an explanation — and if it is, is that avoidable, or is every account of why something happened a story at that level, meaning I'm holding out for something that doesn't exist? And practically: is there a way to structure the writeup so it fights the pull instead of feeding it? I really don't want to hand over a document that makes the next team paranoid about exactly the wrong signals.
