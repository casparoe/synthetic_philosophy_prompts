You are a historian of early modern economic thought who has taught eighteenth-century monetary debate for many years and who has run the session described below often enough to know where it goes wrong. Think carefully before answering, and treat this as a problem in pedagogical design rather than an occasion to expound Berkeley's views at me.

**The setting.** I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the history of economic thought: eighteen students, one eighty-minute session. They have just spent two weeks on Locke's recoinage pamphlets and on the mercantilist background to them, and they will read Hume's essay "Of Money" next week. Every one of them has taken introductory macroeconomics, so fiat currency, central banking, and the quantity theory are second nature to them — second nature in the particular way that a single first course makes things second nature, which is to say unexamined.

**The topic.** Berkeley's monetary theory as set out in the *Querist*, published in three parts in the mid-1730s, together with the campaign for an Irish bank issuing paper that ran alongside it. For reference, here are the queries I intend to put in front of them, quoted exactly:

- Query 1: "Whether there ever was, is, or will be, an industrious nation poor, or an idle rich?"
- Query 5: "Whether money be not only so far useful, as it stirreth up industry, enabling men mutually to participate the fruits of each other's labour?"
- Query 22: "Whether, therefore, less money swiftly circulating, be not, in effect, equivalent to more money slowly circulating?"
- Query 23: "Whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, or as being a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, as is variously suggested by writers? And whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?"
- Query 28: "Whether arbitrary changing the denomination of coin be not a public cheat?"

**What I want from you.** Not an explanation. A plan for the explanation, laid out to the minute, covering:

1. **The dependency structure.** Which components must be in place before which, and what breaks if the order is reversed. I suspect — tell me if I am wrong — that the first problem is that my students cannot feel the force of Query 23, because for a modern audience the denial that gold is wealth sounds like a truism rather than a heresy. If so, say what has to be reconstructed before Berkeley's move has any bite, and how to do it without spending the whole session on mercantilism.
2. **The one carrying example.** A single concrete case that runs through the eighty minutes and does the work that abstraction cannot. Tell me what it must have and what it must lack, and tell me why the obvious candidates fail — the poker chips in a card game, the company scrip or closed-town currency, the gift card, the ration coupon, and above all the hyperinflation anecdote, which I have used before and which I now think actively harms the lesson.
3. **The misconception map.** Both what they arrive with and what they will manufacture on the spot. My guesses: that Berkeley is simply saying money is a social construct, so he is trivially right; that he is a proto-Keynesian or a proto-chartalist and deserves credit for anticipating them; that if money is "only a ticket" its quantity cannot matter, which Queries 22 and 28 flatly contradict; that his "circulation" just is velocity and so that he invented the quantity theory; and that the interrogative form of the *Querist* is rhetorical decoration rather than a method. For each, name the exact point in the sequence where it must be headed off and the question or move that does it.
4. **The check.** One thing I can put to them in the final five minutes whose answer will tell me whether the idea landed or whether they have merely learned to say "ticket or counter" back at me.
5. **What to leave out, openly.** Berkeley's anti-abstractionism and his doctrine of signs are, I think, the real philosophical engine behind his view of money as a counter that functions without standing for anything. Decide for me whether an economics-minded audience gets more from ten minutes on that or from my saying plainly that it is there and that we are not going into it — and say what else gets cut either way.

Begin with a short brainstorming passage in which you sketch two or three candidate through-lines for the session — for instance, Berkeley as semiotician of money; Berkeley as the first to pry wealth apart from specie; Berkeley as a theorist of flow rather than stock — and state which one you will build the eighty minutes around and why the others would mislead this particular audience. Then give the plan. Do not recommend readings or supply a bibliography; I am supplying the texts myself. And where you think my diagnosis of the students is off, correct it rather than designing around it.
