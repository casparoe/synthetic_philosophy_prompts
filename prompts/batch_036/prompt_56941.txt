I'm a fourth-year PhD student in political philosophy writing a dissertation on Scanlonian contractualism and the aggregation problem. The chapter I'm stuck on concerns social risk: what a government owes each person when a policy spreads a small probability of a grave harm across an enormous population in order to avert a larger but far less certain one. My supervisor's standing objection is that the chapter never leaves the space of toy cases — lotteries, mine collapses, one-in-a-million risks borne by a million people — and so I want to run the machinery over a real decision with a public record and an unusually clean normative structure: the 1976 United States swine flu immunization program. Please write as a moral and political philosopher who works on contractualism, risk imposition, and public health ethics.

Take the following as given rather than trying to recall more detail, and if some further fact strikes you as load-bearing, flag it instead of supplying it. In January and February 1976 an outbreak of influenza A at Fort Dix, New Jersey infected a couple of hundred recruits; one of them, Private David Lewis, collapsed and died after a forced march. Laboratory work at the CDC identified a swine influenza A strain related to the 1918 pandemic virus, and the strain never spread beyond the base. CDC director David Sencer completed a memo in mid-February calling for mass immunization of the entire U.S. population; President Ford consulted a blue-ribbon panel that included Jonas Salk and Albert Sabin, announced the program on television in late March, and Congress appropriated $135 million. Manufacturers refused to supply vaccine without government indemnification, which the administration secured, so liability for harms rested with the government rather than the firms. In July an FDA Bureau of Biologics researcher, J. Anthony Morris, was dismissed for insubordination and went public with doubts about the vaccine's safety, after which several manufacturers paused production. Vaccination began on October 1, 1976; reports of Guillain–Barré syndrome followed; the program was suspended in mid-December after roughly 43 million Americans — about a quarter of the population — had been vaccinated. The excess risk is usually estimated at about one additional GBS case per 100,000 vaccinated, on the order of 400 excess hospitalizations and around two dozen excess deaths. The anticipated pandemic never came. Sencer himself later defended the decision on the ground that when lives are at stake it is better to err toward overreaction than underreaction, and the campaign is now routinely invoked in accounts of American vaccine hesitancy. In 2009 an H1N1 pandemic did occur, and researchers later found an enhanced immune response to it among people vaccinated in 1976. I don't want citations, a bibliography, or named secondary sources of any kind — I want your own analysis, and I want you to be explicit wherever the theory runs out of things to say.

Here is the apparatus I want you to work with, which is my own rather than Scanlon's. Call a contractualism *risk-relativized* when the strength of a person's complaint about a policy is fixed by their ex ante expected harm: the person who actually contracted GBS then has a complaint no stronger than each of the other 43 million, all of whom faced the same one-in-a-hundred-thousand risk and came out fine. Call it *outcome-relativized* when the complaint is fixed by what actually befell the person: the paralyzed patient's complaint is simply a complaint about paralysis or death, and the 43 million unharmed have, in effect, no complaint at all. My working hypothesis is that risk-relativized contractualism handles mass-risk policy better, because it allows the numbers on the risk side to do aggregative work that outcome-relativized views forbid, and that 1976 is a sharp test of this because both structures are simultaneously visible in it. Before arguing, spend a short passage sketching the decision problem — the options actually on the table in 1976, the parties to whom justification is owed, and what each view takes as its object of assessment, choices or prospects — and then work through the following, in order:

1. Set the case up in contractualist terms. Who are the parties: the vaccinated, the Fort Dix recruits, the merely possible pandemic victims, taxpayers who absorbed the indemnification, later cohorts whose trust in immunization was damaged? What were the genuine alternatives — not "vaccinate everyone versus do nothing," but watchful waiting, stockpiling, vaccinating only high-risk groups or only the military, a limited trial first? Which comparator makes the reasonable-rejection question well posed, and does the verdict move if you change it?

2. Run the episode on outcome-relativized contractualism. What does the prohibition on aggregating small claims say about trading two dozen actual deaths against a pandemic that did not happen? If the view is forced to say the program was impermissible because it produced real deaths and no real saved lives, is that a reductio of the view, an indictment of the decision, or evidence that the view is being pointed at the wrong object?

3. Run it on my risk-relativized view. Does it yield a determinate verdict, and does it license summing 43 million probability-weighted complaints into something that can outweigh the pandemic side? If it does, say plainly what remains of "justification to each person" once each person's standing is an expected value — whether I have simply relabeled cost–benefit analysis, or whether something contractualist survives the relabeling.

4. The identifiable-victim asymmetry runs backwards in this case: the beneficiaries were statistical and counterfactual, while the victims were real people who developed paralysis, testified, and litigated. Does contractualism have the resources to treat that testimony as evidentially relevant — bearing on the magnitude of the risk, or on what reasonable rejection requires — rather than merely psychologically potent? Or must the view hold that the moral shape of the episode was untouched by who ended up harmed?

5. Handle uncertainty and hindsight. Should the permissibility judgment be evidence-relative, keyed to what Sencer and Ford could reasonably believe in March 1976, or fact-relative? Does the "reasonably" in "could not reasonably reject" already do the evidence-relativizing work, and if so, is contractualism structurally unable to criticize the decision while condemning its outcome? Say what the 2009 pandemic, and the later finding about 1976 vaccinees, should and should not change.

6. Two institutional features that a philosophical reconstruction tends to skip: the indemnification bargain, which moved the burden from four manufacturers onto the taxpaying public in penny-sized shares, and the dismissal of Morris, an internal dissenter whose doubts later looked prescient. Is dispersing the burden across everyone a contractualist improvement, because the party bearing the risk is the party that must be answered, or a device for rendering the aggregation problem invisible? And does a contractualist account of legitimacy have anything to say about silencing a dissenter inside the deciding institution, as opposed to about the decision itself?

Finish with a verdict rather than a summary. Tell me whether the episode supports my hypothesis, refutes it, or shows that the ex ante versus ex post axis is simply the wrong place to look for the trouble in contractualism's treatment of risk. If your honest conclusion is that contractualism has no distinctive deliverable on cases like this — that it either collapses into expected-value reasoning or falls silent at exactly the point where the decision had to be made — say that and defend it. I would much rather have a decisive negative result than a balanced survey. Please also mark clearly, as you go, the places where the theory is silent, since knowing where it stops speaking is half of what I need for the chapter.
