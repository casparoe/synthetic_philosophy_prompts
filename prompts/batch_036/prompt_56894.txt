I teach a required 75-minute epistemology slot inside a health-informatics master's program. The students have all done a stats sequence — they can derive Bayes on demand, they've drawn ROC curves, and a couple of them have shipped clinical prediction models — and none of them have done any philosophy. Some are a little smug about that. Next week's topic is the closure principle and Nozick's tracking account, and the session has to land two things: that knowledge isn't the same as being right most of the time, and that on Nozick's account you can know perfectly ordinary stuff while failing to know you're not a brain in a vat, with closure as the casualty. Whiteboard only, no slides, and I get interrupted constantly.

Here's my own idea, and I want you to stress-test it rather than just accept it. I want to carry the whole session on the ROC curve. My pitch: the two tracking conditions are a test's two rates wearing a disguise — the if-p-were-false-you-wouldn't-believe-it condition is specificity, the if-p-were-true-you-would-believe-it condition is sensitivity — so a model that catches 99% of cancers but also lights up on 40% of healthy scans is a believer that tracks badly in exactly Nozick's sense. Then I flip it with a case of my own: a sepsis flag that predicts "no sepsis" for every single patient on a ward where sepsis is rare. It's right almost always, and its specificity is zero, and I want them to feel the gap between accuracy and tracking in their bones before any possible-worlds talk happens. Does that hold up, or does it break somewhere I should know about? I have a nagging feeling it does, on two counts: Nozick is asking about one nearby world where the belief is false, whereas these rates are frequencies over a whole population, so the analogy quietly turns a modal condition into a statistical one; and the base-rate business — great specificity, still mostly false positives when prevalence is low — seems to push toward the *opposite* of what the skeptical argument needs. Tell me where it's load-bearing, where it misleads, what to say the moment a student pushes it too far, and if it's a trap what I should swap in that still lives in their world.

Then plan the actual sequence. Does the skeptic come first or last? I can see opening with the vat to motivate why anyone would want to deny closure, and I can see building the tracking conditions first out of the ROC material and letting the closure failure drop out as a surprise near the end. Pick one and tell me which fails less badly and why, and lay out what has to be in place before each piece — I'm assuming they need some grip on "the nearest world where p is false" before sensitivity means anything, but they'll hear that as science fiction, so tell me how to get it across without Lewis. Also give me the misconception map with the exact point in the sequence where each one gets headed off, plus the sentence or two I'd actually say. The ones I'm braced for: sensitivity is just a false-positive rate so Nozick is doing statistics with extra steps; the whole thing is a word game since nobody really doubts they have hands; "you don't know you're not a vat" gets heard as "you don't know anything," so half the class leaves thinking Nozick is a skeptic; and my sharpest student will ask why the nearest world where I have no hands is a vat world rather than a world where I lost them in an accident last year — which I think is the real crux of the whole position and I don't know how to answer it at this level.

Last bits. What should I cut, and cut out loud so they know it's cut — safety versus sensitivity, the problem of which method a belief comes from, Gettier? And give me one end-of-class check, a short vignette or question I can put on the board, that seperates the students who actually got the closure failure from the ones who just enjoyed the vat story. Ideally the same check also catches the accuracy-equals-tracking slide, because that's the one that will follow them into their jobs.
