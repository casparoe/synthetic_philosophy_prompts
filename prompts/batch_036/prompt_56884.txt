My flatmate Nadia is three years into a metaphysics PhD and her chapter is about dispositions — what it takes for something to be fragile, or soluble, or conductive, whether or not it ever actually gets triggered. I've been baiting her at the kitchen table for weeks and she's handed down verdicts on a whole stack of cases, most of which she invented on the spot while drawing on napkins. Here's the track record as best I can reconstruct it. A normal wine glass sitting on the counter, tapped with a spoon: fragile, obviously. That same glass packed in foam inside a box, so that no realistic thump to the box would do anything to it: still fragile, and she was emphatic that the packaging doesn't change the glass. A copper wire hooked up to a monitoring gadget that instantly switches in a blocking field the moment current starts to flow, so no current ever actually gets through: still conductive, because the gadget is extrinsic to the wire. A plastic bead that's insoluble in everything but which a wizard (her word) would dissolve if you dropped it in water: not soluble — she said that's just not what solubility is. And then the one she admitted was harder: a glass whose molecular structure is exactly like ordinary glass, but built into that very structure, as part of what the glass is made of, is a self-repair mechanism that re-bonds any incipient crack within microseconds, so striking it never actually gets it to break. Her verdict: not fragile. She also said a wine glass with a hairline crack is more fragile than an identical intact one, while insisting both are simply fragile and that she didn't want to hear about assigning fragility scores or degrees. Finally, when I asked whether a uranium atom is disposed to decay even though there's no fact about when, she said yes, but visibly wasn't sure whether that was a problem for her own view. She threw around the words fink, mask, and I think mimic, and name-dropped Lewis once, but I didn't follow any of it and I don't want a bibliography or a reading list from you — I want the analysis.

The case I haven't sprung on her yet is one I made up after watching a behind-the-scenes featurette. A prop department makes a goblet out of a soft, tough, entirely non-brittle polymer — you can drop it on concrete and it bounces. But bonded into the wall of the goblet, chemically integrated so that it's one object and not a coating you could peel off or a device sitting next to it, is a micro-sensor and a tiny shaped charge, calibrated so that any sharp tap with a spoon makes the goblet burst into convincing shards. Don't worry about wether prop departments actually do this; assume the engineering works and assume the goblet, sensor and charge included, is a single object. Is the goblet fragile?

So: what do you predict she says, and more importantly, what rule has she been running? Give me the candidate rules that fit all of the above, in plain language (standard labels attached only if they actually have them), what each one says about the goblet, which one you'd bet on and why, and one or two further cases I could put to her that would genuinely discriminate between the survivors — including the case that would separate the rule you think she holds from its nearest rival, which I suspect is not the goblet itself. I'd also like to know which of her earlier verdicts she'd have to retract if she turns out to be holding the rule you think most likely. And be straight with me about how confident you are in the prediction, and point at the single step in your reasoning you're least sure of. My own hunch is that the foam-packed glass and the self-repairing glass are doing a lot more work here than they look like they're doing, since the only difference between them seems to be whether the interference sits inside or outside the object, but I could easily be wrong about that too.
