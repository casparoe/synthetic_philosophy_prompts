**DRAFT: Self-Deception as an Etiology-Transparency Problem**

**1. The puzzle.** The standard difficulty with self-deception is static and structural. A paradigm case of deception requires that the deceiver know what the deceived party does not. If I deceive myself into believing that my manuscript will be accepted, then something in me must register the rejection of the evidential case, while something else in me must fail to register it. Since both somethings are supposed to be me, and since belief is widely taken to be a state that cannot simultaneously hold and fail to hold toward one and the same content, the description looks incoherent. Sartre tried to house the contradiction in a split between levels of consciousness. The deflationists, of whom Mele is the most cited, tried to dissolve it by denying that any intention to acquire the belief is required: on that view, self-deception is simply belief arrived at through the ordinary machinery of motivated cognition — attention, evidence selection, vividness, and a lowered threshold for what counts as sufficient reason. A third strategy questions whether belief is the right framework at all, and treats the state as a kind of non-doxastic acceptance or imaginative rehearsal.

**2. The reframing.** I want to suggest that the interesting obstacle is not static but dynamic, and that it concerns the transparency of a belief's causal history rather than the content of the belief. Think of the agent as a prediction problem for herself. Suppose that for practical reasons I adopt a procedure for acquiring the belief that p — I resolve to believe it, because believing it would make me happier, braver, or more persuasive. If I know that I have done this, then I have also acquired a piece of evidence: this belief of mine was produced by a process I selected for its non-evidential consequences. For an agent who conditions on everything she knows about the provenance of her own states, that evidence is defeating, or at least heavily discounting, in just the way that learning a thermometer was chosen for its agreeable readings is discounting for its readings. The intervention therefore undermines itself at the moment of adoption. The structure is the toxin puzzle transposed to doxastic conduct: a plan that cannot be carried out by an agent who knows she has it.

Call the resulting constraint the **etiology-transparency requirement**: a motive can function as a cause of belief only insofar as the agent does not take that motive to be operative in her assent.

**3. The claim.** Self-deception, then, does not require an agent who believes p and does not believe p. It requires an agent who believes p and lacks the second-order belief that her belief in p is attributable to a motive rather than to her evidence. What does the deceptive work is a false or absent belief about the belief's etiology.

Two consequences. First, the static paradox evaporates, because no single moment need contain a contradiction: at time t you believe p, you also retain the suppressed evidence that not-p, and what is untrue is your standing implicit account of why you believe p. Second, what makes the state sustained deception rather than a one-off error is that the second-order ignorance must be maintained. If you were to ask yourself, right now, why you believe this, you would in many cases answer correctly. The available strategy is therefore to avoid asking. Self-deception is motivated assent plus motivated inattention to the question of why one assents. It needs no contradictory state; it needs a missing belief and a motive for keeping it missing.

**4. What the view predicts.** It should be harder to sustain self-deception in calm retrospective settings, where the causal history of the belief is conspicuous, and easier in real time, under arousal, and in the presence of an audience, where attention is directed at the object rather than at one's own process. It should be more available in domains where people are generally poor at reading their own motives, and least available in domains where they are good. It should be vulnerable to interventions that force an agent to articulate the etiology of a belief — and people should resist those interventions, misperform them, or convert them into a ritual of reassurance; the resistance is itself confirming. And it predicts that careful self-tracked calibration should improve numerical forecasting without doing much for beliefs that carry identity stakes, since the audit procedure becomes the new site of motivated reasoning.

**5. Two problems I cannot solve.**

*(a) Bootstrapping.* If the motive must be unrecognized, how does the agent get started? My answer is that the earlier steps do not require the belief: choosing which evidence to gather, whom to consult, and how long to deliberate can all be knowingly motivated, and the transparency requirement only bites at the point of assent. But this makes my account look like the deflationary one with an extra condition bolted on at the end, and I cannot yet show that the extra condition is doing any work the deflationist could not do without it.

*(b) Strength.* The requirement may simply be false. People appear to succeed at believing things they know they have decided to believe — the condemned man reciting affirmations, the believer attempting to will faith. If such cases are genuine, the constraint collapses, and I am left with the weaker and vaguer claim that the belief is only partly installed and walled off from the parts of the mind that would test it. That sounds plausible to me, but it sounds like a placeholder rather than an account.
