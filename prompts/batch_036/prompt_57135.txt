Our reading group is basically a rerun of the old Aunt Hillary thought experiment, and two people in it have been talking past each other all term, so I got volunteered to make them co-author something.

Camp A ("it's really a mind"): a leafcutter ant colony — the ants, the fungus garden they farm, the microbes riding along on their cuticle — is a genuine cognitive system. It remembers where it's been (trail network, nest architecture, where it piles its dead), it solves problems no individual ant solves, and "the colony believes the nest is too warm" is the kind of claim that can be true or false rather than a manner of speaking. Camp B ("it's a description we choose"): all there is are ants, gradients and dirt; colony-level talk is a compression trick doing the same job temperature does, and whether the compressed thing is *literally* a mind is settled by how we pick our words, not by looking harder at the ants.

Draft the joint statement a good-faith adversarial collaboration between them should produce. I want:

- a numbered section of claims both can sign, including the ones each has to swallow to get it (both accept that ant-level facts plus unlimited compute fix everything the colony does; both accept no single ant represents a colony-level state);
- a table of the cruxes — sub-questions where settling the thing would actually move at least one of them — with, for each row: A's formulation in wording A would sign, B's formulation in wording B would sign, a concrete case or experiment both agree bears on it, A's prediction, B's prediction, and a verdict on whether that row is a real disagreement or a verbal one;
- a closing section on what each side would take as evidence against thier own position.

Push on these cruxes, or trade them for better ones: (1) transparency — an engineered swarm of ten thousand cheap drones doing distributed search, where the base level is fully known and reprogrammable, so nobody gets to hide behind in-principle-but-intractable; (2) misrepresentation — the engineers plant a fake trail so the colony "believes" food is somewhere it isn't, and the question is whether the colony can be *wrong* in a way that leaves residue after you've said everything there is to say about the individuals; (3) identity — split the colony in half, or replace every ant over a year, and ask what survived; (4) whether "mind" is smuggling in a subject that neither camp has actually argued for.

If you end up deciding some of the fight is about the word "mind" and not about the ants, say which part, its worth knowing exactly, and say what you'd have to stipulate to make whatever's left show up as a genuine disagreement.
