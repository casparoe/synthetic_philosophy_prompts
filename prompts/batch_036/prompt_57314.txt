I TA an intro epistemology section and I'm also sitting in on a reading group that has now spent nine straight weeks on the regress problem — foundationalism vs. coherentism vs. infinitism — and it's going nowhere in a way that feels different from ordinary hard-problem stuckness. Everyone in the room already knows every move: the trilemma itself (the Agrippa/Sextus version and Hans Albert's Münchhausen retelling of it), the circularity and isolation objections to coherentism, the fact that BonJour spent years defending coherentism and then gave it up for something foundationalist, Klein's infinitism with its claim that the chain of reasons only has to be available to the subject rather than actually run through, the finite-minds objection to that, the doxastic/propositional justification distinction as a way of defusing it, the reply that availability just collapses into for-any-n-you-can-produce-n-reasons which is a finite capacity claim. Nobody has budged an inch, and what strikes me is that the two sides don't even seem to be trading in the same currency — when a foundationalist says the chain has to stop, and an infinitist says it never has to stop, I'm not convinced those are contradictary claims about the same thing.

Here's the wrinkle I want you to deal with, because I can't tell whether it's an insight or just me doing epistemology badly with tools from somewhere else. My flatmate does program semantics and he listened to me describe the debate over dinner and said, roughly: you people are arguing about least versus greatest fixed points and nobody's noticed. Model a body of justification as a directed graph, nodes are beliefs, edges are support. Then foundationalism is the claim that the graph has to be acyclic and well-founded — every descending path bottoms out — which is exactly the condition that lets you define things by well-founded recursion. Infinitism is what you get if you drop well-foundedness and handle infinite descending chains coinductively, which in his world is not a bug, it's routine, you just work with greatest fixed points and bisimulation instead of least ones and structural induction. Coherentism is the case where you let cycles back in, and the circularity objection is only compelling if you assume support is a non-expansive, all-or-nothing relation; if instead edges carry weights and a cycle returns attenuated — a contraction, so the operator has a unique fixed point — then a cycle stops looking like begging the question and starts looking like a bounded feedback loop, which is what a lot of actual evidential support looks like anyway. There's a second thought in the same neighborhood: the regress mode in Sextus was never meant to establish a structural thesis about justification, it was a dialectical device for generating equipollence and suspension of judgment, so maybe the whole contemporary debate is an artifact of converting a tool for stopping an argument into a premise for a conclusion about the shape of reasons.

So: diagnose this for me. What kind of disagreement is it, actually — substantive, partly verbal about what a reason is or what justification is a property of, two groups answering a structural question and a capacity question under one heading, or a hidden formal question that nobody has bothered to state? Then tell me whether the fixed-point recasting above would change anything or just relocate the dispute — and if it relocates it, relocate it to where, and is the new location more tractable or less (I suspect it moves the argument to what the weights on the support edges are and whether they're empirical, which might be a better place to fight). Relatedly: is the intuition that circular support is vicious a substantive constraint or a taste? And finally, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a theorem about which graph shapes admit well-behaved fixed points, a new kind of case that discriminates between the views, a change of question, something else — or should we expect none, and why?
