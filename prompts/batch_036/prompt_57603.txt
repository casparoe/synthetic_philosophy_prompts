I have spent most of the last fifteen years reading LessWrong and its successors, and for a good while I was the person in the comment threads defending effective altruism against what struck me as lazy criticism. My position has drifted: not towards the critics, exactly, but towards a suspicion that one particular critique has been answered badly, and that the badness of the answer has gone unnoticed because the answer looks rigorous. I have drafted an essay around this idea for a small blog I keep — a few hundred readers, mostly former rationalists who now work in policy — and I would like a serious critical read before I post it. A friend who works at a grantmaker read an earlier version and told me it was fine but unoriginal, which is the least useful feedback imaginable, since it does not tell me whether the unoriginality lies in the diagnosis or in the proposed fix. The draft is below; it is roughly finished, though the coinages are provisional and I would not mind hearing that they are bad.

--- BEGIN DRAFT ---

**The error bars are not where the objection lives**

1. A familiar objection runs roughly as follows. Cost-effectiveness analysis of the kind practised by the movement's evaluators systematically favours interventions whose benefits can be counted — deaths averted, cases prevented, income gained — and systematically disfavours those whose benefits resist counting: political advocacy, institutional reform, research capacity, or simply refraining from something harmful. Because the analysis issues in a ranking, and rankings drive allocations, the objection concludes that a bias in what is measurable becomes a mechanism for importing quietism about structure into decisions about money. The standard reply is that this attacks a naive methodology nobody holds: evaluators publish wide uncertainty ranges, write long caveats sections, and frequently say in terms that they cannot assess an intervention quantitatively at all. The objection, on this view, mistakes humility for false precision.

2. My claim is that the reply fails, and that the reason it fails generalises. There are at least two ways an estimate can be uncertain. The first is uncertainty about parameters within a model whose shape is agreed: how large the treatment effect is, how much of the observed effect is attributable to the intervention rather than to confounders or regression to the mean, how well it transfers to a different population. The second is uncertainty about whether the model contains the right variables at all — whether the quantity being estimated is the quantity that ought to be estimated. These are not two degrees of the same thing. Widening a distribution over a fixed quantity does not, in principle, represent the hypothesis that the quantity is the wrong one to distribute over. An interval running from small effect to very large effect is silent on, and cannot be stretched to cover, effect of a kind we are not measuring.

3. What makes this interesting rather than pedantic is that the failure is self-concealing. A reader who encounters a cost-per-outcome figure, a range, and a caveats paragraph cannot tell whether that range encodes parameter uncertainty, structural uncertainty, or — as I suspect is the normal case — a mixture in which the structural component has been absorbed into the parametric one, because the parametric one is the only component the model can express. The objection that you are not measuring what matters most is thus answered by pointing at an instrument that was never capable of answering it, and the answer is accepted because the instrument looks appropriately modest. I want to call this humility that has been relocated rather than earned. The same shape of difficulty recurs in the movement's career advice: an earning-to-give comparison models expected salary and expected marginal value of donations, with explicit uncertainty attached, but cannot easily represent that the comparison rests on assumptions about counterfactual replacement — whether the post would otherwise have gone to someone equally committed — and about the effect of the career on the person's later values. Those are structural, not parametric, and the uncertainty attached to the salary figures does not touch them.

4. If the diagnosis is right, the remedy is not better estimates but a different kind of published statement. Each ranked intervention should carry a short mandatory section listing outcomes deliberately excluded from the model, together with: (a) a qualitative argument, not a number, for why their exclusion is unlikely to reverse the ranking against the leading alternative; (b) a stated trigger — the sort of evidence that, if it appeared, would reopen the ranking; and (c) a standing entry in a public register, so that the trigger stays attached to the recommendation after the analysis is superseded. Three features distinguish this from the caveats paragraph that already exists. It is enumerative rather than discursive, so that an omission becomes an item somebody can be shown to have missed. It is comparative, because what matters is not whether an exclusion is regrettable in isolation but whether it changes an ordering. And it persists, because the point of a trigger is that it can be checked later by someone else.

5. I have not fully answered two objections to my own proposal. The first is opportunity cost: within the framework I am criticising, the analyst-hours spent writing exclusion statements are hours not spent on interventions whose benefits are countable, and if my structural claim bites only in a minority of cases the expected loss is real. My only response is that the cost is trivial relative to the size of the portfolios concerned, which may be a rationalisation in a tidy form. The second I shall call humility theatre. The requirement asks analysts to enumerate what they could not measure, and enumerating what one could not measure is precisely the task at which a quantifier is worst. The predictable result is a well-formatted list of negligible exclusions, while the exclusion that actually matters is absent because nobody thought of it. On this objection my proposal manufactures the appearance of the self-awareness it was meant to produce, and does so more convincingly than the caveats paragraph it replaces.

6. A note on scope. The argument does not show that unmeasurable values should dominate measurable ones, that advocacy beats bednets, or that past allocations have been badly wrong: a structural gap is perfectly compatible with the ranking it produces being correct. Nor is my claim that the relevant community lacks the conceptual vocabulary. People in the forecasting half of the same broad movement discuss the difference between getting a model's parameters right and getting its shape right with considerable sophistication, and there are already practices of dividing a portfolio across incompatible value frameworks precisely on the grounds that one cannot settle which framework is correct. My claim is narrower and, I think, more awkward: that this vocabulary has not migrated into the cost-effectiveness half of the movement, and that its absence is masked by an uncertainty quantification which looks like humility and functions like an immune response.

--- END DRAFT ---

Three things I would like from you, in this order of importance. First, press hard on section 2: is the parameter-versus-structure distinction actually load-bearing, or is it a distinction without a difference once you remember that any sufficiently wide parametric interval can be defended as a crude proxy for model uncertainty? In particular, consider the reply I may have straw-manned — namely that structural concerns are not meant to be handled inside the cost-effectiveness analysis at all but one level up, in the choice of which causes and which outcome types to model in the first place, so that the humility I say is missing is located elsewhere in the process. If that reply works, my whole essay is about a misunderstanding of institutional division of labour, and I would rather find that out now. Second, tell me where I have misdescribed practice. I have deliberately named no organisations and cited no documents, so I may have attributed to evaluators a methodology they do not actually use; where I say something already exists under another name, or where my claim in section 4 that the requirement would be novel is plainly overblown, I want to be told bluntly and shown the feature of existing practice that contradicts me. Third, assess section 4 as an institutional proposal rather than as philosophy: how much analyst time would it realistically consume, would it survive contact with a funder who wants a number by Thursday, and is the trigger device in (b) coherent or merely decorative? If you know of bodies that already require something structurally similar — in regulation, in environmental assessment, in drug labelling, in fiscal budgeting — say what makes their version work or fail, because I would like to borrow whatever machinery has already been debugged. What I do not want is a summary of my own argument returned to me, a list of things to read, or the observation that reasonable people disagree about the weight of unmeasurable goods, which is true and beside the point I am making.
