So I've been going back and forth with one of my PhD students for two weeks about Friedman's 1953 methodology essay, and I've genuinely lost track of which of us is winning. He walked in with this thing numbered up like a proof, and every time I try to put my finger on a bad step he shifts something and it reads fine again. Here's his version, lightly cleaned up:

(1) A theory is nothing but an engine for generating predictions about observables; its assumptions do no scientific work except insofar as they're load-bearing for those predictions.
(2) So whether the assumptions are descriptively true of what they're about — whether firms really do maximize, whether the billiard player really has the trigonometry in his head — is beside the point when we're appraising the theory.
(3) Abstraction is what gives an engine its power: you get more predictions out of less input by leaving out the messy particular. And to leave out is to misdescribe. "Firms maximize profits" is false, and it's false in precisely the way that buys generality.
(4) Therefore falsity-in-the-assumptions and compressive power rise together. Unrealism measures abstraction, abstraction measures reach.
(5) Reach — how much of the phenomenon gets predicted, and how far outside the data the theory was fitted to — is the only thing that makes a theory significant.
(6) So the more unrealistic a theory's assumptions, the more significant the theory. He insists this is the F-twist and that it's *right*, not merely a report of what Friedman said.
(7) But then unrealism is a virtue you can purchase. Take any theory and bolt on an assumption true of nothing — "all economic agents are also amateur oboists" — and you've made it more unrealistic without touching a single prediction. By (6) it's now more significant.
(8) Or go further. Let T be a true-assumption theory of a fishery: real boats, real skippers, heterogeneous beliefs taken off actual survey data. Let T\* be formally identical except that every assumption of T\* is the negation of the corresponding assumption of T. If significance tracks unrealism, T\* scores at least as high as T, and strictly higher if the negations sit further from the truth than T's claims do.
(9) So we ought to prefer, other things equal, a theory on which the fleet goes out because the skippers are chasing a sea serpent that doesnt exist, over one on which they go out because they're chasing fish that do. And if T\* then fails to predict, that only shows the predictions were doing all the scoring work and (6) was never a criterion in the first place.

The conclusion he's perfectly happy with and I'm not: unrealism isn't a criterion of theory choice, it's a symptom; nothing but predictive success has ever scored a theory; and every version of the inverse-realism thesis either collapses into that or licenses the sea serpent.

I don't want to just concede (9), because I do think there's something real underneath the twist — the successful theories in economics have embarassingly false assumptions, and that doesn't look like a coincidence to me. But I can't say where the argument actually breaks. Two things I want from you:

(a) Name the step. Which line is the first one that's false or unsupported, and why did it read as fine on the way past? My hunch is that (3)→(4) is doing something slippery with the word "unrealistic," but he'll say I'm just asserting that falsity isn't a scalar quantity, and honestly I don't have an account of what would make it scalar or non-scalar.

(b) Suppose I refuse to give up (6) — suppose I want to keep the provocative inverse claim, because that's the reason anyone still reads the essay. What do I have to add or restrict to block (7) and (8)? Give me the restriction, and price it: does the restricted version still say anything that a plain predictive-accuracy instrumentalist wouldn't have said already?

Things I want engaged rather than gestured at. First, whether (2) and (6) can both be held: (2) looks like it takes the assumptions off the scoreboard entirely, and (6) puts them back on, scored negatively. Is that a contradiction, or a change of subject, or something else? Second, whether the oboist clause in (7) is genuinely harmless — my worry is that it shows "degree of unrealism" isn't well defined at all, since you can always buy more of it for free, and a measure you can inflate at will can't rank anything. Third, what the defender of T over T\* is supposed to say if the two fit the data equally well and differ only in the truth of their assumptions.

And one case, handled concretely. This is his example, which I've stolen and made worse. In some fishery model, assume every skipper has perfect foresight of tomorrow's price and catch, and every boat is identical. That model predicts landings within a couple of percent over a decade. The rival model has true assumptions — measured heterogeneous beliefs, actual vessel data — and fits landings just as well in-sample, but on the one year when the quota changed, the perfect-foresight model was the better predictor out of sample. Which one does your diagnosis tell us to keep? And does your answer turn on whether perfect foresight is false in a respect that matters for the quota year or in a respect that doesn't? I want the work done, not the slogan: if your answer is that the whole thing rests on distinguishing respects in which an idealization is false from respects in which a distortion is false, then tell me what makes a respect relevant, other than the bare fact that getting it right would have helped the prediction. Because if that's all there is to it, (9) wins and I've lost the argument.

Finally — if you think the whole thing rests on an equivocation between two senses of "assumption," the ones that actually generate predictions and the ones that are only scaffolding or illustration, say so up front, since I suspect that's his best available move and I want to know whether it rescues (6) or quietly kills it.
