Quick background: I'm the clinical ethicist at a mid-size academic medical center — bioethics PhD, not a lawyer, not really a philosopher — and next Thursday I have twenty minutes plus Q&A at the joint sitting of our IRB and the Patient & Family Advisory Council. What I'm defending is a change in how our biobank does consent: instead of asking people again for each new study, we take one broad authorization at enrollment (the kind the revised Common Rule opened up for storing and later using identifiable biospecimens), keep a standing opt-out registry, and re-contact people only for a short list of sensitive categories. The more provocative half of the talk, and the part I actually believe, is that the "ask again every time" model everyone treats as the safe option is not a more valid consent, it's a less valid one: repeating consent requests inside an ongoing treatment relationship doesn't protect voluntariness, it wears it down, so a thinner consent can be the better consent.

My argument has three moves, and the objections will presumably aim at these. One: what valid consent requires is understanding the *kind* of risk and who will end up holding the data, not understanding each specific future protocol — comprehension of types, not tokens. Two: voluntariness degrades with repetition; by the fifth request, a yes from a patient who trusts her cardiologist is closer to compliance than to choice, so consent fatigue isn't a practical cost of dynamic consent but a moral one. Three: capacity is decision-relative, so a blanket finding that people can't grasp "unspecified future research" is hard to square with letting the same people make narrow treatment decisions that same afternoon.

The room: the chair is a health-law attorney whose whole instinct is documentation and liability, and he's been told by our EU collaborator's data protection officer that broad consent fails the requirement that consent be specific and informed. Two senior cohort investigators like broad consent but think my opt-out registry is unenforceable across partner sites and will want numbers on re-contact burden. One advocate is the parent of a child with a rare disease who has signed things she now regrets, and she regards a broad authorization as a signature that means nothing. Our staff ethicist works out of the four-principles framework and will say I'm trading autonomy for convenience. There's an informatics lead, and a psych resident who reads a bit of philosophy and will insist that only threats coerce, never offers, so my voluntariness claim is just confused. About half the room has no academic background at all.

Give me a prep sheet, not a defense of the view. Roughly eight objections in the order they'll actually arrive, weighted by how likely and how damaging each is, phrased the way that person would phrase it (the attorney doesn't talk like the advocate). For each: a reply I can deliver in under two minutes standing up, and a blunt note on where the reply is soft. Then tell me what to concede in my opening three minutes to buy credibility, which single reply has to land for the proposal to survive the vote, and the one question I'm praying doesn't come up. Open with one sentence naming the objection most likely to sink me. Hard cap: 850 words — if you're over, shorten the replies rather than dropping objections.
