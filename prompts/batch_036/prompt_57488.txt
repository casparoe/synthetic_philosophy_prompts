I sit on the board of a small family foundation that has to decide, this year, how to spend down a $30 million endowment over twenty years. The money came from my mother's estate; she was a mathematician, and the one instruction in her letter was that whatever ranking we produced had to be coherent — "if you can't tell me which option you prefer after looking at all three, you haven't actually thought about it." Six months of scoring models later, my own pairwise judgments cycle, and I can't tell whether the cycle is a defect in my thinking that a better model would dissolve, or a real feature of the options.

Here is the cycle. The three live proposals are:

- **A**: roughly 200 small grants (~$150k each) to direct-service programs with well-measured, near-term outcomes.
- **B**: about 40 mid-sized grants (~$750k each) to regional capacity-building programs with moderately well-measured outcomes.
- **C**: one large (~$30m) grant establishing a research institute aimed at a transformative, poorly measurable long-run payoff.

My pairwise verdicts: A is better than B, because on the criterion of expected number of people materially helped, A wins clearly. B is better than C, because on the criterion of risk-adjusted, diversified impact, C is an unacceptable single point of failure. C is better than A, because on the criterion of long-run significance — what will still matter in fifty years — a scatter of small grants amounts to nothing. Notice that a different criterion seems to do the work in each comparison, which is presumably why the ranking cycles.

I want to know how much weight to give the thought that this is not merely my sloppiness. So let me state a proposition precisely, using a distinction I've coined for my own purposes. Call an intransitivity **benign** if it can be eliminated by refining the description of the options — making the relevant criteria explicit, fixing the weights, or precisifying a vague predicate. Call it **recalcitrant** if the cycle survives every such refinement, including refinement of the weighting scheme itself.

**T**: there is at least one possible case in which all-things-considered betterness, over fully specified options, forms a strict cycle that is recalcitrant rather than benign.

I want your credence in T — a number, not a hedge, and not "it depends on which metaethical view you hold." Please also give a second number: your credence in the weaker claim that betterness is intransitive in *some* philosophically interesting sense, including the versions that invoke vagueness, imprecise equality, or parity rather than outright cycling.

Two further things. My own credence in T is about 0.35; a fellow board member's, after reading much the same material, is about 0.05, and he thinks I'm simply confused about weights. I'd like you to say which sub-question most plausibly separates us. And no reading list, no paper titles, no citations — I can't look anything up and I don't want guessed-at references. If you think one of my sub-questions is ill-posed, assign it a number anyway and flag it in a sentence.

Please structure your answer in exactly these sections, in this order:

1. **Bottom line** (under 80 words): the two numbers, one sentence each.
2. **What T does and does not claim** (about 150 words): whether my benign/recalcitrant distinction marks a real joint, and whether T smuggles in any assumption I haven't noticed.
3. **Decomposition** (about 450 words): four to six sub-claims whose conjunction or disjunction fixes T — for instance, whether a single overall betterness relation exists at all over such options; whether the correct weighting scheme across criteria is always determinate; whether boundary vagueness or parity can sustain a cycle without there being any fact about which option is better; whether spectrum-style arguments (many small benefits versus fewer large ones, where each step looks like an improvement but the endpoints look worse) generate cycles or only apparent ones. Give each a probability, one or two sentences of justification, then show the arithmetic by which they combine — state whether you're using a product, a weighted average, or something else, and why.
4. **Sensitivity** (about 200 words): which single sub-claim my total credence is most sensitive to, and what argument or piece of evidence would move it by at least 0.15.
5. **Our board's cycle** (about 250 words): classify each of A>B, B>C, C>A as benign or recalcitrant under my definitions, and say what would have to be true for a single scoring model to fix it — and whether you think a model that fixed it would be tracking something real or merely overriding a genuine indeterminacy.
6. **Where 0.35 and 0.05 disagree** (about 150 words): the sub-question that most plausibly explains the gap between me and my colleague, and what I could put to him that isn't just a restatement of my intuitions.
7. **How I could be wrong** (about 100 words): the single most likely error in the way I have framed T.
