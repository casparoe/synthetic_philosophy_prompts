Just spent four hours arguing with a friend of mine who sits on the jury for a national prize that goes to whoever made a particular scientific discovery — two names maximum — and I've been quietly keeping score of how she votes, becuase I'm fairly convinced she's running a principle she won't state out loud. Half her data is live nominations, half is her take on the famous old disputes, which she has firm views about too.

- Newton and Leibniz: won't say either deserved it, on the grounds that calculus was obviously coming. But she'll happily talk for twenty minutes about why the continent used Leibniz's notation while Britain sulked with fluxions for decades. She also thinks the Royal Society's committee on that dispute was corrupt in procedure regardless of who was substantively right, since Newton as president is generally reckoned to have written its findings up himself.
- Darwin and Wallace: says the joint reading at the Linnean Society was the right shape of answer, and that a jury permitted only one name shouldn't have been asked the question at all.
- A man who worked a result out completely in the 1930s and never published; the manuscript surfaced in an archive decades later. She voted no retroactive credit — having it in a drawer isn't doing science. Though she also mentioned that his field already had all the pieces in print.
- A discovery credited to a PI where the instrument that made it possible was built by a lab technician. She got the technician onto the citation and filed a dissent when the rules wouldn't stretch.
- Same result out of three labs inside a year, six months after the enabling technique was released: declined to award, minuting that the prize would really have gone to whoever had the best postdocs and the biggest machine.
- Someone who published the right result via a derivation he couldn't defend, with the correct derivation supplied two years later by another person: priority to the second.

And here's the new one, which is what we actually fell out over. A postdoc has the whole thing, writes it up, and her supervisor sits on the draft because it cuts against the programme his grants are built on. She leaves the field entirely. Eleven years later two well-funded groups get it independently within weeks of each other, each citing the other's preprint. Two names allowed, and the postdoc is alive and contactable.

So: what does she vote? Give me your best guess and the rule you think she's running — but also be honest about which other rules fit all six of those verdicts equally well and come out differently here, because that's the bit she kept sliding past. Then tell me which further case would actually settle it between the rivals, and whether you think her rule is the right one or just the one she's used to. Answer in British English please.
