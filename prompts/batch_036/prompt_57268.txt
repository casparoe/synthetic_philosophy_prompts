**Working title: Molyneux's problem doesn't discriminate between the theories**

1. The puzzle is usually sold as a litmus test. A man born blind, now adult, has learned by touch to tell a cube from a sphere of the same size and material; suppose he is made to see, and both objects are set on a table before him. Could he, by sight alone and before touching, say which is the cube? The standard reading is that the answer sorts the camps. The empiricist must say no: ideas of sight are new, unconnected to ideas of touch, so there is nothing for the visual cube to be matched against and no recognition is possible. The rationalist must say yes: shape is grasped by the intellect, and the intellect is indifferent to which channel delivers the occasion for its exercise. So the thought experiment looks like a way of deciding between two theories of ideas without running any experiment at all.

2. My claim is that the puzzle fails as a test in this role. Not because the answer is unknowable, but because each camp can give either answer without strain, and because the two answers are not even contradictories until you pin down what is being asked.

3. Start with the pinning down. "Could he know which is the cube" runs together at least three separable abilities: (a) making a perceptual discrimination between the two objects on sight alone; (b) applying the word — saying "that one is the cube"; and (c) knowing it in the full sense, meaning being disposed to act and to reason on it, e.g. to predict that if he configures his hand thus-and-so and reaches, he will feel the familiar corners. (a) and (b) can come apart from each other and from (c): a subject can discriminate without a name for the discrimination, and can produce the right name by luck or by an eliminative strategy (the sphere-ish one looks smoother, so the other is the cube) that carries no commitment about what his hand would find. The interesting question is (c), and Molyneux's formulation doesn't isolate it.

4. The substantive distinction I want to lean on is between possessing a concept and having it connected to an input channel. Possession is one thing; hookup is another. Consider someone who learned to read only in capitals and is then shown a page set in minuscule. They cannot read it. But we do not conclude that they lack the concept of the letter A; we say they have not acquired the lowercase forms. On the same model, the blind man has the concept cube from touch and lacks the visual font for it. What he is missing is calibration, not conception.

5. Now the payoff. Given 3 and 4, the empiricist can cheerfully accept the negative answer while rejecting the framing: ideas are channel-specific, so there is no visual idea of cube, hence no matching, hence failure — a failure perfectly consistent with his possessing something we would call a concept. And the rationalist can also accept the negative answer: the intellect grasps the cube without difficulty, but the sensory deliverance is confused and unread, and the fault lies in the report from the senses rather than in the understanding. Symmetrically, a positive answer is available to both. An empiricist who allows that some features of experience are not tied to one sense — motion, for instance, since the eye can trace an edge much as the finger does — has a route to yes. A rationalist who insists that sensation is opaque and that the intellect needs a legible input before it can operate has a route to no. So the armchair prediction, and even the real experiment, would not adjudicate between the two theories of ideas. What it would bear on is a question about the format of perceptual representations — a question the old vocabulary cannot express, since it has only "idea" for what we would now separate into content, format, and calibration.

6. Here is the twist I most want checked. There is a variant of the question that does have a determinate answer, and it is the one the debate usually treats as messier. Replace cube and sphere with three objects and five objects. The blind man can count by touch. Set three small objects on the table: could he, without touching, know that there are three? I claim yes, and not as a guess, because number has a canonical amodal format. One-to-one correspondence is available to any channel capable of individuating items, and individuation is far more primitive than shape-recognition. Shape concepts, on the other hand, are format-bound: a shape concept is roughly a specification of how a thing would feel or look from here, from there, under rotation — a sensorimotor profile rather than a label — and profiles do not port between formats. Number concepts are format-free: they specify how many, and any channel that can itemize can supply that. So the numerical version is the clean one and the shape version is the ill-posed one, which is exactly backwards from the way the puzzle is normally presented.

7. Conclusion, tentatively: Molyneux's problem is not a deep question about ideas. It is an artifact of a vocabulary with a single term doing the work of three, which is why each generation has been able to refill the specification with its own theory and then read its own theory back out of the answer. Its longevity is evidence about the language, not about the mind.
