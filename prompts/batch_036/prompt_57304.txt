I'm the clinical lead on a work group that maintains the diagnostic classification our country's public child and adolescent mental health services run on — it's what decides who gets seen, what a school has to put in place, and whether a family qualifies for a disability allowance. We meet twice a year, get about twenty-five proposals a cycle, mostly new categories, and each one gets a 90-minute session. Our review template checks four things: can the criteria be applied reliably by different raters, is there evidence the category picks out a real group, does it change what a clinician would actually do, and can it be told apart from what's already in the manual. That's the whole thing. Nothing in there asks what happens to the kids once the label exists, which is the part I care about most, because I keep coming back to Hacking's point that human kinds arent like chemical kinds — classify people and they move, they talk back, they reorganise themselves around the classification, and then the category's own evidence base shifts underneath it. He had the historical cases (fugue, multiple personality, and the fact that deleting a category remakes people just as much as adding one does), but I've never seen anybody turn that into something a committee can actually run on a Tuesday morning.

So: write us the procedure. Ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops at "reject," where it stops at "we can't settle this, it goes to the full comittee as a values question rather than a fact question," and what would count as evidence for the looping questions given our limits — no social scientist on staff, one clinical epidemiologist who thinks all of this is untestable philosophy, and field trials that run six months maximum across about a dozen clinics. It has to handle deletions and pure renamings, not just additions. And be straight with me about which steps really follow from the theory and which ones are reasonable-sounding guesswork wearing a checklist costume, plus where the procedure will systematically misfire (my guess: rare categories, and anything that spreads through the internet faster than we can measure).

Then run it on three live proposals.

1. Adding "anticipatory school avoidance disorder," ages 8–15: recurrent physical complaints in the hour before school, refusal episodes at least three times a month across a term, marked relief when the school day is cancelled. I drafted this one myself. In the field trial, 41 of the 67 labelled kids were using the manual's own vocabulary to describe themselves at six-month follow-up, 9 of them said they'd started answering intake questions differently after seeing the criteria sheet, and three districts have already begun citing the label to approve full-time home education.
2. Deleting "oppositional conduct pattern, juvenile" and folding it into a dimensional severity score inside a broader conduct category. Substance of the criteria barely changes; roughly four thousand kids currently carrying the label would stop having it.
3. Renaming "childhood emotional lability syndrome" to "emotion dysregulation disorder." Identical criteria, identical prevalence, no clinical change whatever — a parent coalition wants it because the old name gets used as an insult in staff rooms. My own view is that a rename is a nothing-change and shouldn't eat a session; tell me if the procedure disagrees with me.

Two housekeeping things. Don't hand me a bibliography or a reading list, I'm not chasing anything down — just make the reasoning visible. And every question the procedure asks has to be statable in plain language, because the final committee includes two parent representatives with no philosophy background and they will (rightly) refuse to sign off on anything that sounds like it's hiding behind jargon.

On proposal 1, I know I'm attached in a way that isn't healthy. If the procedure kills it, kill it and say so plainly rather than softening the verdict.
