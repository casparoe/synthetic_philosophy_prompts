I'm TA-ing an upper-level undergrad course this term — philosophy and CS cross-listed, roughly "belief, knowledge and computation" — and the professor is out with a busted wrist through the whole exam period, so for the first time in my life I'm writing the final on my own. I sat this exact exam four years ago and got a B on the essay, and I remember precisely why: the question asked about the problem of logical omniscience and I reproduced the standard recitation, that possible-worlds models of belief make an agent believe every logical consequence of what they believe, that this is obviously false of real people, and then a shopping list of proposed repairs. All true, all worthless. Since then I've come to think the interesting question is whether any of those repairs actually fixes anything, or whether they just move the problem somewhere less visible — and that's the thing I want this year's students to have to grapple with. (Separately, my flatmate does ML and announced at dinner last week that their system "isn't logically omniscient because it's neural, it can't even do modus ponens reliably," and I wanted to say something smart about how failing to be closed under consequence isn't the same as being free of the problem, and I froze. So, personal stakes.)

Here's what I need: one essay question, answerable in 75 minutes, worth 25 marks, that a student cannot pass by reciting. Please anchor it in a concrete little case rather than in abstractions — something like an agent who believes p and believes p → q and has no defeaters, is competent, isn't asleep or distracted, and still simply hasn't drawn the conclusion, so that on the semantics there's no world left where they fail to believe q. I want the crux to be a forced choice about what a repair has to deliver: if you add impossible worlds, or an awareness operator, or restrict closure to consequences the agent has actually spotted, or take Stalnaker's line that omniscience is a harmless idealisation of the same species as frictionless planes — what does each one have to give up in order to work, and does what it gives up amount to the thing that made the possible-worlds picture worth having in the first place? Specifically, I want the question to make students notice that a fix which makes belief sets arbitrarily fine-grained can end up with no constraints at all on what can be ascribed to an agent, which is arguably worse than the disease, and that the idealisation defence has to explain why dropping computational limits is unlike dropping friction — the idealised-away factor here seems to be what makes the difference between two agents' beliefs, not just a distortion of something else. Make the question demand that they compare at least two repairs head to head and say which they'd keep and why.

Deliverables, in this order: the question text as it would appear on the paper; a model answer of about 700 words written in the voice of a strong student, argumentative rather than encyclopaedic, reaching an actual verdict about which repair survives; and a marking scheme out of 25 that names four to six specific moves the answer has to make and says how many marks each is worth. The scheme should explicitly draw the line between an A and a B by naming moves only an A contains — I'm thinking something along the lines of "a B lists the repairs and their costs; an A explains what standard a repair has to meet to count as solving the problem rather than redescribing it." Then give me five mistakes students genuinely make on this topic and how much each should cost: for instance treating the problem as merely about memory and time rather than about how content is individuated, thinking the trouble only arises for knowledge and not belief, assuming that giving up closure under known implication and giving up necessitation are the same concession, or declaring impossible worlds a solution without saying how those worlds get individuated so that the semantics doesn't collapse into taking the belief set as primitive. Half my class are CS students who've done modal logic but no philosophy, so please don't build in any requirement that they know particular papers or names — concepts only, no citations or bibliography anywhere in the answer or the scheme.
