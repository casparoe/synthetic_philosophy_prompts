I am drafting a novel set in an invented delta province, and I have run into a technical problem that I would rather solve properly than wave at. The famine in this book must not be a shortage. I want to dramatize the entitlement argument — that people starve not because there is less food in the world but because their claim on the food that exists has collapsed — and I need a case built to order, because the versions I invent on my own keep sliding back into a failed harvest or into a hoarder, and either of those lets my reader off the hook with a villain.

I know the Bengal material reasonably well: the wartime inflation, the removal of rice and boats from the coast ahead of a possible invasion, the landless laborers who had been shifted from payment in kind onto money wages that did not follow prices, the record harvest that arrived only after the worst of the starvation. But Bengal had a war in it, and a war hands a novelist a villain for free. I want the same result with the war taken out, which is why my constraints are as severe as they are.

What I am asking for is a single scenario — one paragraph of prose — followed by your analysis. The paragraph should read like fiction rather than like a caption for a model, because I intend to lift it into a chapter.

The specifications, which I need met simultaneously rather than approximated:

1. Over the course of the crisis, the total quantity of food reaching the province rises. Not holds steady — rises. And it should rise for the very reason people are dying, so that the two facts share one cause.
2. A well-defined class starves anyway: landless agricultural laborers formerly paid partly in kind with a share of the harvest, recently moved onto money wages.
3. No war, no requisition, no blockade, no export ban, no hoarder, no price control, no cyclone, no crop disease, no corrupt granary official. Every actor behaves in a way that is individually rational and locally unremarkable. At least two of them must be people a reader would call decent, and one must be sincerely trying to help.
4. The mechanism must be traceable step by step with rough but plausible numbers — what moves first, what follows, over how many months, and the ratio between the food price and the money wage at each stage.
5. Include a second class that is unambiguously better off and would testify in good faith that nothing is wrong.

Then, after the scenario, please show your work:

- State what the food-availability account predicts here and exactly where it goes wrong.
- State what the entitlement account predicts, and locate the failure precisely: which of the laborers' entitlements gives out, and whether this is a boom-side or a bust-side collapse.
- Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work. Then remove it — suppose the money wage is indexed to the food price, or suppose the province is closed to trade — and tell me whether the famine survives, and what it becomes if it does not.
- The availability theorist's obvious counter is that a provincial aggregate conceals a district-level shortfall, so please build the case so that the starving district is itself a net importer, taking in more grain than it did before. If you cannot do that without cheating, say so rather than quietly doing it.
- Say how a defender of the availability account would attack the case as rigged, and whether the attack lands.

The scenario must generate these verdicts out of its facts. It fails if it simply stipulates them.

One last request, and it matters to me more than it may sound. Give me a single concrete object to hang the whole mechanism on — a line in a ledger, a price chalked on a warehouse wall, a ration card, a boat, a wage envelope — one thing a reader can see and hold, since I have to render all of this as scene and not as explanation.
