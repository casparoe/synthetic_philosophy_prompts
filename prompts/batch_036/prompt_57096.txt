I write literary science fiction — two small-press collections, nothing anybody's heard of — and I've been circling the same story for about a year without getting it down. I'm going to hand you the whole scaffolding, and I'd much rather you just build the thing than talk to me about it.

Setting: an island commune, call it four thousand people, that settled the equality-of-what argument by administrative fiat some thirty years back and has been insufferably smug about it ever since. Once a year there's a re-auction. Everything the island produces or imports gets divided into bundles, every resident gets the same number of tokens, everybody bids, and afterwards a machine checks whether anyone would genuinely rather have someone else's bundle. That check is the entire legitimacy test — they call it the envy audit, and if it fails the year's allocation is void and everybody does it over. On paper they have equality of resources, and they consider the question closed.

The trouble, which is the actual story: bundles convert into lives at wildly different rates. A woman whose body burns through painkillers and heating oil spends her tokens on the same list as her neighbours and comes out of the year able to do far less than they can. And there's a clinic, the part I want you to live inside. It offers cheap, safe, irreversible preference revision. If your wants have outgrown your bundle — if you've acquired a taste for things the island can't supply and can't stop wanting them — you can walk in on a Tuesday and walk out wanting less. The clinic is subsidised, popular, well-run, and it is the commune's standing answer to every complaint: the problem isn't your share, it's the shape of your appetite, and the shape of your appetite is fixable for roughly the price of a good pair of boots.

Protagonist: an intake assessor at the clinic, twenty years on the job. Their whole task is sorting complainants into two boxes — conversion cases, whose bundle genuinely doesn't reach them and who get topped up out of a compensation fund, and taste cases, who get offered the procedure. The boxes look clean and seperate. The assessor has started to suspect they arn't. Here's the hook I want to make hurt: the assessor went through the clinic themselves when they were young, for reasons they've never explained to anyone, and can no longer reliably remember what they wanted before.

Shape of the piece: one file lands on the desk that the categories can't handle, and a call has to be made on it. Give me roughly two to two and a half thousand words, first or third person, your choice. I want the reader to feel what each possible verdict costs somebody without anyone delivering a speech about distributive justice — the political philosophy goes in the plumbing, not the dialogue. No character says capability, welfare, functionings or primary goods out loud; nobody namechecks Rawls or Sen or Dworkin. The jargon survives only in forms, in the notices on the clinic wall, and in the slogans people mutter under their breath.

Two things I'd hate to see: the commune as a strawman dystopia, and the clinic as sinister. Make the case for the clinic strong, and make it be made by people who are right about most of it, including at least one person the assessor likes and trusts. And don't blow the system up at the end — no revelation, no uprising, no failed audit. The island keeps going. Somebody just has to sign something.

Format, please: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, no section labels, nothing that isn't the story — continuous prose start to finish, the way you'd type it into an email. If you feel you have to tell me something about the choices you made, do it in one plain prose paragraph after the last line, and no lists there either.
