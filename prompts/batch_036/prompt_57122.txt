I write for a general-interest magazine about science and ideas, and I'm building an interactive flowchart to run alongside a piece on contested pasts — Atlantis, Anatoly Fomenko's New Chronology, ancient-astronaut readings of carved Mesoamerican sarcophagus lids. Please work as a philosopher of science who takes the demarcation problem seriously, and think it through before drafting: first list the candidate dimensions along which such claims differ (is a falsifier nameable, is the falsifier one that could actually be checked by an outsider, is the proponent's attachment to the claim negotiable, what does the claim imply about the competence or honesty of specialists, does it require a permanent conspiracy of record-keepers), note in a line each dimension you reject and why, and only then build the tree.

Three constraints. Every node must be a question a general reader can answer about a claim without already suspecting where it leads — never a restatement of the verdict. Each leaf gets a name for the kind of claim it picks out, and at least two leaves must be non-pejorative, each with a sentence on what the tree obscures by filing a claim there. Then run five cases through it: Plato's Atlantis as he actually left it, Fomenko's chronology, an ancient-astronaut reading of a tomb sculpture, and two controls that were treated as crankery before the evidence arrived — Wegener's continental drift and the Norse settlement at L'Anse aux Meadows. If your tree cannot separate the controls from Fomenko, say so plainly instead of quietly adjusting a node until it can.

Finally, map the combinations of answers your dimensions permit that no real case occupies, and diagnose each empty cell as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Close by stating how confident you are that you have the right dimensions, and name the single node you'd most expect a specialist to attack.
