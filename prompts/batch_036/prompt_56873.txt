Need help with about four minutes of opening remarks for a dinner marking 25 years of a fertility clinic. Room will be maybe 400 people: mostly adults conceived there with donor sperm, a lot of their parents, some clinic staff, two or three bioethicists. I'm one of the donor-conceived, not a philosopher by training, though I've read a bit.

The organizers asked me to steer clear of the miracle-of-life speech, and also of anything that sounds like the donors were a freezer full of interchangable options that everybody in the room got drawn from. My problem is that I believe something close to the second thing, only I think it's the opposite of an insult. I hold that your particular origin is necessary to you — a different straw and a different person exists, not you with a slightly different history — so nobody in that room is anybody else's understudy or backup.

Write the remarks so that lands as dignity to non-philosophers rather than as a metaphysics lecture. Two constraints I care about: (a) don't let it slide from "your origin is necessary to you" into "your origin is what makes you you," because there's a pair of identical twins in the room off the same egg and sperm; (b) the fear I most need to avoid confirming is "one inch to the left in the tank and I wouldn't be here." I can't say that's false, but it can't be the note people carry home.

Afterwards, separately: tell me where you think the view I'm leaning on is shakiest — which objection, raised by somebody over coffee, should make me wish I'd worded things differently — and flag any spot where the speech is resting on a contested reading of the idea rather than something its defenders would all sign up to.
