At a reading group on the foundations of inductive inference, one of us argued that the Humean problem of induction is dissolved rather than solved once a learner states its prior explicitly: on this view, what we call induction is simply conditionalisation on a prior, so the live question becomes which prior to adopt rather than whether induction can be justified at all. A second member cut in with 'no free lunch — that's a theorem now', the room nodded, and we moved on.

I do not think 'no free lunch' picks out a single claim, and I suspect the reading our exchange required is not the reading people usually have in mind. Could you lay out, as a menu rather than a continuous exposition, the distinct precise theses that this slogan can be made to carry? For each entry I would like: the thesis actually being asserted; the assumptions it depends on, in particular what distribution over problems, objective functions or environments it presupposes and whether the methods being compared are fixed in advance of the problems they face; whether it is a proved theorem, a corollary of one, or a heuristic dressed up as one; and whether it is true, defensible, or false as stated.

Please make sure the menu covers at least the following: a claim about average performance across all possible objective functions, of the kind proved for search and optimisation; the separate family of results concerning the generalisation error of learning algorithms; a counting claim to the effect that, appropriately weighted, there are as many environments in which method A outperforms method B as conversely; a Humean claim about the underdetermination of theory by evidence; the older economic aphorism about costs and unearned advantage; and whatever claim about inductive bias being 'paid for' is intended when people say a method's success on some problems must be bought with failure on others.

Then assess, for each reading, whether it is even the right kind of claim to bear on our disagreement. Specifically: does any version of the result show that making a prior explicit cannot help, or does it merely show that some prior or bias must be present — which, if so, leaves my colleague's position untouched, since his point concerned transparency about assumptions rather than escape from them? Does anything proved under a uniform distribution over all functions license conclusions about non-uniform but still very broad distributions, where the conclusion may not survive? And is the folkloric version people quote weaker than the theorems actually proved, or stronger?

Finally, tell me which reading those who repeat the slogan most often intend, which reading an argument like ours would need, and whether the gap between the two is what makes the phrase so efficient at closing down a discussion.

Please write your answer in British English.
