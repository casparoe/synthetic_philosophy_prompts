I write about science and ideas for a weekly magazine, and I am currently reporting on the revival of sortition: the Irish citizens' assemblies, the vogue for deliberative polls, a start-up selling randomly selected "people's panels" to local councils, and a live proposal in one European parliament to seat a lottery-chosen chamber alongside the elected one. Almost everyone I interview, on every side, invokes the same piece of mathematics — Condorcet's jury theorem — as though it settled whether a hundred amateurs can out-think a dozen specialists. One of my sources, a game theorist who has spent the past few years turning into a public sceptic about all such bodies, has sent me an argument that he claims shows the theorem proves absurdly too much, and that anyone citing it is therefore either confused or selling something. He would like it quoted at length. My editor has given me 1,800 words by Thursday and I would rather not spend them laundering a piece of sophistry, so I need to know whether the argument holds up, where it breaks if it does not, and what breaking it costs.

Here it is, as he sent it, lightly tidied. (1) If a group votes between two alternatives, one of which is correct, and each member independently has a probability p greater than one half of voting for the correct one, then the probability that the majority is correct rises with the size of the group and tends to one as the group grows without limit. (2) The theorem's only requirement on p is that it exceed one half; the size of the margin does not affect the limit, so a body of members each at 0.51 is, in the limit, as reliable as a body of members each at 0.99. (3) Hence for any fixed panel of experts, however expert, there is some size of barely-competent group that is more likely to be right than the panel is; and since a public is larger than any panel, the public's verdict is the more reliable one. (4) Enlargement, moreover, never costs anything: each additional member is by hypothesis better than chance, so each addition raises, or at least does not lower, the majority's reliability, which means there is no such thing as admitting too many voters. (5) Consequently, on any question with two candidate answers, the judgement of a large inclusive public outweighs the judgement of a small specialist body, and the more inclusive the franchise the more trustworthy the collective verdict, with no upper bound on the value of inclusion. (6) That is unacceptable, since it implies that on whether a novel pathogen is airborne, whether a given fiscal expansion will prove inflationary, or whether a structure designed by open vote will stand, the opinion of everybody is likelier to be right than the opinion of the relevant specialists, and it implies that no electorate can ever be too ill-informed. (7) Nor can the theorem be rescued by saying that its hypotheses simply fail in practice — that real electorates violate independence through shared media and shared biases, that many voters are worse than chance so that majority reliability tends to zero rather than to one — because the same objection applies with equal force to juries, cabinets, editorial boards, scientific communities and prediction markets, all of which are correlated and none of which is composed solely of experts; if correlation and partial ignorance void the theorem, it is void everywhere, and what is left is a curiosity about imaginary urns. (8) So the theorem either proves that a large public beats a small expert panel on every factual question, which is absurd, or proves nothing about actual collective decisions; and either way the epistemic defence of majority rule and of selection by lot fails, leaving those institutions to be justified, if at all, on grounds of legitimacy, representation or the control of power rather than on their getting things right.

Which step would you reject, and is the chain valid as it stands or only valid given a reading of the early steps that the later ones quietly abandon? If it is valid, tell me which premise is cheapest to surrender and price that surrender honestly. I am especially interested in step (7), which my source thinks is the decisive move and which I cannot answer: is there a principled line between groups for which the theorem's hypotheses approximately hold and groups for which they do not, or is any such line just a restatement of a preference for whichever bodies one already trusts? Step (3) also worries me, though I cannot say why — something about "for any panel there is a group" and "the group is larger than the panel" sitting in the same sentence — and step (4) looks to me like a claim about what enlargement does rather than about what it would do under conditions enlargement itself may destroy, but I may be reading my own hopes into it. For what it is worth, I want to keep (1), because it is mathematics and I have no standing to dispute it, and I do not want to concede (8), because most of the institutions I am reporting on sympathetically would lose their best argument; tell me plainly if I cannot have both. Please write the answer in British English, and please reason the case through rather than pointing me at a literature — I have no time before Thursday to read anything, and I would rather have your diagnosis than your bibliography.
