A friend of mine is drafting an op-ed for a Scottish broadsheet, timed to the recurring speculation about a second independence referendum, and the spine of his argument is an analogy he says he absorbed from a decade of commentary: a state is a marriage, and independence is a divorce. His version runs like this. No-fault divorce exists precisely because we came to think it wrong to hold two people in a union one of them has left; the marriage may be perfectly valid and the vows sincerely made, and yet the fact that one party no longer wants to be in it is reason enough to dissolve it. So, he argues, if a majority in Scotland votes to leave, Westminster's insistence on the legality of the Union and on undertakings made in 1707 has the same shape as a husband's insistence that his wife is bound by vows she no longer endorses: technically correct, morally bankrupt. He draws a second conclusion from it, that the rump state has no standing to set conditions and only a duty to negotiate the terms of separation, because a court granting a decree does not get to decide whether the marriage should have existed in the first place. The unionist side, he notes, runs the same analogy backwards: divorce means dividing the home and the debts, the children's interests are paramount and they did not choose this, and nobody walks away with the whole house. He wants to keep the analogy because his editor thinks it is the most vivid thing in the draft, and he has asked me whether it can bear the weight he is putting on it. My instinct is that it cannot, but I cannot say precisely where it fails or how much of the argument fails with it.

So please audit it. First, give me the mapping explicitly — a table of correspondences is fine — filling in the slots the draft leaves vague: who are the two spouses (the peoples, the parliaments, the states as corporate persons?), what plays the part of the vows and of the matrimonial home, what corresponds to the children, to the pre-nuptial agreement, to the no-fault regime, to the court that issues the decree, and what, if anything, corresponds to the fact that a marriage is entered voluntarily while citizenship is acquired by birth and by territory. Second, separate the disanalogies that actually bear on his conclusion from the merely decorative ones. I can already see that a divorce is private between its parties while secession redraws the jurisdiction other people live under, that a divorce does not move a boundary that third parties stand on, that a departing spouse takes a share fixed by contribution and need rather than the geography she happens to be standing on, and that only one side of a secession can invoke exit while either spouse can file. Tell me which of these is load-bearing and which he is entitled to shrug off, and why. Third, say what the analogy quietly imports that the target lacks — in particular whether it smuggles in an answer to the boundary problem, since a divorce presupposes that the parties are already fixed and identifiable whereas the contested question in Scotland, Catalonia and Quebec is exactly who constitutes the unit entitled to decide (and hence whether the vote belongs to Scotland alone or to the whole United Kingdom), and whether it also smuggles in an agreed exit procedure and a tribunal above both parties that in most of these cases simply do not exist.

Then assess whether the conclusions survive. Does the no-fault line show that a majority vote confers a right to leave, or only that it confers a right to be taken seriously in negotiation — a far weaker claim, and roughly what the Supreme Court of Canada's 1998 secession reference arrived at, namely no unilateral right to secede but an obligation on the rest of the country to negotiate following a clear majority on a clear question? Does the unionist counter-use fare any better, or does it too depend on features of marriage that do not carry over, such as a pre-existing body of rules for splitting assets and a judge with authority over both parties? Finally, tell me whether there is a better analogy in this neighbourhood — a co-owned building whose owners cannot take a wing with them, a club membership, a tenant breaking a shared lease, a corporate spin-off, a redrawing of municipal boundaries — and rank the two or three candidates by how much of the real structure they preserve, saying for the best of them which feature of secession it still cannot reproduce and the analyst has to supply by argument. Two constraints on your reply: please do not send me citations, cases, or a reading list, since I am working without access to sources and want only your own reasoning; and please do not be diplomatic for its own sake. If the analogy turns out to be doing nothing but supplying vividness, say so, and if one of its imports is in fact a genuine insight that both sides in these debates have been ignoring, say that too.
