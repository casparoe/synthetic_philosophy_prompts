I'm giving a twenty-minute talk, with ten minutes of open questions, in the plenary slot of our regional astrobiology consortium's annual meeting in the spring, and the room is going to be a strange mix. There will be two payload scientists — one lead on an in-situ organics and biosignature instrument suite, one doing contamination control — who think entirely in terms of limits of detection, false-positive and false-negative rates, and who have a mission science requirements document that needs a yes-or-no answer; an origin-of-life chemist who runs bench-top autocatalytic network experiments and is deeply impatient with taxonomy in any form; a philosopher of biology who works on biological individuality and the units of selection; a policy person seconded to planetary protection drafting who will hear anything I say as a licensing question; and a scattering of synthetic biologists who build protocells and minimal cells and probably think my whole framing is quaint. The thesis I'm defending is that life is not a natural kind at all but a graded property, and that the property in question is what I call regulative autonomy: the degree to which a system's boundary and its displacement from equilibrium are maintained by control processes the system itself generates, with a second-order clause requiring that failures of those control processes be detected and corrected from within rather than merely damped by the environment. On my account a candle flame scores very low, an obligate intracellular parasite with a stripped-down genome scores in an unfamiliar middle region, a ribosome scores near zero, and a terrestrial bacterium scores near the top.

Here is the argument as I plan to give it, so you know what the objections should be aimed at. First, every candidate necessary condition — metabolism, heritable reproduction, a bounding membrane, homeostasis, evolvability by natural selection, net export of entropy — is either satisfied by systems nobody calls alive or absent from systems everybody does, and the failures cluster around viruses and viroids, prions, organelles and reduced endosymbionts, cryptobiotic spores and tun-state animals, cryopreserved embryos, and self-sustaining autocatalytic networks in a flask. Second, the extension is not closed under any finite conjunction of such conditions, and the reason is not our ignorance of chemistry but that there is no determinate fact being approximated. Third, and this is the move I most want to defend, the appearance of a sharp threshold in terrestrial biology is a survivorship artifact: everything we encounter sits near the top of the scale because everything below it either never got going or was outcompeted, so a smooth gradient looks like a cliff, and we then mistake the cliff for a joint in nature. Fourth, the practical upshot: the question "is there life on Mars" is malformed as posed, and the well-formed question is how much regulative autonomy, if any, a given sample exhibits, which means detection programs should report graded evidence with an explicit scale rather than a binary call. I have an answer to the obvious flame-and-fire objection — first-order self-maintenance without internal error correction — but I'm not confident it survives contact with an autocatalytic network that has feedback inhibition built in, and I'd rather you tell me that now than have the chemist tell me from the third row.

What I want is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the view. Give me the eight or so objections this particular audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to the talk if I fumbled it, and phrase each one in the vocabulary that asker would use — the instrument lead will talk about thresholds and requirements flow-down, not about natural kinds, and the policy person will talk about what my continuum permits — with a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds and an honest note on where that reply is soft or merely a deferral. Flag separately which of these I should neutralize inside the talk itself, and tell me the one or two concessions I should make in my opening two minutes so that they don't get extracted from me as victories later. Tell me which single reply has to land for the plenary to be judged a success, and which line of attack I am privately dreading, including a fallback answer I can give if I've genuinely got nothing better. I'm not asking for a literature survey or for pointers to papers behind these objections; I want the questions in the room.
