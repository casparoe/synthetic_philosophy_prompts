Been chewing on the epistemic-democracy stuff for years now — the Overcoming Bias / LessWrong lineage where somebody waves the Condorcet jury theorem at referendums and sortition and considers the argument closed. I want a case that actually stress-tests one load-bearing piece of it, and I want you to build the case, not describe the general issue.

Here's the spec: design a referendum in which publishing a genuinely good, nonpartisan expert analysis of the measure makes the collective verdict *worse* than it would have been with no analysis at all — and worse in a way a naive JCT optimist would not have predicted, since the analysis makes every single voter strictly better informed than they were.

Constraints, and these matter:

- Realistic institution, ordinary stakes. A county or municipal bond measure in California (with the kind of impartial fiscal analysis the Legislative Analyst writes up for the ballot pamphlet), or a Swiss-style cantonal/federal vote where households get the booklet with the government's recommendation in it. School bond, transit levy, zoning change, that level. No sci-fi, no emergencies, no fictional agencies.
- The expert analysis has to be honest and unbiased — correct in expectation, with an honest error bar attached, produced by a competent non-captured office. If you get your result by making the analysis wrong, slanted, or stale, the case is a dud, because then the obvious fix is just "write a better analysis" and nothing interesting happened.
- The voters can't be dumb, lazy, or manipulated. No ads, no dark money, no misinformation, no elites herding anyone. Each voter reasons competently about the evidence in front of them and updates sensibly.
- The deterioration has to come purely from what a *shared* signal does to the correlation structure of the electorate — not from anyone holding irrational beliefs, and not from turnout effects.
- One paragraph for the scenario itself. Then give me rough numbers: estimated individual competence before publication and after, and what the theorem would say about the aggregate in each case.

Then show your work:

1. State what the straightforward JCT application predicts here and exactly which assumption it's leaning on to get there.
2. Show what actually happens instead, and point at the precise spot where independence leaks out. I want to see *why* the same voters with strictly better private information produce a strictly worse collective verdict — the mechanism, not just an assertion that it does.
3. Run the counterfactual: same electorate, no analysis published, better outcome. And tell me how you know it's better rather than just different — what's the fact of the matter about the right answer in your scenario that lets you score both runs.
4. Name the single feature of the case doing the work, then say what happens to the verdict if I remove it — e.g. if the analysis gets disaggregated to the local level, or if only part of the electorate receives it, or if the office publishes the raw model instead of a point estimate.
5. Finish with how a transparency advocate or an epistemic democrat would attack the case, and whether you think the attack lands.

So you know what counts as cheating: I'm not trying to argue against voter information pamphlets, and I don't want a rant about how the public is too stupid for direct democracy. What I want to know is whether "more information → better aggregation" is a theorem-shaped claim or just a vibe, and whether there's a real regime where the information gain and the independence loss trade off against each other on ordinary inputs. A case that stipulates the bad outcome instead of generating it, or one where the voters are secretly sheep in disguise, doesn't do that job.
