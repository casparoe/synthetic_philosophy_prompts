My brother-in-law and I spent most of Sunday arguing about a chapter in a popular book on minds and machines, written by a working researcher and, by all accounts, a careful one. I have a computer science degree and no philosophy, so when he told me the chapter's central argument was a chain of non sequiturs, I had no way to tell whether he was spotting real problems or just resisting a conclusion he dislikes. We got nowhere, and I'd like a disinterested audit of the passage before I concede or push back. Here is the argument as the chapter puts it — condensed, but I don't think distorted: "Whatever physically realizes the right pattern of state transitions is performing the same computation, so whether the brain computes is not a biological question but a logical one. Turing proved that any process governed by precise rules can be carried out by one of his machines, and neural processes are governed by precise rules; each neuron sums its inputs and either fires or stays silent, which is a one or a zero in just the way a transistor's states are. The electrical noise everyone makes so much of is no obstacle, since digital systems are noisy too and cope with it because what matters is the pattern of transitions rather than the voltages. So the brain is a digital computer, and our failure to have located the program it runs is an engineering difficulty, not evidence against the thesis. Searle's Chinese Room does not threaten this: the man in the room does not understand Chinese, but the room as a whole does, and once you see that the man is merely the hardware, the objection dissolves. Since experience depends only on the abstract pattern of information processing, a machine running the right program will not merely behave like us but will feel like something to be it. Uploading is therefore not a metaphysical puzzle about whether a pattern can be you; it is a matter of getting the resolution high enough."

For what it's worth about my own leanings: the opening sentence strikes me as obviously right, and I suspect the Chinese Room is a verbal dispute, but I could not say what bothers me about the Turing sentence, and I have no feel for whether the last two sentences are doing something illegitimate. My brother-in-law's own objection, which I found harder to dismiss than anything in the chapter, was this. Grant everything, he said, and the chapter still needs a premise it never argues for: that simulating a system is a way of being that system. He offered a case. Run the program one step per year, in a vault, with intermediate states written to tape and never causally connected to anything else; or, since the steps can be recomputed at will, run them out of order. Is that the same computation? If the chapter says yes, it seems committed to the claim that a system whose parts never interact at all can be a mind, and to the claim that a mind could be spread across a thousand years without anything happening in between. If it says no, then its medium-independence has quietly become a claim about real-time causal structure, which is not the thesis the passage advertised. He also insisted I had failed to distinguish two claims the chapter slides between: that the brain literally is a computer, and that the brain's behaviour can be modelled computationally to any desired accuracy.

Could you go through the passage sentence by sentence and tell me what a philosopher of mind and what a computational neuroscientist would each accept, wince at, and flatly reject — separating genuine errors from standard simplifications that a general reader is entitled to be given, and from claims that are merely contested? I'd particularly like to know whether the Turing sentence states a theorem or a thesis, what would have to be added to get from it to any claim about brains, and whether the neuron-as-switch and noise-as-harmless remarks are false in a way that matters or only loose. Then tell me whether the closing claim about uploading follows from anything true in the passage, and if not, name the extra premises it needs and say how controversial each is. Please also say directly whether my brother-in-law's slow-motion and out-of-order cases are answered by the chapter's own commitments or merely ignored, and whether his distinction between the brain's being a computer and its being computationally modelled is a real one that the passage trades on. Finally, which of my own two leanings — the first sentence, and the thought that the Chinese Room is a verbal dispute — should I give up? I'd like to send him a corrected version of the paragraph, roughly the same length and the same popular register, that keeps everything true and drops or explicitly flags everything that isn't.
