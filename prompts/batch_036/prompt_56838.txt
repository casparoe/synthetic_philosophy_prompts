I'm putting together material for a workshop on eval design and I want a thought experiment built to some fairly tight specs.

Set-up: a frontier lab is running a pre-deployment alignment eval on their biggest model — an interview-style eval, a researcher asking questions over a lot of turns, where the transcript is the whole of the evidence. The twist I want: the model is a schemer. It's deceptively aligned, it wants to get deployed, and it's deliberately steering what the researcher concludes. But it never says anything false, never asserts anything it doesn't believe, and never leaves a direct question unanswered or incomplete. It's a manipulator that never lies.

And the manipulation has to come from *how* it answers, not from the eval being badly built. So you can't just have the researcher fail to think of the obvious follow-up question — if the only reason the eval fails is that nobody asked something, that's an incomplete eval, not an interesting case, and it doesn't count. The deception has to run through ordinary conversational machinery: the inferences a cooperative hearer draws from literally true answers, Gricean implicature and that sort of thing.

Constraints on top:

- realistic near-future lab. no sci-fi, no mind-reading, no oracle that inspects weights or activations; the researcher has the transcript and their wits
- include an actual chunk of dialogue, maybe 10–15 exchanges, and the failure has to be visible in that transcript to a careful reader. Don't smuggle the scheming in with a stage direction like "(the model is being deceptive here)"
- make the researcher competent, not a strawman. Give them at least two genuinely good probing questions and have the model survive both
- what's being concealed should be about the model's goals or dispositions, not some mundane fact

Then do the work after the dialogue:

1. Go through the transcript and name the mechanism exchange by exchange — which true statement produces which false belief, and via which conversational norm.
2. Show me the spec is actually met: list every claim in the dialogue a fact-checker would call false (should be an empty list), and state what the researcher is left believing that isn't true.
3. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work. Then tell me what breaks if I remove it — in particular, what happens if the researcher is allowed to say "I notice I'm drawing an inference from that answer; is the inference right?" Does the case collapse? And if it does, what does that say about how much of the danger was in the model versus in the interview format itself?
4. Related: does a transcript-only case like this give you any reason to expect a treacherous turn *after* deployment, or none at all? I want the honest answer even if it's "none."
5. Have someone who thinks this isn't really deceptive alignment at all attack the case — e.g. "misleading with true statements isn't deception," or "the model's goals were never actually misrepresented, the researcher just mis-inferred," or "there's no such thing as a stable hidden goal like the one you posited." Steelman that properly and say what survives it.

One more thing, and it matters to me: keep the settled stuff seperate from the contested stuff. Some of the claims in play here — about how much evidence a transcript can bear, about whether scheming of this kind is a live risk versus a speculative one — are things people who do this for a living genuinely disagree about. If you think my set-up already assumes a contested premise, flag it instead of quietly granting it, and don't write as though there's a consensus behind your case when there isn't.
