So, three weeks from handing in my afterword and I've gotten weird about the thing. You know the convention — the note at the back of a historical novel where you tell people what you made up. Mine is going to say stuff like 'Margaret is a composite of two real women,' 'there is no record that these two ever met, though if they had it would have been in the autumn of that year,' 'the dialogue is of course invented.'

What struck me on this pass is that the note itemizes inventions and never, ever itemizes what's documented. Nobody writes 'the riot in chapter nine is attested in three seperate contemporary accounts, the price of bread is from a municipal ledger.' The documented material is communicated purely by omission — whatever isn't confessed is implied to be true. And then readers and reviewers treat the note as a warranty, and the whole thing gets quoted back at me in interviews. Meanwhile the same publisher, on my books set in the present, prints the opposite boilerplate on the copyright page: all characters imaginary, any resemblance to actual persons living or dead purely coincidental. Same house, same list, same season, two disclaimers pointing in opposite directions.

Here's what I want you to work out for me. What theory of the relationship between a story and the truth does the confessional afterword actually presuppose? It seems to me it can only make sense if a novel about real people is in the business of making claims that readers are entitled to believe — otherwise why warn them at all. If that's right, which rival accounts of fiction does the practice quietly rule out, and is it consistent with the boilerplate on the copyright page of my other books, or are those two things running on incompatible premises about what a novel asserts?

Then I want you to look at where the confession draws its lines, because I think they're arbitrary and I can't say why. I confess the invented dialogue — which no reader could possibly have taken for verbatim transcript — and I say nothing about the invented interior life, the motives and the private thoughts I've given a person who died a hundred and forty years ago and can't object. Why is the one disclosable and the other not? What view of what can go wrong in a novel, or of what a reader does with one, does that asymmetry presuppose? And the composite disclosure is stranger still: saying Margaret is a composite of two real women makes a factual claim about two real women while supposedly disclaiming factuality.

Tell me which parts of this practice the theory strictly requires and which parts merely fit comfortably with it — I suspect a lot of it is underdetermined, that two quite different accounts of what a novelist owes the dead would produce the identical afterword. And then say what my note would have to become under three alternatives: one where novels assert nothing at all and the note is a category error, one where I owe a real person the same duties a biographer owes, and one where the wrong isn't falsity but something more like speaking in a dead person's voice without leave.

Last thing, and be blunt: is there a reading on which the afterword isn't a disclosure at all but a permission slip — a ritual that lets me invent more freely because I've confessed to inventing a bit? I'd rather hear that from you now than from a reviewer in the spring.

No reading list, please, and don't send me off to go look things up — I want the reconstruction argued out from the practice itself. I don't need you to know my books.
