I chair the allocation committee of a regional program that distributes donated courses of an enzyme-replacement therapy for a rare metabolic disease. The manufacturer gives us a fixed number of courses each quarter — eight this quarter, against thirty-one applications — and each course must go whole to one patient; we cannot split a course between two people. The committee is five of us: two clinicians, a pharmacist, a patient advocate, and me. None of us has any training in ethics. We meet for two hours, decide the entire slate, and the board signs off on a written rationale for each course awarded. Applications arrive on a standard form that records the applicant's current severity, expected time to irreversible organ failure without treatment, expected time to that point with treatment, and the probability that this particular patient will respond at all. It records nothing else, and we are not permitted to gather anything further. Committee turnover is high, so whatever we adopt must be runnable by someone reading it cold, in about ten minutes per case.

Our current written policy says, in effect, that we should award courses so as to produce the greatest number of successful treatments, because that is what our funder measures and reports on. I can no longer defend that, but I also cannot defend its obvious opposite. Two things have gone wrong this year. In the spring we awarded four courses to patients whose disease was stable but who had a high probability of responding, and turned down two who would have died within a year without treatment but had mediocre response probabilities; scored on the form, the four were the better bet, and nobody in the room thought the outcome was right. In the fall we had eight courses and nine applicants of essentially identical severity, and settled the ninth place by drawing lots. One board member objected in writing that randomizing between people in the same position tells the ninth family we did not care which of them lived; another objected that giving those nine an equal chance was itself unfair to a tenth applicant, less severe but the only one still helpable, whose course we had already spent on a coin toss. I do not know how to answer either objection, and I suspect they pull in opposite directions.

Please write the decision procedure our committee should adopt for a quarterly slate. Ground it in what the debate over whether harms to different people can be added together — and what that implies for choosing between the many and the few — has actually established, if anything, including the arguments that a loss does not become greater for being spread across more people, that claims of unequal strength do not simply pool, and that a chance procedure can express equal concern rather than indifference. Tell me which of these positions the procedure builds in, since I will have to say so out loud when a family asks.

The procedure should:

1. Set out the questions to ask of a slate, in the order they must be asked, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a refusal, and where it hands the decision to chance or to a person because the question is one of values rather than of the facts on the form.

2. State, for a slate in which several applicants would suffer the same loss and there are not enough courses for all of them, whether equal chances are required or merely permitted; and whether chances should be weighted by severity, and if so how the weights are to be derived from a form that reports severity in months rather than on any common scale.

3. Explain why the order of the questions matters. Show what changes if we ask about severity first and then about numbers, versus the reverse, and whether any ordering makes our slate-to-slate verdicts consistent or whether the procedure is exposed to cycling however we arrange it. If it is exposed, say what a committee should do about that rather than pretend it away.

4. Say whether an applicant turned down in an earlier quarter has any claim arising from that earlier loss, and if so how the procedure registers it without degenerating into a queue ordered by past disappointment.

5. Run it on three slates: (a) eight courses, nine applicants of identical severity; (b) eight courses, four applicants who would each die within eighteen months without treatment and eleven who would each lose hearing permanently, response probabilities roughly equal; (c) the spring slate as described. For each, give the awards, the refusals, and the sentence we would write to the board. Then say whether any of the three verdicts would change if our pharmacist is right that one formulation could be divided so that a course serves two patients at half dose each, and why divisibility should or should not make a difference.

6. For at least one of these, give me the verdict the committee will hate, and state what the procedure has to sacrifice to avoid it.

7. Tell me how to write the short rationale accompanying each award so that it does not read as indifference to the people we refuse, and specifically how to answer the board member's objection to lotteries — without either abandoning randomization where the procedure calls for it or claiming the objection has been met when it has not.

8. Mark plainly every step that is a judgment call wearing the clothes of a procedure, and every kind of case the procedure cannot settle at all.

I would rather have a procedure honest about what it gives up than one that looks decisive on paper and collapses in the room.
