For the past decade I have read and occasionally written for the rationalist blogs — LessWrong and the various successors and offshoots — and this autumn I am running a ninety-minute session for a meetup group of about sixteen adults: two or three with economics or computer science backgrounds, the rest without formal philosophy, but all of them people who will change their mind about a Dutch book far faster than about an intuition pump. I want an activity that makes them feel, in their own accounting, the price of refusing to trade two values off against each other — and I want them to arrive at it before anyone names it.

The conceptual target, stated for you rather than for the participants: the claim that some pairs of options stand in none of the three standard comparative relations — neither better, nor worse, nor equally good — but in a fourth, parity, as Chang characterizes it; and the money-pump objection to that claim. The specific worry I want the room to run into is that parity behaves like indifference when it comes to choosing, while failing to behave like indifference when it comes to substituting: an agent who will accept a side payment to move from X to Y, and later a side payment to move back, finishes poorer by a sequence in which no single step was one she could identify as a mistake.

Design the activity to that end, with the following in hand:

- Two ledgers the rules present as non-fungible — call them standing and security, or craft and care, your choice — plus a small number of ordinary transferable tokens that are conspicuously fungible. The presentation should tempt participants to honour the separation, and the offer sequence should make honouring it expensive. Small improvements matter here: the pump needs the steps to look individually negligible.
- No lecture beforehand, and the words incommensurability, parity, and money pump stay unspoken until the debrief.
- A move-by-move transcript of one table's play under competent, self-interested participants, with the arithmetic shown, demonstrating that the drain actually lands. If it does not survive rational play, redesign it rather than fudging the numbers; I intend to check this before I print anything.
- Tokens, a paper scoresheet, or a shared whiteboard ledger — no real money, and nothing a participant can lose that they will resent.
- A timeline in minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments of play onto the machinery, then sets out the escape routes with their prices attached: accept an exchange rate after all and surrender parity; deny transitivity; adopt a standing policy against trading on parity; restrict the set of offers the rules permit. For each, say what it costs and how the sharpest person in the room will object. Include whether there is any place in the game for the voluntarist line — that parity leaves the agent to commit rather than to calculate — and if the activity structurally forecloses it, say so plainly.
- A frank account of what will go wrong the first time I run it. My own prediction is that one table spots the cycle in round two and simply refuses to trade, which is to say they discover commensurability on their own; I would rather you build a way to turn that into the best outcome in the room than treat it as a broken table.
- Finally, what the activity cannot reproduce. The pump traditionally requires an agent who cannot see the whole sequence, and mine will; and the cases that actually motivate parity — a career against a relationship, a landscape against a livelihood — do not come with a fungible payment sitting conveniently beside them. Tell me what I will have to supply by argument once the tokens are put away.
