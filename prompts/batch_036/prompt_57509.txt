Two people from my rationalist reading group got into it after the meetup and I was the one driving, so I never got a verdict. They were arguing about Buridan's ass, but the abstract version: an agent facing two options it ranks as exactly equally good.

1. Dana said the case shows expected-utility maximization is incomplete as a theory of choice. It tells you what to do right up until a tie and then stops dead. Anything that has to actually act needs a tiebreak rule bolted on from outside, so decision theory isn't the whole story about rational choice.

2. Rui said that's not a gap, it's the correct output. With a genuine tie everything available is optimal, so the theory says "either" and there's nothing left to fix. Demanding it select one is demanding information that doesn't exist.

3. Dana: fine, but suppose the bolted-on rule is "take whichever option is listed first." The agent knows that rule is arbitrary — it can see for itself that being listed first isn't a reason. Acting on something you know isn't a reason is pretty much the definition of irrational.

4. Rui: arbitrariness is free when the options are truly tied. There is no better rule on offer, by hypothesis, so you can't be faulted for using this one. Complaining that a rule is arbitrary only bites if some non-arbitrary rule were available, and there isn't one.

5. Dana's closer was that arbitrariness isn't free at all: the agent persists over time and sits around other agents, so which tiebreak rule it commits to is observable, gets matched by people trying to coordinate with it, and shapes what it ends up wanting later. The one-shot tie calculation ignores all that leakage.
