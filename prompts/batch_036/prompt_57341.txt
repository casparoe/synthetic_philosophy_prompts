I am contributing an essay to an edited collection on chance and narrative, and my chapter is built around the opening of Tom Stoppard's *Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead*: a coin spun in a bare landscape, heads called, heads landing, again and again well into the dozens, with Guildenstern growing steadily more disturbed and Rosencrantz merely pleased with his record. I want to read that scene through the Principal Principle and the later debate about undermining futures, and I want the reading to be faithful to both the play and the philosophy rather than conscripting one to illustrate the other.

The difficulty my draft keeps circling is this. The scene is almost always described as a suspension of the law of averages, and that framing is too weak to bear much weight. The sharper question is what Guildenstern ought to believe about the next flip. Deference to objective chance looks like precisely the discipline he lacks: whatever the chance of heads is, his credence should match it. Yet the record in front of him is past evidence, and past evidence is supposed to be admissible; admissible evidence is supposed to license him in abandoning the hypothesis that the chance is one-half, not in clinging to it. So the opening may not be a failure of deference at all but a correct exercise of it, which makes Guildenstern's unease about something else entirely — the discovery that the world has stopped supplying the assumptions his deference was meant to track.

Now add the frame Stoppard supplies. These two are inside *Hamlet*; their deaths are already settled by a script they cannot read, and the Tragedians who keep turning up stage, in dumb show, an ending that turns out to be theirs. Notably, the streak does not stop when they gamble with the Player's troupe: the coin goes on landing heads against fresh opponents, which is hard to read as luck of any kind. If chance supervenes on the whole pattern of events, including the ones not yet on stage, then the run of heads looks like exactly the sort of local evidence that can undermine a true chance hypothesis — and the coin may be perfectly fair in a world that has already decided how everything falls out. Is that what the opening stages?

I would like you to work through the following, and to be willing to tell me the answer is no. Is the opening of the play a genuine instance of the undermining problem, or does the analogy fail because the play's world is not a Humean world, or because the characters lack any chance hypothesis to undermine? Does the structure — the script inside the script, the audience's knowledge outrunning the characters' — do any philosophical work that the formal debate cannot do for itself, for instance by exhibiting what it feels like from the inside to have one's total evidence point away from a chance claim that is nonetheless true? And where does the reading break: the coin is never established as fair or unfair, Guildenstern is a poor philosopher who reaches for supernatural explanations and for the thought that the same moment may be repeating, and the play's real subject may be ignorance, narrative, and death rather than probability.

Please do not quote the play — I will be checking every passage against the text myself, so paraphrase the moments you rely on and keep them identifiable. No bibliography or list of further reading; name a philosopher only where naming them does real argumentative work, and feel free to reconstruct the positions rather than to cite them.

Finally, I need the chapter to be defensible in front of people who work on this literature, so please distinguish carefully between what is settled and what is live and contested — including whether Lewis's revised principle is a genuine improvement on the original, whether it is even a principle about single-case chance, and whether Humeanism about chance survives the undermining objection at all. Do not present a contested position as though it were the consensus.

Structure the response as follows:

- **The scene** (~200 words): a plain account of the coin-flip opening and what each of the two men makes of it.
- **What deference demands** (~400 words): the Principal Principle applied to the next flip, and what admissibility does and does not license here.
- **Undermining, and whether Stoppard stages it** (~450 words): whether the play is a Humean world and the streak an undermining sequence, or whether the comparison collapses.
- **Where the reading breaks** (~350 words): the respects in which the scene resists the technical gloss, stated as frankly as you can manage.
- **What the play gives the philosophy** (~300 words): anything the drama supplies that the formal argument cannot supply on its own.
- **Settled and contested** (~250 words): explicitly labelled, separating common ground from open disagreement.
