I'm a high school senior writing a philosophy-and-history essay for a school prize, and I'd like you to take on the role of a professional philosopher with serious training in development economics — someone who has actually worked through Sen's entitlement analysis and the historical literature on the great famines of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and who knows how to build a case that survives a skeptical judge. Please think carefully before writing, and begin with a short planning section rather than going straight into prose.

Here is the position I want you to argue for, as strongly and as honestly as you can. It is mine, but my teacher thinks it is confused, so I need it stated in its best form.

The familiar slogan is that famines are political crimes rather than natural disasters — that droughts and blights are natural, while mass starvation is not. I think three quite different claims get fused together under that slogan, and that the fusion is precisely what makes it feel undeniable:

(A) a causal claim: in every large famine, human decisions are among the necessary conditions of the deaths;
(B) an incidence claim: who starves is determined by people's entitlements to command food rather than by aggregate supply — roughly Sen's move when he shows that food can be present while a whole group loses the means of getting at it;
(C) a culpability claim: some agent with the capacity to prevent the deaths knew, or culpably failed to know, that a feasible and not-ruinously-costly course of action would have prevented them.

My thesis is that (A) is close to vacuous and (B) is purely descriptive, so neither can support the word crime; only (C) can, and (C) is by far the hardest of the three to establish. Moreover, in the Great Leap Forward famine — the case people reach for when they want an unambiguous political crime — (C) comes apart between levels of the state, and the defensible conclusion is that what occurred was not killing by plan but culpable ignorance manufactured by an institution: reporting incentives that made false news profitable, the punishment of the officials who reported bad news upward, no press and no elections to force the truth through, and a household-registration and commune structure that made it very hard for villagers to leave and look for food elsewhere. That is a real crime of institutional design, but describing it as intentional mass killing mislocates both the wrongdoing and the remedy.

Make the case. Specifically, I want you to show:

1. Why (B), even when it is exactly right, does not get you to (C): entitlement analysis tells you the shape of the mortality, not its moral status.
2. That the subsistence-farmer case is the pressure point. When the people who starve are the ones who grew their own food and the crop failed, saying that their entitlements collapsed is little more than restating that they had nothing to eat — so the Irish famine, which is usually wheeled in as the paradigm political crime, does far less work for the entitlement framework than people assume.
3. How the Great Leap case supports my version of (C) rather than the intentional-killing version, using the striking fact that death rates varied enormously across provinces, and the fact that in at least one hard-hit area people died within sight of stored grain that officials would not release because their priority was meeting delivery quotas.
4. Why Bengal in 1943 does not rescue the slogan. Wartime inflation destroying the purchasing power of landless laborers, fishermen, and transport workers, together with the stripping of boats and rice stocks from coastal districts against a possible invasion, looks like the cleanest instance of (B) in the record — and yet the culpability there is diffuse in a way that makes crime harder to pin down, not easier.

Then handle the three objections I expect from the judge: that (A)/(B)/(C) is a distinction without a difference, since real famines always involve all three and the separation is artificial; that my reading of the Great Leap is far too charitable, given that the leadership was warned and disciplined the man who did the warning, and given that local cadres used force to keep villagers from leaving; and that my account of Ireland is simply wrong, because what made it a crime was that food kept moving out of the country.

Structure: first the planning section as bullets — the steps of the argument, with the strongest counter to each step — then the essay itself, 900 to 1200 words, using numbered premises wherever the reasoning is doing real work. Argue the position rather than surveying it; I can get a survey anywhere. Please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations, and do not invent quotations or figures. Where you are leaning on a contested historical claim, flag it in a parenthetical rather than dressing it up as settled.
