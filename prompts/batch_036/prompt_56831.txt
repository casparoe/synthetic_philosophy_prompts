I'm working on Henry Sidgwick's method in psychical research, and I want to know how far his verdicts are governed by one rule or by several. Consider the record. The census of hallucinations published in 1894, whose committee he chaired, aggregated thousands of first-person reports and set the number of apparitions coinciding with a death against what chance would predict; the excess was accepted as showing some real connection, while he declined to say what produced it. The slate-writing demonstration the Society arranged in 1887, in which a skilled conjuror performed for experienced and honest members who then wrote down inaccurate accounts of what they had seen, was treated as settling how little a witness's competence and sincerity are worth. At the Cambridge sittings with Eusapia Palladino in 1895, once she was observed freeing a hand and using her feet on the furniture, her mediumship was regarded as permanently discredited and she was barred from further experiments with the Society: fraud detected in one instance condemned the whole body of phenomena, not merely the observed trick. And in his published reply to the British Medical Journal that November he insisted the Society had never issued a report in favour of any professional medium's performances.

Now a case he never saw. After his death in 1900 and Myers's in 1901, automatic scripts from several automatists working far apart — Verrall, Coombe Tennant, Alice Fleming — were collected by Society insiders. Each fragment is unintelligible on its own; laid side by side, they were said to interlock into allusive references to classical texts and to a single plan that no writer knew in full, and they were presented as the work of one discarnate intelligence: Gurney, Myers, and Sidgwick himself.

Which rule or rules explain which verdict, and are they really the same rule? What does each imply about the cross-correspondences, and where do they pull apart — in particular, does the aggregate-statistics instinct that licensed the census cut for or against a body of thousands of fragments whose pattern was only identified by interpretation afterwards? Give me your best guess at his verdict and the reasoning he'd have offered, and then tell me which further fact about the scripts would discriminate between the candidate rules: whether a chance baseline could have been specified before the fragments were compared, whether any living person knew the whole design, whether the scripts were sealed and dated before their counterparts arrived, or whether an outsider could reconstruct the pattern without the compilers' gloss.

Please answer in continuous prose only, the way you would write a letter — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
