I am a graduate student in philosophy, and in two weeks I will present the central chapter of my seminar paper to an advanced graduate seminar on probability and cosmology. The position I defend is a restricted form of the self-indication assumption, which I call evidence-relative SIA: the usual boost in favor of hypotheses postulating more observers is applied only when a hypothesis's total observer count is fixed independently of the evidence in question, so that the boost is blocked in cases like the presumptuous philosopher while the thirder verdict in Sleeping Beauty is preserved. My argument is that the unrestricted weighting and the restricted weighting agree on every case in which the evidence is genuinely about one's own place in the world, and disagree only where the size of the reference class is being inferred from a fact that is itself a consequence of that size; I then argue the restriction is principled rather than ad hoc because it tracks a distinction already needed elsewhere in confirmation theory.

The room will contain five or six formal epistemology graduate students who work on imprecise credence and betting arguments, two philosophers of physics, and three faculty: a committed halfer who thinks the whole debate rests on a confusion about what one's evidence is, a committed thirder, and someone who works on the cosmological measure problem and is skeptical of any reasoning that treats observer counts as likelihoods at all.

Identify which objection I should concede early and gracefully, which single reply has to land for the chapter to survive the session, and the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
