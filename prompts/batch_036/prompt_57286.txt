**The proposition.** Let P be the claim: *the no-free-lunch results do substantive philosophical work on Hume's problem of induction — that is, someone who fully understands both Hume's argument (that inductive inference has no non-circular, non-question-begging justification, since any justification must itself appeal either to induction or to the uniformity of nature) and the no-free-lunch theorems ends up with a materially different assessment of the justificatory status of inductive inference than they would have had from Hume alone.* I am not asking whether the theorems are true, and I am not asking whether they are useful to practitioners. I am asking whether they are philosophically non-redundant with respect to Hume, in the sense that they license some conclusion about induction that Hume's own premises did not already make available.

**A stipulated term I want you to use.** For the purposes of this exchange, call the following the *diagonalist move*: the inductivist's reply that no-free-lunch comparisons are averages over an ensemble of target functions, whereas the performance of any actual algorithm in any actual world is fixed by the single on-diagonal pairing of that algorithm with the target function we happen to inhabit; so the inductivist can concede the theorem entirely and still say that her evidence bears on which pairing obtains, which is a question the theorem never addresses. Note that this reply is available whether or not one thinks the uniform-random environment is a device for counting environments in which one algorithm beats another rather than a claim about the actual world — a distinction the theorem's own authors press — and whether or not one thinks the folkloric slogan version is weaker than what was actually proved.
