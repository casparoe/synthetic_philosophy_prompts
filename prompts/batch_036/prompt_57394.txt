And here's the student's script, unedited:

"Descartes holds that beasts are automata — machines made by God rather than by human art, but machines all the same — and so they have no thoughts whatsoever: no sensations, no desires, no awareness of any kind. His reason in Part V isn't that we can't get inside them, but that they fail two tests we could in principle apply to anything that looked like a person. First, such a machine would never use words, or assemble signs, the way we do in order to declare our thoughts to other people; it might produce noises appropriate to what's in front of it, the way a parrot does, but it wouldn't be talking. Second, even if it managed to say some things well it would fail at others, because it acts only from the fixed disposition of its organs, each organ being arranged for one particular action, whereas reason is an instrument that can be turned to any situation whatever. So a machine — and therefore a beast — would need something like an infinite variety of internal arrangements to match the range of things a person can respond to sensibly, which Descartes says is morally impossible.

When the dog is kicked, everything that happens is mechanical. The blow moves the skin and the nerves, which are little tubes, and the animal spirits — a very fine, fast-moving bodily fluid — flow through them up into the brain and back out to the muscles, so the leg is withdrawn and air is forced out through the throat in a way that happens to sound like a yelp. A heat in the region of the heart keeps the whole thing going and does all the work the scholastics handed to the vegetative soul: digestion, growth, circulation of the blood. Nothing mental has to be posited anywhere, and Descartes is perfectly happy to compare such a body to a clock — the behaviour comes out of the arrangement of the parts, just as a clock's striking comes out of its wheels.

Yes, I think it does follow, and in fact it's the same argument. In the Sixth Meditation Descartes shows that the mind is indivisible while the body is divisible — I can conceive of my body as halved or as losing a limb, but I cannot conceive of my mind as having parts — and from this he concludes that mind and body are really distinct substances. But if the body is divisible and the mind isn't, then whatever is nothing but body has no mental properties at all, and beasts are nothing but body: Descartes never credits them with a soul of any kind, and he ties the incorruptibility of the human soul to its indivisibility. So the beast-machine doctrine is just the real distinction argument pointed at animals, and the Part V tests are only the observable signs that let us apply it. There's also a shorter route from the cogito: I'm certain of my own thought because I can doubt everything else and doubting is thinking; a beast cannot doubt, so it cannot think either.

One awkward consequence is worth flagging. If a body arranged the right way can produce speech and adaptive behaviour, then the only thing standing between me and a very sophisticated dog is the guarantee that my clear and distinct perceptions are true. So the beast-machine doctrine ultimately rests on the proof of a non-deceiving God, and without it Descartes couldn't tell himself apart from an automaton — which is presumably also why he needs the divine guarantee to get the external world back at the end of the Meditations."
