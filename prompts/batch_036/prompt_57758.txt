Question 3 (20 marks; suggested length 400–500 words). Compare the French and the German constitutional settlements governing the relationship between the state and religious communities. Your answer should (a) identify, for each country, the historical instrument that produced the present arrangement and sketch the political circumstances of its adoption; (b) explain the mechanism, if any, by which public money or public administrative machinery reaches religious bodies in that country; (c) assess whether either arrangement amounts to an establishment of religion; and (d) conclude by identifying one respect in which each country's official self-description — laïcité in France, benevolent or cooperative neutrality in Germany — sits awkwardly with actual practice.

Student's answer: "France and Germany both describe themselves as secular states, but they arrived at that self-description from opposite directions, and the machinery they ended up with is not the same. France's settlement comes from the law of 9 December 1905, which ended the concordatory regime Napoleon had put in place in 1801–1802 and which had made the Catholic clergy in effect salaried civil servants. The 1905 law, prepared under Aristide Briand's influence, provides in its second article that the Republic neither recognises nor salaries nor subsidises any religion, and it accordingly struck all religious expenditure from state, departmental and communal budgets. The obvious exception is Alsace-Moselle, which was German territory in 1905 and which, on its return to France in 1919, kept the old concordatory regime: there the clergy are still paid by the state and religious instruction is still given in the public schools. French laïcité is therefore best understood as a rule of separation with one large territorial carve-out. Germany's settlement comes from the Weimar Constitution of 1919, whose church articles were carried across into the Basic Law of 1949 as Article 140. The result is not separation but cooperation: religious bodies meeting the constitutional requirements are corporations under public law, and in that capacity they may levy a church tax on their members, which the Land revenue offices collect on their behalf out of the state's own machinery in exchange for a handling fee. The rate is set by each Land and currently stands at eight or nine per cent of the taxpayer's income. Membership is registered with the civil authorities, and the only way to stop paying is to make a formal declaration of withdrawal before a state office; the church itself has no say in the matter. Article 140 thus establishes a qualified establishment in favour of the two historic churches, Catholic and Protestant, which is why Germany can describe itself as neutral while funding them. The contrast is usually drawn too sharply. In both countries the state's relation to religion is ultimately a matter of administrative convenience: France owns the buildings and Germany collects the money. Neither has a state church in the English sense, where the monarch is Supreme Governor of the Church of England and its bishops sit in the upper house. Once that is granted, the difference between laïcité and the German cooperative model is a difference of technique rather than of constitutional principle."
