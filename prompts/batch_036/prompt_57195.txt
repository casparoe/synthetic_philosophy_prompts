I am convening the closing session of a small reading group in philosophy of mind, and I would like to open it with a poem rather than a paper.

The subject is Douglas Hofstadter's strange-loop picture of the mind: the brain — which in his strange-loop books he nicknames the careenium — is a seething substrate of neurons, and the symbols inhabiting it (his simmballs) are large-scale, fuzzy, statistical patterns that push one another about. On that picture the simmball for the self is only one such pattern among many, but it is the one that eventually acquires the power to push the others, and to push itself, so that the careenium ends up taking orders from a pattern it is itself made of. Our group's sticking point is the familiar objection: if the neural account of a decision is already causally complete, then talk of simmballs pushing looks like a convenient redescription rather than a further push. Hofstadter's counter, as I read him, is that the higher level's causality is genuine but loose and statistical — the sort that licenses saying a whirlpool or a tornado does things, without imagining a little mechanism underneath competing with the water molecules.

Please write a poem of 24 to 30 lines that stages this argument in its form. My specifications are as follows.

1. Two interleaved registers, each confined to its own short stanzas: a symbol-level register, in which a person mid-sentence decides to retract something they were about to say; and a neuron-level register, in which the very same moment is inventoried — ion fluxes, thresholds, the arrival and cancellation of activations. The two registers must describe one event, not two, and the reader should be able to work out that they are the same moment.

2. Let the registers begin as strangers to one another: the neural stanzas clipped, third-person, list-like; the symbolic stanzas fluent, first-person, narrating. Then, partway through, let their syntax begin to bleed — a symbolic line's subject governing a verb that lands in the next neural stanza, or a neural clause completing a symbolic thought — so that by the end the reader can no longer assign the pushing to either level.

3. Use a refrain of a single word (push, fire, drive, or something better) returning four times, once in each movement of the poem, and each time doing work at a different level of description, so that its fourth appearance is genuinely ambiguous between the two registers rather than belonging to either.

4. The final two lines should form one sentence split across both registers, such that neither line is grammatical on its own. This is the strange loop made literal; it must not be announced or explained within the poem.

5. Do not use the words careenium or simmball anywhere in the poem, and do not name Hofstadter, supervenience, or causal exclusion. The ideas belong in the machinery of the verse, not in the vocabulary. Someone who knows the debate should be able to point to where the exclusion objection bites and where the poem answers it; someone who does not should simply receive a good poem about changing one's mind in mid-sentence.

Please write in British English throughout.

After the poem, add a note of no more than 150 words. Say where the poem takes liberties with the ideas — in particular, whether the ending lets the symbolic register win, and whether that amounts to endorsing a contested view of downward causation rather than merely depicting one. State how confident you are that the characterisation of the position in my second paragraph is fair to Hofstadter, and single out the one claim in your note about which you are least sure.
