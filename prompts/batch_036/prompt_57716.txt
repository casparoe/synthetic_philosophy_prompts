I spent about thirty-five years doing mark-recapture work — mostly seabird colonies, a few years on freshwater mussels — and since I retired I've been reading far too much history and futurism instead of gardening. Lately I keep going around in circles on this argument about forecasting how long political orders last. On one side you have the people who say start from the base rate: look at the empirical distribution of how long empires and great powers have actually survived, treat your favourite country as one draw from that distribution, and adjust only when you have a real reason. On the other side you have people who say that's a catagory error, that states aren't organisms, that the units aren't comparable, and that the environment has genuinely moved outside its historical range. Then there's the doomsday-argument version, where your own position in the sequence is supposed to be evidence all by itself without any history at all. I can follow the words each side uses, but I can't hold the argument in my head the way I can hold a survival model, and that's frustrating me.

So here's what I want. Restate the whole dispute in the vocabulary I actually think in — hazard functions, censoring, competing risks, multi-state models, apparent versus true survival, non-stationarity, the risk set, and the choice of a reference class treated as the choice of a conditioning set — as faithfully as you can manage. Then give me the residue report, and I mean it as three separate piles: which claims come across cleanly, which ones you had to bend or pad to make them fit, and which ones have no expression whatsoever in a survival-analysis framework. Be scrupulously honest about the bending, and don't quietly assume my framework is the correct one and then congratulate yourself on how well the translation went. For concreteness, here's roughly how the two sides look to me: the base-rate people say N is small but the hazard is what it is and the optimists are just describing their own priors back to themselves; the inside-view people say the death of a state isn't an absorbing state at all (Rome didn't die so much as become something else, the Ottomans became Turkey), that the sample of empires everyone quotes is censored and selected in ways nobody admits, and that the hazard function isnt stationary because the covariates — nuclear weapons, industrial growth, bureaucratic capacity — have left the range where you have any data. My own hunch, which I want you to test rather than flatter, is that the whole thing collapses once you notice that "the base rate" isn't a determinate object until you've fixed the conditioning set, so what is being presented as a deep disagreement about how predictable history really is is actually a disagreement about which covariates you're entitled to condition on and whether conditioning on them counts as extrapolation off the support of your data. If that's right, say so plainly — and then say what is still left over after the collapse. Is anything genuinely substantive still in dispute, or is all the remaining noise bookkeeping?

I'd much rather you commit than hedge. Pick a position on the question in the last paragraph — is the outside-view move legitimate, illegitimate, or legitimate only under conditions you specify explicitly — and defend it the way you'd defend a modelling choice to a hostile reviewer, with reasons and not with a literature tour. And tell me where my own discipline is misleading me, because I have a suspicion it is: in mark-recapture you can never separate true survival from permanent emigration, so everything I ever published was apparent survival and we all knew it and lied about it politely. My guess is that exact confusion is running through the empires debate in both directions, not only in the direction I've noticed, and that both sides are arguing past each other partly because neither has decided what counts as the absorbing state. If you think the analogy cuts the other way — if survival analysis is importing assumptions about units and clocks that make the historical question worse rather than clearer — argue that instead. Either way I want a definite answer, not a menu.
