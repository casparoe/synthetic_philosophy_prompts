I am writing a seminar paper on the reasons for the commandments in medieval Jewish rationalism, and last week a classmate of mine — well trained in analytic philosophy, no yeshiva background, and not trying to be provocative — offered an argument in class that neither our professor nor I could dismiss cleanly. I have tried to write it out as carefully as I can, because in its informal version it sounds like a cheap gotcha and in its careful version it does not. Our seminar has been reading the third part of the Guide's treatment of the law, so the premises are meant to be Maimonides' own, not imported. Here is the argument, as I would defend it:

(1) In the Guide's discussion of the reasons for the commandments (Part III, roughly chapters 26 through 32), Maimonides explains the sacrificial cult by reference to a historical predicament: Israel took shape among nations for whom worship simply was sacrifice, and a people cannot be carried abruptly from a settled habit to its opposite — he likens the matter to weaning — so the Torah retained the familiar form while redirecting it exclusively to God, in order that the idolatrous practices attached to that form be abolished.

(2) On that account, the commandment to sacrifice is not an end in itself but a means ordered to a further end: the eradication of idolatrous worship and the implanting of correct opinions about God.

(3) A rule that binds only as a means to an end binds only insofar as, and only for as long as, it serves that end; once the end is achieved, or once the means ceases to serve it, the rule has lost the reason that made it a rule and with it its claim on the agent.

(4) The end has been achieved. The cults whose practice the Torah was redirecting have been extinct for many centuries, and the opinions the redirection was meant to secure — that God is one, incorporeal, and alone to be worshipped — are now the common property of most of humanity, in large part through the two religions that Maimonides himself, in his code, credits with having prepared the world for the Messiah.

(5) Therefore the sacrificial commandments no longer have the reason that made them binding; and since the same reasoning reaches every commandment he explains by reference to ancient circumstance — much of the cult's apparatus, the purity regulations, and the fixed prayers that rabbinic tradition substituted for sacrifice — the ritual law as a whole is a stage in a pedagogical process rather than a permanent structure.

(6) But Maimonides holds the Torah to be immutable; immutability is among the principles he enumerates in his commentary on the Mishnah; and the Laws of Kings in his code have the messianic king rebuilding the Temple, gathering the dispersed, and restoring the commandments as they were, sacrifices included.

(7) Hence either Maimonides is straightforwardly inconsistent — holding at once that the cult is a temporary expedient whose rationale has expired and that it will be permanently restored — or the historicist account of the reasons is not his real view but an accommodation to his readers, in which case the Guide's treatment of the law cannot be taken at face value anywhere.

I cannot accept either branch of (7). If I am forced to choose, I would rather keep (1) and (2), because the historicist reading is much of what makes Maimonides philosophically interesting, and I would rather not concede that he is writing esoterically, since once that move is allowed the whole Guide becomes unusable as evidence for anything. But I do not know where to break the chain, and I have come to suspect that I have compressed two different notions into a single word somewhere in the middle without noticing. So: tell me which step fails, or, if none of them fails, which premise is least costly to surrender; explain why that step looked unobjectionable to me when I wrote it out, since I want to understand the pull it had and not merely that it was resisted; and price the alternatives for me — what a defender of Maimonides must give up to reject (3), and what I would have to give up in order to keep (3). I would also ask that you be explicit about epistemic status as you go: distinguish what is settled among scholars of the Guide from what is genuinely contested among people who know the text well, and where the disagreement is precisely about whether the tension I have described is real, say so rather than presenting one side as the agreed reading. Finally, flag any place where your reconstruction of a Maimonidean commitment is your own interpretive gloss rather than something he plainly asserts, since my paper will be read by someone who knows the sources far better than I do.
