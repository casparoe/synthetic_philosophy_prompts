I am a junior and a member of my school's student council. This spring the council will ask the assembly to ratify a new student constitution, and Article XI of the draft is what our faculty adviser calls an entrenchment clause: it provides that the article guaranteeing the open-campus and free-expression provisions may be amended only by a two-thirds vote of the full council, followed by a majority of the student body in a referendum held no sooner than the following autumn. Two objections have come up in debate and I have not answered either of them well. The first is that a body which can always repeal its own rules has not really been bound at all, so the clause is at best a delay and at worst a fraud dressed up as principle. The second, which I find harder, is that our class has no standing to bind the classes after us: most of the students who will live under Article XI did not vote for it, and the "we" that speaks through it will be a "we" that has already graduated.

For the ratification assembly I have four minutes, and instead of a speech I would like to read a poem. Please write it.

The poem should work through the episode in which the ship's men stop their ears with wax and lash their captain upright against the mast so that he alone may hear the singing, having first ordered them that if he begs to be let go they are to bind him faster. I want the constitutional reading to be the real subject, with the ship doing the work. Specifically:

1. Between thirty and forty-two lines, free verse, no rhyme required. I will read it aloud in a gymnasium to about four hundred teenagers, so every line has to be speakable in one breath and nothing should depend on being seen on the page.

2. One line must recur, word for word and punctuation for punctuation, once in each stanza — I would like six stanzas. This recurring line is the entrenched article: it may not be revised as the poem goes on, and it does not accommodate itself to the stanza it lands in. Everything around it changes; it does not. Make it an imperative of roughly ten to fourteen syllables that could be read either as an order given on a deck or as a sentence of governing text, so the poem can move between the two registers without ever altering it.

3. The line should first appear as something a calm man says to other men while the water is still flat. By the final stanza its wording must be identical and its meaning different, and that difference — not any statement about it — should be the argument of the poem.

4. The turn belongs in the penultimate stanza, where the line appears for the first time inside quotation marks and in the mouth of someone who was not on the ship when it was given: a younger student, a later council, whoever you choose. That is the dead-hand objection arriving inside the form rather than being announced outside it.

5. The ship carries two devices and I need the poem to keep them apart, because the objection I cannot answer turns on the difference. The wax goes into the rowers' ears and changes what is able to reach them; the rope goes around the man at the mast and changes what he is able to do once he has been reached. One is a filter on what gets in, the other a lock on what gets out, and a governing text can be either kind of thing. Let the poem notice which kind Article XI actually is — a delay in what an assembly is permitted to hear and say, or the removal of an option from the table — and refuse to blur the two, since most of the students objecting have been treating them as the same objection.

6. Carry, without naming, the knot I have not solved: if the man at the mast is still the same reasoning man when the singing begins, the rope was unnecessary; if he is not, then the rope has replaced a person with a judgment, and the rowers are enforcing the decision of someone who is no longer present. On the ship this is worse than it sounds, because the rowers' ears are sealed — they never hear the pleading at all, only see the brows working and the shoulders straining against the lashings, so the standing order they obey was given to them in advance about a voice they will never be in a position to hear. Whoever holds the rope must decide, without evidence, whose voice is coming out of the bound man, and the poem should make the reader feel that the rowers' position is not comfortable obedience but a guess they cannot check.

7. The closing stanza should concede the first objection honestly — that a sovereign which can always repeal has bolted nothing, that the rope is a delay and not a lock — and then claim something for the delay itself. No triumph, no shrug, no benediction. Say what an intervening autumn and a two-thirds vote are actually for, and let that stand next to the plain fact that we are the ones who chose the length of the delay.

8. Do not use the words democracy, freedom, liberty, rights, precommitment, social contract, Ulysses contract, or constitution anywhere in the poem. No archaic diction, no "thee," no invented classical names beyond what a high school audience already carries. The deck, the wax, the rope, the eyebrow-sign, and the vote have to do all of it.

After the poem, please add three or four sentences — not an essay — that I can adapt into my spoken introduction, telling me in ordinary language what the recurring line is meant to be doing and where you intended the turn to fall, so that I can point the room at the right moment instead of hoping they find it.
