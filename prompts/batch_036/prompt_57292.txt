I write a weekly column on science and ideas for a national magazine, and for the past month I have been working on a piece about how patients are supposed to weigh a one-time, irreversible risk against a better average outcome. What began as reporting has turned into an argument with my own family, and I would like an outsider's judgment on the reasoning rather than on the medicine. My brother is thirty-four and carries an inherited neuromuscular variant that has left him, over six years, at a stable but visibly limited level of function — call it a three on a five-point scale, where five is what he had before symptoms and one is wheelchair-bound with round-the-clock care. His team has offered him two paths. The established drug will, with near certainty, hold him at three for the rest of his life: no improvement, no further decline, no surprises. The experimental infusion produced near-normal function in 84 percent of trial participants, held 15 percent roughly where they started, and triggered an acute immune reaction that left 1 percent permanently at one. On any arithmetic I can perform, the second path is far better on average, and the health economist I interviewed for the column insisted there is nothing to puzzle over: a rational patient simply discounts each outcome by what an extra unit of function is worth to him once he already has that much, and the numbers do the rest. My brother has refused the infusion, and I cannot make his refusal look like a calculation.

What makes it hard to write him off as merely squeamish is that he is not a cautious man. At twenty-nine he left a secure engineering post to start a firm he gave himself a one-in-five chance of failing, and he skis off-piste without a second thought. His own explanation is that a firm and a season of skiing are things he could have lost and rebuilt, whereas, in his words, this is the only copy of his life and he will not hand the whole of it over for a better average. Two further things he says strike me as making the situation worse rather than better. First, he told me that even if the trial's catastrophe rate had come in at one in a thousand rather than one in a hundred, he would still refuse — but that if the manufacturer were choosing for a pool of a hundred patients like him, they ought to give it to all of them. Second, his daughter has now tested positive for the same variant; she cannot be treated for years, and he says without hesitation that he will choose the infusion for her, though he cannot tell me what about her case is different.

I am not after medical advice, and I would rather not be told that people are entitled to their own risk attitudes, which is what everyone says when they have nothing better to offer. I want an account of whether any principle makes his pattern hang together. Please answer in exactly five numbered points, two or three sentences each, with no introduction and no closing summary: first, whether his refusal can be explained by how much he values each level of function at all, and if it cannot, what else must be doing the work; second, which general requirement of consistency he is surrendering, and whether the startup and the ski slopes commit him to precisely what he says he rejects; third, whether his verdict about the hundred strangers contradicts his verdict about himself, or exposes a real difference between the two situations; fourth, whether the decision for his daughter is a different decision or the same one in disguise; and fifth, the single question I could put to him that would reveal whether he has a view or only a mood.
