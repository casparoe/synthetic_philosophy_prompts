I spent thirty-one years running the dispatch office of a regional haulage firm. I left school at sixteen, have no academic training, and have never taken a philosophy course, so what follows is not scholarship. It is an argument I have been turning over for four years, since reading a popular account of split-brain surgery and finding that I could not let it go. I have written it up as a short essay, and I should like your comments on it. The specific questions I want answered are listed at the end.

Two things before you begin. First, please be blunt: I would much rather be told the essay fails than be encouraged. Second, I have no access to a university library and cannot obtain journal articles, so please do not answer with a reading list or with references I cannot chase. I want your judgment of the argument as it stands on the page.

**One Firm, Two Dispatchers: on how many minds a split-brain patient has**

**1. The facts as I understand them.**

In some cases of severe, drug-resistant epilepsy, surgeons divide the corpus callosum, the broad band of fibres joining the two halves of the brain, so that seizures cannot spread from one side to the other. I know the cut is sometimes only partial and that other connections of various kinds survive; for the sake of the argument I want the cleanest case, in which the two hemispheres have been given no channel at all by which to exchange information.

Because vision is cross-wired, a picture shown only to the left half of the visual field arrives in the right hemisphere, which in most people has very little speech. Asked what he has just been shown, the patient reports that nothing appeared. Yet his left hand, which the right hemisphere controls, can reach out and pick the object from a row of alternatives. Occasionally the two hands work against one another: one buttons a shirt the other is unbuttoning, or one pulls a trouser leg up while the other pulls it down.

What strikes me most, and what the popular accounts tend to bury under the drama, is that this open conflict is rare. Outside the laboratory these people hold jobs, drive, joke, and appear to be — and unhesitatingly take themselves to be — ordinary single persons.

**2. Two different properties that both get called "unity."**

I believe the word hides two things.

Call the first *pool unity*: information arising anywhere in the system can be brought to bear on any decision the system makes. There is one common pool to draw from.

Call the second *claimant unity*: there is exactly one thing that owns the whole lot — one seat of "this is me, this is mine, this is what I am doing" — and it owns even those movements that conflict with one another.

In an ordinary person the two travel together, and so neither is noticed. My central suggestion is that the split-brain patient pulls them apart. Pool unity is badly damaged. Claimant unity is not.

**3. The dispatch office.**

Some years ago we put a partition down the middle of my dispatch room to reduce noise. The two dispatchers could not see each other's boards and could not speak to one another. On the first morning one sent a lorry north with a load, and the other, working from a board he could not check, sent the same lorry south. The driver rang in, understandably furious.

Was that one firm or two? Obviously one firm, badly organised. What would have made it two firms was not poor communication but separate ledgers: separate accounts, separate debts, separate names on the door, each side bearing its own consequences and keeping its own record. Two dispatchers who cannot speak to one another, but who draw on the same account, answer to the same owner and write into the same book, are one firm with a wall in it.

A split-brain patient is that case. There is one body, one ledger of consequences, one reputation, one set of memories carried forward into tomorrow, one person who must explain to his wife what his hands have between them done.

Those who say "two minds" are, I think, measuring the wall and inferring the number of firms. But the number of firms was never settled by how well the dispatchers talk.

**4. Why the counting question cannot be settled by bandwidth.**

Suppose we relax the partition: the two dispatchers may exchange one written bulletin at midnight, summarising the day. Pool unity has now come in degrees — the channel can be widened or narrowed, delayed, made lower in resolution. But claimant unity does not seem to admit of degrees. Either one firm owns the day's decisions or it does not; nobody has seven-tenths of an owner.

If one of these properties comes in degrees and the other does not, they cannot be the same property. And if they are not the same property, then no measurement of the channel — however precise, however neurological — can tell you how many subjects there are. The counting question has to be answered by something other than bandwidth.

**5. An objection I have tried to meet.**

It will be said that the patient's confident "I" is cheap evidence. Normally only one hemisphere speaks, and that hemisphere is a tireless explainer. In one well-known demonstration, a chicken's foot is shown to one hemisphere and a snow scene to the other; asked to indicate related items from a larger array, the two hands each select what belongs to their own picture — a chicken and a shovel — and then, asked why the shovel, the speaking side produces a reason that ties the two together, something to the effect that you need a shovel to clean out the chicken shed. It has confabulated, and it did not notice itself doing it. If the talking half invents connections that fluently, perhaps its claim to own everything the silent hand does is more of the same: an impression of unity manufactured by a narrator, not unity.

My reply has two parts. First, the claim is not only spoken. The silent hand's own conduct makes claims too — it reaches, refuses, points — and in those rare conflicts the person corrects himself, is embarrassed, apologises. Second, and more important, ownership is not chiefly a matter of how things seem to the owner. It is a matter of where the consequences land. The patient, and not his right hemisphere, is the one who is embarrassed, who must answer for the behaviour, who is sued, and who remembers the whole business tomorrow. If claimant unity is constituted by where consequences land and by the single record from which a life is carried forward, then a narrator's say-so is neither necessary nor sufficient for it, and the objection passes straight through my position.

**6. Conclusion.**

The split-brain patient is one subject with a divided mind, not two subjects sharing a body. The verdict "two minds" is reached only by quietly adopting pool unity as the criterion — and pool unity is not what anyone actually cares about when they ask whether a person is one. What they care about, when they ask whether someone is one person, is responsibility, survival, being somebody rather than nobody. That is claimant unity. It may be that the entire controversy is a dispute over which property the phrase names. But if that is so, it is worth saying plainly, because it makes the experimental results beside the point: no amount of clever testing can tell you which property a word was standing for.

**What I should like you to tell me**

1. Does the distinction survive contact, or does claimant unity collapse into pool unity as soon as you press on it? In particular: what is doing the work in section 3 may be nothing more than the fact that there is one body. If so, say that plainly, and tell me whether a bodily criterion is something I can consistently adopt or whether it wrecks the rest of the essay.

2. Section 4 argues from "one property comes in degrees, the other does not" to "they are not the same property." Is that step sound, or have I assumed the thing I wanted to prove? Consider the possibility that ownership itself comes in degrees — divided loyalty, weakness of will, self-alienation — and tell me what is left of my argument if you are right.

3. Go through section 1 and mark anything I have overstated or simply got wrong. I am working from popular accounts and I know that. I would especially like to know whether my "cleanest case" is a real kind of patient or an idealisation that does not exist, and whether the rarity of hand-versus-hand conflict supports my position or quietly undermines it.

4. Section 5 contains what I suspect is the weakest joint in the essay. Does my second reply — ownership is where the consequences land — actually answer the confabulation worry, or does it change the subject? If there is a sharper version of that objection which I have not met, please state it.

5. Does the conclusion slide between three different claims: that there is one subject of experience, one agent, and one bearer of praise and blame? If it does, tell me which of the three my argument actually establishes, and whether the other two are entitled to come along.

6. Try to construct the strongest counterexample to my criterion: a case in which consequences land in one place and one continuous record carries everything forward, and yet you would insist there are two subjects rather than one. If you cannot construct one, say so, because that would suggest my criterion is empty rather than correct, and I would rather know it.

7. Finally, two general questions. If you think I have reinvented a distinction philosophers already draw, describe that distinction to me in your own words and say where mine departs from it and whether the departure matters — describe it, do not cite it. And tell me whether the central claim is worth pressing at all, or whether I have drawn a distinction without a difference.

Please answer the seven points in order, and end with the three moves you think are weakest, ranked, together with your view of whether the thesis can be repaired or should be abandoned.
