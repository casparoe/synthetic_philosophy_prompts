I'm a backend dev with zero philosophy coursework, and our tiny book club picked "Molyneux's problem" for this month, so somebody pointed me at an old free online copy of Locke's Essay Concerning Human Understanding and I've been slogging through book 2, chapter 9 ("Of Perception"). I got stuck on the paragraph headed "Sensations often changed by the judgment," and specifically on this bit:

"When we set before our eyes a round globe of any uniform colour, v.g. gold, alabaster, or jet, it is certain that the idea thereby imprinted on our mind is of a flat circle, variously shadowed, with several degrees of light and brightness coming to our eyes. But we having, by use, been accustomed to perceive what kind of appearance convex bodies are wont to make in us; what alterations are made in the reflections of light by the difference of the sensible figures of bodies; the judgment presently, by an habitual custom, alters the appearances into their causes."

Two things I can't get my head around. First, what exactly is the claim about the flat circle? Is he saying the raw visual input literally is a 2D shaded disc, so that what I'd naively call "seeing a sphere" is never a seeing at all but an inference — and when the judgment "alters the appearances into their causes," does the flat-circle idea get replaced, or kept and tagged, or what? Second, the famous question Molyneux sent him turns up in this same section as far as I can tell: roughly, whether a man born blind who can reliably tell a cube from a globe by touch, if he were suddenly made to see both sitting on a table, could tell which is which by sight alone before touching them. Given the machinery in the quoted paragraph, what does Locke's own theory predict for that guy? My read is that he has the shaded-disc data but no accumulated "habitual custom," so he's just stuck — but then I don't see why it was worth agonizing over, since the theory seems to hand him the answer for free. Unless the real question is whether the cube-idea he got from touch and the cube-idea he'd get from sight are the same idea at all, in which case I'd like to know what would settle that.

The part I most want checked, tho: I've been explaining this to coworkers as an ML story — vision is a model trained on decades of labeled images, the newly sighted man is an untrained network, so Molyneux is really asking whether weights learned in the tactile modality transfer to the visual one. Does that analogy actually track what Locke is doing here, or does it smuggle in stuff he'd reject, like the idea that the "judgment" is a step the mind performs rather than something that just happens? He says the alteration occurs "without our taking notice of it" and "presently," and I suspect those words are doing work my analogy flattens out. Also minor stuff: why does he keep qualifying with "in grown people" — does he think infants see nothing but flat shaded patches, and if so does that make the blind man's situation less special rather than more? And is Locke here endorsing Molyneux's answer or just reporting it? I couldn't tell from the passage and I don't want to argue about it at the meetup on a guess.
