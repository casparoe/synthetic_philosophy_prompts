I cover science and ideas for a national magazine, and I'm reporting a piece on whether habitat loss might, perversely, be good news for animal welfare. On a press call about a proposal to drain a coastal wetland, the agency's ecologist waved off an animal-welfare group's objection by saying the works would not upset the balance of nature, then added that the balance is no kinder to the creatures the group wants to protect than a bulldozer would be. Within a few minutes both sides had used the same four words to mean opposite things, and now my piece has to say which, if either, is entitled to them.

Could you lay out, as a numbered menu I can work from, the distinct claims that the balance of nature might be making? I want the ladder to run from the modest claim about what actually keeps wild populations in check, through the stronger claims about stability, self-correction after disturbance, and some kind of optimality or tuned efficiency, up to whatever normative claim may be smuggled in at the top. For each rung, give me the claim in precise form and say whether it is a platitude, a live and defensible empirical thesis, something ecologists have largely abandoned, or simply false. Then tell me which of these readings, if any, says anything at all about whether the animals in a habitat are on net better or worse off, and which reading each of my two sources would need for their sentence to come out true. I am most interested in a question I can't yet answer myself: whether the most defensible version of the slogan is neutral on welfare, or whether it quietly does more work for the welfare group than for the agency that reached for it.
