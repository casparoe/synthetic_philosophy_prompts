I'm the secretary of a small community land trust — volunteer board, forty-something member households, mostly people who are not going to read legal philosophy, which is why I'm asking you instead of them. Our bylaws say any amendment needs two-thirds of the board. Two years back we added a clause saying the affordability provision (the bit that keeps our houses permanently below market) can only be changed by a four-fifths vote of the full membership rather than the board. Everyone thought that was sensible and unremarkable. Last month a two-thirds majority of the board voted to delete the four-fifths clause, and then, at the same meeting, voted to loosen the affordability provision itself. Both sides think they are obviously right. The two-thirds people say: the amending power is two-thirds, it has always been two-thirds, nothing in the document says the entrenchment clause is itself entrenched, so it went out the door like any other clause. The four-fifths people say: on that reading entrenchment is meaningless, since any entrenchment is exactly one ordinary vote away from not being an entrenchment, and the whole point was that a board majority should not be able to unwind it later. And here's the part that's actually making me crazy: our bylaws say procedural disputes get settled by the board chair, and it was the chair who ruled the motion in order and put it to a vote. So the thing that's supposed to settle the dispute is itself the disputed thing.

When I went looking for whether this shape has a name I found a pile of adjacent stuff but nothing that looked like a direct hit. There's entrenchment and "manner and form" in the Parliament Act world (my understanding is the 1949 Act got made using the procedure set up by the 1911 one, and people have been arguing about whether that was legitimate ever since, including in the litigation over the hunting ban); there's the part of the German Basic Law that just declares certain things flatly unamendable; there's the Senate nuclear option, where a bare majority changed what a standing rule meant by appealing a ruling of the chair; and I stumbled onto something called a paradox of self-amendment, plus Hart's rule of recognition and Kelsen's basic norm, which seem to be about whether a rule can validly change the rule that authorizes it. But my thing feels narrower and less metaphysical than all of that. Nobody at our meeting is denying the amendment was procedurally well formed. The situation is that there are now two candidate rule-books, each produced by a chain of steps that was valid under the immediately preceding rule-book, both chains running back to the same original text, and no procedure inside either one picks a winner — the tie only ever gets broken by something outside the rules, like a court whose own authority comes from the contested text, or custom, or whoever is still sitting in the room. And then the second instance, where the referee's authority is the very thing in question, feels related but maybe not the same.

So does this have an established name, and if it does, is it one name or two? Please do the carving rather than the labelling. Tell me which of the neighbours above is closest and exactly how my case differs from each — in particular whether it's just a constitutional crisis (which sounds to me like a mood rather than a structure), whether Hart's rule of recognition shifting under our feet is the same phenomenon or a diagnosis of it, and whether any of this is what people mean by the Gödel-in-the-constitution story, which I suspect is really about a system being able to authorize its own destruction rather than about two live rule-books coexisting. Keep it seperate from plain vagueness too: our text isn't ambiguous, everyone agrees what the words say, they disagree about which book is currently in force. And keep it seperate from the can-God-make-a-rock-He-can't-lift business, since ours isn't about logical possibility at all. If there's no real term for it, coin one, define it, and give me the boundary conditions — what has to be true for a situation to count, plus two near-misses that don't (does a rule that's genuinely ambiguous but settled by a long interpretive practice count? does a coup count?). Last thing: I'd much rather you commit than hedge. Pick the single best fit or your own coinage and argue for it, and tell me plainly how confident you are. And if you're not sure that some legal philosopher you mention actually said the thing you're about to attribute to them, mark it as a guess instead of dressing it up — I don't have library access and I can't check you.
