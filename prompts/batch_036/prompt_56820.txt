I convene a small interdisciplinary reading group on the philosophy of mathematics — eight or nine regulars, a mixture of two set theorists, one topologist, and several philosophers whose formal training stops at first-order logic — and a session we held last term on the axiom of choice produced an argument that has still not been settled. The illustration at the centre of it is the one Bertrand Russell made familiar: from infinitely many pairs of shoes you can specify a selection without any fuss, since you simply take the left shoe of each pair, whereas from infinitely many pairs of socks that differ in no relevant respect there is no rule that singles one sock out of each pair, and something further seems to be required. One member, a philosopher, deployed the illustration to argue that the axiom is entirely unobjectionable: it merely generalises a procedure we already accept, the shoe case shows what a choice function amounts to when a rule is available, and the sock case shows only that the rule is sometimes missing — so resistance to the axiom is squeamishness about infinite lists. Another member, constructivist by inclination, used precisely the same illustration in the opposite direction: in the sock case we are assured that a selection exists which could never be exhibited, written down, or checked, so the axiom is not mathematics at all but a piece of metaphysics about an extravagantly populated universe of sets.

I should like a careful audit of the illustration rather than a summary of either position. Please set out the correspondence explicitly — a table is welcome — identifying what in the socks-and-shoes story plays the role of the index set, of the indexed family, of the choice function, and of that rule whose presence or absence is doing all the work; then separate the disanalogies into those that genuinely bear on the two uses described above and those that are merely colourful. I would like particular pressure applied to the following. Whether the observation that no rule picks out a sock is being allowed to slide into the observation that no selection exists, and what everything in the debate turns on that slide. Whether an illustration that is naturally about a countable family of pairs says anything whatever about the situations in which the axiom's full strength is felt, and whether the two members are simply arguing about different axioms. Whether the sock case is repaired by noting that a well-ordering of the whole pile would collapse it back into the shoe case — and if so, whether the illustration then presupposes the very principle it was offered to motivate. And whether the picture of choosing as a succession of acts, one pair after another, quietly imports a finitist anxiety about completing an infinite task that the axiom, read as a single existential claim about sets, does not in fact incur.

Finally, tell me whose conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table — the philosopher's, the constructivist's, neither, or some third position the illustration obscures — and propose a replacement if you think one is needed. Our topologist suggested recasting the matter in terms of sections: a choice function is a section of the projection from the disjoint union of the family onto the index set, the shoe case is a projection admitting a canonical section, the sock case one that does not, and, she added, the squaring map on the punctured complex plane shows that sometimes even a continuous section fails to exist. Say whether that recasting improves on Russell's socks or merely relocates the difficulty, and be explicit about what it smuggles in of its own; I suspect the continuity requirement is doing covert work here, since nothing in the axiom of choice asks that the selection vary continuously with the index. I am not after references, a bibliography, or a reading list — only the reasoning, and a clear verdict on how far each analogy can carry the weight placed on it.
