I am counsel to the Board of Electors of a port city of roughly 400,000 — a standing five-member body that keeps the municipal register of electors and rules on petitions to be added to it. Two years ago the city council extended the municipal franchise to sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds and to residents without national citizenship who have lived here three years. Petitions have arrived at eight to twelve a year ever since, and this cycle's docket is harder than the last: an association of commuters who live in surrounding towns, work in the city, pay its earnings tax, and are subject to its traffic, transit, and policing rules but cast no ballot in it; a business federation asking that member firms be entered on the register with votes apportioned by headcount of employees, on the ground that firms bear the city's licensing, zoning, and business-rate regime; and former residents and their descendants displaced in the 1960s when a highway corridor was cut through their neighborhood, asking for a vote in the authority that now controls the redevelopment land.

The constraints on any procedure you write me are these. The commissioners are laypeople — a retired head teacher, a small-business owner, two lawyers, a union official — and none has training in political theory. Each petition gets a twenty-minute slot; published plain-language reasons must issue within thirty days; disappointed petitioners appeal routinely, so the reasons have to survive being read cold by a judge. The board may add a category to the register where the enabling statute already permits it, deny, or refer to the council a remedy it cannot create itself. It may not amend the statute, and it may not invent offices. Every step of the procedure has to end in something a commissioner can vote yes or no on, and there must be a recorded form of dissent.

The substantive problem is that the board keeps walking into what I understand is called the boundary problem: the composition of the electorate cannot be settled by a vote of that electorate without assuming the answer, so whatever rule we apply has to be justified from outside the democratic procedure it authorizes. The two principles we hear most often are both unusable as they stand. "Everyone affected should have a say" sweeps in commuters, downstream towns, unborn residents, and corporations, and says nothing about weight. "Everyone subject to the city's coercive rules should have a say" is narrower, but threatens to enfranchise anyone a police officer can stop. In practice the board has been reaching for a third test, and I want it tested rather than assumed: a claimant belongs on the register only if the city can make that claimant worse off by rules the claimant had no part in making, and the claimant lacks any exit, avoidance, or counter-pressure route comparable to what people already on the register have. Adopt that test, sharpen it, or reject it and replace it — but tell me which you have done, and show me where the difference lands on this docket.

What I need is a written decision procedure the board can adopt as a standing order and run without theory: the questions asked in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; a fixed menu of outcomes, which must include something better than a bare no — at minimum a status under which a group's interests are counted in the reasoning and must be answered in the published reasons without anyone getting a ballot, and a status short of full franchise such as a reserved consultative seat or a suspensive veto; a rule for partial or issue-bound franchise, since a commuter may have a claim on transit and policing and none on schools (the board is aware that United States courts have tolerated landowner-only, valuation-weighted voting in special-purpose districts precisely because the district's remit was narrow and its operations bore disproportionately on landowners, and wants to know whether that logic is available to a general-purpose city); a default rule that terminates the inquiry when the questions come out even rather than letting a petition stall; a re-opening trigger stating when a granted status is revisited, so that today's inclusion does not become unarguable tomorrow; explicit marking of every step at which the board is in fact fixing the boundaries of its own authority and cannot appeal to the electorate for cover; and explicit marking of the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on all three petitions, in docket order. I expect it to hand down at least one result the commissioners will dislike — one refusal they will find harsh and one grant that runs against their instincts — and if it does not, that is a sign the procedure is tracking their preferences rather than their reasons. For each petition give the outcome from your menu, the two or three sentences that would actually appear in the published reasons, the strongest objection from the losing side, and the reply the board should make.

Close with what the procedure cannot do: the classes of petition it will mishandle, the petitions it will decide correctly for reasons that will not bear being said aloud in public, and the places where it relocates the boundary problem instead of dissolving it. No citations, no reading lists, no bibliography; name a theorist only where the name would change what a commissioner does on the day. Plain language throughout, since the text will be published. The whole thing should fit in about two pages of standing order plus the three applications.

Begin with the procedure itself, with no preface, no restating of this brief, and no scene-setting about the boundary problem; make its first line the one-sentence operative test that the remaining steps implement.
