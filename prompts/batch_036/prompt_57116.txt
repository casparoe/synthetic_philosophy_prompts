ok so I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background whatsoever, and I'm stuck on something at work that turned into a weird ethics argument in a design review and I want to hear it argued by people who are actually good at it.

The setup. We run a messaging app. When a user dies, the family currently gets a menu of options. Option A: we keep everything — messages, photos, the whole archive — in an append-only store that we can't legally alter after the fact, and we offer a replica of the person (a chatbot fine-tuned on their data) that reads out of that archive when it answers. Option B, which a new regulation is pushing us toward and which half my team genuinely prefers: the replica is only available if the family agrees that after 30 days we cryptographically destroy the source archive. Messages, photos, backups, index logs, all of it. The replica survives because it's weights, not records. So option B amounts to: you get a convincing living memory of the person, purchased by erasing the only evidence in our systems that they existed.

Three things make it messier than it sounds, and I want them engaged with, not waved at:

- the replica interpolates. It will happily produce sentences the dead person never wrote, in their register, and families keep telling us "that's exactly something she'd have said." Fidelity isn't something we can guarantee or even measure.
- under option B the data is gone from storage but it already shaped the weights, and those weights get folded into a shared model later. So there's a residue of the person that nobody can point at, query, or delete on request.
- under option A almost nobody actually reads the archive. The replica hits it millions of times a day; humans basically never do. We have a complete record functioning as a lookup table.

Write me a dialogue between someone who defends the duty to remember along Avishai Margalit's lines and someone who defends deliberate, institutionalized forgetting along David Rieff's lines, both sitting in on our review, arguing about which option we should make the default. Rules:

- both of them competent, no strawmen. They should attack each other's actual moves rather than give parallel speeches.
- they have to fight about the specific weird parts above. Especially: does the option B replica count as remembering anything at all, given that nothing it produces can be checked against a record? And does the case for forgetting, which as far as I understand it is about societies after mass violence and about official memory, even reach a private family's decision about one person's chat log? I want each of them pressed hard on the limits of their own view there.
- at least one real concession from each side, and at least one moment where someone deploys a fact about how the system actually works that the other hadn't priced in.
- plain language on the philosophy, I'm not trained, but don't soften the argument to make it easier on me. Don't bother naming books or papers, I'll dig up the reading myself.
- somewhere around 1500 words. It doesn't have to resolve, but I do not want it ending on a summary — end on something I hadn't thought of before I wrote this.
