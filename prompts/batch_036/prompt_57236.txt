I am drafting a short essay for a reading group in my department on the philosophy of technology; the session's theme is delegated cognition, and participants have been asked to bring something deliberately rough so that it can be attacked productively rather than admired. My background is in epistemology, but two of the people in the room are machine learning engineers, and one of them has already told me he expects the piece to amount to a re-run of the old panic about calculators, spell-checkers, and satellite navigation. I would like comments on the draft below with that audience in mind: I care far more about whether the argument survives contact with a sceptic than about whether it reads attractively.

The draft, in full:

Worries about thinking machines doing our thinking for us usually take the form of a skill-atrophy story. The calculator made us worse at arithmetic, the map application made us worse at navigation, the search engine made us worse at remembering, and the assistant that composes our prose will make us worse at composing prose. On this picture the loss is a loss of performance, measured against a baseline of capacity the person once demonstrably had, and it can in principle be detected by testing the person with the tool taken away.

That picture is inadequate, because the most important thing we get from an explanation is not the answer. I want to distinguish three ways in which a person can emerge from an exchange with a knowledgeable system worse off than they went in.

Substitution: the system performed the operation the person would otherwise have performed, so the corresponding capacity was never built. This is the calculator case. It is real, it is bounded, and it is measurable.

Fluency illusion: the person follows a clear, well-ordered explanation and mistakes the ease of following it for the possession of it. The system has not done the work for them; it has done the work in front of them, and the difference has been misrecorded in the person's own estimate of their grasp.

Question-impoverishment: the person loses the ability to generate the questions whose answers would reveal what they do not know. On my account understanding is not a store of propositions but a disposition to press on them — to ask why this rather than that, what follows if a premise is withdrawn, where the account goes silent. A system that supplies well-formed answers to well-formed questions also, quietly, supplies the space of questions: it anticipates the obvious follow-ups, flags the standard confusions, and closes gaps before the learner has noticed them as gaps. After enough of this, the learner can still recognise a good question and can still answer questions put to them, but no longer produces the questions that would have exposed the holes.

The third failure is the one that matters, and it is the one the standard remedies cannot reach. The usual advice is to use these systems actively rather than passively: interrogate them, ask better questions, demand the derivation rather than the result. But if what has eroded is precisely the capacity to interrogate and to ask better questions, that advice assumes the thing it is supposed to restore. The same defect infects the obvious way of measuring the problem, since any assessment that relies on users reporting their own understanding is relying on people who cannot say which questions they should have asked.

I do not want to overstate this into nostalgia. The anxiety that external aids will ruin our memories is very old: in one of the dialogues Socrates tells a story about the invention of writing and warns that learners who depend on written marks will carry a show of wisdom without the thing itself, and comparable complaints attended print, and later the arithmetic machines of the last century. Nor do I think question-impoverishment is inevitable. It depends on the design of the interaction. A system that answers only after the user has committed to a guess, or that returns a deliberately incomplete account and requires the user to say what is missing, pushes in the opposite direction.

What I offer, therefore, is a diagnostic rather than a lament. Call it the counterfactual-question test. Having received an explanation, the user closes the system and writes down three questions they would have asked had the explanation been wrong: which premises the result depends on, what evidence would discriminate it from a nearby alternative, and where it would break. The test does not measure retention; it measures whether the user can still generate pressure on the account. My conjecture is that scores on this test decline with sustained use of an explanation-giving system even when retention scores do not, and that the divergence is the signature of the third failure.

End of draft.
