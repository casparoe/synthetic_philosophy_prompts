I'm teaching a first-year grad methodology seminar — 22 people, mostly sociology and poli sci with three philosophers mixed in — and next week's 80-minute slot is social ontology. I want an in-class game, not a lecture, that makes them feel the gap between two claims they're going to walk in treating as one claim: (a) everything social is nothing over and above what individuals do and think, and (b) therefore you can always explain a social outcome by citing individual-level facts. Basically everyone in the room already concedes (a) and thinks that settles the whole debate, and I want conceding (a) to stop feeling like a win. Something in the Kincaid-style ballpark, where the supervenience claim is true and does no explanatory work, but don't make them read anything beforehand.

Here's the skeleton I've got. Keep the bones, but I'm sure it's got problems — fix them and tell me what you changed.

Everyone gets an index card each round and secretly writes an integer effort bid, 0–10. Effort costs 1 point per unit. There's a bowl with 12 tokens in it; the 12 highest bids each take one, ties broken by me drawing lots. A token is worth 5 points, and points are the only payoff. Round 1 everyone is playing for their own points. Round 2 I quietly hand a third of the table a slip saying their payoff is now their three-person team's total. Round 3 another third get a slip saying they should bid one more than whatever they think the median bid will be. Round 4 is a policy round where I announce effort now costs half as much, or twice as much. Round 5 I drop four extra tokens in the bowl without saying why. After every round each player writes one sentence answering "why did total effort come out at that number," I collect them and read a bunch aloud before putting the aggregate on the board.

What I'm after: total tokens claimed is 12 every single time under three completely different psychologies and two different cost regimes, and total wasted effort tracks the number of slots rather than anybody's dispositions. And when they read their own explanation sentences aloud, nobody's sentence mentions the number 12.

The holes I can't figure out. Is 12 slots for 22 players the right ratio or should it be meaner? Should bids be revealed after each round, and if so does that wreck the round-3 imitator condition? Is the slip-passing for teams and imitators even runnable with index cards in the time, or do I need a cheaper way to switch psychologies without breaking the flow? And somebody is definitely going to say the outcome was rigged by the token count so the game is stacked in the holist's favour from the start — build in a beat that either earns that objection or disarms it, because right now I've got nothing.

Give me the full rules, a materials list (index cards, poker chips, whiteboard, no computers), a per-round timeline that honestly fits 80 minutes including the debrief, and the debrief questions in the order I should ask them. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. In particular: what do I do if a sharp student reverse-engineers the structure in round 2 and starts organising the table to hold everyone's effort down? My instinct says that's the best thing that could happen and I have no idea how to steer it. And finish with a straight answer about which piece of the holist position this game cannot reproduce and I'll just have to say out loud myself.

Don't use the phrases methodological individualism, holism, or multiple realizability until the very last beat. Also — they've all seen Schelling's checkerboard segregation model, so if you want an emergence-from-micro demo it has to do something that one doesn't. The point isn't that order comes out of individual choices, it's that the same order comes out of wildly different psychologies and that tinkering with the psychologies doesn't move it.
