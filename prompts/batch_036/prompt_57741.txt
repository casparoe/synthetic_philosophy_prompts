On the evening of Friday the fourteenth, a mutual-credit cooperative will formally open for business in the assembly room of a town whose last mill closed in 1998, and I have been asked — rather improbably, given that I earn my living writing about eighteenth-century Irish economic thought — to deliver the keynote address before the first credits are issued. Please write that address: approximately 1,200 words, eight minutes spoken, in the first person, ready to be read aloud.

The room will hold about 150 people. The majority are the founding members: a plumber, two bakers, a childminder, a haulier, a woman who runs the community kitchen, and a contingent of retired mill workers who remember the last time a scheme like this was proposed and failed. Two town councillors who voted against the pilot will be sitting in the second row. Three economists from the regional university who study complementary currencies will be there and will notice anything sloppy. A reporter from the county paper has already characterised the credits in print as play money, and will be taking notes. The chair of the cooperative, who recruited me, has warned that she will physically intervene if the speech turns into a seminar.

The address must be argued entirely from inside Berkeley's monetary theory, and the theory must do structural work rather than supply colour. Specifically:

1. The speech may never concede — not even as a rhetorical set-up, and not even for one clause — that the credits are worth anything because of what stands behind them. There is no reserve, no backing, no commodity, and the address should take the question "but what is it backed by?" head-on and dissolve it, using Berkeley's insistence that money is not wealth but a counter or ticket whose entire purpose is to be handed on and to set people working, and his claim that a fertile land and the industry of its inhabitants are inexhaustible funds of real wealth whatever the counters conveying it happen to be made of. Do not quote Berkeley aloud anywhere in the speech; name him at most once, in the first two minutes, and thereafter let the argument carry the attribution on its own.

2. Use Berkeley's account of signs — the point he presses in Alciphron that we can reason with tokens and act on them without having any clear idea, or any resembling image, of what they stand for — as the hinge of the middle section. This is where the speech should stop being an economics defence and become something the members recognise: a token does not need to look like the thing it stands for in order to bind people to one another.

3. Confront the fact that Berkeley lobbied the Irish parliament for a national bank on exactly these principles and got nowhere. Use the failure; do not mourn it.

4. Handle the genuine gap between his scheme and ours. Berkeley lodged the power to issue tokens, and to limit the quantity issued, in a public institution answerable to a legislature. The cooperative has no such institution, and the address must say plainly who in that room supplies the authority instead, and why keeping the volume of credits proportioned to the trade they are meant to carry is the members' job rather than an abstraction. This should be an argument, not an exhortation.

5. Defend the cooperative's carrying-cost rule, under which a balance held idle for a full year loses a small percentage of its face value, on Berkeleyan grounds about money that never moves. Then concede the real cost of the rule: a token that slowly decays cannot function as a store of value for the member saving toward something large, and the speech must acknowledge that to the retired mill workers without softening it or resolving it with a slogan.

6. Ban the jargon: no "fiat," no "endogenous money," no "nominalism," no "velocity," no "monetary sovereignty." Give me two genuine moments of humour, warm rather than clever, and one passage in which the reporter's objection is answered so that he can accept the answer without losing his scepticism or his face.

Close on something the members can literally do on Monday morning, in one or two sentences, and stop there.
