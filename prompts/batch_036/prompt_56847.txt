I am in the third year of a doctorate on Wittgenstein's late epistemology, and our reading group is closing its year-long run through On Certainty with an informal supper at which each of us has been asked to bring something: a puzzle, a disputed translation, a passage nobody has managed to make sense of. I have rather presumptuously volunteered to bring a poem. There will be six or seven of us around the table, all of whom know the text closely and several of whom have spent the past year telling me that I read it badly, so anything that merely name-drops hinges, doors, or G. E. Moore's hands will be received in silence. I should also confess my own heresy, because it is what I would like the poem to test: I have been arguing that hinges are better thought of as verbs than as propositions, as things one does rather than things one assents to, so that Moore's demonstration is not a claim at all but a move, a way of taking up a tool. If the poem can make that flicker into plausibility without ever stating it, I shall consider the supper a success.

What I want, formally, is a sestina of thirty-nine lines — six six-line stanzas and a three-line envoi — in unrhymed lines of roughly ten or eleven syllables, with the end-words rotating in the standard pattern (each stanza taking the previous stanza's last end-word first, then its first, and so on round). The reason for choosing so unforgiving a form is that the rotation should serve as the structural analogue of the hinge: the rule is never announced, never justified, never once interrogated inside the poem, and yet it is what makes every stanza possible. The same six words keep returning in new positions, doing new work, and no line ever steps outside the pattern to check whether the pattern holds. Please choose end-words that are concrete and that can bear more than one sense — a hand, a name, a floor, a key, a word like "still" or "here" — since I want each of them to be readable across the stanzas either as a thing standing fast or as something being done, with the reading shifting quietly as the syntax shifts around it. None of the six should be an abstraction, and none should be a word one could naturally put after "it is certain that".

The content needs to be right in the ways this particular table would notice. Hinges must not be made to sound like the self-evident axioms at the base of a foundationalist pyramid: the whole interest of the view is that these things are held fast by everything around them rather than holding everything up from below, that they are not known and not justified, and that calling them certain is already a misuse of the word. Doubt should appear in the poem as a practice that can only be carried on against a background that is not doubted, and the sceptic should not be made into a fool — his trouble is that his doubt is idle, not that it is false. Where justification runs out, it should run out into a way of going on, into something performed rather than something seen. And the hinges should come across as communal and historical, inherited and losable, belonging to a whole way of acting together rather than to a private mind. The envoi, finally, is where I want the riverbed: in those three lines the bed itself should be shown to move, so that what has stood fast for five stanzas turns out to have been shifting all along, while the water keeps running in what looks from the bank like the same direction. One firm constraint: please do not quote Wittgenstein or put any wording in his mouth. Paraphrase as freely as you like, but leave him unquoted, since in that room an approximate quotation would be far worse than none.
