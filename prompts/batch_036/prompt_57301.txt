You are a professional philosopher of science — someone who has spent a career on the question of how philosophical work relates to the actual practice of the natural sciences, and who is comfortable enough with experimental physics to follow an argument about significance thresholds without flinching. Please think the problem through carefully before you write. If it helps, begin with a short rough pass in which you jot down candidate readings of the sentence I am about to give you, and only then present the finished analysis.

I should explain my situation. I retired four years ago from a national laboratory, where most of my working life went into designing, commissioning, and running neutron scattering instruments. For decades a particular remark circulated among my colleagues — usually credited to Feynman, though I have never once seen anyone produce the occasion — and I confess that I deployed it myself more than a few times: philosophy of science is about as useful to scientists as ornithology is to birds. I am not interested in its provenance. Please spend no words on whether he actually said it, and offer me no references, no bibliography, no suggested reading, and no verbatim quotations; I have no way of checking any of that and would much rather have your reasoning. Where you touch on what particular people have held, paraphrase, and flag your uncertainty if you feel it.

Reading philosophy seriously in retirement, I have found that I cannot say what I used to mean by that sentence. The incident that made me want it unpacked happened about two years before I left. A postdoc in our group asked, at a weekly meeting, whether our convention of demanding five standard deviations before announcing a discovery could be justified at all, or whether it was simply a local norm that had hardened into a rule; and, connectedly, whether an excess at three sigma should count for more when an entirely different experiment had already pointed the same way. A senior colleague ended the discussion in about four seconds with the ornithology remark, the room laughed, and we moved on to the beam schedule. I thought then, and think still, that the laugh came a little too easily.

What I should like is a menu rather than a verdict. Specifically:

1. Lay out the distinct precise claims that this one sentence might be expressing — I would expect at least six — each stated so that it could be argued for or against on its own. Be explicit about the dimensions along which they differ: at minimum, whose utility is at issue (the individual scientist at the bench, the enterprise of science over generations, or the standing of science in public life), what kind of utility (helping produce results, helping avoid errors, or supplying concepts one is using already whether one acknowledges it or not), and whether the claim is a sociological report about an academic specialty or a thesis about where knowledge comes from.

2. For each reading, give a verdict — trivially true but inert in argument; substantive and defensible; substantive and doubtful; or simply false — with a sentence or two of justification.

3. For each reading, say whether it is something a working physicist could hold without thereby committing himself to a piece of recognizably philosophical work. I mean this sincerely: I want to know whether the sentence can be asserted at all on purely scientific grounds.

4. Attend to the comparison itself. What must be true of ornithology, and of birds, for the analogy to bear the weight placed on it? Where does it strain, and where does it simply collapse? I will only observe that birds do not read ornithology and cannot act on it, and that ornithology is descriptive whereas much of what my colleagues had in mind was not; you will know better than I what to make of that.

5. Tell me which reading my senior colleague most plausibly meant in that meeting, and which of the readings would genuinely have licensed shutting the postdoc down as opposed to merely expressing impatience with him. What should the postdoc have said back?

6. Briefly, at the end: does the same menu cover the harsher cousins of the remark that I have also heard over the years — that philosophy has died because it failed to keep pace with physics, and that philosophers for the most part do not understand the theories they write about — or are those making different claims that require a decomposition of their own?

Please write in prose with numbered entries, formally but without ceremony, and resist the temptation to conclude that the truth lies somewhere in the middle.
