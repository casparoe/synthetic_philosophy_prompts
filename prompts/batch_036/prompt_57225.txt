I am a novelist, and for several years I have been building a book whose narrator cannot be certain that she has an inner life: a character who suspects, for reasons she never quite explains to the reader, that her consciousness is a residue that makes no difference to anything she says, does, or decides. To keep myself honest about whether her suspicion is even statable, I wrote out in argument form what I took to be the decisive objection to the metaphysics she has drifted into. The argument has since outgrown the novel, and I can no longer tell whether it is sound or a sleight of hand.

Call the principle in the first step the trace principle, since in the book it is the principle on which a narrator's testimony is supposed to be about anything at all.

(1) A judgment is about some feature of the world only if that feature made a difference to the judgment: holding the laws and the rest of the causal history fixed, a world in which the feature is absent is a world in which the judgment is different.

(2) Epiphenomenalism holds that phenomenal features are produced by physical ones but produce nothing in turn, and so make no difference to the neural states that issue in reports.

(3) Therefore, on epiphenomenalism, no judgment satisfies (1) with respect to any phenomenal feature, and the judgment that I am in pain is not about pain.

(4) A judgment that is not about pain cannot have its truth or falsity settled by how pain is related to anything else; so the epiphenomenalist's own thesis, that consciousness is causally inert, is neither true nor false but idle.

(5) Yet (1) is not a principle about consciousness in particular. It bears on any feature that fails to make a difference to the judgment that purports to be about it: events in the past, which are gone and can reach my present state only through what they left behind; numbers and propositions, which on a platonist picture cause nothing whatever; the experiences of other people, which never touch mine; the future.

(6) If (1) is true, then historical belief, mathematics, and the ascription of minds to others are idle in exactly the way (4) says, and thought in general is unmoored from its subject matter.

(7) That result is intolerable, so (1) is false, and the objection stated in (3) and (4) collapses along with it.

(8) But if (1) is false, then nothing constrains what a judgment is about, and I have no way to insist that my narrator's testimony is about her life rather than a fiction she has composed for the telling — which was the only reason I wanted the principle.

The difficulty is that (1) through (7) read like a refutation and (8) reads like the bill for it, and I cannot hold both at once. What I would most like to keep is (1), because it is what makes testimony a serious undertaking rather than a performance. What I most need to lose is (6). I would be sorry to lose (4), since the novel depends on the possibility that my narrator's doubt is not merely mistaken but unsayable.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I want more than a verdict: tell me why that step looked unobjectionable to me when I wrote it down, what it seems to promise and what it quietly assumes. Then tell me whether I have run together relations that should be kept apart — whether making a difference to a judgment, carrying information about it, and being about it are three relations or one; and in particular whether a lawful, exceptionless tie between a neural state and a phenomenal state, with no causation running from the second to the first, could carry the weight I put on (1). If it could, explain what my narrator's self-reports become on that reading, since the directness and apparent incorrigibility of them were what made her doubt frightening in the first place. Finally, if the argument can be repaired rather than abandoned, give me the smallest revision that lets me keep both (1) and the intelligibility of her testimony, and say which of the two I am really trading away if no such revision exists.
