I'm a backend engineer, zero formal philosophy, so please bear with the framing.

Setup: we have a service that spits out probability-shaped numbers all day (chance this deploy breaks prod, chance this transaction is fraud, chance the on-call page is a real incident). Everything is a single float today. One coworker keeps pushing that we should switch to ranges, on the grounds that a single number is fake precision and there's a whole imprecise-probability literature that says you should carry a set or an interval of numbers instead. Another coworker says ranges are worthless because at the end you still have to pick an action, and a range doesn't tell you which one. I dont want to take a side until I know what I'd even be demanding of the range approach.

So: write me the spec. What does a way of representing degrees of belief with something other than one number have to be able to do before its worth the engineering cost? I want the full list of requirements -- the situations it has to handle without falling over, the differences between cases it's not allowed to blur together, what it has to do when new evidence arrives, what it owes you when you have to act, and which ordinary facts about probability and about being well-calibrated it can't just abandon. For every requirement, give the concrete case that motivates it, stated the way I'd have to state it to someone who just wants to ship the thing.

A few things I've half-heard of and don't know whether they belong on the list at all. There's the urn with red, black and yellow balls where you know exactly how many red but not the black/yellow split, and people's bets in that case look flat-out incoherent under any single number -- is handling that a requirement, or is it just a curiosity? There's the problem, I think called dilation, where doing an experiment makes your interval wider no matter which result comes back, even when you already agreed the experiment was irrelevant -- is "never do that" a hard requirement or one camp's preference? Then there's the gap between "50/50 because the coin is fair" and "50/50 because I genuinely have no clue," which I suspect is the entire motivation for this stuff, and I want to know whether any framework actually keeps those apart or just relabels them. And: does the action rule have to be part of the framework, or can it be bolted on afterwards -- maxmin, or whatever else people use -- and can you even score or calibrate a set-valued forecast the way you'd score a normal one?

Mark each item as non-negotiable or as somebody's taste, and say whose.

Then the part I care about most: if some subset of these can't all be had at once -- if being honest about ambiguous evidence, never dilating, and still producing an action pull in different directions -- say so plainly and show me exactly where the squeeze is and which requirement you'd drop first. That's the real answer I'm after, not a brochure. A table works if it makes the trade-offs easier to read.

American English, please.
