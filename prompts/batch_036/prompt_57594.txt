I am a third-year writing the seminar paper for a course on democratic legitimacy, and the argument below — which my study group and I assembled over two meetings, partly as a reductio of the epistocratic position and partly in earnest — has come apart in my hands. Each premise looks true to me when I consider it on its own, and they cannot all be. I would be grateful for a diagnosis rather than a summary of the literature.

The setting is a hypothetical our seminar invented, and I need to describe it precisely because the argument turns on its details. The charter of a midsized city, Corvin Falls, is amended so that universal suffrage is retained but each resident's ballot is multiplied by a weight between 0 and 3, fixed by that resident's score on an annual civics examination. The examination is written and scored by a commission of eleven members drawn by lot from the resident roll for a single one-year term; the commission must publish its rubric ninety days in advance, and its membership is renewed by lot every year.

(1) If a procedure A for making collectively binding decisions is known to be less likely to reach correct decisions on the matters A decides than some feasible alternative B, then subjecting people to A's decisions wrongs those people.

(2) In Corvin Falls, weighted suffrage is more likely than one-person-one-vote to reach correct decisions, since the examination tracks competence and competence tracks correctness. (Grant this for the sake of argument; it is the epistocrat's empirical claim, and the argument needs it assumed rather than believed.)

(3) So one-person-one-vote in Corvin Falls wrongs its residents, and weighted suffrage is required rather than merely permitted.

(4) But the weights are only as good as the examination, and the examination only as good as the commission; sortition over an unfiltered roll is less likely to yield a competent commission than at least one alternative — say, a commission drawn from the highest scorers of previous years.

(5) A procedure whose authority is derived cannot transmit authority its source lacks: if the appointment of the commission is unjust, so are the weights, and so is the suffrage those weights constitute.

(6) Now apply (1) and (5) to the appointment itself. Whatever procedure selects the selectors, there is a further question of what selected that procedure, and at each level some alternative is better at selecting; so no level is ever free of a superior alternative, and by (1) and (5) no level is ever just.

(7) If no procedure at any level is just, then nothing in Corvin Falls is just — not weighted suffrage, not one-person-one-vote, not sortition, not the amendment process that installed the whole scheme.

(8) A principle of justice whose satisfaction is impossible does not merely go unsatisfied; it disqualifies itself, since it would condemn every arrangement actually available to human beings. So (1) is false.

(9) And with (1) goes the normative engine of the epistocratic case as we have been running it: if no one is wronged by being governed through the less reliable procedure, the complaint against universal suffrage is not a complaint of injustice at all, and one-person-one-vote survives by default rather than by argument.

What I cannot make sense of is how a set of observations about hierarchies of selection should be able to deliver (9), a substantive claim about which regime is defensible. That makes me suspect either that the argument is valid and I have simply misjudged one of the premises, or that one of the transitions is doing something illicit that I cannot name.

So: is this argument valid, with the live question being which plausible premise is least costly to abandon, or does it contain a step that fails in a way that is invisible when the step is read on its own? Identify that step or premise, explain why it looked unobjectionable when I first wrote it down, and then price the two or three most economical repairs — say explicitly what the person who adopts each one has to give up elsewhere. If I am forced to choose, I would rather keep (1) than accept (9), and I would like to keep (5) as well, since without it I have no account of how institutional authority is ever inherited; but I want to know what holding on to them costs, and whether some fourth option is cheaper than either.

Please indicate how confident you are in the diagnosis you settle on, and single out the one point in your answer about which you are least sure.
