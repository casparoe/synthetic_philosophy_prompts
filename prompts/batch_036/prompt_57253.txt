I am a local historian, and in six weeks I am to give the address at the rededication of the famine memorial in the town where I was born. I have spent the last two years cataloguing the parish archive, which is why the committee invited me and why I now wish they had not.

The ceremony marks the 175th anniversary of the worst year. About three hundred people will be standing in a field: descendants of the families who stayed and of the families who walked down to the port and never came back; the historical society, which erected the memorial and runs the day; four classes of schoolchildren who have been taught poems about it; the parish priest, who reads a psalm afterwards and is not my adversary; and two government ministers, one of whose departments part-funded the rededication and who will be photographed at the wreath-laying.

The committee has written to me courteously and firmly. They ask three things: that the address be unifying; that I not reopen old wounds by assigning blame; and that I describe what happened as a natural catastrophe which our ancestors endured with fortitude. Their proposed sentence is that the crops failed and the people starved. A version of that sentence is already carved on the plinth, chosen in the 1970s when the memorial was first raised.

Here is my difficulty, which is the archive. The port books show grain and meal coming in by sea right through the two worst years; the tonnage landed does not fall below the years before. Two of the three mills ran continuously. The price of meal in the town roughly quadrupled between the harvest and the following spring, while the wage paid on the relief works was fixed by a board in the capital and did not move at all. The relief committee's letter book contains eleven requests, made over nine months, that the works wage be raised to meet the price of food; every one is refused, most without a reason given, once with the reason that a higher wage would draw labour away from the farms. The estate agent's rent book shows rent collected in cash, in full, from households whose only source of cash was the works, including through the months when the works were closed for the season. There was no newspaper published in the town, and the refusals arrived by post from men who would never set foot in the parish. Nothing in these records shows a collapse in the amount of food. They show a collapse in people's power to command it.

I take my bearings from the entitlement analysis of famine associated with Amartya Sen — the distinction between there not being food and people not being able to reach the food that is there — and from the thought that a famine, on that analysis, is an event in a system of permissions and refusals rather than an event in the weather. I am not asking you to argue for that framework against its critics. I am asking you to write a speech in which it is doing the work.

One detail I cannot get out of my head, and which I want in the speech. The burial register records the miller's wife among the dead of March. The relief committee's minutes record that through the autumn she gave away meal from the mill door to whoever came. She had food; she gave it away; she died anyway. She is the person in this archive who shows that individual generosity was not the missing thing, and I do not want her reduced to a sentimental beat.

Please write the address. It should run about ten minutes aloud — say 1,200 to 1,500 words — and it must survive being heard once, outdoors, in wind, by people who will not read it afterwards. Beyond that, the following constraints are not negotiable, and where they pull against each other I want to see the strain handled rather than hidden.

1. No economist, philosopher or historian is named from the podium, and no term of art is spoken. Do not put the word "entitlements" in anyone's mouth at a wreath-laying. The analysis has to live in the grammar instead: in who is made the subject of which verb, in what the sentences permit a listener to conclude for themselves. If a point can only be delivered by invoking an authority, it will fail in this room.

2. The speech must state, in language a twelve-year-old in the third row can follow without effort, what would have to be true for a famine to count as a natural disaster — and must then say plainly which of those things my archive shows and which it does not. The audience should not have to do the arithmetic afterwards.

3. It must not name the ministers, the department, or any living person, and it must not use the words crime, guilt, murder or genocide. I am not certain those words are wrong here. I am certain that if I say one of them at the wreath-laying, the ceremony ends and nothing said after it is heard. Find the way to put the substance of an indictment into the room without them.

4. It must not desecrate the old inscription or the people who chose it. Those words were put on the stone by the children of survivors who had every reason to prefer a heaven that had taken their grandparents to a government that had starved them, and who were mourning rather than writing history. The speech must be able to hold both that they were not fools and that the sentence they chose does not survive the port books. If it cannot hold both, it is not a speech I can give.

5. It should read aloud eight or ten names from the burial register, with their occupations where the register gives one, and then the bare numbers from the winter in which the register stopped recording names and began writing only a count and a single repeated word. I want the room to hear what an aggregate is made of and then to hear the aggregate.

6. It should make something of the fact that no one with the power to answer those eleven letters ever had to look at the parish, and of the fact that a ceremony like this one — people standing in a field, insisting on a record — is the opposite of that arrangement. I would rather the address end on what looking does than on what our ancestors suffered.

7. It must not become a theodicy, must not console the room with the afterlife, must not use the phrase act of God except to take it apart, and must not close with an exhortation to learn lessons that flatter the listeners. The priest is reading the psalm; I am not competing with him, and I am not borrowing his equipment.

8. It must be such that the ministers can stay for the wreath and the young people from the city who have said publicly that they will walk out and unfurl a banner if the address is soft can also stay. Not by splitting the difference — by giving each of them something true that they cannot refuse in front of three hundred people. If you judge that impossible, say so and write the speech I ought to give anyway.

After the speech, add a note of a few hundred words: which of the committee's three requests I have met, which I have met obliquely, and which I have declined; where in the text each declination sits and what protects it from reading as a breach of hospitality by a guest who was invited by the people he is contradicting; and any place where you think I have asked for something that cannot be done in this form.
