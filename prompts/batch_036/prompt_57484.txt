> A regulative norm governs an activity that can be described independently of the norm: the activity is there first, and the norm tells you how to conduct it well. A constitutive rule works the other way round — the rule is part of what makes the activity the activity it is. The standard illustration is a game. The rules of chess do not regulate the antecedently intelligible business of moving carved wood about a board; they are what make certain movements moves in chess at all. Williamson's claim is that the knowledge rule is constitutive of assertion in this sense: it is the rule in virtue of which assertion is the speech act it is, and a speaker is subject to it simply in virtue of performing that act.
>
> Two features of the rule are worth isolating. First, it states a necessary condition, not a maximising requirement. It does not oblige anyone to assert everything she knows; it says only that knowledge is required of what one asserts. Second, and importantly for part (b), constitutive rules can be broken. A player who moves a bishop as a rook moves has not thereby stopped playing chess; she has made an illegal move in a game she is still playing. Constitutivity does not entail inviolability.
>
> The knowledge rule is usually distinguished from Grice's maxim of quality, and it is worth being precise about why. The maxim governs conversational contribution generally, and it is framed in terms of belief and evidence: do not say what you believe to be false, and do not say that for which you lack adequate evidence. That framing makes it close to a justified-belief rule rather than a knowledge rule, and it is regulative in the sense above — it tells contributors how to conduct an activity they could engage in without it. The knowledge rule, by contrast, is supposed to individuate the act.
>
> The pressure on the knowledge rule comes from cases in which a speaker justifiably believes and truly asserts something she does not know. Gettier cases and lottery cases are the familiar examples. Telling a colleague that her ticket will lose seems improper, and the impropriety is not obviously explained by the ticket's failing to lose, since in the ordinary run of things it does lose; and in Gettier cases the assertion is true and well supported, yet intuitively not something the speaker was in a position to make. The standard defence of the knowledge rule is the excuse strategy: such assertions are improper but blameless, since the speaker could reasonably have taken herself to know.
>
> The excuse strategy is not empty, but it has to be handled carefully. Excusing must be cashed out independently of the rule being defended. If the reason the speaker is blameless is that she reasonably believed what she said, then it is the reasonable-belief standard, not the knowledge standard, that generates the criticism she avoids, and the knowledge rule does no work in the explanation. That is a real difficulty, and it is the difficulty the derivation objections press, since a qualified knowledge rule can be derived from the claim that assertion is the expression of belief together with general norms governing conversational criticism — in which case the rule governs assertion without constituting it.
>
> My own view is that the counterexamples do not refute the knowledge rule, though not for the reason usually given. Retreat to a weaker norm will not solve the problem. The truth rule — assert p only if p is true — is sometimes proposed as a rival, but it is in fact the more demanding of the two, since it requires not merely that the speaker be in a good epistemic position but that the world cooperate. Gettier cases are no friendlier to it: in them the speaker asserts a truth she does not know, and so she has not done what that rule requires of her either. Nor does the derivation objection dispose of the knowledge rule, since a rule that can be derived from other norms governing the same activity may still be the rule that constitutes it.
>
> The deeper point concerns what constitutivity commits one to. Because the knowledge rule is constitutive, an utterance made without knowledge is not, strictly speaking, an assertion; the speaker has produced only something that seems to be one. Put this way, the Gettier and lottery cases are not counterexamples to the knowledge rule at all, since nothing in them falls within the rule's scope — there is no assertion there for the rule to be violated by. The examples are genuine, but they are misdescribed. This is what one should expect of a constitutive rule: its authority is not a matter of tracking our intuitions about particular cases, and the fact that it cannot be refuted by such cases is a consequence of its status rather than a defect in it.
