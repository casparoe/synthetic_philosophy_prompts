I run a small machine-learning platform team at a mid-size logistics company, and for the past year our fortnightly reading group has drifted, without anyone deciding it, from distributed-systems papers into foundations. I have become the de facto defender of our day-to-day practices, which is awkward, because the further we read the less sure I am that those practices rest on anything. I have no formal philosophy training — Hume by way of a podcast, the no-free-lunch results by way of a Wikipedia article and a staff engineer who will not let the subject go — so I want to put the problem to you in the only form I actually trust: a list of verdicts I have collected out of arguments with my team, and a request to be told what, if anything, they add up to.

Here is what I say when the cases come up at work.

1. A classifier trained on two years of logs from one service, 98% offline accuracy, redeployed to the same service the following week with the traffic mix unchanged: the number means roughly what it says. The identical model with the identical number pointed at a second service, different log schema, different customer population: the number means nothing, and no increase in sample size repairs it.

2. An enormous model that could plausibly have memorized its training set, scoring perfectly on it, licenses no claim about held-out inputs. A model two orders of magnitude smaller, same sample, same perfect training score, licenses a fair amount. I know that "capacity" is carrying the entire load here and I cannot say what entitles me to it.

3. I have run SHA-256 over something like ten million inputs across our fleet. I would bet my salary that our Java library's digest for a file nobody has hashed yet matches the reference implementation's, without running either. In ten years of production I have never observed a collision, and I will not bet a dollar that the next billion files are collision-free. Same function, comparable sample sizes, opposite verdicts. My defense is that the first follows from how the thing is constructed while the second is a regularity in the world — but everything I know about how it is constructed, I know by running implementations of it, so I am not confident the defense survives contact with itself.

4. A physical law fitted across four orders of magnitude and then extrapolated ten further, on the strength of symmetry and conservation arguments rather than the quality of the fit: licensed, and in my judgment more strongly licensed than case 1 with its honest validation split, even though I cannot name one observation inside the extrapolated regime.

5. A colleague's feature that correlated with churn across the full 24-month training window, held on every holdout split we generated, survived cross-validation, and collapsed the week it went live. Procedurally he did nothing wrong. My verdict is that the inference was already illegitimate before it failed and the failure merely revealed this — but I have no test that would have told us in advance, and if the test is "project only properties that are somehow natural," I cannot characterize naturalness except as having worked so far, which is where we began.

6. Averaged uniformly over all possible objective functions, every algorithm performs alike, so an algorithm's edge on the problems we care about can only come from an assumption matched to those problems and never from anything intrinsic to the algorithm. I accept this outright, and I accept the first thing my staff engineer draws from it: choosing a model class is a wager rather than a discovery, and there is no best algorithm simpliciter. I do not accept the second thing he draws from it — that because an inductive bias cannot be justified in advance, the only defensible way to pick one is to take whatever wins on our internal benchmark and stop pretending the benchmark measures anything beyond itself. Those two steps look to me like the same move, and I suspect that keeping the first obliges me to give up the second, but I cannot articulate the difference.

7. I cannot use "the future will resemble the past" as a license for anything. I can produce systems where it stopped being true overnight — a feature that was predictive until a UI redesign — and systems where it holds against absurd odds. A principle that endorses both discriminates nothing, and I would rather have nothing than that.

The case I keep reopening is not a model but an alerting rule: if p99 latency exceeds 400 ms for three consecutive minutes, something is broken. It has fired 412 times and been right 409. I trust it considerably more than I trust the classifier in case 1, which had far more data and a proper validation split, and the only reason I can offer is that the rule is short enough for me to enumerate the ways it could be wrong, whereas I cannot do that for the classifier at all. I do not know whether that is a principle or a preference for things I can read in one sitting.

So: is there a principle, or a short ordered set of them, that generates these verdicts rather than merely failing to contradict them? I want it stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without consulting you, with each clause tied to the case that forced it in. Then tell me what it makes of the alerting rule. Then give me the cost: which of the seven I would have to surrender, and if you believe all seven can be saved, I want the bill itemized — what machinery each rescue requires and which rescue you would decline to perform. Last, and this is what keeps the reading group going: if the principle turns out to be a substantive assumption about the kinds of problems the world happens to hand us, it looks like precisely the thing the no-free-lunch result says cannot be justified independently of a distribution over problems. So say which of three things it is — an empirical claim about our corner of the world, a stipulation we adopt and pay for, or something else entirely — and say what observation would count against it.
