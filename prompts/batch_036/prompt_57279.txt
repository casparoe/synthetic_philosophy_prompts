I am a doctoral candidate in political philosophy, and this year I hold the graduate fellowship to our state legislature's temporary commission on intergenerational wealth transmission. The commission has nine members: three legislators, two estate attorneys, an economist, a county assessor, a small-business owner, and two citizen appointees. In October it votes on the design recommendation it will forward to the legislature. Its charge is limited to design and justification — assume the legal authority to enact whatever we recommend already exists, and do not spend words on statutory drafting, appraisal procedure, or interaction with federal law.

Two designs are before us and the commission is split roughly down the middle.

Design A, the transfer-side scheme: a tax levied on a decedent's estate before distribution, with an exemption set per estate and progressive rates above it. Its intuitive rationale is that the taxable event is the act of conferring.

Design B, the receipt-side scheme: each living person's receipts from all donors are tallied into a cumulative lifetime account; the exemption is set per recipient per lifetime; progressive rates apply to that cumulative total rather than to any single transfer. Its rationale is that the taxable event is the acquisition of an unearned advantage, and that the morally relevant unit is a recipient's lifetime total, not each transaction.

The commission has asked me to circulate a position memo under my own name recommending one of the two — or a hybrid, but only if the hybrid is principled rather than a split of the difference. Roughly 1,300 words, headed sections, with the recommendation stated in a single sentence at the top. It should be written so that a commissioner who disagrees can respond to reasons rather than to rhetoric.

Please address the following.

1. The baseline. State explicitly what equal opportunity means in the memo's argument: the benchmark against which a given receipt counts as an advantage at all. Distinguish a starting-gate conception, on which justice requires an equalization of initial stakes at some point in a life and thereafter tolerates drift, from a continuous conception, on which advantages traceable to circumstance rather than choice must be counteracted whenever they appear. Then show which conception each design quietly presupposes and where the fit is imperfect. My own suspicion is that Design A makes sense only on a donor-centered account of what is wrong with bequest, while Design B makes sense only on a continuous conception. Argue with that suspicion; do not simply ratify it.

2. Legibility. Our economist keeps pressing the point that most transmitted advantage never passes through a taxable event: tuition paid directly to a school, unpaid work in a family firm, a down payment, access to networks and school districts, trust interests that never vest in a form anyone would call a receipt. If she is right, the memo must confront whether equal opportunity can bear the justificatory weight we are putting on it, or whether the tax has to be defended on some other ground — preventing a hereditary political caste, say, or a declaratory function. Choose a ground, defend the choice, and say what follows for the design on that ground that would not follow on the opportunity ground.

3. The foundation question. Recommend a treatment of bequests to donor-directed charitable vehicles. A family can convert a taxable inheritance into a permanently named institution that employs relatives, keeps board seats, and locks its mission in perpetuity, while the transaction is classified as a gift to the public. Give the commission a short, applicable test for when such a vehicle should be treated as a continuation of the dynasty rather than a departure from it. The criteria should be things a commissioner could actually check — control, naming, family employment, spend-down versus perpetuity, and the like — and the test must resolve the borderline case in which the family surrenders control but retains the name.

4. The losing side. Give the strongest case against your recommendation. I do not mean the double-taxation talking point or capital flight, though you may dispose of both briefly. I mean the objection that parental partiality is a genuine human good, that the freedom to provide for one's children is partly constitutive of the family as an institution, and that Design B is hostile to that good rather than merely to its excess — together with its practical corollary, that a cumulative lifetime account falls hardest on recipients of illiquid family assets, a farm or a workshop, at the precise moment they receive them. Render this so that a commissioner who holds it recognizes it as her own view and can see what would answer it.

5. Falsifiability. Close with what the commission does not know and cannot settle by argument alone, and identify the one or two empirical findings that, if our economist produced them, would most change the recommendation. Commit in advance to what those findings would be.

Write for commissioners, not for a journal: no citations, no named authorities, no invented statistics, and no reading list. Attach a label to a philosophical position only where the label is doing work in the argument.
