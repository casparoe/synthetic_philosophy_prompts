I'm three quarters of the way through a novel and my protagonist has a device in her head that I made up, and I want to get the philosophy underneath it right because two of my readers are grad students and they will absolutely eat me alive. So: after an aneurysm she had her corpus callosum severed, but the surgeons left in a synthetic bridge, a sort of dial the family can turn overnight — anywhere from zero percent bandwidth between the hemispheres up to a hundred, in whole numbers. Nobody has that. It's fiction. But I want it to function as a real thought experiment, not just set dressing, and my agent already thinks chapter nineteen is too talky so I can't put a lecture in the book, I have to know the answers myself first.

What I need from you is a case built to spec rather than a tour of the literature. Here's the spec. One domestic scene — she's at the table with her husband and her daughter on an ordinary Tuesday, maybe she's cooking, whatever you like, but no lab, no tachistoscope, nothing flashed to one visual field, no anesthesia, no amnesia, no seizure — with the dial parked somewhere in the middle, call it forty percent, in which four accounts of what's going on inside her come apart. The four accounts: the two-subjects view, Nagel's line, that a disconnected brain houses two streams of consciousness; the interpreter view, Gazzaniga's, where there's really one self and it's the left hemisphere's running commentary, the other hemisphere having states but no grip on the I; the deflationist reply to both, that consciousness was never unified in the way we picture it so the surgery doesn't take anything away; and the broadcasting-or-integration view, on which phenomenology requires information to be globally available, so a half-connected brain has a genuinely thinned-out inner life. The scene has to be built so all four predict exactly the same observable behavior — identical words, identical use of her two hands, identical little pause before she answers her daughter — and they disagree only about how many subjects are in that kitchen and how much there is that it's like to be her. That's the whole reason I want the case: I want a reader to feel that nothing she could notice, from inside or outside, settles it. And be straight with me if it cheats, if the scene only works because I've smuggled the verdict into the setup instead of letting the theories generate it.

Then do the check in prose. Walk each of the four accounts through the scene and say what each is committed to about who's in there and why the behavior underdetermines it.
