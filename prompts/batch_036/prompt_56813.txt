Okay, this one's close to home. I TA a third-year philosophy-of-mind course (the "minds and machines" one) and I've just spent the weekend marking the essay pile, and easily a third of them made the same move: something — Godel, quantum computers, an analog chip — "disproves the Church-Turing thesis," therefore brains aren't computers, QED. The essays aren't stupid, they're just completely unreflective about what kind of claim the thesis even is, and nobody seems to notice that "effective calculability" is doing all the work. To make it worse, my brother-in-law has just gone and joined a startup whose pitch deck literally says their hardware is "beyond Turing," and I've realised I can't articulate why that's not a claim you can settle by press release. I've been handed the final exam question for the course, and I want to use it to kill this line of argument dead.

So: write me one exam question on the Church-Turing thesis and its epistemic status, for third-year philosophy students, answerable in 45 minutes, worth 25 marks.

The question has to have a concrete stem rather than being "discuss the epistemic status of CTT." Build the stem around an announced device: a lab claims to have built something that, given a number n, always halts and prints the right yes/no answer about whether the nth Turing machine halts — and be specific about the awkward details the students have to wrestle with (it isn't a simulation that happens to be fast, its outputs can't be independently checked because checking them is the very problem it supposedly solves, the engineers admit it only works reliably for inputs below some threshold, and nobody outside the lab has reproduced it). Then ask the students to say which reading or readings of the thesis the device threatens, what would have to be true for it to be a genuine counterexample rather than a case of us just re-labelling the concept of a procedure, and whether any of it supports the step to "human minds aren't Turing machines."

Crucially, it must be unanswerable by reciting the thesis or by name-checking the usual suspects — I want the marks to come from students seeing that "it's only a definition, so nothing could disprove it" is not a safe harbour either, since that reading either makes the thesis trivially true and then irrelevant to the minds question, or it quietly assumes the notion of procedure that's actually under dispute. And they need to handle the asymmetry between what a human could in principle carry out with pencil and paper versus what a physical process could do, without collapsing the two.

After the question, give me a model answer of about 700 words, then a marking scheme with five or six must-hit points, the marks each is worth out of 25, and a line on how students typically botch each one — plus, separately, two or three specific answers that *look* good and should be marked down (the fluent Godel-sentence essay is the one I most want to catch). No reading lists or citations, I don't need them, just the question, the answer, and the scheme.
