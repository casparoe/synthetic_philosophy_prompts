I'm working on a section about pain asymbolia and what it's supposed to prove about why pain is bad, and I think the literature has been leaning on the case in one direction only. The asymbolic — sensory discrimination intact, aversive charge apparently gone, reports feeling the pain but not being bothered by it — always gets wheeled in against the view that awfulness is a property of the experience itself, and in favour of the view that a pain's badness is a matter of your being averse to it, wanting it to stop. I want to see whether that intuition survives running the dissociation the other way.

So: build me a case, to spec.

One patient, one paragraph, realistic neurology. Stroke, lesion, whatever the plausible substrate is — no implants, no invented pharmacology, no sci-fi. He's on a rehab ward. Everything the two sides already agree on is held fixed; the only variable is the one below.

The spec:

- He has the asymbolic disconnection: no aversion to the pain, no desire for it to stop, doesn't ask for analgesia, isn't moved to protect the limb. But his motivational life is otherwise completely normal — he still wants his coffee, still wants his granddaughter to visit, still cheats at the crossword. Selective, not global apathy.
- Unlike the textbook asymbolic, though, he reports that the pain feels exactly as awful as it did before the lesion. I want that report to be as evidentially strong as you can make it without begging the question, so give him a pre-lesion calibration — say years of pain diaries with unusually fine-grained descriptions — so his present reports can be checked against a record of what awfulness meant to him back when he was still normally averse.
- No instrumental harm: the ward manages his body on a fixed schedule regardless of what he asks for, so nothing bad is going to accrue downstream of his indifference. Whatever badness is in question has to be intrinsic to the episode.
- No second-order distress: he's not bothered by not being bothered, not alienated from himself, no reflective regret.
- Hardest constraint: the evidence that the awfulness is still there may not be preference-shaped. Nothing in the case can rest on his choosing between pains, ranking what he'd rather have again, or anything else a desire theorist could just read off as a suppressed aversion. Intensity discrimination, quality recognition, matching his own old diary entries — that kind of thing is fair game. Say which of these you used and why they don't collapse back into preference evidence.

Then do the check. State what each of these says about whether he is badly off in the way a person in pain is badly off, and why: (1) awfulness is a felt property of the experience; (2) a pain is bad for you insofar as you're averse to it; (3) unpleasantness is a matter of the experience's content — imperative or evaluative, your pick, but say which and stick to it; and (4) if you like, the view that suffering is about pain's disruption of agency. Tell me where 3 lands; my guess is it has the most awkward time here, and I want to know whether that's right.

Then the honest part: which single feature of the case is doing the work; what happens to the verdicts if you delete the diaries, or make the flatness global, or let the ward stop treating him; and how the desire theorist attacks the case. I especially want to know whether their best attack is actually available to them or whether it assumes what's in dispute.

If you think the case can't be built to spec without stipulating the verdicts instead of generating them, say so plainly and tell me which constraint has to go. And tell me where you think the design is most likely to fail — where your reasoning is shakiest, and what consideration would change your mind about which feature is doing the work. If it turns out the reverse case just reads as neutral in the same way the asymbolic one does, I'd rather know that; its a finding about the debate, not about my case.
