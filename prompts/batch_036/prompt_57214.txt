Ok so this turned into a 45 minute argument in slack and I want an actual answer, not a shrug.

Git puts two people on every commit, author and committer, and both are one person. When we squash-merge a PR that had 40 commits from five people, the result is a single commit with one name on it — usually whoever clicked the button. If we feel guilty we hand-paste `Co-authored-by: ...` lines into the commit message, which is just free text, git doesn't know or care that its a list of humans. Meanwhile the contribution graph on GitHub only counts a commit toward you if the author email matches your account, so there's a standing incentive for the one name in that field to be yours.

My own guess is that none of this means anything. Git was built for the kernel mailing list, patches showed up by email from one person, and the author/committer split is just "who wrote the patch" versus "which maintainer applied it." Path dependence, not a theory of action.

Except our whole process points the other way. Nothing lands without two approvals, we say "we shipped X" in the retro, we're explicitly told nobody owns a service alone, and we rotate on-call partly so no single person is the cause of anything. And someone on my team wants to go all the way: use one shared identity, `platform-team@`, as the author on literally every commit we make, so the record stops pretending one person did it.

So what I'm after:

- What is the current design assuming about who the doer of a joint piece of work is? I keep feeling like it's collapsing three different things onto one individuated person — who gets credit, who gets blamed, and who you go ask when you need to understand the code — and like those might come apart. Does it really assume that, or am I reading a philosophy paper into a database schema?
- Pull apart what the schema strictly commits us to from what it merely makes convenient. I'd bet several different accounts of joint action could live happily with one-author commits, and I want to know which ones and what the differences would actually show up as.
- Is my path-dependence guess even available to me? Can the design be fully explained without it encoding any view of agency — or does the fact that we kept it, and then built contribution graphs, promotion packets, and blame-free postmortems on top of it, change what it commits us to?
- For the shared-identity idea: what would have to be true about my team for that to be more than a bookkeeping trick? I have a vague sense that a group can only be an author if the group has intentions of its own somehow, and that "five people who share a codebase and a standup" might not qualify. Lay out what the qualifying conditions would be and whether a team like mine plausibly meets them.
- What would a version control system that took genuinely joint work seriously look like? For contrast, Wikipedia has no byline at all — the article belongs to the project and the history is basically an audit log. Is that the other coherent option, or is there a third shape?

One more thing that I think matters: for us attribution isn't only credit. "Who wrote this" is how you find the person to ask, and its how you decide whose judgment to trust about whether a revert is safe. So if the design is making a claim about agency, I suspect it's also making a claim about knowledge and about blame, and I don't think those are the same claim.
