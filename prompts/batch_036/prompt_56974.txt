I am in Year 13 and studying Government and Politics at A-level, and I have been trying to read around the question of electoral reform rather than depending entirely on what we are told in class. A friend lent me her revision guide, whose account of the debate runs as follows:

'First-past-the-post elects the candidate with the most votes in each constituency, so the party that wins the most seats normally forms a government able to pass its manifesto. Its great defect is that seats do not match votes: a party with a fifth of the national vote can be left with almost nothing, and millions of votes are cast for candidates who were never going to win. Proportional representation corrects this by matching seats to vote shares, so that a party winning twenty per cent of the vote wins twenty per cent of the seats, no vote is wasted and every voter ends up represented by someone they actually voted for. Its costs are equally clear: because it tends to produce coalition government, the smaller parties can demand policies that very few people voted for, and the composition of the government is settled by bargaining between party leaders after the election rather than by the electorate on the day. This connects to Duverger's law, which holds that first-past-the-post produces two-party systems; any country that switches to proportional representation should therefore expect its party system to fragment. The direct link between one MP and one constituency, which supporters regard as the system's great virtue, also disappears under proportional representation, since voters there simply choose a list of candidates drawn up by party headquarters. The alternative vote, rejected by British voters in the 2011 referendum, is sometimes offered as a compromise, since it keeps single-member constituencies while requiring the winner to have majority support.'

I found that broadly convincing when I read it, and I remain inclined to think that first-past-the-post is unfair, chiefly because it routinely hands a parliamentary majority to a party that fewer than half of those voting supported.
