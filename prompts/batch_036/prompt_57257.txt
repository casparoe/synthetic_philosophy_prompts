I teach a graduate seminar on weakness of will and a colleague of mine over in the behavioral economics group keeps reaching for the same picture, and I want to know how much weight it can bare before I let it anywhere near my syllabus. The picture: a person with a self-control problem is like a household containing two members — a far-sighted planner who has the household's real long-run interests at heart and an impulsive doer who keeps spending the money the planner had set aside. Saving apps, hiding the credit card in a drawer, Odysseus lashed to the mast — all of these are just the planner tying the doer down. That part is descriptive. The problem is the work he makes it do. Because the planner's ends are supposedly the person's own true ends, outside help — an auto-enrollment default, a sin tax, a nudge — isn't overriding the person's will at all, it's enforcing it, so the autonomy objection just evaporates, and the doer's complaints are like the complaints of a kid who's been made to do his homework. He also says hyperbolic discounting is the mathematical version of the same story: the very shape of the discount function shows there are two agents pulling in seperate directions, and later regret is the planner saying I-told-you-so, which is why the planner's ranking gets to be the metric of welfare rather than the doer's.

So give me the correspondence table — what plays the planner, the doer, the shared household, the budget, the tying-down, the helpful outsider — and then the audit. Which features of the household carry over and which don't, and among the ones that don't, which actually threaten his argument versus which are just cosmetic? Three things I especially want pinned down: (a) whether anything in the formal treatment of hyperbolic or quasi-hyperbolic discounting implies two agents rather than a single agent whose preference ordering shifts with the temporal perspective she happens to be taking up at the moment of choice; (b) what the analogy quietly imports about there being a true self whose preferences get to count — my suspicion is that it hands him a solved problem about aggregation and about whose preference is authoritative that the model itself never argues for, and I'd like to see exactly where the smuggling happens; and (c) whether the paternalism conclusion survives once you notice that the doer is the one who is literally present at the moment of choice while the planner is, at best, a set of dispositions laid down earlier. I should also say the analogy seems to get something right that a plain single-agent model struggles with — the phenomenology of inner conflict, and why commitment can feel like an exercise of agency rather than a restriction on it — so don't just pile up disanalogies, tell me which of them are load-bearing.

And if you think there's a better source domain for time-inconsistent agency — a repeated game among successive selves, a thermostat, a parliament with an upper house, a principal-agent contract with hidden action, whatever you'd nominate — say which one and how far it carries before it starts begging the same questions. No bibliography needed, I just want the argument.
