I am drafting the framing chapter for a volume of new essays on the new riddle of induction, and I have run into an obstacle I did not anticipate: I cannot write a single sentence stating what my contributors disagree about that all of them would accept. The riddle itself is seventy years old and needs no introduction for anyone in the volume. A predicate applies to a thing just in case either the thing was examined before some fixed future date and is green, or it was not so examined and is blue — call it grue — along with its mirror image bleen. Every emerald examined so far is both green and grue, so the evidence appears to support two incompatible generalizations with equal force; and since each family of predicates is definable from the other plus a date, no asymmetry of logical form is visible from either direction.

The camps, roughly as they will appear in the volume:

1. Entrenchment. Projectibility is a matter of a predicate's past record of successful projections, and grue has no such record. The standing objection is that this redescribes the phenomenon instead of explaining it, and that it is embarrassed by newly minted predicates — transuranic, superconducting, exoplanetary — which have no record whatsoever and are nonetheless projected without hesitation.

2. Purity. The definition of grue smuggles in a reference to particular episodes of examination; only purely qualitative predicates, statable without that sort of indexicality, can be projected. The standing objection is the symmetry argument: in a language taking grue and bleen as primitive, green is the impure predicate, so purity cannot be a property of predicates apart from a language, and the criterion owes an independent account of which primitive vocabularies are privileged.

3. Naturalness. There is a metaphysically privileged structure to the world's properties, and it is a property's proximity to that structure — not its syntax, not its history — that makes it projectible and makes its generalizations lawlike. The standing objection is that this purchases the answer with an unexplained primitive, and invites the rejoinder that we have no non-question-begging reason to expect the future to resemble the natural properties rather than the gerrymandered ones.

4. Probabilism. The riddle is mis-posed: support is always relative to a prior, so there is no context-free fact that the emerald evidence confirms the green hypothesis. Grue-type hypotheses carry negligible prior for agents like us, and that is the whole story. The standing objection is that this relocates the riddle into the priors, where the only reason on offer for having green-favoring priors is that they have worked.

5. Deflation. There is no general inductive logic awaiting vindication; the riddle is an artifact of a certain philosophical picture of justification, and once we look at how predicates are actually acquired and applied there is nothing left to solve.

6. Embrace. Grue generalizes Hume's point, and the correct conclusion is that no non-circular inductive justification exists; camps one through five are restatements of the problem dressed as solutions to it.

My hypothesis, which I want tested rather than flattered, is that the impasse owes nothing to the hardness of the question and everything to an unnoticed disagreement about what an answer must be. Each camp appears to be producing a different kind of object: a criterion, a diagnosis, a metaphysical ground, a formal model, a therapeutic dissolution. And each appears willing to sacrifice a different item from a shared list of desiderata that nobody states explicitly: non-circularity; explaining the asymmetry between green and grue rather than merely registering it; leaving open that we might turn out to be wrong, so that the conclusion is not analytic; and applicability to predicates never before used. If those four cannot be satisfied jointly, then what looks like a competition among rival theories is really a disguised impossibility result, and my chapter should say so plainly.

Three pieces of test material I would like the diagnosis to engage rather than set aside. First, a predicate whose extension is closed — was born before 2000 — which is temporally indexical and hence impure by the second camp's lights, yet entirely safe to project, since nothing new can fall under it. Second, the same construction with the cutoff date in the past rather than the future, where the symmetry collapses because the evidence base is no longer confined to the pre-date region. Third, a purely spatial gerrymander containing no temporal indexical at all: green if first mined in one designated country, blue otherwise. Where the camps classify these differently, I want to know whether the divergence signals substantive disagreement or merely different targets.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Verbal, turning on what confirmed, projectible, or justified is being made to mean; substantive but plural, several distinct questions answering to one heading; a disagreement about what counts as a successful theory; a hidden empirical or formal question — for instance, whether a learning-theoretic result about which hypothesis spaces permit reliable convergence would settle anything, and if so what it would have to assume in advance; or an ill-posed question, or simply a hard one. Ground the classification in what each camp is independently committed to, not in a tally of who seems more plausible. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a theorem, a change of question, an experiment, or the abandonment of the project — and give me an honest verdict on whether anything could actually end the dispute, or whether no progress should be expected and why. I am not asking you to adjudicate between the camps or to recommend a solution, and I do not want references, attributions, or a reading list.
