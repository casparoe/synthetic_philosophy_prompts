So for my philosophy elective we spent a week on this evidence thing with ravens — the idea that "all ravens are black" is supposed to be the same claim as "anything that isn't black isn't a raven," so a photo of a green apple technically counts as evidence about ravens, which sounds like a joke but apparently isn't one. We get to do a creative project instead of the essay and I want to do a short story, but I really don't want the obvious version where someone lectures about how counterintuitive it all is. I want the logic baked into the world instead.

Here's the setup I came up with, please run with it: a city runs a Confirmation Bureau that literally pays residents per submitted observation that supports the town's official hypothesis, which is "all ravens are black" (the town is weirdly into ravens, its a football mascot thing that got out of hand). People upload photos and get paid off a published rate card. My main character is a shut-in on the fourth floor who has never seen a raven in her entire life and makes real money photographing white sneakers, green apples, and her neighbor's beige mailbox out her window. The Bureau counts her as their single top earner three quarters running, which means on paper she's doing more ornithology than any of the actual birdwatchers, and some of them are furious about it.

Can you write about 1500 words (anywhere from 1400-1800 is fine) and hit these:

- A scene at the counter where a clerk explains the rate card to a newcomer, and the explanation has to make the reader feel why an apple photo earns a check at all — without anyone naming the paradox, naming any philosopher, or using words like contrapositive or Bayesian. It should come out as boring bureaucracy, like a fee schedule at the DMV.
- The rate for shoe-and-apple photos collapses to basically nothing partway through. She goes to find out why, and the reason has to be about what everybody in town already knows by then — like, something happened that made those photos stop telling anyone anything they didn't already have. Don't explain it in a monologue, let her work it out across two or three encounters.
- One character who insists only real ravens should get paid, and who is genuinely not stupid about it — give them a decent argument that survives being answered, so the reader is still on the fence at the end.
- The ending is a choice she actually makes that costs her something, not a moment where she has a realization and stares out the window.
- No raven appears on the page until the final paragraph at the earliest, and even then I don't want it to be a big symbolic moment.

Also please don't cite anything, no bibliography or list of sources at the end, and don't put any quotes from real books in it — I just want the story, I'm not asking you to prove you read stuff. After the story, add like five or six sentences (separate from the fiction, clearly labeled) saying which rule of the rate card is doing the work for which part of the puzzle, and then being honest about the one part your story doesn't actually solve. I want to be able to tell my teacher where I fudged.
