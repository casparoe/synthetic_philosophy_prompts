I am a third-year doctoral student working on the epistemology of objective chance, and I need help turning a half-formed idea into a two-page chapter proposal for my dissertation committee, which will also serve as the application for a twelve-week summer research grant in my department. Here is the idea, as far as I have got.

The usual statement of the Principal Principle tells an agent whose evidence is admissible to set her credence in an outcome equal to that outcome's known chance. Every formulation I know of assumes that the chance function is a feature of the world that the agent's doxastic state merely tracks. My project drops that assumption. Consider a set-up in which chance is itself a function of credence: a source whose probability of firing in the next minute is, as a matter of the physical law governing it, one minus the agent's current credence that it will fire — a contrarian feedback law. Deference then stops being an instruction and becomes an equation, and the questions I want to ask are the questions one asks about such equations: does a solution exist, is it unique, and is it stable under revision?

Two features make this more than a puzzle. First, the mathematics is not uniform across feedback laws. Where the law is continuous, a fixed point is guaranteed but need not be unique; the identity law makes every credence self-deferring, so radical indeterminacy looks like a new problem for deference rather than a cure for one. Where the law is discontinuous — say threshold-gated — there may be no fixed point at all, and then no credence satisfies the Principle. Second, and more troubling, the chance hypothesis in such a set-up is not a proposition about the world independent of the agent's own state: it refers to her credences, so the conditional in which deference is standardly formulated appears to be ill-formed, and the admissibility clause, which was designed to exclude precisely the information that would let credence and chance come apart, now seems to exclude the agent's knowledge of her own mind.

I keep oscillating between two claims and cannot yet tell which is the interesting one: that the set-up is coherent and shows deference to be indeterminate, or that the incoherence of a credence-referential chance hypothesis shows that chance cannot depend on credence — which would be a substantive metaphysical thesis about chance rather than a result about credence. I also want to know whether the project can absorb two adjacent matters without bursting: the familiar problem of chance assignments that supply the agent with evidence against the very hypothesis that generated them, and the regress that threatens if the feedback law must refer not to first-order credence but to the agent's credence about her credence.

Please write the proposal. I need a precise statement of the question, phrased so that it has a determinate answer rather than an open-ended theme; an account of why the existing positions leave it open, covering both the standard justifications of deference (the accuracy-based and the Dutch-book-style ones) and the Humean accounts of what makes a chance a chance; the approach I should take, week by week across the twelve weeks; a defence of that approach against the obvious alternatives, since I can imagine being told to formalise it, to dismiss the device as physically incoherent, or to dissolve it by distinguishing occurrent from dispositional belief, and I want the proposal to say why I am doing something else; a description of what a successful result would look like and, explicitly, of what would count as failure; and a candid assessment of scope, including which of the two claims above I should stake the chapter on and which of the two adjacent matters I should cut. Please also anticipate the objection that a feedback device of this kind is science fiction and therefore idle, and give me the response I should have ready. Do not include a bibliography or a literature review by citation; I will supply the references myself.
