I'm in year four of a philosophy PhD, and the chapter I'm actually supposed to be writing is about omission causation in the tort-and-responsibility corner of my dissertation. For weeks I've been collecting my own verdicts on cases and I can't get them to line up. My supervisor keeps pushing me toward the view that an omission only causes relative to some expected or normal alternative — fine as a slogan, but I don't think it survives my list, because my list has cases where the expectation runs the wrong way and I still feel the causal pull.

Here's the list.

1. Lifeguard. On-duty lifeguard at a public pool, head down in his phone, a kid drowns ten feet away in the shallow end. Caused it. No hesitation at all.
2. Strong swimmer. Same pool, same ten feet, an off-duty adult who swims competitively and could trivially have reached the kid. Watches from the deck, does nothing. My verdict: also caused it. This is the one I'm least comfortable with and I keep landing back on it anyway.
3. Ten thousand people who could each have grabbed the kid if they'd happened to be standing right there, but were in other cities. Didn't cause it. Obviously.
4. Famine appeal. I didn't donate; a child died of something the appeal was covering. Didn't cause it. But I don't want to justify that with "I made no difference," because the standard pitch for these appeals is that a particular donation buys a particular treatment — if I take the pitch seriously I did make a difference, and I still want to say didn't cause. If the pitch is false and it only ever works in aggregate, then I'm relying on a premise I don't believe just to save the verdict. Notice I've written that one as a problem with my own reasoning rather than as a clean intuition.
5. Contract security. A firm contracted to patrol a lot at night doesn't; a car gets stripped. The thief was coming regardless — he'd cased the lot, knew the patrol schedule, and picked a gap in it. Didn't cause the theft, and that feels right to me.
6. Same firm, same night, same theft, except now we stipulate that any patrol would definately have scared him off. Caused it. So 5 and 6 differ only in counterfactual dependence, and my verdicts track that, which suggests difference-making is doing real work for me.
7. Double prevention. A would-be rescuer is about to jump in and someone grabs his ankle; the victim drowns. The ankle-grabber caused it, clearly. Now: instead of grabbing him, I take the only phone on the deck and make a long personal call, so he can neither summon help nor be talked into jumping. Also caused it, I think, and I think the two are the same kind of thing — but one is an act and one is an omission, and whatever I end up saying has to explain why that difference doesn't matter here while mattering enormously elsewhere on this list.
8. Late lifeguard. He looks up at twelve seconds, sees the kid struggling, freezes; by twenty-five seconds its unrecoverable. Caused, but "caused the death" feels like overreach and "caused the death's being unrecoverable" feels like a lawyer's dodge. I want one thing to say here and I don't have it.
9. The pair that breaks me. An off-shift nurse walks past a corridor that isn't her ward, no assignment, no handover, sees a visitor slumped against the wall going into cardiac arrest, keeps walking. Caused it, I say. A hospital janitor in the same corridor at the same instant, same inaction, no medical training. Didn't cause it, I say. Nothing about duty, contract, employment, or local expectation separates these two — she's off shift and nobody was expecting anything from her — and the only thing that separates them is capacity and knowledge. But if capacity is the discriminator, cases 3 and 4 explode, since there were plenty of capable people in both.

So. Is there a principle that gets 1 through 9, or nearly all of them? I want it stated as clauses, not as a slogan, with a line on which case each clause is doing work for and what it costs to keep that clause. If the honest answer is that you can save everything at the price of an ugly extra condition, or have a clean account that forces me to give up 2 and 9, tell me which trade you'd take and argue for it. And tell me straight which of my verdicts your principle can't accommodate — don't quietly redescribe the cases until they fit, which is what I've caught myself doing all month.

Then tell me what the principle says about a case I haven't listed, because I suspect it'll be embarrassing: nobody alive today cuts emissions, and in 2060 a hurricane kills one particular named person. Under 3 and 4 that looks like mass non-causation; under 2 and 9 it looks like the largest collection of difference-making, highly capable omissions there has ever been. What's the verdict, and is it one I could defend to a tort lawyer without her laughing at me?

Last thing, and it's the one I'd most like pressure on: transitivity. If the lifeguard's failure causes the death, and the supervisor's failure to check on the lifeguard causes the lifeguard's failure, does the supervisor's omission cause the death? I want to say yes. But if I say yes I have to say the same about the company that hired the supervisor and the city that licensed the company, and I arrive back at case 3 by a staircase instead of by a leap. Where do I cut, and can the cut be given as a principle rather than a shrug?

No literature review please — I've got the stack of papers on my desk and I've read most of them. I want the analysis, the clauses, and an honest account of where you think I'm fooling myself.
