I am a fourth-year doctoral student in political theory working on historical principles of distributive justice, and for the past two years I have held the graduate-student seat on the Hallowell Parks and Recreation Youth Basketball Commission. It is a small, unglamorous body — seven commissioners, mostly retired teachers and one owner of a sporting-goods store — and my role has historically been to read the agenda packet and say nothing. Last month that changed, and the chair has now asked me to circulate a written opinion before our meeting on the 14th, because I am, as she put it, "the only one on the commission who has thought about this kind of thing for a living."

Here is the situation. Four seasons ago the league replaced its sliding-scale fee waiver with an access-token system: every registered household receives a set number of tokens each season, allocated by a formula that weights household income, distance from the fieldhouse, and hours of community clinic participation, and each token buys one hour of supervised court time. Tokens were deliberately made transferable, and the league even hosts a swap board on its website, because a family on the south side might want to trade a Tuesday slot at the fieldhouse for a Thursday slot at the rec center. What we did not anticipate is a private travel-ball outfit, Ridgeline Elite, posting standing buy orders on that board: eighteen dollars a token, any quantity, no questions. They have since acquired something on the order of 1,400 token-hours — the overwhelming majority of the Saturday morning slots at the indoor fieldhouse, which is the only facility with evening-grade lighting now that the outdoor courts go dark at five — and they resell the bundled access to their own clients, roughly two-thirds of whom are not league members at all. The need-based households have the cash and no court time. Several of them are furious; several others are furious at the proposal to undo the trades, because one family spent the money on a transmission and cannot return it. The operator's counsel has been extremely pleasant about all of this and keeps observing that every single transfer was voluntary, that his clients paid above any price the league would ever have set, and that the sellers are, by revealed preference, better off than they were.

The commission's charter contradicts itself, which is the reason this has landed on me. Section 3 provides that access to facilities "shall be allocated by need and participation, and never by ability to pay." Section 7 provides that "no member shall be deprived of any entitlement acquired through voluntary exchange with another member." Both are in force; neither has a priority clause. We have five live options: take no action and let the market clear; void completed trades retroactively; declare tokens non-transferable prospectively, the way one might treat a scholarship or a food-stamp benefit; permit transfer among members but bar bulk acquisition by outside operators and institute a standing league buyback at a fixed price with a cap on holdings; or negotiate a block arrangement in which Ridgeline pays the league a lump sum and surrenders a defined number of Saturday hours back into the need-based pool. Please draft the memo I will circulate: a clear recommendation among these (or a combination, or a sixth option if you think we have missed one), a concrete implementation timeline keyed to the spring registration window, and a section that gives the strongest honest case for the outcome I do not recommend. Two things matter to me. First, the argument has to be made from the facts of our case — most commissioners have never read political philosophy, and a memo that reads like a seminar paper on entitlement theory will lose the room, so allude to the general distinction between historical and patterned standards only in passing, if at all, and cite nothing. Second, do not let the memo slide past the hardest structural question, which is this: one commissioner insists that any fix we adopt will simply be upset again within a season unless we ban trading permanently and stand ready to keep intervening, while another insists that a one-time decision to make tokens non-transferable is not continuous interference at all but a design choice about what the entitlement is in the first place. Take a side on that, and tell me whether the fact that Ridgeline has already resold hours to families who never touched the swap board changes what rectification we can permissibly demand. Around 1,100 words, memo format with headings, no footnotes and no bibliography.
