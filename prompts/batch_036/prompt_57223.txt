For the past four years I have directed a regional surveillance programme for a notifiable bacterial infection — I will call it disease V, and I have deliberately renamed it here so that what follows can be treated as a purely evidential problem rather than a clinical one — in a district of roughly 800,000 people. Our official case definition, adopted before I arrived and repeated verbatim in every training manual we issue, states that all cases of V present with a characteristic rash. Because the confirmatory PCR assay is costly and slow, our standing protocol orders it only for patients who present with that rash. Over three seasons we tested 1,140 rash-positive patients, of whom 214 were confirmed. We have never assayed a single patient without a rash, and so we have never, as a matter of arithmetic, had the opportunity to encounter a case of V that lacked one.

At last week's steering-committee meeting a colleague — a capable and well-read epidemiologist, not a naive one — defended the case definition by observing that it is logically equivalent to the claim that everyone in the district without the rash is free of V, and that the district contains something on the order of three-quarters of a million such people, essentially all of whom have gone about their lives without ever being diagnosed with anything. Each of them, he argued, is a confirming instance of the definition, so our confidence in it ought to be overwhelming: on his reckoning we hold hundreds of thousands of favourable data points against at most a hypothetical single counterexample. I found this simultaneously compelling and somehow fraudulent, and when I went looking for a name for the feeling I ran into Hempel's raven paradox and the equivalence condition, which appears to be precisely the structure of his argument. There is a further complication I have not known how to weigh: the original description of V in our district was written by clinicians who partly identified the disease by the rash itself, so I sometimes wonder whether our case definition could be contradicted by any observation at all. And separately, a neighbouring district whose protocol tests every febrile patient regardless of rash reported nine confirmed rash-negative cases of V last year, and I cannot work out how much those nine should be permitted to outweigh everything we have accumulated at home.

Please describe the most important implications of the raven paradox for how I should think about my own data and about my colleague's argument. Specifically: is his reasoning a faithful application of the equivalence condition, or has he quietly changed the subject — and if it is faithful, why does the conclusion feel like a cheat? How much of the intuitive force of his claim depends on the fact that those hundreds of thousands of rash-free people were never tested, so that the "observations" in question are administrative defaults rather than measurements, and would the evidential weight change if we randomly sampled and assayed a thousand of them? What does the paradox imply about whether our case definition is falsifiable at all under a protocol that only ever assays the rash-positive, and about the role the sampling process itself plays in confirmation? How should the nine rash-negative cases from the neighbouring district be weighed against our 214 confirmations — is the lesson about sample size, about how the sample was selected, about the possibility that the two districts are not even stating the same hypothesis, or about some combination of these? I would like the underlying reasoning laid out first and then the practical upshot stated plainly enough for a single committee slide. I am not asking for references or a reading list — I want the analysis itself — and I am entirely willing for you to conclude that my colleague is mistaken, provided you engage with the strongest version of his case rather than a weakened one.
