I am drafting the opening section of a dissertation chapter on moral relativism and have hit a wall on a passage in Plato's Theaetetus — the one near the end of the long examination of Protagoras' measure thesis, just before Socrates wanders off into the comparison between the philosopher and the courtroom orator. I believe it falls around Stephanus 172a–b, but I am working from a print copy I cannot check just now, so please do not treat my location as exact and do not build anything on it.

I cannot reproduce the translation I am using, and I would rather you did not guess at its wording, so what follows is my own close paraphrase, written out for you. Treat everything in it as my phrasing, not Plato's. If you think the paraphrase has flattened something that matters — a qualification, a shift in the terms used, a distinction I have collapsed — say so plainly instead of working around it.

Paraphrase of the passage: Socrates says that whatever a city takes to be just is just for that city, and whatever it takes to be fine or noble is fine for it, for as long as it takes them that way; on such matters no city is wiser than another, because in each case the city's own view settles what is so for it. But when it comes to the expedient — what is in a city's interest, what will benefit it — the same doctrine does not carry through. Here a city's view cannot make things so in the same way, because the expedient concerns the future, and what a city expects to turn out well may turn out otherwise. That is why a city calls in advisers, and why on these questions one person can be wiser than another: the physician concerning health, the farmer concerning the crops, the trainer concerning the body, the orator or general concerning what will benefit the city. The expert is the one whose expectations about how things will turn out come true.

What I need is help getting a grip on the argument, not on its historical setting. Specifically:

1. What is the temporal clause doing? Is the qualification for as long as it takes them that way a device that keeps relativism safe from contradiction — a city may revise, and each view is true for its own period — or does it quietly install something other than present belief as the truth-maker, so that truth becomes a relation between a claim and a stretch of time? If the latter, is the resulting view still relativism, or has it become a form of indexicalism, and does the difference matter for a chapter about moral relativism?

2. Why should the just and the expedient come apart at all? My working hypothesis is that the difference turns on whether satisfaction is simultaneous with the believing or deferred: a city's belief about justice is made true by being held, while its belief about its own interest must wait on events. Is that a coherent way to state the point, or does it smuggle in a fact–value distinction the text has no use for? Can the asymmetry be drawn without any such distinction — for instance, purely in terms of who is in a position to be corrected, and by what?

3. Is the expert being characterized epistemically, as a reliable predictor, or productively, as someone able to bring the outcome about? My paraphrase leans on the first, since it says the expert's expectations come true. But on the second reading the adviser stands to the city's future as the physician stands to the patient's health: not forecasting but making better. That matters, because it would let Protagoras retain a relativism about the better and the worse while giving up relativism about truth. Is there anything in the passage as I have rendered it that decides between the two readings, and which one leaves the measure thesis more intact?

4. If expertise can be redescribed as the ability to replace a worse condition with a better one without ever calling the earlier view false, has Socrates shown that some people are wiser, or only that some are more effective? What would a relativist have to concede for the inference from effectiveness to wisdom to go through, and is there a version of relativism about justice that is immune to it?

5. Does the individual case inherit whatever the city case enjoys? The passage seems to move between the city as measure and the person as measure without marking the move. Is there pressure on the collective version — a city's view of justice is deliberated, internally contested, and revisable, so it is not obviously analogous to a perceptual appearance that simply happens to a perceiver — and if the analogy breaks, does the argument get easier or harder for Protagoras?
