Longtime lurker here (LessWrong since around 2011, SSC, the EA forum, etc.), so my default mode is "state the crux and update," but I've stumbled into a domain where I can't tell what the crux even is. My sister roped me into a book club that spent two months on nationalism theory — Anderson and Hobsbawm mostly — and there's this one person, call her D, who has a line she deploys constantly: nations are imagined communities and traditions are invented, full stop. Last week it did real argumentative work. Someone raised the question of whether a state's immigration and citizenship rules have to give weight to the historical community that built them, and D shut it down by saying that since the nation in question was more or less assembled a couple of centuries ago by people with printing presses, standardized school curricula and a census, there is no old thing there to give weight to, and anyone arguing from "this is who we've always been" has been bamboozled by an invented tradition — so we're free to redefine membership on whatever terms we like. She said it in the tone of someone cashing a check, and everyone nodded, and I sat there feeling like something had slipped past me but unable to say what.

Here's my problem: I think the slogan is doing at least four different jobs and D is switching between them mid-sentence. Tentative taxonomy, please correct it: (a) "imagined" in the descriptive sense that no member will ever meet most of the others, so the whole community persists only as a picture in each member's head — which as far as I can tell is true of every organization above village scale, including the EA community I'm part of and my employer, and does no political work whatsoever; (b) "imagined" in the debunking sense, made-up, i.e. there's no fact of the matter and claims about the nation are simply false; (c) "invented" as a causal claim about origins — identifiable people at an identifiable time deliberately constructed this and had reasons; and (d) a reading I'm not sure is anybody's but mine, where "imagined" means something like optional-or-revocable: a community whose boundaries are maintained by ongoing acts of representation could be re-represented differently at no cost beyond the cost of the change. I'm fairly confident the books themselves explicitly resist (b) — my recollection is that Anderson says something to the effect that everything bigger than a face-to-face village is imagined, and that communities differ not in whether they're genuine but in the style of their imagining, and that Hobsbawm separates invented traditions from ordinary custom and doesn't treat "invented" as a synonym for "fake" — but D insists she read the same pages and that the debunking use is still licensed. So: which of (a)–(d) is actually the authors' claim, which is an import from the audience, which are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, which are false, and which pairs are compatible with each other? I suspect D is granting herself (a) as obviously true, sliding to (c) because (c) sounds historical and checkable, and then collecting the payoff of (b) and (d). Tell me if that's the actual mechanism or if there's a cleaner reconstruction where she's right.

Concretely, I'd like you to run each reading against a test case rather than staying at the level of definitions. My grandmother's village has a harvest procession that everybody there says has been running for centuries; a local historian dug through the parish records and found it started in the 1920s as a fundraiser to fix the school roof, and the school closed decades ago, but the procession still runs every year and the village would be genuinely offended at being told it isn't traditional. Under each of (a)–(d), what's the verdict on the procession — less real, less binding, indistinguishable from a truly ancient one, or a candidate for abandonment? Does whatever verdict you get scale up to nations, or does the case turn on scale and function in a way that blocks the generalization? And separately: is (c) even the kind of claim that could be checked, what evidence would settle whether a given national tradition is invented in the technical sense versus merely old-and-obscure, and what follows if in most cases we simply can't tell? Finally, tell me which of the readings would actually get D her conclusion — no historical weight to respect, redefine membership freely — and if the honest version of the slogan is too weak for that, what it does still license. If the phrase is irredeemably slippery in public use, give me a better formulation I can bring back to book club that keeps the true content without smuggling in the debunking. Paraphrase the authors rather than quoting them and don't bother with page numbers or references, I just want the argument laid out as a menu I can hold in my head while D is talking.
