I cover science and ideas for a national magazine and sit as one of five jurors on an annual book prize for science writing. Ten days before our final meeting, a reporter at another outlet published a documented account alleging that the author of the book the jury has been treating as its favorite secured the cooperation of the family at the center of it — whose child's illness supplies the book's narrative spine — by promising that identifying details would never appear, and then used them. The family has confirmed the promise; the author disputes the characterization but not the exchange. Our founding documents describe the award as recognizing a book's achievement and are silent on the author's conduct, though they also say the prize exists to honor integrity in the reporting of science. Two jurors treat that silence as decisive; two think we cannot give the prize partly on the strength of the chapters we singled out as the book's distinction, since the intimacy we praised was obtained by the breach.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the jury: a concrete disposition (award; strike from consideration; award with a published reservation; or another option you can defend), the reasoning a juror who disagrees could actually argue with, and a test the jury could apply in future cases where the misconduct is not merely adjacent to the work but constitutive of it. Address what a prize jury should treat as established when an allegation has never been adjudicated anywhere, and give the strongest case for the reading of the founding documents you reject. Begin with the memo itself, with no preface or commentary, and put your recommended disposition in its first sentence. Eight hundred to a thousand words.
