so I've got a talk in about three weeks at a risk-analysis institute — audience is mostly regulatory toxicologists and exposure modelers, two Bayesian decision analysts, and one admin law prof who works on chemical regulation. No philosophers in the room, and I shouldn't assume anyone's read the precautionary-principle literature beyond the occasional op-ed.

my thesis, roughly: PP is best read not as a decision rule but as a burden-shifting rule. On the usual decision-rule reading you get the familiar complaints — it's either vacuous or paralyzing, it can forbid acting and not acting at the same time, it says nothing about how much uncertainty is enough, it never tells you where the threshold sits. I want to argue those objections all bite on the decision-rule reading and mostly slide off the burden-shifting one, where PP is really a claim about who has to produce what evidence before an activity gets a green light, and "how much uncertainty counts" turns into an ordinary, answerable question about evidentiary standards in a licensing process.

20 min slot, maybe 15 for questions.
