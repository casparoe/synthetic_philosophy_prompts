So, background: I'm a backend engineer, zero formal philosophy, and I got dragged into a thing at work. My team has this governance doc for our on-call/escalation rules (there's a long-running joke calling it our constitution), and last year everyone voted to lock one rule so it can't be amended for five years — a deliberate self-binding move. Somebody defended it with the Ulysses story, tie yourself to the mast while you're calm so the future you can't do something stupid. I said okay but that only works if the guy doing the tying and the guy who gets tied are the same guy, and while chasing down whether anyone had actually made that argument I ended up in an 1789 letter from Jefferson to Madison claiming one generation has no right to bind the next. It is way harder to read than I expected and I don't trust my own reading. Here's the chunk I'm stuck on, starting from the premise he announces:

> I set out on this ground which I suppose to be self evident, “that the earth belongs in usufruct to the living;” that the dead have neither powers nor rights over it.

then, after a bunch of stuff about inheritance and creditors:

> To keep our ideas clear when applying them to a multitude, let us suppose a whole generation of men to be born on the same day, to attain mature age on the same day, and to die on the same day, leaving a succeeding generation in the moment of attaining their mature age all together. Let the ripe age be supposed of 21. years, and their period of life 34. years more, that being the average term given by the bills of mortality to persons who have already attained 21. years of age. Each successive generation would, in this way, come on and go off the stage at a fixed moment, as individuals do now. Then I say the earth belongs to each of these generations during it's course, fully, and in their own right. The 2d. generation receives it clear of the debts and incumbrances of the 1st., the 3d. of the 2d. and so on.

and then the next paragraph, which is where I really get lost. He runs some arithmetic off Buffon's mortality tables that I skipped, and lands here:

> What is true of a generation all arriving to self-government on the same day, and dying all on the same day, is true of those on a constant course of decay and renewal, with this only difference. A generation coming in and going out entire, as in the first case, would have a right in the 1st year of their self dominion to contract a debt for 33. years, in the 10th. for 24. in the 20th. for 14. in the 30th. for 4. whereas generations changing daily, by daily deaths and births, have one constant term beginning at the date of their contract, and ending when a majority of those of full age at that date shall be dead.

> Then 19. years is the term beyond which neither the representatives of a nation, nor even the whole nation itself assembled, can validly extend a debt.

What I want help with, roughly in order of how much I care:

- The first model is obviously an idealization — everybody synchronized, generations swapping in and out like a batch job. But in the real rolling case he says it's the same thing "with this only difference," and yet the answer changes from a clean slate every generation to a 19-year timer. Is that really just a difference of degree, or has the claim quietly gotten weaker/different? What principle actually generates the number — is it something like "once most of the adults who voted are dead, the survivors were never represented"? And why a majority of people who were of full age at the signing date, rather than a majority of everyone alive, or unanimity?
- He says 34 years in one paragraph and 33 in the next. Carelessness, or is there a reason?
- Does the whole thing depend on the property premise? A debt is a claim on land and its output, which is why "the dead can't own it" bites. A rule or a law isn't obviously that kind of thing, so I can't tell whether the argument would carry over to laws and constitutions at all (I think he later claims it does, but that's from memory, so treat it as unverified). Where's the weakest link in extending it?
- The Ulysses comparison. Is Jefferson's objection that a society stretched across time isn't one continuing agent, so there's no single self available to precommit — or that it is one agent but the later members never consented? Those feel like genuinely different objections with different consequences and I can't tell which one the text is doing. My suspicion is the synchronized-generation model exists precisely to make the generations into separate agents, which would mean the rolling case is where he's exposed. Am I reading that in, or is it there?
