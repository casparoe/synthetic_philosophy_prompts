I am putting together a session for a graduate reading group on the interwar socialist calculation debate, and instead of re-running Mises against Lange on their own terms I want to stage a counterfactual with an earlier witness. Charles Babbage died in 1871, half a century before the argument that rational allocation is impossible without market prices, and well before the reply that a central board could imitate the Walrasian auctioneer by successive approximation. Yet he is a strangely apt judge. In his 1832 study of machinery and manufactures he tried to show how a master manufacturer must decompose a complex product into its constituent operations, determine precisely how much skill each operation actually requires, and apportion labour and cost accordingly — the analysis later tagged with his name and drawn on by Marx in *Capital* when discussing the division of labour. He was at the same time a producer of logarithm tables compiled by teams of human computers, a would-be founder of statistical inquiry into industrial life, and a man who spent years trying to persuade the British Treasury to underwrite a machine that could carry out long calculations without a human hand at the crank — an arrangement that eventually collapsed, with the engine unfinished at his death. Now suppose Babbage were handed the contemporary exhibit that defenders of computational planning like to cite: a retailer or platform firm of continental scale that moves hundreds of billions of dollars of goods each year through internal transfers that are set by algorithm rather than by price, with no market clearing most of what passes between its own warehouses, fulfilment centres, and delivery fleets. Which side would he take?

I would like a real answer rather than a diplomatic split. Would Babbage have seen that firm's logistics system as the vindication his Analytical Engine never received in his lifetime, and concluded that a Lange-style planning board equipped with sufficient computation could indeed solve the problem Mises posed — perhaps even that his own factory analysis had already contained the solution in embryo? Or would his experience push him the other way, toward Hayek's insistence that the knowledge that matters is dispersed, local, and largely unarticulable: had he not learned, in specifying operations for his engine's workshops and in pricing the labour of his own collaborators, how much detail resists being written down in advance for a distant administrator? Consider also the possibility that he would reject the framing altogether — for instance by distinguishing the problem of computing an optimum from the problem of knowing what to compute, or by denying that a single firm's internal hierarchy is even the right analogue for a whole economy.
