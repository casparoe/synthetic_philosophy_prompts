I'm one of three faculty on my department's doctoral exam appeals panel, and I've been asked to circulate the written decision before we meet Wednesday, hence this slightly frazzled email. Here's the case. A fourth-year PhD student in cognitive neuroscience failed the methods section of her qualifying exam. Her dissertation uses a pretty standard preprocessing and multivariate-decoding pipeline for fMRI data, and one of her examiners, who works on replication and measurement, pressed her about a specific choice inside that pipeline (a smoothing parameter, roughly) that a couple of recent papers suggest can inflate the very kind of effect she's studying. She didn't take the bait and argue it. What she said, per the transcript, was that she couldn't treat the pipeline as an open question and still have a dissertation, because every piece of evidence she could be shown against it — the validation studies, the simulations, the cross-lab replications — gets read through assumptions of the same kind, and that a doubt which puts the whole frame in question isn't a doubt with an answer waiting for it, it's just noise. Two of the three examiners scored that as refusal to engage and called it dogmatism; the third thought she was making a real point badly. Her appeal leans on Wittgenstein's On Certainty and the idea of hinge propositions, and her advisor says the examiners effectively demanded she justify her framework from outside it, which on that picture can't be done at all.

So I need the panel's decision memo, and I don't want it to be just "we sympathize, appeal denied." Please draft it. It should (1) say plainly what the panel ought to do — uphold the fail, overturn it, or send it back for a re-exam under conditions we spell out — with reasons the student and the examiners could actually push back on rather than talk past; (2) propose criteria the department could apply in future orals, i.e. some workable test for telling a commitment that genuinely functions hinge-like (where declining to argue for it isn't an epistemic fault) apart from an immunizing move that protects a theory by refusing to look at the challenge — and I'd like you to sort through the obvious comparisons for that second category, Popper's worry about ad hoc rescue stratagems (he has a name for it, something with "conventionalist" in it, but don't trust my label), the Duhem-Quine thought that any claim can be kept no matter what happens if enough other adjustments get made elsewhere in the system, and Lakatos on degenerating research programs. Tell me which of those comparisons actually bites in this case and which one is being overclaimed, because I suspect at least one is a trap; (3) take the losing side seriously. The examiner I respect most thinks that the moment hinge-talk gets into the rubric at all, we've handed every student a get-out-of-criticism card, and honestly I feel the force of that.

Two more things. Our chair keeps glossing the doctrine for people as "Wittgenstein showed that some of our beliefs are simply beyond rational criticism, and science rests on a handful of them" — fold in a line or two on whether that's fair or a distortion, since the memo will be read by people who only know the view through him. And I took a Wittgenstein course years ago and what stuck was the riverbed business, the idea that some propositions harden into channels for the fluid ones and that hardening and loosening happens over time, which if right would matter a lot for the rubric: a hinge this year isn't automatically a hinge in ten. But I don't trust my recall. So be explicit about which claims I've just made about On Certainty are the settled core of the text, which are one contested reading among several — including whether Wittgenstein is putting forward a positive epistemology at all, whether hinge commitments are even truth-apt, and whether stretching this from ordinary Moore-style cases to a scientific methodology is a legitimate application or a hijack — and which specialists would reject outright. Don't let the memo state as consensus something people who know this stuff are still fighting over, and mark where you're least sure. No bibliography please, I can't chase sources before Wednesday. Aim for maybe 800–1100 words, something a committee could vote on, and put the very last line in exactly this form and nothing after it: Verdict: UPHOLD / Verdict: OVERTURN / Verdict: REMAND (pick one).
