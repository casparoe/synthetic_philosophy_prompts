I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether the genome is literally a code, and neither of us won, so I am bringing the argument to you in a more disciplined form. She does synthetic biology; I work on the philosophy of biology but have never touched a pipette, which is part of why the argument kept sliding. Her position is that the codon table is a code in the same sense that ASCII is a code: an arbitrary mapping between symbols and what they stand for, implemented by machinery that consults the mapping, so that the genome is genuinely a message rather than merely a cause. My position is that this is a metaphor we have stopped noticing, because the mapping is not consulted by anything but is instead built out of molecular affinities — anticodon pairing, synthetase specificity — so that what we call the code is a description of a causal mechanism rather than a rule anyone follows. Around two in the morning we managed to agree on a rough test, which I will state as our apparatus and which you should feel free to attack rather than merely apply. Something counts as a code, we said, only if it passes three tests: the sign-to-meaning assignment must be arbitrary in the sense that it could have been otherwise without the whole system failing; some component must use the assignment, rather than merely embody it, in determining what happens next; and the message must survive recoding, so that you can swap the mapping and keep the content. We also flagged a fourth worry without resolving it: whether there is anywhere in the system that corresponds to a codebook — an item distinct from both the message and the machinery that could in principle be rewritten.

Now I want the apparatus run on a specific episode rather than on intuitions, and the episode is the discovery, beginning at the very end of the 1970s and consolidating in the early 1980s, that the code is not one code. When human mitochondrial DNA was first sequenced, it turned out that the codon read as a stop signal in the standard table is read in vertebrate mitochondria as tryptophan, that the codon read elsewhere as isoleucine is read there as methionine, and that two codons read elsewhere as arginine function there as stop signals; in ciliates such as Tetrahymena and Paramecium the two codons that serve as stops in the standard table read as glutamine instead. Biologists have since catalogued dozens of such variant tables across mitochondria, ciliates, and bacteria. The coda to the episode is the synthetic one: in the last decade or so one group rewrote every amber stop codon in an E. coli genome into the ochre stop, deleted the release factor that had been reading amber, and thereby freed the amber codon to be reassigned to a non-canonical amino acid, producing a viable lineage; another group went further and assembled a wholly synthetic E. coli chromosome that uses only sixty-one codons, having replaced every instance of two serine codons and of amber throughout the genome with synonymous alternatives. Take these two halves of the story — the natural variants and the engineered rewrites — and analyze them through our three tests, one test at a time. For each test, say what the episode puts on its side of the ledger and what it puts against: whether the variant tables show that the assignments really could have been otherwise, or instead show that only a narrow set of reassignments is chemically reachable, which would make the arbitrariness look like an artifact of our describing the mapping as a table at all; whether anything in a mitochondrion with its reduced transfer-RNA set, or in the recoded cell with one release factor deleted, is properly said to use the mapping as opposed to be the mapping — and here I want you to confront the fact that deleting a single protein changed what a codon meant; and whether what survives a genome-wide recoding is a message in any sense my friend would accept, given that what is held constant is the set of proteins produced rather than anything like a content that could have been expressed in other symbols. Then say where the apparatus simply has no purchase, and I suspect there is such a place, because the standard explanation of why variant codes are rare and confined to genomes that have shrunk is a frozen-accident story of the kind associated with Crick in the late 1960s: the assignments were arbitrary once, but every subsequent change became lethal, so the present table is neither convention nor necessity but a relic. If that story is right, tell me whether the code is still a code, or whether the episode shows that codehood is a property a system can have had and lost while continuing to look exactly the same from the outside.

Finally, and this matters to me more than settling the argument with her: tell me what the episode shows about the apparatus itself. Was our three-test account confirmed by the case, embarrassed by it, or is it a case the account was never built to handle — and if the tests need repair, say which one fails first and how you would reformulate it. Please be explicit about how much confidence you have in each of your main claims, and single out the one point in your analysis where you think you are most likely to be wrong or to be leaning on a contested reading of the biology, rather than distributing a uniform air of assurance over the whole thing. I would also like the answer as continuous prose, the way one writes a long letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bolded phrases, no tables, and no lists of further references, since I cannot chase them anyway. Let the argument develop in paragraphs, and if you need to distinguish the three tests as you go, do it in sentences.
