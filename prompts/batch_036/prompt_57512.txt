I'm on the board of a 40-unit housing co-op and we've been stuck for four months in a fight that I've started to think is really a logic problem in disguise.

Our bylaws, Article 12:
- 12(a): any provision may be amended by a two-thirds vote at a general meeting.
- 12(b): Article 12 itself may be amended only by three-quarters, and only if the same amendment is confirmed at a second meeting at least 30 days later.
- 12(c): no amendment may take away members' right to vote on amendments.

In March, 81% voted to replace Article 12 with a new one reading: amendments now require unanimous written consent of every member, and Article 12 may not be amended at all. It was confirmed at the April meeting with 88%.

Dev, who voted against it, has an argument that new Article 12 is void — and not just void, but that nothing done under it can be valid either. He wrote it out in steps and mailed it to the whole membership:

(1) An amendment is valid only if some rule in force at the time authorizes it.
(2) No rule can confer validity on itself; validity always has to come from somewhere else.
(3) Old 12 authorized the replacement of old 12 — that's what 12(b) is there for.
(4) But new Article 12 takes effect at the same moment old 12 is repealed. So at the instant the new rule starts being law, the rule that authorized it is no longer law.
(5) A rule whose authorizing source has been extinguished isn't valid. Hence new 12 is void and old 12 still governs.
(6) Likewise for anything amended under new 12, and for old 12 itself once you notice it was adopted under an earlier version that got replaced...
(7) And the original bylaws weren't adopted under any prior rule at all — there was no constitution before the constitution. So the chain begins with an unauthorized act.
(8) Therefore either all our bylaws are void, or their validity comes from outside them, from members simply going along. And if that's where validity comes from, Article 12 isn't doing any work; it's a memo about what we'd have done anyway.

He says somebody told him this is Ross's paradox about self-amending rules, and that ours is a worse version because of 12(c).

The conclusion is obviously too strong — Dev is still paying his assessments under bylaws he claims are void, and if (8) holds no legislature could ever repeal its own enabling statute. But I can't locate the bad step. My hunch is (4) to (5) does something with time thats not allowed, except Dev says the temporal objection misses the point, because the new rule has authority only if it inherits it from the old one, and inheriting from something dead is precisely what's in question. So: which step actually fails, and why did every step look fine to me on a first read? Then price the alternatives. If I drop (2) instead, haven't I licensed a body voting itself permanent power? If I drop (1), what's left of the claim that we're ruled by rules rather than by whoever turns up? And seperate question — does Dev get to say the unanimity requirement violates 12(c), on the grounds that a right to vote you can never exercise is no right, or is that a different argument with different problems?
