I'm staff counsel to a state clemency board — five members, we make a recommendation, the governor can ignore it. We get maybe eight or nine capital petitions a year and each one gets about three weeks from a two-person analyst team, none of whom are philosophers and none of whom have time to read anything. Our worksheet right now asks one question in five different costumes: is there enough doubt or enough unfairness here that we shouldn't let this execution go forward. Its a mess and it lets members smuggle their priors in under whatever heading they like.

Here's the hunch I want you to build on. I think there are at least three genuinely separate reasons to commute that we keep collapsing into one another, and they need different evidentiary standards, different sources, and they fail in different ways: (1) doubt about whether this person actually committed the crime; (2) the sentence is out of line with what comparably situated people got, including his own co-defendants; (3) desert doesn't transfer across time — the 62-year-old on the table isn't, in whatever sense retributivism actually needs, the person who merited death at 28. Add whatever grounds you think I'm missing, and tell me if you think one of these three isn't really a separate ground.

Write the procedure. I want: the questions in the order an analyst should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the places where it terminates in "deny," and every point where it stops being procedure and hands the call to the board because the remaining question is about values rather than facts. Where a standard can't be reduced to a threshold, say so plainly instead of inventing one. Then tell me which conceptual distinctions you deliberately dropped because an analyst working from a cold trial record can't apply them, and what we lose by dropping them.

Run it on three cases:

- Guilt is solid, confirmed, nobody disputes it. He was nineteen, three co-defendants who were at least as culpable took pleas and are alive today, and he's been unblemished for thirty-four years. I expect our procedure to say something here, but I want to know whether it says it on ground (2), ground (3), or neither.
- No individualized doubt about this defendant at all. But the conviction leaned heavily on a forensic technique that has since been broadly repudiated, and there's a documented cluster of exonerations built on the same technique in the same era. Nobody has any new evidence about *him*. Does the procedure let that kind of information carry weight, and how is it different from someone telling us "roughly four percent of people convicted this way turn out to be innocent"? Be precise about whether that number is even the right shape of thing to feed into a decision about one man.
- He's probably guilty, the disparity argument is weak, but he has advanced dementia and there's a real question whether he understands what's being done to him or why. I know there's doctrine saying you can't execute someone who doesn't grasp it, but our board treats that as the courts' business. Does the procedure have anywhere honest to put this, or does it have to say "not our question"?

One of those three should come out in a way you think I won't like. Say which and why.

Also include: a section on what the procedure can't do and where it'll misfire, including why deterrence and denunciation shouldn't appear anywhere on a single-case worksheet even though they're central to why the institution exists; what an analyst does when the record is silent, which for grounds (2) and (3) is most of the time, because a trial record was built to answer a different question than either of those; and a one-page version for board members who will not read the analyst version. Flag anything that's really a judgment call dressed up as a step. Plain English, no citations or bibliography — if there's a distinction from punishment theory or the law of proof that I haven't named, describe it in your own words and I'll chase down the sources myself.

Around two thousand words total. And be willing to tell me that one of my three grounds is bogus.
