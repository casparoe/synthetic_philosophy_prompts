For twenty-two years I worked on the shop floor and then as a full-time officer, and since 2019 I have been the education co-ordinator for our regional council of unions. I have no degree and no academic training of any kind. What I do have is a two-day residential course, which I wrote, on how other countries' labour markets actually work, and an obligation to set a written assessment at the end of it.

The students number about twenty. Most left school at sixteen or eighteen. Between them they have decades of experience of bargaining rounds, disciplinary hearings, redundancy negotiations, and of living inside the benefits system as claimants. They are not stupid and they are not in awe of print, but they are not readers of academic social science, and I will not pretend otherwise in the exam paper.

The constraints: forty-five minutes, one essay question, twenty-five marks, closed book, no internet. Two tutors mark it, neither of whom is an economist, so the scheme has to be workable by someone with a checklist and a conscience. No bibliographies, no named authors, no jargon I have not taught on the course; if a technical term is genuinely unavoidable, the model answer must define it in one line.

Here is the material I have written myself, in the voice of the briefing papers that arrive in our inbox every month. It is not a quotation from anything that exists and must not be presented as one; students will be told it is an extract from a research institute's policy paper.

> "Denmark combines one of the highest tax burdens in Europe with unemployment below the European average, a flexible labour market, and a workforce that is among the most productive in the world. Its citizens report some of the highest levels of trust in one another and in their government of any population on earth. What this shows is that high taxes and generous benefits do not destroy the willingness to work, provided they are accompanied by an active approach to getting people back into jobs. There is no cultural or historical reason why these arrangements cannot be reproduced elsewhere. The obstacle in this country is not economic but political: no government has yet been willing to pay for the scheme properly or to explain it honestly to the public. We therefore recommend the introduction of a Danish-style earnings-replacement benefit of up to ninety per cent of previous pay for the first year of unemployment, funded from general taxation, together with a statutory obligation to accept suitable work or training from the first month of a claim. Any country that adopts these two measures can expect Danish outcomes; a country that adopts only one should expect to fail, and its failure will not be evidence against the model."

What I want from you is four things.

First, the exam question in the exact wording my students will see, printed above the passage. It must ask them to identify what they judge to be the single most serious problem with the argument, and to explain what would have to be true for the proposal to survive that problem. I do not want a "list the weaknesses" question. Listing is what my students are already good at; it tells me nothing about whether they understand.

Second, a model answer of about seven hundred words — one that a genuinely strong student could have produced in forty-five minutes without notes, not a seminar paper dressed down.

Third, a marking scheme totalling twenty-five marks, built around named moves rather than impressions, that says explicitly what separates a first-class answer from a merely competent one, and what a competent answer will have missed. Include a band for the student who writes a fluent list, and say what caps it.

Fourth, three or four answers I should expect to be common and weak, with the mark each should be capped at and one line on the misunderstanding it reveals.

Two points on which I want you to be straight with me rather than agreeable. My own instinct is that the last sentence of my passage is the fatal one, since a proposal that has arranged in advance to explain away its own failure does not look capable of being wrong. Tell me whether that is right, or whether there is something worse in there that I have failed to see. And I should add two things I teach: on the Saturday afternoon we spend two hours on how far Denmark, Sweden, Norway and Finland differ from one another, and the students are consistently startled by how far; and I teach that the Danish unemployment benefit is not paid by the tax office but through funds a claimant must have joined in advance, most of them connected to the unions, and that it is capped at a level well below ninety per cent of a good wage. I do not know how much weight either of those facts can bear in a forty-five-minute answer, and I would rather you told me than quietly left them out.

If you think the passage is too easy or too hard, or that the question will not discriminate between the students who understand and the students who remember, say so in a couple of lines at the end. I would far rather hear it now than during the marking.
