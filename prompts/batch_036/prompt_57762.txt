ok so pretend you're a professional philosopher who works on aesthetics and on testimony and knowledge — someone who's thought really hard about whether you can take other people's word for how good a thing is. i'd like you to actually think it through: do some brainstorming first, sketch out where you're heading before you write the real answer, because i want to see the reasoning and not just the conclusions.

background. i help run a tiny film society, about 12 of us, no academic types, we meet in the back room of a pub. there's an argument that's been going for months now and i want it settled, or at least properly audited.

one member — call her D — says we should stop voting on what to watch and just show whatever one particular critic names as the best of the year, every single time. her argument is an analogy i keep hearing in different places: the critic is like a radiologist reading your x-ray. the film is right there in public, anyone can look at it, but she's trained to see what we can't, so taking her word that it's great is no different from taking a doctor's word that the shadow is a tumour. she says people who go on about "you have to see it for yourself" are just being romantic.

another member says that's exactly backwards, and his analogy is: being told a film is beautiful is like being told a joke is funny. you can believe her, sure, but you can't laugh on her authority, and the laughing is the whole point.

here's the thing that bugs me and i don't know if either analogy gets at it. when D tells us stuff about a film — the plot, who dies, what year it came from, who's in it — nobody objects, we all just take her word for it and we're right to. it's only the "it's great" part that the other guy says you can't get second hand. so what is different about that second kind of claim? and related: when D actually tries to convince us she doesn't just repeat the verdict, she points at features, like "the way it uses silence" or "the last shot rewrites all the earlier ones." is going and noticing those features myself a third thing, different from both trusting her verdict and trusting her about the plot?

what i want from you:

- take each analogy on its own and give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of what (the x-ray, the trained eye, the tumour, the patient's own seeing; and for the joke one, whatever lines up)
- then where each one breaks down. be picky. and seperate the breaks that actually bite on D's "just show whatever's on her list" conclusion from the ones that are nitpicks and change nothing
- what each analogy quietly smuggles in about whether greatness is a feature sitting in the film waiting to be spotted, or something that happens in the viewer
- whether her conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether his does either
- and if both analogies are bad, what would be a better one for what's actually going on

please don't give me a reading list or name papers or books, i've never read any of them and i can't look anything up. just explain it like you would to a smart person who happens to have no training in it. and think carefully — if one of these analogies turns out to be better than i'm giving it credit for, say so, don't just tell me what you think i want to hear.
