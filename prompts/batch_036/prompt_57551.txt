Background: for about three years I've been running a small community inside a persistent-world sandbox MMO — I'll just call it Ashen Reach, the real name doesn't matter — and part of the job is adjudicating disputes: stolen builds, land grabs, one guy who made a hobby of hunting new players' characters until they rage-quit. To keep myself honest I wrote down the principles I actually appeal to when I write those rulings. A friend in my philosophy-of-mind reading group read the list and said it's incoherent in at least two places, then wouldn't say where, which is infuriating. So I want an outside audit. Here's the list, roughly as I wrote it:

(1) Virtual realism, more or less the line Chalmers defends: the stuff in a virtual world is real stuff, not a picture or a pretense of stuff. A virtual axe in Ashen Reach is a genuinely existing digital object — a pattern realized in the engine that actually does what axes do in that world — and "it's only a game" is not an answer to the question of whether it exists.
(2) So virtual events are real events and virtual harms are real harms. When someone levels a build another player sank 400 hours into, that's an actual wronging that happened, not a symbolic or make-believe one. I specifically don't want to cash this out as "and it upset him" — the wrong is in what was done to his stuff, not in his reaction to it.
(3) Wrongdoing needs a victim, though. Nothing is wrong unless it sets back the interests of something that has interests — suffering, frustrated projects, lost options. Disgust and "that's just sick" don't count as reasons on my list.
(4) Characters that are just game AI have no experiences and no interests. Killing an NPC is in the same moral category as deleting rows in a spreadsheet. I'm not at all tempted by the idea that in-game characters might matter morally.
(5) Single-player story games are fiction. I play a lot of them, and I'd say the protagonist doesn't exist and the plot never happened. Someone who plays a murderer in a story game hasn't done anything wrong — there's nobody there.
(6) The value of virtual things comes entirely from what real people do with them and mean by them. Nothing in the bits is precious in and of it's self. An axe on a dead server nobody logs into is, in the only sense I care about, nothing.
(7) Whether something is wrong shouldn't depend on which side of the screen it happened on. Same agent, same intentions, same real-world consequences: same verdict, game or no game.

Two cases I actually rule on, which is where I think the trouble starts. First: a player built a memorial garden in Ashen Reach for her late husband — a real one, he died — and another player found it and deliberately razed it, saying he was just playing the game. I told him that was one of the worst things anyone had done on my server, worse than stealing ten times the market value in materials, and I meant it. Second: there's a single-player horror game with a mod that lets you do extremely graphic things to photorealistic children, and I think that mod is genuinely wrong to make and wrong to use, even though by my own lights nobody in it is harmed. A classmate also threw two objections at me that I honestly can't evaluate: (a) if virtual objects are "real," aren't you just saying fictional characters are real, i.e. taking an ontological free lunch nobody should accept? and (b) how can something be real if it blinks out of existence the second the server shuts down — doesn't (1) require virtual things to be independent of the machine, and doesn't (6) make them dependent on people's attitudes instead?

So: go through it and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other, ranked by severity rather than as a flat list, and for each one say what would have to give, what the cheapest repair is, and what that repair costs me elsewhere — including in the two cases above, since a fix that makes me retract the garden ruling or the mod ruling isn't a fix I'll take. Also tell me which of my worries are overblown, including whether (a) and (b) are real problems or whether I'm conflating something. And don't assume I'm attached to any particular item: if the overall cheapest package is to drop (1) or (2) and be some kind of fictionalist about game worlds, say so plainly and spell out what I'd lose.
