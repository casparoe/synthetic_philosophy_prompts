I coordinate a year-long interdisciplinary seminar spanning philosophy of biology and comparative cognition. Two camps in the group have been arguing past each other since September, and I would like to force them to produce a joint document.

Camp A defends the fourfold classification of minds into Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures — not as a teaching device, but as marking real discontinuities. On their view each rung adds a genuinely new form of control over behaviour, and the top two rungs in particular are not explicable as the rung below plus more memory, more search, or more trials.

Camp B is a continuist. They accept that the ladder describes real phenomena and they teach with it happily, but they hold that every rung dissolves under sustained pressure: what Camp A calls Popperian cognition is operant conditioning operating over internally represented states, and what Camp A calls Gregorian cognition is cumulative social niche construction — an existing capacity amplified by external scaffolding rather than a new kind of mind. On Camp B's account the ladder is a genuine gradient, and the further claim that its steps are steps in kind is a taxonomic stipulation dressed up as a discovery.

Both have agreed to an adversarial collaboration. Write the joint statement that the two of them could put their names to.

It should contain the following.

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. Include at minimum: that evolution is continuous and admits of no sharp boundaries; that the ladder has real descriptive and pedagogical utility; that there are genuinely hard intermediate cases, with named examples drawn from the comparative literature (tool traditions that may or may not be cumulative, symbol-system training in great apes, solitary invertebrates that solve novel detour and jar-opening problems, species that plan cache recovery without any apparent shared symbols); and that some form of internal modelling of the consequences of behaviour occurs well below the rung where Camp A wants to locate it.

2. The residual disagreements, each stated three times over: once in wording Camp A would accept as a fair description of their own position, once in wording Camp B would accept for theirs, and then a neutral formulation of the issue that neither side thinks misstates it. Camp B must not be characterised as denying the phenomena, and Camp A must not be characterised as positing evolutionary saltations.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling any one of them would actually move a side. The candidates I have in mind are (a) whether the criterion for a new rung is mechanistic — a novel computational resource — or functional, a novel ecological payoff; (b) whether the image of hypotheses dying in the organism's stead rather than the organism dying is a mechanism claim or a metaphor; (c) whether external symbolic scaffolding changes what counts as an explanation of a given behaviour, or merely widens the range of an explanatory schema already in place; (d) whether discreteness in kind is an empirical question at all, or a question about the purposes a taxonomy serves. Say which of these turn out to be the same question in different vocabulary, and rank the remainder by how much movement they would produce.

4. For each surviving crux, one concrete case, experiment, dataset, or thought experiment about an engineered system that both sides would agree bears on it, together with each side's prediction recorded in advance — stated so that being wrong would be falsifying, or at least embarrassing.

In addition, the seminar has been circling one specific exchange, and I want the document to resolve it explicitly. Camp B's standard move is what we have been calling the rung-collapse argument: take any rung and show that its signature capacity can be reproduced by the rung below plus a quantitative increase in memory, in search, or in number of learning trials. Camp A's standard reply is that this argument is not uniformly available, because at some rung the increase required ceases to be merely quantitative. The document should say where the rung-collapse argument succeeds, where it fails, and whether the disagreement about it concerns the comparative facts or concerns the threshold at which an explanation counts as complete.

Finally, if you conclude that some portion of the dispute is verbal, say which portion and why, and state what each camp is left disagreeing about once that portion is set aside — including whether what remains is a disagreement about the world or about the job descriptions of a classification scheme.

Format: a numbered agreed-claims section; a disagreements table with columns for the neutral statement of the issue, Camp A's formulation, and Camp B's formulation; a cruxes section with the paired predictions; and a short closing section separating the verbal from the substantive. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations — I want the argumentative substance, not the sourcing. Keep both camps anonymous and do not attribute any position here to a living researcher.
