I am a novelist by trade, and for two years I have been sitting in on a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind and medicine at the university where my partner teaches; this spring the convenor, half in jest and then half in earnest, has asked me — the seminar's resident non-philosopher — to set one of the questions for the final paper. The last month of the course has been given over to pain: the familiar two-component picture of nociception, the syndrome neurologists have called pain asymbolia, and the rival accounts of what makes pain bad for the person who has it. My current novel is narrated by a clinician in a chronic pain service, and I want the question to carry the kind of pressure a case can carry in fiction: not an abstraction to be classified, but a person whose reports are at odds with her conduct, in a way that forces the student to say where the badness of pain is supposed to live.

The case I have drafted, and which I would like you to refine, complete, and turn into an examinable question, runs roughly as follows. Call the patient H. She has had twelve years of intractable facial pain following a nerve injury, and she has enrolled in a trial of a reversible pharmacological agent that reproduces asymbolia on demand in otherwise intact subjects. Stipulate, for the sake of the question, that under the agent H. retains sensory discrimination in full: she localises a pinprick exactly, grades its intensity on the scales she has always used, and describes its quality without hesitation. She also retains every protective disposition the clinicians can test for — she flinches, she guards the affected side, she withdraws, she declines to submit to the stimulus again, and she says plainly that she would refuse a further round of whatever injured her. When pricked during examination she laughs. She reports that nothing she feels is unpleasant, and that she no longer minds. Asked to complete the questionnaires she completed before the trial, she completes them accurately, explaining that she is answering as her former self would have answered. She does not wish the agent discontinued; her family believes she has been diminished. The question I want put to the students is whether the agent, for H., is a cure or a mutilation — and I want them required, in answering, to state which account of pain's badness their verdict commits them to, to say what their own case does to the two accounts they reject (in particular, to the imperative and the evaluative treatments of unpleasantness, and to the view that identifies it with a felt quality whose aversiveness is intrinsic to it), and to say what would have to be true for H.'s testimony about her own condition to settle the matter rather than merely report it. It should be said explicitly on the paper that no credit is available for re-describing the sensory–affective dissociation, and that the marks go to the student who can explain why H. refuses a repeat of the stimulus while insisting she minds nothing. The paper is an unseen three-hour final; the candidates are a mixture of philosophy students, literature students, and a few pre-meds, and they will have read the standard material but cannot be assumed to have thought past it.

What I need from you, then, is three things. First, the question as it would actually appear on the paper: self-contained, with all of H.'s clinical details stipulated in it so that no outside knowledge of the literature is required to begin, and pitched at a level that a strong candidate can engage with in about fifty minutes of a three-hour sitting. Second, a model answer of roughly eight hundred words that takes a definite position on the cure-or-mutilation question rather than balancing it. Third, a marking scheme out of twenty-five which names the moves a first-class answer must make, the marks attached to each, and — just as importantly — the particular errors that should cost marks; I expect to find among them the treatment of asymbolia as a species of analgesia, the use of the word suffering univocally across a question in which it does at least two jobs, the assumption that a patient's report settles the metaphysics of her state, and the conflation of the imperative and evaluative positions as though they failed in the same way. Then close with a short note telling me where you think the exercise is weakest: which of these positions your model answer has probably caricatured in order to reach a verdict, whether any stipulated feature of H.'s case is medically implausible or quietly begs the question in favour of the conclusion you have her examiner defend, and what single consideration — a piece of clinical evidence, an objection from a theory you have set aside, a better description of what asymbolic patients actually do — would move you to the opposite verdict. I would much rather have the soft places in the marking scheme pointed out to me now than discover them in the examiners' meeting.
