I am putting together a seminar exercise on supertasks, and what I need is a pair of engineered cases rather than a survey of the literature. The question the pair is meant to settle is narrow: whether the standard objection to Thomson's lamp — Benacerraf's point that the lamp's state at the limit instant is not entailed by its states at all earlier instants, so that "the task has been completed" either describes nothing or quietly imports a stipulation — is an objection to supertasks as such, or only an artifact of Thomson's particular choice of a mechanism that keeps reversing itself. To test that, I need one case in which the objection genuinely fails but nothing interesting is gained, and one in which the objection fails and something interesting is gained.

**Case A is supplied. Analyse it; do not redesign it.** A lamp with a toggle switch, off at t = 0. The switch is scheduled to be thrown at t = 1/2, 3/4, 7/8, …, 1 − 2⁻ⁿ, …, so that infinitely many throws are scheduled before t = 1. The mechanism is unreliable: at the n-th scheduled instant it succeeds in changing the lamp's state with probability 4⁻ⁿ, independently of every other instant, and does nothing at all otherwise. An observer watches the lamp continuously throughout the minute.

**Case B is yours to design.** It must satisfy all of the following:

1. Its state at t = 1 is determinate, and the determinacy must be derived from the mechanism rather than asserted — no clause of the form "at t = 1 the device is then defined to be…".
2. It must not be idle in the way Case A is. What the observer learns at t = 1 must be a mathematical fact about something other than the device's own behaviour — name the specific question the device settles — and for at least one of that question's possible answers, no amount of pre-limit observation, however long the observer waits and however closely, may asymptotically settle it. In Case A the observer's warrant converges to certainty as t → 1; in Case B there must be a branch in which it does not converge at all.
3. You may use one idealization and one only: the one Thomson already used, namely infinitely many operations each of which is individually ordinary and finite, packed into a finite interval by a geometrically shrinking schedule. No operation may be performed at t = 1 itself, no component may act on the device's limit state, the observer may have no powers beyond those of an ordinary observer at earlier times, and every stage must involve only finitely much work. A mechanic should be able to build any finite initial segment of your device.
4. Be concrete enough that a reader can say exactly what the observer sees at, say, t = 0.999, and exactly what changes at t = 1.

A case that fails these counts as a failure, not as a variant: stipulating the limit state, letting the fact learned at t = 1 be a fact about what the device will do next, or upgrading the observer all defeat the purpose, since the purpose is to see whether determinacy at the limit can be bought without buying anything else. If you judge any specification unsatisfiable, say so and show why, rather than quietly weakening it. No references or bibliography — I want the reasoning.

Please answer in these sections, in this order:

1. **Case A: what the observer sees** (≤150 words). The timeline of the lamp's states as the minute proceeds, described from the observer's point of view.
2. **Determinacy in A** (~200 words). Derive the claim that the lamp has a determinate state at t = 1, and say what its state is relative to its state just before t = 1. Then handle the residue: there are histories in which the switch succeeds infinitely often. State their probability, and say whether determinacy holding only almost surely is enough to answer Benacerraf, or whether the residue reopens the objection.
3. **Idleness in A** (~200 words). Show that the observer's warrant for the proposition *the state the lamp now displays is the state it will display at t = 1* converges to certainty as t → 1, and draw the conclusion about what, if anything, the completion of this supertask adds to what was already on display.
4. **Case B: the construction** (~350 words). The device, its schedule, its components, and the question it decides.
5. **Determinacy and non-idleness in B** (~300 words). Why each relevant component's state stabilizes without any limit clause; why the fact revealed at t = 1 is mathematical rather than a fact about the device's future; and the explicit identification of the branch in which pre-limit evidence never settles the answer, contrasted point by point with the convergence in Case A.
6. **The load-bearing feature** (~150 words). Name the structural property of your construction that is doing the work. Then remove it and say precisely how Case B degenerates — in particular, whether it collapses back into Thomson's original lamp. Finally, state what class of questions a device of this kind can decide, and how that class compares to what an ordinary computer can decide.
7. **Idealizations and the sceptic's reply** (~250 words). List every idealization your Case B uses beyond the compressed schedule, and say which of them you take to be objectionable. Then give the strongest argument available to someone who holds that no physical process can complete infinitely many stages, and say whether that argument condemns Case A, Case B, and Thomson's original lamp equally, or whether it discriminates among them — and if it discriminates, on what grounds.
