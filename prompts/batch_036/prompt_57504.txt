Our platform team is putting together a 20th-anniversary page for the company wiki about how our stack changed over the years, and two of my coworkers got into a genuinely heated argument about how to write it. I was only there to fix the diagrams, so I didn't take a side, but they've asked me to find a tiebreaker and I don't know where to start. Roughly how it went:

(1) Marcus wrote the first draft as a chain of upgrades: Perl scripts on a shared box → a PHP app → a Rails monolith → services → and now back to a modular monorepo. Each section explained what was broken about the previous setup and how the next one fixed it, and it ended with "and that's how we got here, which is a lot better."

(2) Dana said that's exactly backwards. She called it whig history or something like that. Her objection: the draft grades every old decision by whether it happened to lead to the thing we use now, so anything that got dropped reads as a mistake even if it was a reasonable bet at the time. She pointed at the section on the in-house job queue someone built in 2011 and abandoned three years later, which the draft calls "a detour."

(3) Marcus said it *was* a detour — we don't run it, and the page's whole job is to explain to a new hire why the current system looks the way it does. He said a history that refuses to call better things better isn't being neutral, it's just being useless.

(4) Dana said she isn't refusing to make judgments, she's refusing to make retroactive ones. Deploy time went from four hours to ten minutes, fine, that's real, put it in bold. But she went back through the old postmortums and most of that gain came from stuff that had nothing to do with the architecture changes: hosted CI, faster machines, and a testing culture that showed up when one specific person got hired. The draft hands the credit to whichever decisions survived, and she said that's a selection effect dressed up as a causal story.

(5) Marcus said that critique proves too much. If you can't say the surviving option was better, you have no answer for the new hire who asks why we shouldn't go back to services, or who cheerfully reinvents the 2011 job queue. He said Dana's version would read like museum labels — "the team chose X given the constraints of the period" — and people would come away thinking all the options were equally good.

(6) Dana said she'd take museum labels over a story where we're the smart ones and everyone before us was just climbing toward us. She also said his draft is unfalsifiable in the other direction: it can't imagine any present where some different set of choices would have looked just as inevitable. Marcus said any account that treats the outcome as an accident isn't explaining anything, it's just listing events.

That's where it stalled and both of them were annoyed. Can you go through moves 2 to 6 one at a time and say who's right in each? Where they're both partly right, tell me which part of the point actually lands and which part doesn't — don't just split everything down the middle. Then say who's more right overall about how the page should be written, and whether there's something neither of them is seeing. Please no general essay on the philosophy of history and no book recommendations, I just want the argument refereed.
