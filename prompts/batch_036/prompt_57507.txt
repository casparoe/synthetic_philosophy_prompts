I have been working through the St. Petersburg problem on my own, and what I want is not the history of it but an account of what my own valuations commit me to. Below are eight games and what I say about each. All of them use the same device: a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads, and n denotes the toss on which that first head appears. Amounts are in dollars. Assume each game is a one-off offer from a counterparty who can genuinely pay, that I have about $50,000 to my name and no other stakes riding, and that wherever a game could leave me owing money, anything beyond my total wealth is absorbed by the house — though I should like to be told whether that last stipulation is doing more work than I think it is.

1. Pays $2^n. Worth about $10 to me. I would not pay $100 for it, and that is not caution; I am confident.
2. Pays $2^n, capped so that it never pays more than $2^20; its expectation is therefore a finite $21. Worth about $9 — a dollar less than game 1, not twelve dollars more. The same goes for game 1 when I picture the house keeping only a million dollars in the vault.
3. Pays $2^(2^n). Worth exactly what game 1 is worth: $10, not a cent more. I cannot feel any difference between the two games, and I do not believe there is one to feel.
4. Pays $2^n + $1, that is, a dollar more than game 1 in every possible outcome. Better, and worth about $11. If your rule implies the increment should come out slightly under a dollar, I want the argument, because my instinct says exactly a dollar.
5. Pays +$2^n/n when n is odd and −$2^n/n when n is even. This game has no value for me. Not a small value, not a negative one: no value. I will not name a price to buy it or a price to take it off someone's hands. Part of my reason is that I know the corresponding series of terms, 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 …, can be rearranged to come out to anything at all.
6. Pays a dollar more than game 5 in every outcome. Strictly better than game 5 — of that I am certain — and still without a price. Better, but not worth more, because worth more does not get a grip here.
7. Pays +$2^n/n² when n is odd and −$2^n/n² when n is even. Here my reaction changes: this one does have a value, and I take it to be roughly the eighty cents the arithmetic delivers. I still would not play it.
8. A million independent plays of game 1, all purchased at a single common per-play price. I would pay about $20 per play, roughly double the single-play figure, because at that volume the large outcomes stop being hypothetical. My rough calculation puts the typical total payout around twenty million dollars, and I believe the per-play figure keeps creeping upward as the number of plays grows, with no ceiling in sight.

The rule I have been reaching for is this: two games are worth the same to me if they differ only in outcomes that will not turn up in a million plays; and one game is worth more than another if it pays at least as much in every outcome and strictly more in some. The second clause seems to give me 4 and 6. The first was meant to cover both 2 and 3, but it covers 2 and does not touch 3 at all; and 8 looks like a direct counterexample to it, since the outcomes I am licensing myself to disregard in a single play are precisely the ones that dominate a million of them.

What I would like back:

(a) One or more candidate principles, each stated as a rule precise enough that I could apply it to a payoff schedule I have not shown you. Each rule should take a schedule as input and return either a dollar value, a comparative ranking, or the verdict that there is no value — and it should be explicit which of the three it is returning in each case.
(b) For each clause of each candidate, which of my verdicts motivated that clause, and what the simpler version of the rule gets wrong.
(c) What each candidate says about three further games: one paying $2^n capped at $2^100; one paying −$2^n, that is, game 1 with the sign reversed and me on the losing side; and one paying $2^n/n!, every payoff of which is under $2 and whose expectation is about $1.72. Then add one game of your own devising that you think would decide between your candidates, and say why.
(d) For each candidate, which of my eight verdicts it cannot accommodate, and which of them you would advise me to abandon. If the simple rule costs me two verdicts and an elaborate one saves all eight, state that trade openly rather than resolving it silently in favour of the elaborate one.
(e) Whether the most I should pay to acquire a ticket and the least I should accept to surrender one come apart for game 1 given my $50,000, and whether any of my verdicts are really about that gap rather than about value.
(f) Whether games 5 and 6 are genuinely unpriceable or merely ruinous for me: is no value a substantive claim, or a polite way of saying that every price is unacceptable?

I am not asking for references, for attributions, or for a survey of how this problem has been handled elsewhere. I want the principles themselves, and an honest report on which of my verdicts they force me to give up — including, if that is your conclusion, the finding that none of them survives all eight.
