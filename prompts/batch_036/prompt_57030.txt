I have no academic training in history. For six years I have volunteered at a small county historical society, where my work has been transcribing and indexing nineteenth-century records: workhouse admission registers, the correspondence of the overseers of the poor, the minute books of a charitable society, and a long run of petitions from people asking to be granted relief at home rather than admitted indoors. Recently the society has asked me to write a short guide for new volunteers on how to describe what these records do and do not tell us, and I have found that I cannot write it until I know whether the view I have arrived at on my own can survive scrutiny.

The view is this. For people who were governed rather than represented — the pauper, the inmate, the convict, the enslaved person, the colonial subject — every document that survives was made by an office that was classifying them in order to manage them. What the archive therefore yields about such people is the institution's description of them, not their own: the categories, the recognized offenses, the admissible forms of complaint, and the shape of a legible grievance are all the office's. Practices advertised as recovering the perspective of the governed — reading against the grain, inferring intent from a hostile witness, treating a formulaic petition as strategic self-presentation — can at best reconstruct how these people were seen and what they had to become in order to be seen that way. On this view, sentences of the form "we now know what such people thought about this" are systematically overstated, and much confident social history is really a sophisticated description of bureaucratic forms with the people left in as decoration.

My reasoning in outline: first, the content of a record is shaped by the purposes and the paperwork conventions of the office that produced it; second, for governed groups the only offices keeping durable records about them were offices of control; third, so the surviving content about them is content-under-control; fourth, an uncontrolled self-description would require content that exceeds the forms, and where content does exceed the forms I cannot tell whether I am encountering a person or merely an office's blind spot.

I want a map of this view's exposure — which findings, distinctions, and arguments would actually count against it, rather than the general observation that archives are mediated. Specifically:

- What kinds of evidence would falsify my second premise? My reflex reply to any such evidence is that the document survived only because some institution chose to keep it; I suspect this retreats from a claim about production to a weaker claim about preservation. Tell me whether that is a genuine weakening and how much of the view survives it.
- Is there something about corpora rather than single documents — aggregate patterns across thousands of routine entries — that would let someone know things about the governed that no office intended to record and could not have designed its forms to yield? If so, is that a decisive counterexample or a case my view simply never addressed?
- What about the first-person material that ends up inside an institutional file, such as the petitions I transcribe weekly? State the conditions under which such a document would show the writer shaping the record rather than the record shaping the writer, and say whether meeting those conditions would refute me or only qualify me.
- Does my view prove too much? The same reasoning applied to the letters of statesmen and the minutes of legislatures seems to dissolve into a general skepticism about documents that I do not accept, which suggests I need an asymmetry premise about degrees of control. Can that premise be stated so that it is itself testable, and what would show it false?
- What does my view predict that its rivals do not? I would like at least one risky prediction it commits me to, so that I can tell whether anything at all could go against it.

Then the part I most want answered plainly: has this view been qualified into unfalsifiability? For every counterexample I can construct, I can also construct an absorbing move — mediation, format, preservation, the limits of the reader's inference — and I worry that this ease of absorption is a symptom rather than a strength. If the view is insulated, say so, and tell me whether that is a defect in a claim of this kind or whether it is doing something other than making a falsifiable assertion, for example fixing a burden of proof about how conclusions from such records ought to be phrased. Rank everything you give me from fatal to merely awkward, and distinguish the defeaters that a sincere holder of my view should concede from those they are entitled to dismiss.

Please begin with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
