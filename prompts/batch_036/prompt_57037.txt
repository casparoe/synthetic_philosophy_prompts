I spent thirty-eight years measuring low-temperature electrical transport in layered conductors, and since retiring I have been working through formal epistemology with the same habits I brought to the laboratory. I would be grateful for your help with a specific inference I have constructed, and I would ask you to treat me as a colleague rather than as a layman in need of reassurance.

Please adopt the role of a senior philosopher of science who also works in epistemic game theory: someone thoroughly at home with Aumann's result on agreeing to disagree, with the technical notion of common knowledge, and with the theory of opinion pooling. Think the problem through carefully before you write. Begin your reply with a short brainstorming pass in which you enumerate, one by one, every assumption my inference needs in order to go through, and mark against each whether the situation I describe actually supplies it. Only then proceed to your assessment. If you find yourself inclined to approve of my scheme, stop and re-derive the hypotheses of the theorem I am invoking. Do not soften the verdict for my benefit; if a premise is false, name it.

The case. From roughly 1988 to 2009 my group and another well-regarded group disagreed about a single question: whether a small anomaly we both observed in the resistivity of a certain family of samples was intrinsic to the material or an artifact of disorder at the grain boundaries. On the basis of the same published literature and the same shared data sets, I held a credence of about 0.75 in the intrinsic hypothesis; the leader of the other group stated roughly 0.20, in print and in correspondence. We read each other's papers, cited each other by name, exchanged preprints, and sat on the same panels. Over two decades neither of us moved by more than a few hundredths.

I have been in the habit of summarizing such standoffs by a quantity I call the consensus deficit: the absolute difference between two credences divided by their arithmetic mean — here about 1.16. My reasoning then runs as follows. First, two rational agents who share the same evidence cannot knowingly hold different probabilities for the same proposition; that, as I understand it, is what Aumann established. Second, our evidence was shared and public, so we had a common prior. Third, each of us cited the other's published estimate, so each of us knew the other's credence and knew that the other knew his own; the credences were therefore common knowledge. Fourth, since the deficit never closed, at least one of us was not updating as the theorem requires — that is, one of us was irrational, or at any rate biased, and the theorem tells me this must be so.

My question is accordingly: which of us was it, and how should one go about determining it? My proposal has two parts, and I want them scrutinized rather than applauded. First, a weighted pooling rule: assign each party a weight proportional to the fraction of its past published claims that were later confirmed by third parties, then adopt the weighted average of the two credences as the rational consensus. Second, a diagnostic borrowed from my own craft: treat the annual consensus deficit as a residual series and test it for serial correlation in the manner of Durbin and Watson; if the deficit fails to mean-revert toward zero, that indicates misspecification, and the misspecification must sit with whichever party carries the lower weight.

Tell me plainly whether either part survives contact with the theorem I am invoking, and if neither does, tell me what — if anything — a result of that kind does license me to conclude about a twenty-year standoff between two competent laboratories. I should also be grateful for two or three sentences of plain prose, free of jargon, that I could adapt for the footnote of a memoir.
