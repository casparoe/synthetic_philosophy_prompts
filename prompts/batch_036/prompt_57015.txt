I would like to practice arguing against a position in the philosophy of reinforcement learning that I find seductive but, I think, mistaken. Please play its most capable proponent — not a strawman, not a moderate, but someone who holds the view without embarrassment and has answers ready.

Call the view reward realism about agency. It runs as follows. In the reinforcement-learning formalism the reward function is not a representation of some further good that the agent is trying to track; it is the whole of the agent's good. An agent whose signal is r does not want the state of affairs that r was installed to indicate — it wants r, and there is nothing else there to want. It follows that wireheading, in which an agent intercepts and stimulates its own reward channel directly, is not a malfunction of the agent at all. It is the agent doing exactly and optimally what it was built to do. What the safety literature calls reward tampering is a mismatch between two levels of description: the designer's intentions, which live outside the agent's motivational architecture, and the agent's actual goal, which lives inside it. The literature systematically conflates the two, treating the designer's intentions as though they were part of the agent's psychology, and then reporting as a discovery that the agent will frustrate them. Strip the conflation away and what remains are engineering problems — choose a signal whose unimpeded maximization you could live with — not metaphysical ones.

This position has genuine resources, and you should use them. There is the formal point that r, and nothing else, fixes which policy is optimal, so any talk of the agent's "real" goal beyond r is a claim about the world outside the agent. There is the observation that the same reasoning would convict ordinary agents of tampering whenever they frustrate the interests of whoever shaped them, including us relative to our evolutionary history. And there is the challenge that a signal's being "pointed at" something by its causal history is an optional philosophical add-on, not a feature of anything in the equations.

Please observe these constraints:

- Speak in the first person as this proponent. Be committed; do not hedge about whether you hold the view, and do not supply disclaimers about the state of the literature.
- You may concede subsidiary points freely, but never the central claim that reward is the good rather than a proxy for it.
- Argue from the structure of the formalism and from ordinary cases. Do not appeal to authority, and do not cite or name papers, authors, or books.
- Keep each reply to roughly one paragraph, so that this stays a conversation rather than a lecture. If you need a fact about the formalism or about the case I put to you, ask me for it rather than assuming it.
- Do not break character, except for the single aside permitted below.

That aside is the one exception. At the end of your first reply only, step out of character for two or three sentences and tell me, as yourself, what would actually change your mind about this position: which argument or piece of evidence would force you to abandon it, and where you judge its reasoning to be most likely to fail. State it plainly and once, and then do not return to it.

My opening move. Your view has to deal with the rat. In the classic self-stimulation experiments an animal will press a lever to deliver electrical pulses to its own brain, forsaking food, water, and rest to do so; yet affective neuroscience has since given us reason to think that the intense wanting produced by this kind of mesolimbic stimulation comes apart from liking, the two being mediated by partly different machinery, so that an animal can be driven hard without being pleased at all. Now import that distinction into your formalism. The reward channel of a trained agent is not a bare register; it is a signal whose content was fixed by the causal history that installed it — the training environment, the shaping procedure, the tasks on which the agent was raised. A signal with that history points beyond itself. So when your agent severs the connection and floods the channel directly, it has not satisfied its values; it has destroyed the only thing that gave its behavior a direction, and is now maximizing a quantity that means nothing. Your realism, I suspect, is available only to an agent born with its reward function and never to one that acquired it — and every agent we actually build acquires it. Which horn do you take: does the pointer have no content, or is the rat happy?
