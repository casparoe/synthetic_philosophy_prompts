I have spent the better part of two decades reading rationalist blogs and forums, where the first question asked of any thesis is what observation would count against it. Applying that reflex to the philosophy of technology has left me stuck, because both of the standard positions appear to survive any evidence at all. On one side, the claim that artifacts carry politics in their structure can be defended for any artifact by pointing to some differential effect it has; on the other, the claim that artifacts are neutral and only people are political can be defended for any artifact by pointing to its designers and users. Neither position tells me anything I did not already know. What I want is a criterion I can apply to a description of a thing and get a definite answer from — one that could be shown wrong by a badly chosen case.

I have read Winner's 1980 essay in *Daedalus* asking whether artifacts have politics, including the parkway example, and I know that the historical particulars of that example have been disputed and that Joerges pressed an objection along those lines. I am not asking for a literature map or references; I want the criterion. Here is what I actually believe, arrived at case by case rather than derived from any theory, and I would like to know what principle, if any, generates all of it.

1. Overpasses built low enough that buses cannot pass, so that only car owners can reach a public beach: political, on any criterion I would accept.
2. The same overpasses at the same clearance, built for drainage and sight-line reasons, where nobody involved registered the consequence for buses for a decade and where the effect on who reaches the beach is identical: also political. Intent strikes me as a red herring here, and this is the verdict I hold most firmly.
3. A speed bump where an officer used to enforce the limit: not political. The question of what speed is acceptable was settled in the open, by people who could be replaced; the bump is a cheaper way of carrying that settlement out. The politics is upstream.
4. Air conditioning: not political, despite the sealed office tower, the migration to the Sun Belt, and the changed electoral map. It made available a set of choices that people then made, in the ordinary political way, and could have made otherwise.
5. A facial-recognition system that performs worse on darker skin, where nobody intended it and nobody checked until after deployment: political. My reason is not simply that outcomes are unequal; it is that the artifact distributes a public benefit and burden along a line of subordination that already existed.
6. Shears shaped so that a left-handed grip forces the blades apart, imposing a real recurring cost on left-handers: not political. Left-handers are a minority but not a subordinate group, and nothing about their standing in the community is being settled.
7. Nuclear power as an inherently political technology, in the sense of requiring centralized and hierarchical management: rejected. Solar farms, container shipping, and data centers are at least as technically demanding and admit of either centralized or distributed organization. I suspect discarding this whole category costs me something, but I cannot say what.
8. An opt-out default for organ donation: political. A browser's default search engine: not, or at least not in the same way — yet both are defaults and both allocate something, and the only distinction I can produce is that the second is trivial, which is not a principle.
9. A lock on a door: not political, since it secures a property right the law already settled. Though the lock does something the law cannot, namely make that rule effective at three in the morning with no authority present, and I cannot tell whether that is taking over a decision or merely executing one. This is the case I keep going back and forth on.

Two pairs I cannot reconcile. First, 2 and 6: both are unintended differential effects that entrench a disadvantage, and I call one political and the other not. My stated reason is whether the affected group is subordinate, but that seems to import the very political judgment I was trying to locate in the artifact, and it makes the criterion's output depend on my own views about society rather than on anything about the thing. Second, 3 against 1 and 2: a device that merely carries out a decision made elsewhere is not political, but the overpasses also carry out a decision made elsewhere — unless in case 2 nobody decided anything at all, in which case my criterion must be doing work I cannot yet articulate.

So: state the principle, or the two or three best candidates, precisely enough that I could apply it to an unfamiliar artifact without consulting you again. Tie each clause to the verdict that motivates it — I want to know which of my cases is doing the work of which condition. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon. I would rather be told that 3 and 9 have to go than be handed a criterion with a discretionary fudge factor that saves all nine; if the price of consistency is complexity, show me the complexity rather than hiding it. Say also what would falsify the criterion: what sort of artifact, described in what way, would count against it.

Finally, apply it to three cases on which I am withholding my verdict, so that I can see whether the principle has independent traction. A stair-only apartment building constructed in 1910 and no longer alterable at reasonable cost, versus an otherwise identical building constructed last year under a different code, both excluding the same people. A programming language whose type system renders a whole class of error inexpressible, adopted voluntarily by its users rather than imposed on anyone. And a paper ballot counted by hand in public view versus an electronic machine whose software no outsider can inspect, where my gut says the difference is political but has nothing to do with which party wins and I cannot say why.
