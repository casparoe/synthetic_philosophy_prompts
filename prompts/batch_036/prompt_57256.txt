I am writing to ask for help with a speech, and I should say at the outset that I have no academic training of any kind. What follows is how the problem looks to a man who has spent fourteen years turning it over with a paint scraper in his hand.

I chair a small charitable trust that has just finished restoring a wooden lighthouse tender, built in 1911, which I will call the *Marabel*. She ran supplies to the lights along our stretch of coast until 1968, sat on a mooring for twenty more years, and came to us as a hull in a mudflat. We took her down to her backbone: the keel and the stem are hers, and almost everything else — planking, frames, deck beams, most of the fastenings — is new oak that we felled, sawn, and worked ourselves.

The original material we removed we did not burn. We gave it away in trailer loads to a boatbuilding school four hundred miles up the coast, as teaching stock. Over those same fourteen years their students rebuilt a tender out of it: her old frames, her old planking, her deck beams, her bell, and the carved nameboard from her transom, all of it spliced and scarfed where the rot had gone too far and set up again with new timber of their own. They launched last spring. She now sails, under the name that was carved on the board we gave away, in a harbour she never worked.

Our own launch is in three weeks. The county paper ran a piece in March about which of the two is the real *Marabel* and used the word rivalry, which is not accurate but is not nothing either. Several of their students and one of their instructors are coming as our guests, since four of them spent a summer here. The woman breaking the bottle is the granddaughter of the man who skippered her from 1946 until she was laid up. Her brother holds the other view and has said so in print. She has accepted invitations to both launches and has told me plainly that she does not want to be made to choose, and I have promised her she will not be.

So far as I can tell there are two answers, and I have a stake in one of them. Mine is this: what persisted through the fourteen years was not the timber but the working — one continuous activity, the trade passing from hand to hand, the same name kept and the same water returned to. The other answer is that she is the stuff she was made of: whoever has her timbers has her, and what we have built is a very faithful new boat standing on an old keel. I can see the objection to my own account and I cannot answer it, which is why I am asking. For fourteen years she was in pieces and went nowhere, so on my view what kept her in existence was the work rather than the sailing — and that begins to look like moving the difficulty somewhere less visible. Their account has the mirror-image trouble: their timber stood in a rack for years before a student touched it, and they too had to put in new oak wherever the rot had got there first. Neither crew's boat was ever simply continuous, and neither was ever simply the original material.

What I would like you to write is the address I give at our launch, of about five or six minutes read aloud, to an audience of shipwrights and volunteers, their apprentices, the family, the local council, and at least one reporter. It must satisfy the following:

1. No academic vocabulary, and no naming of philosophers or of the old puzzle about the ship. Everyone in that yard has heard the shape of the argument before; a lecture would lose them in a sentence.
2. It must not call their boat a copy or a replica, and it must not concede that ours is one. It must not disparage them, and it must not settle the matter by saying the question does not matter. To both crews it does.
3. It must come at the nameboard and the shared name directly, early, rather than steering around them, because the paper has already made them the story.
4. It must not lean on the fact that their boat sails on water she never worked. People keep telling me I can win with that. I do not think it settles anything and I would rather not be seen to think it does.
5. It must name Eddie Marsden, who cut most of our new frames and died in 2019 without ever seeing her float, and it must give the granddaughter something true to hear about her grandfather, without requiring her presence to count as a verdict.
6. It must end with a short, speakable sending-off — the thing I say at the actual moment, before the bottle — in which I state what I mean by saying she is the same boat, in terms a shipwright would not be embarrassed to repeat.

Before the speech, in one paragraph and no more, tell me whether you think my own view is right. If you think it is wrong or incomplete, say what I should hold instead and why, and write the speech on that basis instead of on mine. I would much rather be corrected now than stand up in front of both crews and say something I cannot defend. I am not looking for a compromise that turns out to be a shrug: I want a position I can say aloud and stand behind, delivered so that the people who hold the other one can hear it without insult.
