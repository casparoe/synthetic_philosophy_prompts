I'm TA-ing for an undergrad philosophy-of-law seminar and we're doing a unit on punishment next week. The reading is the usual retributivism vs. consequentialism material, and the discussion always lands in the same rut: the retributivists look like they just want revenge dressed up in a suit, and the consequentialists look like they'd happily punish the innocent if the numbers worked out. So I want to hand the students something more concrete to fight about, and I'd like you to write it as a dialogue they can actually read aloud in class.

Here's the setup I cooked up. In the near-ish future a state has a safe, well-tested drug — call it Quietus — that reliably suppresses violent impulses and the reactive emotions feeding them for about five years, with a modest but real side effect of also dulling musical and aesthetic pleasure. The state offers it to some violent offenders as an alternative to a long custodial sentence; the offender signs a consent form, the recidivism data says it works better than prison at preventing future harm, and it costs a small fraction as much. Your two characters: (1) a retributivist — a sentencing judge or a philosopher, doesn't matter — who genuinely thinks desert matters and that punishment is owed rather than merely useful, and (2) a penal reformer with broadly consequentialist commitments who thinks the whole point of the system is harm reduction and that the retributivist is squeamish about a new tool becuase it's new. Neither one gets to be a strawman. Give the retributivist the good arguments: that the drug bypasses the offender's practical reasoning instead of addressing it as a responsible agent; that it damages capacities (aesthetic sensibility, say) that have nothing to do with the offender's culpability and therefore can't be sized to desert; that a choice made under the shadow of twelve years in a cell isn't the kind of consent that can carry that much moral weight. And let the consequentialist land real hits: that we already shorten or substitute sentences when an offender ages out of violence or undergoes some conversion in prison, so what precisely is different when a pill does the same work; that the retributivist's own principles can't explain why a manufactured change of heart is objectionable while a spontaneous one is welcome; that declining the drug means choosing a decade of misery that benefits literally nobody.

Make it genuine back-and-forth — they should pick up each other's actual points rather than alternate speeches, and at least one of them ought to get pushed into conceding something substantive along the way. But no conversions: end somewhere that leaves my students with a live question instead of a winner. Roughly 1200–1800 words, keep the register like two professionals arguing across a conference table (a bit of heat is fine), and don't cite or name-drop specific books and articles, since the students won't have read them. One extra thing I'd like woven in: have them disagree even about what category the thing falls into — is Quietus a punishment, a treatment, or neither, and does that classification actually decide anything?
