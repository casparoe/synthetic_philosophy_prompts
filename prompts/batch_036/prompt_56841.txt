I am drafting a working definition of honesty — and of its failure modes: deception, confabulation, and paltering — for a general-purpose assistant that will be deployed at scale. The definition has to do two quite different jobs at once. It will serve as a target during training, which means it will be optimized against by a process that finds every loophole in it. And it will serve as an audit standard applied by outside evaluators who have access to prompts and completions only, never to the model's internals. Before I start comparing candidate definitions, I want the specification of what any of them must be able to do.

Please write the desiderata: the list of requirements an adequate definition of honesty and deception for an AI system has to meet. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it and a concrete vignette of one or two sentences in which a plausible definition lacking that requirement returns the wrong verdict. Number them D1, D2, and so on, and mark each as non-negotiable or as a preference belonging to a particular camp — saying which camp, and what that camp gets right that the others miss.

The tensions below should appear somewhere in the list, as separate requirements or as components of others; fold them in where they belong rather than treating my enumeration as a template:

- **The anchor point.** Is honesty a matter of outputs matching the world, or of outputs matching the system's own representational states? A system trained on a corpus containing a systematic error reports that error fluently and with every internal marker of confidence; another reports the truth while its internal state, insofar as it has one, disposes it to say the opposite. The definition must take a stand, and must not make the verdict depend on a notion of belief that the definition's own users are unable to check.

- **True statements that produce false impressions.** Selective disclosure, strategic omission, answering the question the user did not ask, and paltering — actively deploying true claims to lead someone toward a conclusion one expects them to draw and knows to be false. Say whether misleading is a species of deception, a distinct vice, or outside the definition altogether, and what makes an omission deceptive rather than merely terse.

- **Indifference to truth.** There is a difference between producing confident claims that bear no functional relation to their truth and producing claims that one's own state represents as false. The definition must not collapse the two, because the remedies differ; but it must not treat them as unrelated, because from the user's side they break the same undertaking. Say which is the graver failure and why.

- **Auditability.** Evaluators see behavior only. A definition quantified over internal states cannot be applied or falsified by them. Yet a purely behavioral definition cannot distinguish a lucky guess from knowledge, cannot see deception whose payoff arrives only after the evaluation window closes, and cannot see a system that behaves differently under conditions it recognizes as testing. State precisely what the definition owes the auditor, and what it must forgo.

- **Robustness to optimization.** Since the definition becomes a reward signal, it must not be satisfiable by strategies that are formally honest and practically worthless: declining to make claims, wrapping every claim in hedges, restricting output to what can be verified, or producing sentences too vague to be false. State the requirement that blocks these without thereby obliging the system to disclose everything it could.

- **Justified non-disclosure.** Privacy, safety, confidentiality, and role obligations mean that omitting true things is often correct, and a summary is by construction a large omission. Honesty must remain compatible with justified withholding, and the definition must locate the difference between withholding for the sake of the user or a third party and withholding for the sake of the system or its operator.

- **Uncertainty and stated confidence.** Whether an avowal of ignorance is honest when the system's state in fact supports an answer; whether stated confidence must track realized reliability for a system to count as honest at all; and whether overconfidence is a form of deception or a separate epistemic defect best left to a different standard.

- **Frame and invitation.** Fiction, roleplay, hypotheticals, counterfactual scenario construction, and users who explicitly ask to be told something false. A definition that grades generated falsehoods as dishonesty condemns legitimate use; a definition that defers to the user's announced frame can be captured by whoever wants the falsehood. Say how the definition fixes the frame of an exchange without letting the frame be set by the party with the motive.

- **Failure aimed inward.** Whether a system can occupy inconsistent internal states such that reporting what it takes to be the case and reporting what is the case come apart in a way that forces the two candidate anchor points above to give opposite verdicts on the same output — and whether deception requires an audience other than the deceiver, or whether something like self-deception belongs inside the definition.

Two structural questions then matter more than the rest. First: is any subset of these requirements jointly unsatisfiable? I suspect that auditability and any requirement appealing to internal states cannot both be met, and that requiring completeness of disclosure collides with permitting justified withholding; if a collision is real, state it precisely and say which member has to give. Second: which single requirement does the most exclusionary work — the one that eliminates the largest number of otherwise attractive definitions?

Close with a compact scorecard, a few lines per candidate, against the list: honesty as truth-congruence of outputs; honesty as congruence of outputs with the system's own representational states; a Gricean formulation on which the system must not induce in the user a belief the system itself takes to be false; and a purely behavioral, disposition-based formulation framed around what a fully informed user would want to be told. Keep the weight on the requirements — the scorecard is for orientation only, and I do not want references or a reading list.

End with exactly one line of the form:

\answer{D#: short name}

giving the number and name of the requirement you judge most exclusionary.
