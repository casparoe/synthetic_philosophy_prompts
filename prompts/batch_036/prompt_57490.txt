I chair a city council's commemoration review panel: six lay volunteers with no legal or historical training, roughly forty petitions a year asking that a street, statue, plaque, or building name be removed, renamed, or recontextualized. We get ninety minutes per petition and must produce a written recommendation that will survive public scrutiny whichever way it goes. Please write a decision procedure we can actually run: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it terminates in retain, where in remove, and where it must stop and hand the matter back to the council because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts.

It has to handle four things we currently fumble.

First, classification. A name on a hospital wing, an equestrian statue in a courthouse square, and a street sign do not do the same work; the procedure must sort the object before it weighs the person. Tell me which of the honor-versus-record distinctions must survive the simplification and which can be dropped, and at what cost.

Second, the standards-of-the-time objection. The procedure may not license pure presentism, but neither may it excuse everything by appeal to the era. I want an explicit test turning on whether the conduct was contestable within the honoree's own milieu and whether the honoree was confronted with it by contemporaries — and a rule for what to do when the historical record is silent on both.

Third, aggregation. A grave wrong against a substantial public legacy: the procedure must say whether these are weighed against each other and, if so, how, without collapsing into arithmetic and without letting the legacy purchase immunity.

Fourth, consistency across objects. One honoree may appear on three streets and a bridge. The procedure must say whether a verdict on one object binds the others, or whether different objects may legitimately diverge, and who resolves a conflict.

Then run it on three petitions: (A) an 1870s physician whose free clinic still serves the neighborhood, and who also performed non-consensual experiments on inmates of the county poorhouse; (B) a suffragist honored with the courthouse-square statue, whose published views on immigration restriction we would now call eugenicist; (C) a bridge named for an industrialist whose fortune rested partly on a company town's wage-scrip system, where the union petitioning for removal is itself funded by his foundation. At least one of these should come out retain — tell me which one will embarrass us and why the procedure is nonetheless right to reach it.

Finally, be honest about the failure modes: where the procedure will be gamed by petitioners, and where it will deliver an answer that is defensible but wrong.
