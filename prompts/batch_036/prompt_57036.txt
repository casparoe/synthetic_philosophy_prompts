I'm auditing a philosophy of physics course this semester — my degree was in chemistry, so anything with a Lagrangian in it washes straight over me, but last week's seminar was on the direction of time and I've been trying to pin down what I actually believe about it. Partly because my flatmate, who's doing the same course, insists my views dont hang together and I can't tell whether he's right or just enjoying himself, and partly because I want to know if there's one principle generating all of my intuitions here or whether I'm just waving at four or five different things and calling it a single view. So here's the list, in roughly the order the thoughts came:

1. A film of two billiard balls colliding and rebounding: I genuinely can't tell which way it's running, and I don't think anything is objectionable about either direction.
2. A film of gas let out of a small partitioned corner spreading to fill the whole box: instantly obvious which way, and the reverse would be objectionable.
3. Same for an egg hitting the kitchen floor.
4. But a film of the smashed egg leaping back up onto the table and reassembling — I want to say that's not just fantastically unlikely. I want to say it's not allowed, full stop. There's a difference between improbable and impossible and this is on the impossible side of it.
5. I do accept that the microscopic laws are (basically) time-reversal invariant, that every trajectory has a reversed twin which is just as lawful. I know there's a little bit of T-violation somewhere in the weak interaction but I don't think that has anything to do with any of this.
6. A stone dropped in a pond: waves travel out to the edges and that's it. The version where waves come in coherently from all the edges and converge on the stone, launching it back up into my hand, looks to me like a perfectly legal solution of the wave equation, but it doesn't happen and never will.
7. I have mountains of knowledge about the past — photos, fossils, tree rings, my own memories, other people's testimony — and essentially nothing comparable about particular future events.
8. Except that I know the sun will rise tomorrow, that the planets will still be going round, that I'll be a year older at my next birthday, that this coffee will be cold in an hour. In some sense I know more about next Tuesday than about what I had for lunch three weeks ago.
9. I can affect the future and not the past, and I think the reason is simply that the past has already happened. It's fixed. That just seems like a complete explanation to me.
10. The fact that we remember one direction and anticipate the other isn't a seperate brute fact about minds, I think — it's downstream of whatever makes 2 through 6 asymmetric.
11. But I also think I can coherently imagine a creature whose memory-like states run the other way, and I would not want to say time itself flows backwards for them. So the direction seems to me to be a single global objective feature, not something that can vary from creature to creature or region to region.
12. The early universe was in an absurdly special, low-entropy state (the past hypothesis, I think it's called?). I'm not willing to shrug and call that a brute starting point — it feels like exactly the sort of thing that demands an explanation. But I feel no comparable urge to explain why the final state of the universe isn't special too, and I can't say why the demand is one-sided.
13. If the universe stopped expanding and started recollapsing, I think things would still get messier on the way in and the arrow would keep pointing the same way. I don't buy that it flips at the turnaround.

So: is there a single principle behind these, or a small number of candidate principles that compete for the job? Give me up to three, stated precisely enough that I could tell whether a new case falls under them, and for each one tie the clauses back to the verdicts that motivate them — which of my numbered commitments each clause is there to capture. Then tell me what each candidate says about cases I haven't listed: a machine that sorts a shuffled deck back into suits and ranks while the room heats up; somebody who claims to remember next Tuesday and what would count as evidence for them; a cup of coffee that spontaneously warms itself by drawing heat out of a cooler room. And then be blunt about which of my verdicts each candidate forces me to abandon, and rank those costs — I don't want a list where every tension is presented as equally bad, because I'm sure some of these only look like they conflict and dissolve once you get the distinction right. Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence — whether there is one principle and which single verdict I have to give up — and then the reasoning, no preamble and no restating my list back to me. And please no bibliography or recommended reading; I can't go look anything up right now, I just want the argument.
