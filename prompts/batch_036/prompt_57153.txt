I've been talked into giving the opening remarks at a two-day workshop next month and I'm kind of dreading it. It's the kickoff for a five-year collaborative project on the history of epidemics, and it's funded, so there's a program officer in the room who is mostly there to hear somebody say the word deliverable. About forty people total: clinical epidemiologists and a couple of climate modelers on one side, social historians of medicine on the other, two literary scholars, and one very famous journalist whose last book argued, in effect, that a bacterium reorganized Europe. He's charming about it, but he'll be in the front row.

The stated purpose of the two days is to agree on a shared vocabulary, and the honest truth is that the two halves of the room don't currently hear the same sentences the same way. When an epidemiologist says the pathogen drove the mortality curve, they mean something precise and defensible about transmission dynamics. When a historian hears it, they hear a sentence in which nobody decided anything: no one built the tenements, no one cut the quarantine budget, no one picked which neighborhoods got the water main. And the historians have their own failure mode, which is writing an outbreak as though it were purely a story about power, with the biology as set dressing you could swap out for a different microbe and change nothing.

So I want the remarks to do exactly one thing: get both halves to stay in the room for two days. Not adjudicate between them. I have two devices I use and I want them in there doing actual work, rather then getting name-dropped:

- The exposure question. Behind nearly any claim of the form "X caused Y" where X is a germ or a climate or a volcano, there's a prior fact about who was standing where, and somebody usually arranged that. Ask who was exposed to X and who made that arrangement, and the sentence either gains a mechanism or turns out to have been an alibi.
- The substitution test. Rewrite the sentence with the non-human term replaced by the human decisions that set the exposure, and see what's left. If nothing's left, the germ was decoration. If the rewritten sentence comes out false or empty in a way you can point to — if it can't explain why this outbreak and not the one three valleys over with the same landlords — then the non-human term was carrying real weight and you keep it.

Constraints, sorry, there are several. Five to six minutes spoken, so call it 700–800 words. No citations, and no named books or authors at all — the room doesn't need me to wave at the literature. I want one joke inside the first sixty seconds that a modeler and a historian can both laugh at without either feeling like the butt of it; the safest target is our own sentences, not each other's fields. And I want one deliberate, flagged personification somewhere in the middle — a sentence where I let a volcano have a vote, say, and then immediately show my work by running the substitution test on it, so the historians can hear that I know what I just did. Otherwise: no microbe, climate, or mountain as the grammatical subject of a verb of intention anywhere in the speech. That's a rule I'm setting for myself because the first time I break it, half the room stops listening.

Also please don't let it drift into actor-network talk about the agency of objects. Nobody needs metaphysics at nine in the morning, and it's the one move that would get both halves rolling their eyes at once. And I'd rather the speech not resolve whether germs count as actors — it should make the case that this is the wrong question for a kickoff, and say why, in a sentence a program officer would find useful.

Format, three sections in this order:

1. **The speech** — the 700–800 words, in paragraphs I can actually read aloud, no stage directions except where I need a beat of pause.
2. **Grammar ledger** — every sentence in the speech where a non-human thing is doing something, each with a one-line note on whether it survives the substitution test or is the flagged exception. Under 200 words.
3. **If the room turns out to be mostly modelers** — one replacement paragraph for the third quarter of the speech, plus two sentences on what the swap costs me.
